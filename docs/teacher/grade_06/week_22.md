# Teacher's Guide — 6th Grade, Week 22

## English Language Arts 6: The Story of My Life, chapters 1-4

**Prepare:** Gather: 'The Story of My Life' by Helen Keller (Project Gutenberg). Download 'The Story of My Life' (Project Gutenberg). Read chapters 1–4 yourself.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Helen was born blind and deaf. She lost sight and hearing from an illness at about 19 months.
- Using commas for every interruption. Dashes add emphasis; parentheses play information down; commas are neutral.

### Day 1 (Monday): Early childhood
*Student:* Read chapters 1-2 and list how Keller introduces her illness and frustrations; note how the adult Keller looks back on her child self.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 1–2. The child lists how Keller describes her illness and frustration, noting the adult looking back.

- **Q:** How did Helen lose her sight and hearing?
  **A:** An illness when she was about 19 months old left her blind and deaf.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): The 'water' moment
*Student:* Read chapters 3-4 and analyze the water-pump moment: how does Keller's word choice show it was life-changing?

*How to teach:* Read chapters 3–4. Reread the well-house passage aloud; analyze word choice.

- **Q:** How does Keller's language show the water moment was life-changing?
  **A:** She says 'That living word awakened my soul, gave it light, hope, joy, set it free!' — words of light and freedom show language opened her mind.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Commas, parentheses, dashes
*Student:* Lesson on setting off nonrestrictive and parenthetical elements; punctuate 15 sentences about Keller three ways and discuss the effect.

*How to teach:* Teach nonrestrictive elements and the three punctuation choices. The child punctuates 15 sentences.

- **Q:** Punctuate three ways: Anne Sullivan her teacher arrived in 1887.
  **A:** 'Anne Sullivan, her teacher, arrived…'; 'Anne Sullivan (her teacher) arrived…'; 'Anne Sullivan—her teacher—arrived…'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Point of view
*Student:* Write a paragraph on Keller's purpose in writing her autobiography, with two quotations.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph on Keller's purpose with two quotations.

- **Q:** Why might Keller have written her autobiography?
  **A:** To show others how language and education changed her life and to honor her teacher; to help people understand the experience of being deaf and blind.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Reading quiz on chapters 1-4 and punctuation quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the reading and punctuation quizzes.

- **Q:** Who was Anne Sullivan?
  **A:** Helen's teacher from the Perkins Institution, who arrived in 1887 and taught her language through finger-spelling.

**Assessment:** Quiz and purpose paragraph.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) born in Tuscumbia, Alabama (1880) 2) an illness at 19 months left her blind and deaf 3) Anne Sullivan arrives March 3, 1887 — 'the most important day I remember in all my life' 4) the doll and the mug/water confusion 5) at the well-house, water poured over one hand while Sullivan spelled 'w-a-t-e-r' in the other. Punctuation: 'Anne Sullivan, her teacher, arrived in 1887.' / '…(her teacher)…' / '—her teacher—'. Purpose paragraph rubric: 4 = states a purpose (e.g., to share how language opened her world) with two quotes explained; 3 = one quote; 2 = summary; 1 = off-topic.

## Mathematics 6: Equivalent expressions

**Prepare:** Gather: algebra tiles (paper). Cut paper algebra tiles: long rectangles for x, small squares for 1.

**Watch for:**
- Distributing only to the first term: 3(x + 2) = 3x + 2. Draw the area model to show 3 is multiplied by both x and 2.
- Combining unlike terms (2x + 5 = 7x). Only like terms (same variable part) combine.

### Day 1 (Monday): Same value, different form
*Student:* Substitute several values into pairs like 3(x + 2) and 3x + 6 to test equivalence, recording results in a table.

*How to teach:* Make a table with x = 0, 1, 5, 10 and evaluate both 3(x + 2) and 3x + 6. Then try 3(x + 2) vs 3x + 2.

- **Q:** Are 3(x + 2) and 3x + 2 equivalent?
  **A:** No — at x = 0 they give 6 and 2.
- **Q:** Values of 3(x + 2) and 3x + 6 at x = 5?
  **A:** Both 21.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Distributive property with variables
*Student:* Use area diagrams to expand 4(x + 3) and factor 6x + 18 (12 problems).

*How to teach:* Draw a rectangle 4 tall and (x + 3) wide, split into 4·x and 4·3. Reverse it for factoring.

- **Q:** Expand 4(x + 3) and 5(2y − 1).
  **A:** 4x + 12; 10y − 5.
- **Q:** Factor 6x + 18.
  **A:** 6(x + 3).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Combining like terms
*Student:* Use paper algebra tiles to simplify expressions like 2x + 5 + 3x + 1 and explain why x + x is 2x.

*How to teach:* Lay out 2 x-tiles + 5 unit tiles + 3 x-tiles + 1 unit tile; group like tiles.

- **Q:** Simplify 2x + 5 + 3x + 1.
  **A:** 5x + 6.
- **Q:** Why is x + x equal to 2x, not x²?
  **A:** Two of the same unknown amount added is 2 times x; x² would be x multiplied by x.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Prove or disprove
*Student:* Decide whether 8 pairs of expressions are equivalent and justify with properties or a counterexample.

*How to teach:* For each pair, the child either names a property (distributive, commutative) or finds a value that gives different results.

- **Q:** Is 2(x + 4) equivalent to 2x + 4? Justify.
  **A:** No. Counterexample x = 1: 2(5) = 10 but 2 + 4 = 6.
- **Q:** Is x · x equivalent to 2x?
  **A:** No: x = 3 gives 9 and 6.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on generating and identifying equivalent expressions.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz independently.

- **Q:** Factor 9a + 6.
  **A:** 3(3a + 2).

**Assessment:** Equivalent-expressions quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) 4(x + 3) = 4x + 12 2) 7(2 + y) = 14 + 7y 3) 12x + 20 = 4(3x + 5) 4) 9a + 6 = 3(3a + 2) 5) 3x + 4 + 5x − 1 = 8x + 3 6) 2(x + 4) and 2x + 8: equivalent 7) 5x − x and 5: not equivalent (x = 2 gives 8 and 5) 8) 6(x + 1) and 6x + 1: not equivalent (x = 0 gives 6 and 1).

## Science 6 (Integrated): Potential energy

**Prepare:** Gather: rubber bands, ruler, ball, magnets, PhET Energy Skate Park. Fill a pan with flour or sand; bookmark PhET Energy Skate Park.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking potential energy only comes from height. Stretched/compressed objects and magnets also store energy.
- Thinking total energy changes on a roller coaster. It converts between forms; without friction the total stays the same.

### Day 1 (Monday): Height and energy
*Student:* Drop a ball into flour or sand from 3 heights and measure crater depth as evidence of stored energy.

*How to teach:* Drop a ball from three heights into flour; measure crater depth.

- **Q:** Why are craters deeper from higher drops?
  **A:** The ball had more gravitational potential energy, becoming more kinetic energy on impact.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Elastic energy
*Student:* Launch rubber bands from a ruler at different stretch lengths and measure distance traveled.

*How to teach:* Launch rubber bands from a ruler at different stretches (aim away from people).

- **Q:** How does stretch distance affect launch distance?
  **A:** More stretch stores more elastic potential energy, so the band flies farther.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Magnetic potential energy
*Student:* Hold two magnets at different separations and explain where energy is stored in the system.

*How to teach:* Hold magnets at different separations.

- **Q:** When is more energy stored in two attracting magnets: close together or pulled apart?
  **A:** Pulled apart — you did work to separate them, and they can snap together.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Energy Skate Park
*Student:* Use PhET Energy Skate Park to track potential and kinetic energy bar graphs as the skater moves.

*How to teach:* Track bar graphs in Energy Skate Park.

- **Q:** Where is the skater's kinetic energy greatest?
  **A:** At the lowest point of the track.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draw energy models for a roller coaster at four points and take a quiz on potential energy.

*How to teach:* Draw roller-coaster energy models; give the quiz.

- **Q:** What happens to energy 'lost' to friction?
  **A:** It becomes thermal energy (and sound); it is not destroyed.

**Assessment:** Energy model and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) higher object = more gravitational potential energy 2) more stretch = more elastic potential energy 3) pulling apart attracting magnets stores more energy in the system 4) at the top of a coaster PE is greatest and KE least 5) at the bottom KE is greatest. Energy model rubric: four points labeled with PE and KE bars that trade off and a constant total (minus thermal energy from friction).

## World History and Geography: Ancient Civilizations: The Indus Valley civilization

**Prepare:** Gather: map of South Asia, images of Mohenjo-daro and Indus seals. Print a South Asia map and images of Mohenjo-daro and Indus seals.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Indus script has been read. It hasn't — no bilingual text exists and inscriptions are very short.
- Accepting the old 'Aryan invasion destroyed the cities' theory as fact. Evidence points more to environmental change.

### Day 1 (Monday): Geography and monsoons
*Student:* Map the Indus and Ganges rivers, Himalayas and Deccan Plateau and explain the monsoon cycle.

*How to teach:* Map rivers, mountains, plateau; explain monsoons.

- **Q:** What are monsoons?
  **A:** Seasonal winds; summer monsoons bring heavy rain, winter monsoons are dry.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Planned cities
*Student:* Study a plan of Mohenjo-daro and list evidence of planning (grid streets, drains, Great Bath).

*How to teach:* Study Mohenjo-daro's plan.

- **Q:** Give two pieces of evidence that Mohenjo-daro was planned.
  **A:** Grid streets, covered drains, standardized bricks, the Great Bath.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mystery of the seals
*Student:* Examine Indus seals and explain why the script is still undeciphered.

*How to teach:* Examine seals.

- **Q:** Why can't scholars read the Indus script?
  **A:** No bilingual text like the Rosetta Stone, and inscriptions are very short.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Decline theories
*Student:* Evaluate three theories for the decline of Harappan cities and choose the best supported.

*How to teach:* Evaluate decline theories.

- **Q:** Which decline theory is best supported?
  **A:** Most scholars favor environmental change — shifting rivers and weaker monsoons.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on Indian geography and the Indus civilization.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz.

- **Q:** Name two cities of the Indus Valley civilization.
  **A:** Harappa and Mohenjo-daro.

**Assessment:** Quiz and decline-theory evaluation.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Indus and Ganges rivers; Himalayas; Deccan Plateau 2) monsoons — summer winds bring heavy rain, winter winds dry 3) Harappa and Mohenjo-daro 4) planning: grid streets, covered drains, standardized bricks and weights, Great Bath 5) script undeciphered. Evaluation — 4: explains three theories (climate change/weaker monsoons, rivers shifting or drying, floods/overused land, outdated invasion theory), chooses one with evidence.

## Visual and Performing Arts 6: Music and other arts: film scoring

**Prepare:** Find a public-domain Charlie Chaplin short (e.g., 'The Immigrant,' 1917) and an example of a famous leitmotif (e.g., the two-note 'Jaws' theme or Darth Vader's 'Imperial March').

**Watch for:**
- Thinking film music should be noticed all the time. Often it supports the scene quietly.
- Choosing music that doesn't match the action. Tempo and dynamics should follow events.

### Day 1 (Monday): Film scores
*Student:* Watch a film scene with and without sound and describe how music changes the meaning.

*How to teach:* Watch a scene with and without sound; describe how music changes meaning. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** How can music change the meaning of a scene?
  **A:** It sets mood and tells viewers how to feel—the same scene can seem scary or funny.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Leitmotif
*Student:* Identify leitmotifs in a film score example and create a leitmotif for a character.

*How to teach:* Identify leitmotifs; create one for a character. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** What is a leitmotif?
  **A:** A short musical theme associated with a character, place or idea.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Score a scene
*Student:* Create music or sound effects to accompany a short public-domain silent film clip.

*How to teach:* Create music or sound effects for a silent film clip. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** Why sync music to action?
  **A:** Matching hits and changes to on-screen events makes the scene more powerful.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Final composition
*Student:* Revise your ABA composition and perform or play back the final version.

*How to teach:* Revise the ABA composition; perform or play back. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What did you revise?
  **A:** Answer varies; should cite feedback.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & critique
*Student:* Evaluate your composition and film score with criteria and write a reflection.

*How to teach:* Evaluate composition and film score; write a reflection. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** How did tempo or dynamics support your film scene?
  **A:** Answer varies; should link specific choices to action/mood.

**Assessment:** Final composition and film score presentation.

**Answer key:** Presentation: final ABA composition with revisions explained; film score synced to clip with a leitmotif. Rubric 1–4 for composition quality, sync and mood, reflection.

## Physical Education 6: Modified softball games

**Prepare:** Gather: whiffle ball and bat, bases, gloves. Whiffle ball set, bases, gloves. Check the space for hazards, have water ready, and start every session with the dynamic warm-up.

**Watch for:**
- Standing still when the ball isn't hit to you. Back up teammates.
- Arguing calls.

### Day 1 (Monday): Positions
*Student:* Learn infield and outfield positions and backing-up responsibilities.

*How to teach:* Learn positions and backing up.

- **Q:** Who backs up a throw to first base?
  **A:** The right fielder (and catcher on infield grounders).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Game 1
*Student:* Play a modified softball game with every player batting each inning.

*How to teach:* Game 1, everyone bats each inning.

- **Q:** How many outs end a half-inning?
  **A:** Three.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Game 2
*Student:* Play again, rotating positions.

*How to teach:* Game 2, rotate positions.

- **Q:** What is the shortstop's area?
  **A:** Between second and third base.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Home run derby
*Student:* Hold a placement derby where points depend on hitting target zones.

*How to teach:* Placement derby.

- **Q:** Why hit to open space?
  **A:** Fielders can't reach it quickly.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Take a quiz on striking and fielding rules and strategies.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz.

- **Q:** What is a foul ball?
  **A:** A ball hit outside the foul lines.

**Assessment:** Rules and strategy quiz.

**Answer key:** Rules and strategy quiz: three outs per inning; positions (pitcher, catcher, first, second, third, shortstop, left, center, right); backing up; force vs. tag; fair vs. foul ball; strategy — throw ahead of the lead runner, hit to open spaces.

## Health 6: First aid basics II

**Prepare:** Find the American Heart Association Hands-Only CPR video and an epinephrine auto-injector trainer video. Use a pillow for practice — never practice thrusts or compressions on a person.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking you should give abdominal thrusts to someone who's coughing hard. If they can cough or speak, encourage coughing; act only if they can't breathe, cough or speak.
- Waiting to see if an allergic reaction improves. Signs of anaphylaxis need the epinephrine auto-injector and 911 right away.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choking
*Student:* Learn signs of choking and practice abdominal-thrust hand position on a pillow (no thrusts on people).

*How to teach:* Learn choking signs (hands at throat, can't speak, cough or breathe) and abdominal-thrust hand position on a pillow (fist above navel, below breastbone).

- **Q:** What is the universal sign of choking?
  **A:** Hands clutching the throat.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Allergic reactions
*Student:* Learn signs of anaphylaxis and how an epinephrine auto-injector trainer is used.

*How to teach:* Learn anaphylaxis signs (hives, swelling of lips/tongue/throat, trouble breathing, vomiting) and auto-injector use with a trainer.

- **Q:** Where is an epinephrine auto-injector used?
  **A:** Pressed into the outer thigh (through clothing if needed), held for the time stated on the device, and 911 is called.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hands-only CPR intro
*Student:* Watch an American Heart Association Hands-Only CPR video and practice compression rhythm on a pillow.

*How to teach:* Watch AHA Hands-Only CPR; practice compressions on a pillow to 'Stayin' Alive.' Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What are the two steps of Hands-Only CPR?
  **A:** Call 911, then push hard and fast in the center of the chest (100–120 per minute).

### Day 4 (Thursday): 911 or not?
*Student:* Decide in 10 scenarios whether to call 911, call a parent, or give first aid.

*How to teach:* Decide in 10 scenarios: call 911, call a parent, or give first aid. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** Someone collapses and isn't breathing. What do you do?
  **A:** Call 911 (or have someone call), start Hands-Only CPR, and send someone for an AED if available.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take the Injury Prevention and First Aid unit test.

*How to teach:* Take the Injury Prevention and First Aid unit test. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** Why push to the beat of 'Stayin' Alive'?
  **A:** Its tempo is about 100–120 beats per minute, the right compression rate.

**Assessment:** Unit test.

**Answer key:** Unit test key: home hazards and alarms; Poison Control 1-800-222-1222; rip current; concussion; heat stroke = 911; helmet law under 18; Check–Call–Care; direct pressure; burns cool water; RICE; choking signs; anaphylaxis = epinephrine + 911; Hands-Only CPR 100–120/min. Proficient = 80%.
