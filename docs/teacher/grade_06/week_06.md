# Teacher's Guide — 6th Grade, Week 6

## English Language Arts 6: Hatchet chapters 5-9: first lessons

**Prepare:** Gather: Hatchet, ReadWorks or CommonLit wilderness survival article, National Park Service 'Ten Essentials' list. Print the National Park Service 'Ten Essentials' list and one survival article (ReadWorks/CommonLit). Preview: Brian gets sick from berries and is attacked by a porcupine.

**Watch for:**
- Including opinions in an objective summary ('This awesome article…'). Summaries report only the central idea and key details.
- Confusing personification with any description. Personification gives human qualities to non-human things ('the fire was a friend').

### Day 1 (Monday): Shelter and food
*Student:* Read chapters 5-6 and list Brian's survival decisions with page numbers; rate each decision smart or risky.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 5–6 (note page numbers). The child lists decisions and rates each smart or risky.

- **Q:** Name one smart decision and one risky decision Brian makes early.
  **A:** Smart: building a shelter under the rock overhang near water. Risky: eating large amounts of the unknown 'gut cherries,' which make him sick.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Self-pity
*Student:* Read chapters 7-8; write a paragraph explaining Brian's lesson that self-pity 'didn't work', citing two quotes.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 7–8. The child writes a paragraph on the self-pity lesson with two quotes.

- **Q:** What does Brian learn after the porcupine attack?
  **A:** That feeling sorry for himself and crying doesn't change anything — self-pity didn't work, so he has to keep trying.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fire
*Student:* Read chapter 9 and annotate how Paulsen describes fire as a 'friend'; identify personification.

*How to teach:* Read chapter 9 and annotate how fire is described like a companion.

- **Q:** How does Paulsen personify the fire?
  **A:** He calls it a friend and describes it as if it were alive and keeping Brian company and safe, giving it human qualities.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Survival nonfiction
*Student:* Read a wilderness-survival article and the Ten Essentials list, then summarize the central idea objectively in 4-5 sentences.

*How to teach:* Read the article and Ten Essentials list; model the first sentence of an objective summary.

- **Q:** What does 'objective' mean in a summary?
  **A:** Neutral — only the text's ideas, no personal opinions or reactions.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Compare Brian's priorities with the article's advice in a Venn diagram and take a chapters 5-9 reading quiz.

*How to teach:* The child completes a Venn diagram and takes the quiz.

- **Q:** Name one thing Brian lacks that is on the Ten Essentials list.
  **A:** Examples: a map/navigation, extra food, extra water, first aid kit, fire starter, sun protection, extra clothing, a light, emergency shelter.

**Assessment:** Objective summary and reading quiz.

**Answer key:** Objective summary rubric: 4 = states the article's central idea in the first sentence, 3–4 key details in the author's order, no opinion, own words, 4–5 sentences; 3 = one opinion or a missing key detail; 2 = retelling or copying; 1 = off-topic. Reading quiz (ch. 5–9): 1) shelter under a rock overhang by the lake 2) 'gut cherries' make him sick; he finds raspberries 3) a bear at the raspberries leaves him alone 4) a porcupine attacks at night; quills in his leg 5) the hatchet striking rock makes sparks, leading to fire 6) lesson: self-pity doesn't work.

## Mathematics 6: Equivalent ratios and double number lines

**Prepare:** Gather: grid paper, grocery receipts. Look for receipts showing several identical items (e.g., 3 cans).

**Watch for:**
- Adding the same number to both quantities (3:4 → 5:6) instead of multiplying. Show with a drink recipe that adding changes the taste.
- Uneven tick marks on a double number line. Both lines must use equally spaced ticks that line up.

### Day 1 (Monday): Defining equivalent ratios
*Student:* Generate five ratios equivalent to 3:4 by multiplying both quantities, then decide whether 6 lists of ratio pairs are equivalent.

*How to teach:* Model making 3:4 into 6:8 and 9:12 by multiplying both numbers by the same number. The child lists five equivalent ratios and checks the 6 lists.

- **Q:** List four ratios equivalent to 3:4.
  **A:** Examples: 6:8, 9:12, 12:16, 15:20.
- **Q:** Are 3:5 and 5:7 equivalent?
  **A:** No — you can't multiply both 3 and 5 by the same number to get 5 and 7.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Double number lines
*Student:* Draw double number lines for a 2-mile per 30-minute walking pace and answer 6 questions about time and distance.

*How to teach:* Draw two parallel lines, miles on top and minutes below, with ticks at 0, 1, 2, 3... miles and 0, 15, 30, 45... minutes. The child answers the 6 questions from the diagram.

- **Q:** Walking 2 miles in 30 minutes, how long for 5 miles?
  **A:** 75 minutes (15 minutes per mile).
- **Q:** How far in 45 minutes?
  **A:** 3 miles.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Price per item
*Student:* Use real grocery receipts to build double number lines for cost and quantity and find the cost of 1, 5 and 12 items.

*How to teach:* Find a receipt item bought in multiples (or invent: 3 cans for $2.40). The child builds a double number line for cost and quantity and finds cost of 1, 5 and 12 items.

- **Q:** 3 cans cost $2.40. What do 1, 5 and 12 cans cost?
  **A:** $0.80, $4.00 and $9.60.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Same speed?
*Student:* Time yourself walking 20 steps, then use a double number line to predict time for 60 and 100 steps; test one prediction.

*How to teach:* Time the child walking 20 steps. Build a double number line (steps vs. seconds) and predict 60 and 100 steps, then test one.

- **Q:** If 20 steps take 12 seconds, how long for 60 steps? 100 steps?
  **A:** 36 seconds and 60 seconds.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a quiz on equivalent ratios and double number lines; correct errors in a different color.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz; the child corrects errors in a different color and says what went wrong.

- **Q:** Is 9:12 equivalent to 12:16?
  **A:** Yes — both simplify to 3:4.

**Assessment:** Quiz on equivalent ratios and double number lines.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) four ratios equivalent to 5:2 — 10:4, 15:6, 20:8, 25:10 2) Is 7:3 equivalent to 14:6? Yes (×2) 3) 7:3 and 10:6? No 4) 3 lb cost $7.50: 1 lb = $2.50, 6 lb = $15 5) 4 km in 50 min at the same pace: 12 km takes 150 min 6) 9:12 and 12:16 are equivalent (both = 3:4).

## Science 6 (Integrated): Mass, material and temperature change

**Prepare:** Gather: two cups, water and sand (or vegetable oil), kitchen scale, thermometer, lamp or sunny window. Weigh water with a kitchen scale (100 g = 100 mL). Same lamp and distance for all cups.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all materials heat up equally. Water needs much more energy to warm than sand.
- Thinking more mass heats faster. Larger mass warms more slowly with the same energy.

### Day 1 (Monday): Plan the investigation
*Student:* Plan an investigation comparing temperature change of 100 g vs. 200 g of water given equal heating time; identify variables.

*How to teach:* The child plans: independent = mass; dependent = temperature change; controlled = lamp, distance, time, starting temperature.

- **Q:** What is the independent variable?
  **A:** The mass of water (100 g vs. 200 g).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mass investigation
*Student:* Run the mass investigation under a lamp, recording temperature every 2 minutes.

*How to teach:* Run under the lamp; record every 2 minutes.

- **Q:** Predict which mass warms more.
  **A:** 100 g — the same energy is spread through fewer particles.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sand vs. water
*Student:* Heat equal masses of sand and water for 15 minutes and compare temperature changes; connect to beaches and coastal climates.

*How to teach:* Heat equal masses of sand and water.

- **Q:** Which warms more, sand or water?
  **A:** Sand.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Analyze
*Student:* Graph both investigations and write how mass and type of matter affect temperature change.

*How to teach:* Graph both; write conclusions.

- **Q:** How does type of matter affect temperature change?
  **A:** Different materials need different amounts of energy to warm; water needs much more than sand.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a CER answering why the beach sand is hotter than the ocean on a sunny afternoon.

*How to teach:* The child writes the CER.

- **Q:** Why are coastal cities' temperatures milder than inland cities'?
  **A:** The ocean warms and cools slowly, moderating nearby air temperatures.

**Assessment:** Lab report with graphs and CER.

**Answer key:** Lab report — graphs: temperature vs. time for 100 g and 200 g water (100 g rises more), sand vs. water (sand rises more). CER rubric — 4: a claim that answers the question, specific data as evidence (numbers from the investigation), and reasoning that connects the evidence to the science idea; 3: claim and evidence correct but reasoning thin; 2: claim with vague evidence; 1: claim only or incorrect. Expected CER: beach sand is hotter because sand's temperature rises more than water's for the same energy (water has a high heat capacity), and ocean water mixes heat into a huge mass.

## World History and Geography: Ancient Civilizations: Sumerian city-states and religion

**Prepare:** Gather: images of the Ziggurat of Ur and Standard of Ur, sugar cubes or blocks. Print images of the Ziggurat of Ur and both sides of the Standard of Ur (British Museum website).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Sumer was one country. It was a group of independent city-states.
- Thinking ziggurats were tombs like pyramids. They were temple platforms.

### Day 1 (Monday): City-states
*Student:* Read about Ur and Uruk and describe how a Sumerian city-state was organized.

*How to teach:* Read about Ur and Uruk.

- **Q:** What was a Sumerian city-state?
  **A:** An independent city and the farmland around it, with its own ruler, god and army.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ziggurats
*Student:* Build a model ziggurat from blocks or sugar cubes and explain its religious and political role.

*How to teach:* Build a stepped ziggurat; discuss its role.

- **Q:** What was a ziggurat for?
  **A:** A stepped temple platform honoring the city's patron god; priests and rulers used it to show their power.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Standard of Ur
*Student:* Analyze the 'War' and 'Peace' sides of the Standard of Ur and infer the social hierarchy.

*How to teach:* Analyze both sides of the Standard.

- **Q:** What does the larger size of one figure suggest?
  **A:** Greater importance — likely the king.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Social pyramid
*Student:* Create a social pyramid of Sumerian society and explain how religion supported the rulers' power.

*How to teach:* Make the social pyramid.

- **Q:** How did religion support rulers' power?
  **A:** Rulers claimed the gods chose them and supported temples, so obeying the ruler seemed like obeying the gods.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph using evidence from the Standard of Ur about Sumerian society.

*How to teach:* The child writes the evidence paragraph.

- **Q:** Name two cities of Sumer.
  **A:** Ur and Uruk (also Lagash, Kish, Eridu).

**Assessment:** Artifact analysis paragraph.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric — 4: clear topic sentence answering the question, at least two specific accurate pieces of evidence (named source, artifact or fact), explanation linking each to the claim, concluding sentence; 3: accurate but only one piece of evidence explained; 2: general statements without evidence; 1: inaccurate or off-topic. Expected evidence: the Standard of Ur's 'Peace' side shows a banquet with the king, the largest figure, at the top and people bringing animals and goods below; the 'War' side shows soldiers, chariots and captives — revealing a hierarchy with kings and nobles at the top, soldiers and workers below, and captives/enslaved people at the bottom.

## Visual and Performing Arts 6: Egyptian art

**Prepare:** Find an Egyptian tomb painting (e.g., Tomb of Nebamun, British Museum). Gather kraft paper or a paper bag, colored pencils, a gold marker, and grid paper.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Egyptian artists couldn't draw realistically. They followed conventions on purpose to show the most important view of each body part.
- Thinking size shows distance in Egyptian art. Larger figures are more important (hierarchical scale).

### Day 1 (Monday): Rules of Egyptian art
*Student:* Analyze a tomb painting for composite view, hierarchical scale and registers.

*How to teach:* Analyze a tomb painting for composite view, hierarchical scale and registers. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What is composite view?
  **A:** Showing each body part from its most recognizable angle: head and legs in profile, eye and shoulders from the front.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Figure study
*Student:* Draw a figure on a grid using Egyptian proportions.

*How to teach:* Draw a figure on a grid using Egyptian proportions (about 18 squares from feet to hairline).

- **Q:** Why did Egyptian artists use grids?
  **A:** To keep figure proportions consistent and follow traditional rules.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tomb painting
*Student:* Plan a 'tomb painting' of your own daily life in registers.

*How to teach:* Plan a 'tomb painting' of daily life in registers. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** What are registers?
  **A:** Horizontal bands that organize scenes, like comic strip rows.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Color and finish
*Student:* Complete the painting with Egyptian colors and a hieroglyph cartouche of your name.

*How to teach:* Complete with Egyptian colors and a name cartouche. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** What is a cartouche?
  **A:** An oval enclosing a royal name in hieroglyphs.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & reflect
*Student:* Explain which Egyptian conventions you used and why they mattered to Egyptian beliefs.

*How to teach:* Explain conventions used and why they mattered. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** Why did conventions matter to Egyptian beliefs?
  **A:** Tomb art was meant to last forever and help the dead in the afterlife, so figures had to be complete and clear.

**Assessment:** Egyptian-style painting with explanation.

**Answer key:** Egyptian painting rubric: 4 = composite view, hierarchical scale, registers, cartouche and Egyptian colors, with explanation tied to beliefs; 3 = one missing; 2 = two missing; 1 = incomplete.

## Physical Education 6: Cooperative games and fitness

**Prepare:** Gather: parachute or bedsheet, balls, pedometer or phone step counter. Use a bedsheet if no parachute; set up step counters. Check the space for hazards, have water ready, and start every session with the dynamic warm-up.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking cooperative games aren't real exercise. Track steps to show they can be vigorous.
- Designing games where players sit out. Good games keep everyone moving.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sheet games
*Student:* Play bedsheet or parachute games (popcorn, mushroom) that require timing together.

*How to teach:* Play sheet games (popcorn, mushroom).

- **Q:** Why does timing matter in sheet games?
  **A:** The sheet only lifts well when everyone moves together.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cooperative tag
*Student:* Play 'everybody's it' and 'freeze tag with rescue' and track steps.

*How to teach:* Play cooperative tag; track steps.

- **Q:** How does 'freeze tag with rescue' keep everyone active?
  **A:** Frozen players are freed by teammates instead of sitting out.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Team circuit
*Student:* Complete a partner fitness circuit where partners must sync reps.

*How to teach:* Do a synced partner circuit.

- **Q:** Why sync reps with a partner?
  **A:** It builds teamwork and keeps pace steady.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Design a game
*Student:* Design a cooperative game with rules and teach it to family members.

*How to teach:* Design and teach a cooperative game.

- **Q:** What makes a game inclusive?
  **A:** Everyone participates, rules can be adapted, and no one is eliminated for long.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Play your designed game and evaluate it for activity level and inclusion.

*How to teach:* Play and evaluate the game.

- **Q:** How can you check a game's activity level?
  **A:** Count steps or heart rate during play.

**Assessment:** Original cooperative game.

**Answer key:** Original game rubric — 4: clear written rules, safety rules, everyone active (no elimination or quick re-entry), inclusive modifications, successfully taught; 3: minor gaps; 2: unclear rules; 1: incomplete.

## Health 6: Self-esteem and growth mindset

**Prepare:** Index cards and the health journal. Find a short article on growth mindset/brain plasticity and a biography excerpt of someone who overcame adversity (e.g., Wilma Rudolph, Malala Yousafzai, Temple Grandin).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking self-esteem means thinking you're perfect. Healthy self-esteem means valuing yourself while accepting mistakes.
- Thinking abilities are fixed. The brain grows with effort and practice (growth mindset).

### Day 1 (Monday): Strengths inventory
*Student:* List 10 personal strengths and ask a family member to add three.

*How to teach:* List 10 strengths; ask a family member to add three. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** Why is it helpful to know your strengths?
  **A:** It builds confidence and helps you use strengths to handle challenges.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Self-talk
*Student:* Rewrite 6 negative self-talk statements as realistic positive ones.

*How to teach:* Rewrite 6 negative self-talk statements as realistic positive ones. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** Rewrite: 'I'm terrible at math.'
  **A:** 'Math is hard for me right now, but I can improve with practice and help.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Growth mindset
*Student:* Read a short article on brain growth with practice and write about something you improved with effort.

*How to teach:* Read the growth-mindset article; write about something improved with effort. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What is a growth mindset?
  **A:** The belief that abilities can grow through effort, practice and learning from mistakes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Resilience story
*Student:* Read a biography excerpt of someone who overcame adversity and identify resilience traits.

*How to teach:* Read the biography excerpt; identify resilience traits (persistence, support, optimism, problem-solving).

- **Q:** What is resilience?
  **A:** The ability to bounce back from difficulty.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Set a mental-health goal and complete a check on self-esteem concepts.

*How to teach:* Set a mental-health goal; complete the check. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** Give an example of a mental-health goal.
  **A:** 'I will write three things I did well each night for two weeks.'

**Assessment:** Goal and check.

**Answer key:** Check key: self-esteem = how you value yourself; growth mindset = abilities grow with effort; positive self-talk example; resilience = bouncing back; one resilience trait. Goal: SMART and related to mental health.
