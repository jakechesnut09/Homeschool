# Teacher's Guide — 6th Grade, Week 2

## English Language Arts 6: Irony and point of view: O. Henry and Saki

**Prepare:** Gather: 'The Ransom of Red Chief' and 'After Twenty Years' by O. Henry, 'The Open Window' by Saki. All three stories are on Project Gutenberg. Read 'The Open Window' first yourself so you can enjoy the twist with your child.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing irony with coincidence. Irony is the gap between what is expected and what actually happens.
- Thinking a twist ending is 'random'. Have the child find the clues (foreshadowing) on a second read.

### Day 1 (Monday): Situational irony
*Student:* Read 'The Ransom of Red Chief' and chart what the kidnappers expect vs. what happens; write two sentences explaining the irony.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Ransom of Red Chief' (aloud is fun). The child makes an Expected/What happened chart, then writes two sentences on the irony.

- **Q:** What do the kidnappers expect, and what actually happens?
  **A:** They expect Ebenezer Dorset to pay $2,000 for his son; instead the boy torments them so much that they pay the father $250 to take him back.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Narrator's voice
*Student:* Analyze how Sam's first-person narration and fancy vocabulary create humor; rewrite one paragraph in plain third person and compare the effect.

*How to teach:* Reread a paragraph of Sam's narration aloud, noticing his big words. The child rewrites it in plain third person and compares.

- **Q:** How does Sam's narration create humor?
  **A:** He uses fancy, formal vocabulary to describe a ridiculous, chaotic situation, and as the narrator he is clearly the one being outsmarted by a child.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Twist endings
*Student:* Read 'After Twenty Years' and 'The Open Window'; mark foreshadowing clues on a second read and discuss which twist was fairer to the reader.

*How to teach:* Read both stories once for fun, then reread to mark clues. Discuss which ending felt fair.

- **Q:** In 'After Twenty Years', who is the patrolman, and why doesn't he make the arrest himself?
  **A:** He is Jimmy Wells. He recognizes Bob as a man wanted in Chicago and, not wanting to arrest his old friend himself, sends a plainclothes officer.
- **Q:** In 'The Open Window', what is the twist?
  **A:** Vera made up the tragic story; the men return safely, Framton flees in terror, and Vera immediately invents another tale. 'Romance at short notice was her speciality.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Context clues
*Student:* Use context clues to define 10 challenging words from the O. Henry stories, then verify with a dictionary.

*How to teach:* Model: read the sentence, guess the meaning from clues, then check the dictionary. The child does 10 words from the stories.

- **Q:** What clue types can help define a word?
  **A:** Definition/restatement, synonyms, antonyms or contrast, examples, and the general sense of the sentence.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a short comparison of how two of the three stories use point of view to surprise readers; quiz on irony and pronoun case.

*How to teach:* The child writes the comparison and takes the quiz.

- **Q:** What kind of irony is the ending of 'The Ransom of Red Chief'?
  **A:** Situational irony — the kidnappers end up paying the victim's father.

**Assessment:** Comparison paragraph and quiz.

**Answer key:** Comparison paragraph (4 = names two stories, explains each point of view — e.g., Sam's first-person narration in 'Red Chief' hides nothing yet his pompous voice makes the reversal funny; the third-person narrator in 'After Twenty Years' withholds the patrolman's identity; 'The Open Window' lets us believe Vera's story along with Framton — and how it creates surprise, with evidence; 3 = accurate but thin; 2 = summary; 1 = incorrect). Quiz: 1) situational irony = outcome opposite of expectation 2) Red Chief irony: the father makes the kidnappers pay $250 to take the boy back 3) After Twenty Years: Jimmy Wells is the patrolman 4) The Open Window: Vera invented the tragedy 5–8) pronoun case items as in Week 1.

## Mathematics 6: Area of triangles

**Prepare:** Gather: paper triangles, ruler, scissors. Cut two identical triangles before Day 1 (or let the child trace and cut).

**Watch for:**
- Using the slanted side as the height of a triangle. The height is perpendicular to the chosen base, and for obtuse triangles it may fall outside the triangle.
- Forgetting to halve. Remind: a triangle is half of a parallelogram with the same base and height.

### Day 1 (Monday): Two copies make a parallelogram
*Student:* Trace and cut two identical triangles, join them into a parallelogram, and explain why a triangle's area is half of b x h.

*How to teach:* Have the child join two identical triangles into a parallelogram. Ask: 'What is the area of the parallelogram? How much of it is one triangle?' Record A = ½ × b × h.

- **Q:** A triangle has base 10 and height 6. Find its area and explain using a parallelogram.
  **A:** 30 square units. Two copies make a parallelogram with area 10 × 6 = 60; one triangle is half: 30.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Right triangles
*Student:* Find areas of 10 right triangles on a grid and write a rule using the two legs as base and height.

*How to teach:* On the grid, point out that the two legs of a right triangle meet at a square corner, so one leg is the base and the other is the height. The child computes the 10 areas and writes the rule in words.

- **Q:** A right triangle has legs 9 and 12 and hypotenuse 15. What is its area?
  **A:** 54 square units: ½ × 9 × 12. The hypotenuse (15) is not used.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Any base, any height
*Student:* For acute and obtuse triangles, draw all three heights with a ruler and show the area comes out the same using each base.

*How to teach:* For an obtuse triangle, model extending the base with a dashed line and dropping a perpendicular from the top vertex. The child draws all three heights with a ruler on two triangles and checks that ½bh gives the same area each time.

- **Q:** An obtuse triangle has base 8 and its height (drawn outside the triangle) is 5. Area?
  **A:** 20 square units (½ × 8 × 5).
- **Q:** If another side of the same triangle is 10, what is the height to that side?
  **A:** 4, because ½ × 10 × h = 20 so h = 4.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Triangle formula practice
*Student:* Solve 12 triangle-area problems including missing-base and missing-height problems (e.g., area 24 sq cm and base 8 cm; find height).

*How to teach:* Model the missing-dimension problem: write ½ × 8 × h = 24, so 4h = 24 and h = 6. Then the child solves the 12 problems, checking each by plugging back in.

- **Q:** Area 24 sq cm, base 8 cm. Find the height.
  **A:** 6 cm (½ × 8 × 6 = 24).
- **Q:** Area 35 sq in, height 7 in. Find the base.
  **A:** 10 in (½ × 10 × 7 = 35).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz mixing triangle and parallelogram area, then correct mistakes and explain one in a short paragraph.

*How to teach:* Give the mixed quiz independently. Then the child corrects mistakes and writes a short paragraph explaining one error and the fix.

- **Q:** A triangle and a parallelogram both have base 12 and height 5. Compare their areas.
  **A:** Triangle 30, parallelogram 60 — the triangle is half.

**Assessment:** Quiz on triangle area including finding missing dimensions.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) b = 6, h = 9 → 27 2) right triangle legs 5 and 8 → 20 3) b = 14, h = 3 → 21 4) area 40, base 10 → height 8 5) area 18, height 4 → base 9 6) parallelogram b = 11, h = 6 → 66 7) triangle b = 7, h = 7 → 24.5 8) obtuse triangle b = 6, outside height 5 → 15. Paragraph: names the error and shows the correct calculation.

## Science 6 (Integrated): The engineering design process

**Prepare:** Gather: index cards, tape, pennies, straws. Gather index cards, tape (measure 30 cm strips), pennies (100+), two books to make a 20 cm gap.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing criteria and constraints. Criteria = what success looks like; constraints = limits (materials, time, cost).
- Redesigning randomly. Change one feature based on how the bridge failed.

### Day 1 (Monday): Defining problems
*Student:* Analyze three everyday products (e.g., a lunch box) and list their criteria and constraints.

*How to teach:* Examine a lunch box: list what it must do (criteria) and its limits (constraints).

- **Q:** For a lunch box, give one criterion and one constraint.
  **A:** Criterion: keeps food cold for 4 hours. Constraint: must cost under $15 / fit in a backpack.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Index card bridge
*Student:* Design and build a bridge from 10 index cards and 30 cm of tape that spans 20 cm and holds the most pennies.

*How to teach:* Explain the challenge rules; the child sketches and builds. Hint: folding cards into beams or tubes adds strength.

- **Q:** Why might a folded (corrugated or U-shaped) card hold more than a flat one?
  **A:** Folding makes the card stiffer, so it bends less under load.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Test and redesign
*Student:* Test the bridge, record the penny count, then redesign and retest once.

*How to teach:* Load pennies one at a time until failure; note where it failed, redesign and retest.

- **Q:** Why record where the bridge failed?
  **A:** It shows the weak point, guiding the redesign.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Decision matrix
*Student:* Score three bridge designs (yours and two sketches) in a weighted decision matrix and choose the best.

*How to teach:* Model a decision matrix with weights (strength ×3, materials ×1, ease ×1).

- **Q:** Why use a weighted matrix?
  **A:** It compares designs fairly on the criteria that matter most.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a short engineering report: problem, criteria, constraints, designs tested and results.

*How to teach:* The child writes the engineering report.

- **Q:** What are criteria?
  **A:** The requirements a successful design must meet.

**Assessment:** Engineering report.

**Answer key:** Engineering report rubric — 4: states the problem, criteria (spans 20 cm, holds most pennies), constraints (10 cards, 30 cm tape), sketches of designs, penny counts for both tests, and explains why the redesign changed results; 3: one section thin; 2: missing data or redesign; 1: incomplete.

## World History and Geography: Ancient Civilizations: Hunter-gatherers and early tools

**Prepare:** Gather: photos of stone tools (Smithsonian Human Origins), smooth stones, clay. Bookmark the Smithsonian Human Origins site for tool images and Lascaux/Chauvet virtual tours.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking early humans were unintelligent. Their tools, art and survival skills show sophisticated thinking.
- Thinking dinosaurs and early humans lived together. Dinosaurs went extinct about 66 million years before humans.

### Day 1 (Monday): Life on the move
*Student:* Read about hunter-gatherer daily life and create a chart of food, shelter, tools and social roles.

*How to teach:* Read about hunter-gatherer life; complete the chart.

- **Q:** Why did hunter-gatherers move often?
  **A:** To follow animal herds and find plants as seasons changed.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tool technology
*Student:* Analyze images of Oldowan and Acheulean hand axes and explain how tools improved over time.

*How to teach:* Compare an Oldowan chopper with an Acheulean hand axe.

- **Q:** How did tools improve from Oldowan to Acheulean?
  **A:** Oldowan tools were simple flakes and choppers made with a few strikes; Acheulean hand axes were carefully shaped, symmetrical teardrop tools that took planning.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fire
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining at least four ways controlling fire changed early human life.

*How to teach:* The child writes four ways fire changed life.

- **Q:** Give four ways controlling fire changed early human life.
  **A:** Cooking food (easier to digest, killed germs), warmth in cold climates, protection from animals, light to extend the day, a social gathering place, hardening tools.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cave art
*Student:* Study images of the Lascaux and Chauvet cave paintings and infer what they show about beliefs and daily life.

*How to teach:* View Lascaux and Chauvet images; infer.

- **Q:** What do cave paintings suggest about early people?
  **A:** They valued animals they hunted (horses, bison, deer), had artistic skill and possibly beliefs or rituals connected to hunting.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a 'day in the life' diary entry of a hunter-gatherer using at least six accurate details.

*How to teach:* The child writes the diary entry.

- **Q:** Name two materials hunter-gatherers used for tools.
  **A:** Stone (flint), bone, wood, antler.

**Assessment:** Historically accurate diary entry.

**Answer key:** Diary rubric — 4: at least six accurate details (moving with seasons/following herds, gathering plants, hunting with stone tools, fire for warmth/cooking/protection, small bands sharing food, shelters of hides/caves, cave art), first-person voice, no anachronisms; 3: four or five details; 2: few details or anachronisms (farming, metal); 1: inaccurate.

## Visual and Performing Arts 6: Shape, form and value

**Prepare:** Pencils, a blending stump or tissue, an egg (or white ball) and a desk lamp. Darken the room for Thursday.

**Watch for:**
- Outlining shaded forms with a dark line. Show edges with changes in value instead.
- Forgetting reflected light. The shadow side of a round object is slightly lighter near the bottom edge because light bounces up from the table.

### Day 1 (Monday): Value scale
*Student:* Draw a 9-step value scale from white to black using pressure and layering.

*How to teach:* Draw a 9-step value scale using pressure and layering, white to black. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** What is value?
  **A:** How light or dark a tone or color is.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Shading techniques
*Student:* Practice hatching, cross-hatching, stippling and blending in four boxes.

*How to teach:* Practice hatching, cross-hatching, stippling and blending in four boxes, each going light to dark.

- **Q:** How do you make stippling darker?
  **A:** Place dots closer together.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Shapes to forms
*Student:* Turn a circle, square and triangle into a sphere, cube and cone with shading.

*How to teach:* Turn a circle, square and triangle into a sphere, cube and cone by adding light and shadow from one direction.

- **Q:** What's the difference between shape and form?
  **A:** Shape is flat (2D: length and width); form is 3D (length, width and depth).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Still life
*Student:* Draw an egg lit by a lamp, showing highlight, core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow.

*How to teach:* Light an egg with a lamp; draw it showing highlight, core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow.

- **Q:** Where does the cast shadow fall?
  **A:** On the surface, on the side opposite the light source.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & reflect
*Student:* Critique your egg drawing with a checklist and redraw one area to improve it.

*How to teach:* Critique the egg with a checklist; redraw one area. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What is reflected light?
  **A:** Light that bounces off a nearby surface onto the shadow side of an object, making it slightly lighter.

**Assessment:** Value scale and still-life drawing.

**Answer key:** Value scale: 9 even steps. Still life checklist: consistent light source, highlight, core shadow, reflected light, cast shadow, smooth gradations. 4 = all; 3 = one missing; 2 = two or three missing; 1 = flat.

## Physical Education 6: Fitness testing: aerobic capacity and strength

**Prepare:** Gather: PACER audio (free online) or measured 20 m course, mat, measuring tape. Download the free PACER audio or measure a 20 m course (or a 1-mile route). Use a mat for curl-ups. Check the space for hazards, have water ready, and start every session with the dynamic warm-up.

**Watch for:**
- Starting the PACER or mile too fast. Pace steadily.
- Curl-ups with hands behind the head pulling the neck. Arms stay straight, fingers slide along the mat strip.

### Day 1 (Monday): PACER or mile
*Student:* Complete the 20-meter PACER test or a timed one-mile run/walk and record laps or time.

*How to teach:* Explain the PACER: run 20 m before the beep; the beeps speed up; the test ends at the second missed beep. Or time a mile run/walk.

- **Q:** When does the PACER end?
  **A:** When you fail to reach the line before the beep for the second time.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Curl-ups
*Student:* Learn correct curl-up form and complete the cadence curl-up test.

*How to teach:* Teach curl-up form: knees bent about 140°, feet flat, arms straight, fingertips slide across a measuring strip, one curl every 3 seconds.

- **Q:** Name two correct curl-up form cues.
  **A:** Arms straight with palms sliding on the mat; head returns to the mat each rep; feet flat; keep the 3-second cadence.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Push-ups
*Student:* Learn 90-degree push-up form and complete the cadence push-up test.

*How to teach:* Teach push-up form: straight body, lower until elbows are at 90°, one every 3 seconds.

- **Q:** What counts as a correct push-up in the FitnessGram test?
  **A:** Body straight from head to heels, lowering until the elbows bend to 90° and pushing back up, in rhythm.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Active recovery
*Student:* Play a low-intensity game (frisbee catch) and record test results in a fitness log.

*How to teach:* Play frisbee catch lightly; record scores in the log.

- **Q:** Why include an easy recovery day after testing?
  **A:** Muscles recover and rebuild; light activity reduces soreness.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Compare your results with the Healthy Fitness Zone charts for your age.

*How to teach:* Compare results with HFZ charts for age and sex.

- **Q:** What does 'Healthy Fitness Zone' mean?
  **A:** A score range linked to good health for your age and sex — the goal, not a competition.

**Assessment:** Baseline aerobic and strength scores.

**Answer key:** Baseline log: PACER laps or mile time, curl-ups and push-ups (all reps at the 3-second cadence with correct form). Scored for completeness and accurate counting; results compared to FitnessGram Healthy Fitness Zone (HFZ) charts on Day 5.

## Health 6: Decision making and valid health information

**Prepare:** Computer access. Print a source checklist (CRAAP: Currency — when written? Relevance — answers my question? Authority — who wrote it, credentials? Accuracy — supported by evidence? Purpose — inform or sell?). Find three sources on one topic (e.g., sleep for teens): a .gov page, a health blog, and an influencer video.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking popular means accurate. Likes and followers don't measure accuracy; check credentials and evidence.
- Thinking the first option you think of is the only one. DECIDE asks you to explore several options before choosing.

### Day 1 (Monday): DECIDE model
*Student:* Learn the DECIDE model (Define, Explore, Consider, Identify values, Decide, Evaluate) and apply it to a scenario about staying up late.

*How to teach:* Teach DECIDE: Define the problem, Explore options, Consider consequences, Identify values, Decide and act, Evaluate. Apply to staying up late to finish a show on a school night.

- **Q:** In DECIDE, what does the 'I' step ask you to do?
  **A:** Identify your values — think about what matters to you (health, family rules, goals) and how each option fits them.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Source detective
*Student:* Compare a .gov site, a health blog and an influencer video using a CRAAP-style checklist.

*How to teach:* Your child rates the three sources on each CRAAP item and ranks them. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** Why is an influencer video often less reliable than a .gov page?
  **A:** The influencer may not have health credentials, may be paid to promote products, and may not cite evidence; government health sites are written by experts and reviewed.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Bias and ads
*Student:* Identify who profits from two health claims online and how that creates bias.

*How to teach:* Find two online health claims (e.g., a detox tea, a supplement); your child identifies who profits.

- **Q:** How does making money from a product create bias?
  **A:** The seller benefits if you buy, so they may exaggerate benefits and hide risks.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Practice decision
*Student:* Use DECIDE on a scenario of a friend daring you to jump off a high wall.

*How to teach:* Apply DECIDE to a friend daring your child to jump off a high wall. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** What are two options besides jumping, and their consequences?
  **A:** Say no and suggest something else (stay safe, friend may tease briefly); walk away (stay safe). Jumping risks broken bones or a head injury.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Evaluate a new health website in writing and take a 6-question quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child evaluates a new health website in writing and takes a 6-question quiz. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** What does 'credentials' mean?
  **A:** Qualifications such as a medical degree or expertise showing someone knows the subject.

**Assessment:** Website evaluation and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) DECIDE steps in order 2) valid source = expert, current, evidence-based, unbiased 3) bias = slant from personal gain 4) .gov/.edu/medical organizations usually reliable 5) check the author's credentials 6) evaluate = reflect on the outcome. Website evaluation: 4 = addresses all five CRAAP criteria with evidence and a justified verdict; 3 = four criteria; 2 = two or three; 1 = one or none.
