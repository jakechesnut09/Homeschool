# Teacher's Guide — 6th Grade, Week 19

## English Language Arts 6: Sound and structure

**Prepare:** Gather: 'Paul Revere's Ride' by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 'The Raven' (excerpt) by Edgar Allan Poe, audio recordings. Print 'Paul Revere's Ride' and the first stanzas of 'The Raven'; find a professional recording (LibriVox or Poetry Foundation).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking every poem rhymes in couplets. Label rhyme with letters to see each pattern.
- Mixing up alliteration (repeated beginning consonant sounds) and internal rhyme (rhyme inside a line).

### Day 1 (Monday): Rhythm
*Student:* Read 'Paul Revere's Ride' aloud and tap the galloping meter; explain how rhythm matches content.

*How to teach:* Read 'Paul Revere's Ride' aloud while the child taps the beat on the table. Ask what the rhythm sounds like.

- **Q:** How does the rhythm of 'Paul Revere's Ride' match its content?
  **A:** Its quick, galloping beat imitates the hoofbeats of Revere's horse racing through the night, building urgency.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rhyme scheme
*Student:* Label rhyme schemes of three poems and identify alliteration and internal rhyme in an excerpt of 'The Raven'.

*How to teach:* Model labeling rhyme with letters; the child labels three poems and marks sound devices in 'The Raven'.

- **Q:** Find alliteration and internal rhyme in 'Once upon a midnight dreary, while I pondered, weak and weary… While I nodded, nearly napping, suddenly there came a tapping.'
  **A:** Internal rhyme: dreary/weary, napping/tapping. Alliteration: nodded, nearly, napping.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Heard vs. read
*Student:* Listen to a professional recording of 'The Raven' excerpt and compare it with your silent reading.

*How to teach:* Read the Raven excerpt silently, then listen to a recording. Compare.

- **Q:** What does hearing a professional reading add?
  **A:** Tone of voice, pacing and pauses create a gloomy, eerie mood and make the rhythm and repeated sounds stand out.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Structure
*Student:* Analyze how one stanza of 'Paul Revere's Ride' builds suspense within the whole poem.

*How to teach:* Choose the lantern/belfry stanza; discuss how it fits the whole poem's suspense.

- **Q:** What is the signal in 'Paul Revere's Ride', and why does waiting for it build suspense?
  **A:** Lanterns in the Old North Church tower: 'One, if by land, and two, if by sea.' Revere — and the reader — wait to see the light, delaying the action.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Memorize and recite 12+ lines of a chosen poem with expression; quiz on sound devices.

*How to teach:* The child recites 12+ lines and takes the quiz.

- **Q:** What is a refrain?
  **A:** A repeated line or word, like 'Nevermore' in 'The Raven'.

**Assessment:** Recitation rubric and sound-device quiz.

**Answer key:** Sound-device quiz: 1) 'while I nodded, nearly napping' — alliteration 2) 'dreary / weary' in one line — internal rhyme 3) 'Nevermore' repeated — refrain/repetition 4) the galloping rhythm of 'Paul Revere's Ride' imitates a horse's hooves 5) rhyme scheme: label a given stanza with letters (e.g., Raven stanza ABCBBB). Recitation rubric: 4 = 12+ lines memorized, expressive pacing, clear volume, eye contact; 3 = minor prompts; 2 = several prompts or flat delivery; 1 = read from paper.

## Mathematics 6: Factors, multiples and the distributive property

**Prepare:** Gather: colored tiles, grid paper. Have colored square tiles or cut paper squares for the Day 1 tiling problem.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up GCF and LCM. GCF is the biggest number that divides both (splitting into equal groups); LCM is the smallest number both divide into (when things line up again).
- Factoring out a common factor that isn't the greatest (36 + 8 = 2(18 + 4)). Correct but not fully factored — ask 'can the numbers in the parentheses still share a factor?'

### Day 1 (Monday): Greatest common factor
*Student:* Find the largest square tile that exactly covers a 24 x 36 rectangle, then find GCF for 10 pairs of numbers.

*How to teach:* Draw a 24 × 36 rectangle. Try 4×4, 6×6 and 12×12 tiles; ask which is the largest that fits exactly with no gaps. Connect to listing factors of both numbers, then the child finds GCF for 10 pairs.

- **Q:** What is the largest square tile that exactly covers a 24 × 36 rectangle, and how many tiles?
  **A:** 12 × 12 tiles; 2 × 3 = 6 tiles (GCF of 24 and 36 is 12).
- **Q:** Find GCF(18, 30) and GCF(42, 56).
  **A:** 6 and 14.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Least common multiple
*Student:* Solve 'hot dogs come in 10s, buns in 8s' problems and find LCM for 10 pairs.

*How to teach:* List multiples of 10 (10, 20, 30, 40…) and of 8 (8, 16, 24, 32, 40…) to find the first match. The child solves the hot-dog problems and 10 LCM pairs.

- **Q:** Hot dogs come in packs of 10, buns in packs of 8. Fewest of each to have equal numbers?
  **A:** 40 of each: 4 packs of hot dogs, 5 packs of buns.
- **Q:** LCM(6, 9)?
  **A:** 18.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): GCF or LCM?
*Student:* Sort 8 word problems as GCF or LCM situations and solve each.

*How to teach:* Teach the cue: splitting into identical groups → GCF; events repeating and lining up → LCM. The child sorts and solves 8 problems.

- **Q:** 24 pencils and 36 erasers go into identical kits with none left. Most kits?
  **A:** 12 kits (GCF), each with 2 pencils and 3 erasers.
- **Q:** One bus comes every 12 minutes and another every 18. Both leave now; when together again?
  **A:** In 36 minutes (LCM).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Distributive property
*Student:* Rewrite sums like 36 + 8 as 4(9 + 2) using the GCF and use area models to show why it works.

*How to teach:* Draw a rectangle of area 36 + 8 split into two parts with a common height of 4 (4 × 9 and 4 × 2). Write 36 + 8 = 4(9 + 2) and have the child rewrite sums using the GCF.

- **Q:** Rewrite 24 + 60 using the GCF.
  **A:** 12(2 + 5).
- **Q:** Rewrite 36 + 8 using the GCF and check.
  **A:** 4(9 + 2) = 4 × 11 = 44 = 36 + 8.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 5 assessment on decimals, long division, GCF/LCM.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test independently and review errors together.

- **Q:** GCF(48, 60) and LCM(6, 8)?
  **A:** 12 and 24.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 end-of-unit assessment.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test sample key: 1) 4.7 + 12.38 = 17.08 2) 15.2 − 8.75 = 6.45 3) 2.4 × 0.35 = 0.84 4) 1,512 ÷ 24 = 63 5) 8.4 ÷ 0.07 = 120 6) GCF(48, 60) = 12 7) LCM(4, 6) = 12 8) LCM(6, 8) = 24 9) 45 + 30 = 15(3 + 2) 10) 18 cookies and 24 brownies on identical plates with nothing left over: at most 6 plates, each with 3 cookies and 4 brownies.

## Science 6 (Integrated): Electric forces and electromagnets

**Prepare:** Gather: balloons, tissue paper bits, iron nail, insulated copper wire, D battery, paper clips. Safety: a bare wire on a D battery gets hot quickly — connect only for a few seconds at a time, hold the wire by its insulation, and never use household outlets.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking static electricity and magnetism are the same. Both act at a distance, but electric forces come from charges and magnetic forces from magnets or moving charges.
- Thinking the battery alone is magnetic. The magnetism comes from current flowing through the coils.

### Day 1 (Monday): Static electricity
*Student:* Rub a balloon on hair and test its pull on tissue bits and a stream of tap water at different distances.

*How to teach:* Rub a balloon on hair; test tissue bits and a thin stream of water at different distances.

- **Q:** What happens as you move the charged balloon farther from the tissue bits?
  **A:** The pull weakens — electric force decreases with distance.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Charges
*Student:* Model positive and negative charges and explain attraction and repulsion between two charged balloons.

*How to teach:* Draw + and − charges on two balloons; hang them near each other.

- **Q:** Two balloons both rubbed on hair hang near each other. What happens and why?
  **A:** They push apart — both have the same (negative) charge, and like charges repel.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Build an electromagnet
*Student:* With adult supervision, wrap wire around a nail, connect to a battery and count paper clips picked up.

*How to teach:* With supervision, wrap wire 20 times around a nail, touch the ends to the battery briefly and count clips.

- **Q:** Why does the nail become magnetic?
  **A:** Electric current flowing through the coiled wire creates a magnetic field, which magnetizes the iron nail.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Variables
*Student:* Test how the number of coils (10, 20, 30) affects electromagnet strength and graph results.

*How to teach:* Test 10, 20 and 30 coils (3 trials each) and graph.

- **Q:** What is the independent variable in the coil test?
  **A:** Number of coils (dependent: number of paper clips).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on electric and magnetic forces and write questions you would investigate next.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz; the child writes follow-up questions.

- **Q:** Name two ways to make an electromagnet stronger.
  **A:** Add more coils; increase the current (e.g., more batteries in series, used safely).

**Assessment:** Electromagnet data and quiz.

**Answer key:** Electromagnet data: a table of coils (10, 20, 30) vs. paper clips for 3 trials, with averages; graph shows more coils → more clips. Quiz: 1) opposite charges attract, like charges repel 2) a rubbed balloon gains electrons (negative charge) and attracts neutral tissue bits 3) electric force gets weaker with distance 4) an electromagnet gets stronger with more coils or more current 5) unlike a permanent magnet, an electromagnet can be switched off.

## World History and Geography: Ancient Civilizations: Golden Age and the Peloponnesian War

**Prepare:** Gather: images of the Parthenon, Thucydides excerpt on the plague. Print Parthenon images and a short Thucydides excerpt on the plague (preview: it describes disease vividly).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Golden Age benefited all Greeks. Athens used Delian League funds for its own buildings, angering allies.
- Thinking Athens won the war. Sparta won in 404 BCE.

### Day 1 (Monday): Golden Age
*Student:* Read about Pericles' Athens and the building of the Parthenon and list achievements.

*How to teach:* Read about Pericles' Athens; list achievements.

- **Q:** Whom did the Parthenon honor?
  **A:** Athena, patron goddess of Athens.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Delian League
*Student:* Explain how the Delian League turned into an Athenian empire and why Sparta felt threatened.

*How to teach:* Explain the Delian League's transformation.

- **Q:** How did the Delian League become an Athenian empire?
  **A:** Athens moved the treasury to Athens, used the money for its own projects and forced members to stay.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): War and plague
*Student:* Read Thucydides' description of the plague of Athens and explain its effect on the war.

*How to teach:* Read Thucydides on the plague.

- **Q:** How did the plague affect Athens in the war?
  **A:** It killed many people, including Pericles, weakening Athens' army and morale.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Consequences
*Student:* Create a cause-and-effect chart of the Peloponnesian War and its result: a weakened Greece.

*How to teach:* Make the chart.

- **Q:** What was a major result of the Peloponnesian War?
  **A:** A weakened Greece, later conquered by Macedonia.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on the Golden Age and Peloponnesian War.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz.

- **Q:** Who won the Peloponnesian War?
  **A:** Sparta.

**Assessment:** Cause-effect chart and quiz.

**Answer key:** Cause-effect chart: causes — Athens turned the Delian League into an empire, Sparta feared Athenian power; events — war began 431 BCE, plague struck Athens (430 BCE) and killed Pericles (429 BCE); effects — Sparta won (404 BCE), Greek city-states weakened, later conquered by Macedonia. Quiz: Parthenon honored Athena; Pericles led Athens; Delian League; Thucydides wrote the war's history.

## Visual and Performing Arts 6: Music of world cultures

**Prepare:** Find recordings: a sitar raga (e.g., Ravi Shankar), guzheng music, West African drumming (e.g., djembe ensemble), and mariachi ('El Son de la Negra'). Gather household percussion and a keyboard or xylophone app.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all world music uses Western scales and meters. Ragas, talas and pentatonic systems are different, complete musical systems.
- Thinking polyrhythm is just playing fast. It's two or more different rhythms played at the same time.

### Day 1 (Monday): India
*Student:* Listen to a sitar raga and identify the drone and tala rhythm cycle.

*How to teach:* Listen to a raga; identify the drone (tanpura) and the tala cycle on tabla. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** What is a raga?
  **A:** A melodic framework in Indian classical music with specific notes, patterns and mood.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): China
*Student:* Listen to guzheng music and play a pentatonic melody on black keys of a keyboard or a xylophone app.

*How to teach:* Listen to guzheng; play a pentatonic melody on the black keys. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** What is a pentatonic scale?
  **A:** A five-note scale (the black keys on a piano form one).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): West Africa
*Student:* Learn a West African polyrhythm with two household percussion instruments.

*How to teach:* Learn a 3-against-2 polyrhythm with two household instruments (one hits 3 evenly while the other hits 2 in the same time).

- **Q:** What is a polyrhythm?
  **A:** Two or more contrasting rhythms played at the same time.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mexico
*Student:* Listen to mariachi music and identify instruments and the son jalisciense rhythm.

*How to teach:* Listen to mariachi; identify violins, trumpets, vihuela, guitarrón and the son jalisciense rhythm (alternating 6/8 and 3/4).

- **Q:** Which mariachi instrument plays the bass?
  **A:** The guitarrón.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a listening quiz identifying four world music traditions by sound.

*How to teach:* Listening quiz identifying four traditions. Have your child work independently; afterward, go over any errors together and let them explain the correct answer back to you.

- **Q:** What is a tala?
  **A:** A repeating rhythmic cycle in Indian music.

**Assessment:** Listening quiz.

**Answer key:** Listening quiz key: India — sitar, tabla, drone, raga/tala; China — guzheng, pentatonic; West Africa — djembe, polyrhythm, call and response; Mexico — mariachi, guitarrón, vihuela, trumpets, son rhythm. Proficient = 4/4 traditions identified with one feature each.

## Physical Education 6: Throwing and catching for fielding

**Prepare:** Gather: softball glove or soft ball, tennis balls, bucket target. Use soft balls or tennis balls at first; set a bucket target. Check the space for hazards, have water ready, and start every session with the dynamic warm-up.

**Watch for:**
- Throwing 'all arm' with feet square. Step with the opposite foot and rotate hips.
- Catching fly balls at the chest. Reach up with two hands above the head.

### Day 1 (Monday): Overhand throw
*Student:* Practice the overhand throw with step, hip rotation and follow-through at a target.

*How to teach:* Practice overhand throws at a target.

- **Q:** Which foot steps forward when a right-hander throws?
  **A:** The left foot (opposite foot).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ground balls
*Student:* Field 30 ground balls using alligator hands and throw to a target.

*How to teach:* Field ground balls with alligator hands.

- **Q:** What are 'alligator hands'?
  **A:** Glove open low to the ground with the throwing hand above it like a jaw to trap the ball.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fly balls
*Student:* Catch fly balls with two hands above the head, calling 'mine'.

*How to teach:* Catch fly balls; call 'mine'.

- **Q:** Why call 'mine'?
  **A:** To avoid collisions and show who will catch it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Crow hop
*Student:* Learn the crow hop to throw farther and measure distance.

*How to teach:* Learn the crow hop; measure distance.

- **Q:** What does a crow hop add?
  **A:** Momentum toward the target, for a longer, stronger throw.

### Day 5 (Friday): Skill check
*Student:* Throw 10 balls at a target from 15 meters and record accuracy.

*How to teach:* Accuracy check.

- **Q:** Where should your throwing arm finish?
  **A:** Across the body toward the opposite hip.

**Assessment:** Throwing accuracy check.

**Answer key:** Accuracy check: 10 throws at a target from 15 m; record hits (6+ proficient). Throw cues: side to target, step with opposite foot, hip rotation, arm long, follow through across the body.

## Health 6: Water, sports and recreation safety

**Prepare:** Gather a bike/sports helmet and a life jacket if available (look for a U.S. Coast Guard–approved label). Read CDC 'HEADS UP' concussion facts and a heat-illness fact sheet.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking you can 'shake off' a head injury. Any suspected concussion means stop playing and tell an adult — 'When in doubt, sit it out.'
- Trying to swim straight to shore against a rip current. Swim parallel to shore until out of the current, then swim in (or float and signal for help).

### Day 1 (Monday): Water safety
*Student:* Learn about drowning prevention, rip currents and life-jacket fit for pools, lakes and beaches.

*How to teach:* Learn drowning prevention (swim with a buddy, only where a lifeguard is on duty, life jackets in boats/open water) and rip currents. Check life-jacket fit: buckle it, lift at the shoulders — it shouldn't ride up past the chin.

- **Q:** If caught in a rip current, what should you do?
  **A:** Stay calm, don't fight it; swim parallel to the shore until free, then swim back at an angle — or float and wave/yell for help.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Concussions
*Student:* Read about concussion signs and why to stop playing and tell an adult.

*How to teach:* Read concussion signs: headache, dizziness, confusion, nausea, blurry vision, light sensitivity, memory problems.

- **Q:** What should you do if you hit your head in a game and feel dizzy?
  **A:** Stop playing, tell the coach/parent, and get checked by a health professional before returning.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Protective gear
*Student:* Inspect sports gear and check helmet fit using the 2-V-1 rule.

*How to teach:* Inspect sports gear; check helmet fit with 2-V-1. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** What is the 2-V-1 helmet check?
  **A:** Two fingers above eyebrows, straps form a V under each ear, one finger under the buckled chin strap.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Heat illness
*Student:* Learn signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke and prevention steps.

*How to teach:* Learn heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, cool clammy skin, dizziness, nausea) vs. heat stroke (very high body temperature, confusion, hot skin, fainting — call 911).

- **Q:** Which is a medical emergency: heat exhaustion or heat stroke?
  **A:** Heat stroke — call 911 and cool the person immediately.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Create a sports safety checklist and take a quiz.

*How to teach:* Create a sports safety checklist; take the quiz. Have your child work independently; afterward, go over any errors together and let them explain the correct answer back to you.

- **Q:** Name two ways to prevent heat illness.
  **A:** Drink water before/during/after, take breaks in shade, avoid the hottest hours, wear light clothing.

**Assessment:** Checklist and quiz.

**Answer key:** Checklist: protective gear fitted, water, warm-up, shade breaks, concussion rule, buddy system for water. Quiz key: rip current — swim parallel; life jacket fit; concussion signs and 'sit it out'; 2-V-1; heat exhaustion vs heat stroke (911). Proficient = 80%.
