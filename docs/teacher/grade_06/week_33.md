# Teacher's Guide — 6th Grade, Week 33

## English Language Arts 6: Research questions and sources

**Prepare:** Gather: library card, online databases (public library), Library of Congress and Smithsonian websites. Get a library card and log in to the library's databases; bookmark Library of Congress and Smithsonian sites.

**Watch for:**
- Asking a too-broad question ('Egypt'). Focus: 'How did Egyptians move the stones to build the pyramids?'
- Trusting any website. Check author, date, purpose and evidence.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose a topic
*Student:* Choose a topic connected to ancient civilizations or science (e.g., how the pyramids were built) and write a focused research question.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm topics; the child narrows to a focused question.

- **Q:** Make 'pyramids' into a focused research question.
  **A:** Example: 'How did ancient Egyptians build the pyramids at Giza?'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Evaluate sources
*Student:* Evaluate six websites with a credibility checklist (author, date, purpose, evidence) and keep the best three.

*How to teach:* Evaluate six websites with the checklist; keep three.

- **Q:** Which is more reliable: a museum website or an anonymous blog?
  **A:** The museum website — known expert authors, purpose to educate.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Library visit
*Student:* Find at least one print book and one database article at the public library on your topic.

*How to teach:* Visit the library; find a print book and database article.

- **Q:** Why use a library database?
  **A:** Articles are from vetted, reliable publications.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Note-taking
*Student:* Take paraphrased notes on cards, each labeled with its source and subtopic.

*How to teach:* Model a paraphrased note card with source and subtopic.

- **Q:** What should each note card include?
  **A:** A paraphrased fact, the source, the page/URL and a subtopic label.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check-in
*Student:* Create an MLA-style works-cited list for your sources and refocus your question if needed.

*How to teach:* Build the works-cited list; refocus the question if needed.

- **Q:** How are works-cited entries ordered?
  **A:** Alphabetically by author's last name (or title if no author).

**Assessment:** Source evaluation sheets and works-cited list.

**Answer key:** Source evaluation sheets: each lists author, date, purpose and evidence with a keep/reject decision. Works cited: MLA format, e.g., Author Last, First. Title. Publisher, Year. — for websites include title of page, site name, date and URL; alphabetical by author. 4 = all sources correct; 3 = minor format errors; 2 = missing elements; 1 = incomplete.

## Mathematics 6: Median, IQR and box plots

**Prepare:** Gather: index cards, graph paper. Write 11 data values on index cards (include one large outlier).

**Watch for:**
- Finding the median without ordering data first.
- Thinking each section of a box plot has the same number of values because sections are different lengths — each quarter holds about 25% of the data regardless of length.

### Day 1 (Monday): Median
*Student:* Line up 11 index cards of data to find the median and see why it resists outliers compared with the mean.

*How to teach:* Order the 11 cards; the middle (6th) card is the median. Compare with the mean.

- **Q:** Data: 2, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 40. Find the median and mean.
  **A:** Median 6; mean 98 ÷ 11 ≈ 8.9. The outlier 40 pulls the mean up but not the median.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Five-number summary
*Student:* Find minimum, Q1, median, Q3 and maximum for 6 data sets and compute range and IQR.

*How to teach:* Split the ordered data at the median, find the median of each half (Q1, Q3).

- **Q:** Five-number summary of 1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12?
  **A:** Min 1, Q1 3.5, median 6.5, Q3 9, max 12; range 11, IQR 5.5.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Box plots
*Student:* Draw box plots for your reaction-time data and a family height data set.

*How to teach:* Draw a scaled number line, mark the five numbers, draw the box from Q1 to Q3 with a line at the median, then whiskers.

- **Q:** What does the box in a box plot show?
  **A:** The middle 50% of the data, from Q1 to Q3.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing box plots
*Student:* Compare two box plots (e.g., Los Angeles vs San Francisco monthly temperatures) and write 3 conclusions.

*How to teach:* Give two box plots (e.g., City A: 55, 58, 62, 66, 70; City B: 60, 66, 72, 78, 84 for monthly highs °F). The child writes 3 conclusions.

- **Q:** Which city is warmer typically and which varies more?
  **A:** City B: median 72 vs 62; IQR 12 vs 8 and range 24 vs 15, so B also varies more.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on median, IQR and box plots.

*How to teach:* Give the box-plot quiz.

- **Q:** Find the IQR of 4, 7, 8, 10, 12, 15, 21.
  **A:** 8 (15 − 7).

**Assessment:** Box plot quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: data 4, 7, 8, 10, 12, 15, 21 → min 4, Q1 7, median 10, Q3 15, max 21, range 17, IQR 8. Box plot drawn with box from 7 to 15, line at 10, whiskers to 4 and 21. For two box plots, the one with the higher median has the larger typical value; the one with the longer box has more variability in the middle half.

## Science 6 (Integrated): Memory and the brain

**Prepare:** Gather: tray of 20 objects, towel, optical illusion images. Gather 20 small objects and a towel; print optical illusions.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking memory is a perfect recording. Memories are rebuilt and can change.
- Thinking illusions mean eyes are broken. The brain interprets signals using shortcuts.

### Day 1 (Monday): Memory tray
*Student:* Study a tray of 20 objects for 1 minute, cover it, and list what you remember; repeat with a memory strategy and compare.

*How to teach:* Study 20 objects 1 minute; list; repeat with a strategy (grouping or story).

- **Q:** Name a memory strategy.
  **A:** Chunking/grouping, making a story, repetition, mnemonics.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Optical illusions
*Student:* Investigate three optical illusions and explain how the brain interprets signals from the eyes.

*How to teach:* Investigate three illusions.

- **Q:** Why do optical illusions fool us?
  **A:** The brain uses assumptions to interpret signals from the eyes, and illusions exploit them.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Research
*Student:* Read two sources about how memories form and summarize them in a one-page infographic.

*How to teach:* Read two sources; make an infographic.

- **Q:** What helps form long-term memories?
  **A:** Repetition, attention, sleep and connecting to existing knowledge.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Body systems poster
*Student:* Create a poster showing how at least four body systems work together when you catch a ball.

*How to teach:* Make a 4-system poster for catching a ball.

- **Q:** Which systems work together to catch a ball?
  **A:** Nervous (eyes and brain), muscular, skeletal, circulatory/respiratory.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take the Unit 8 test on body systems and senses.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test.

- **Q:** What carries signals from the eyes to the brain?
  **A:** The optic nerve.

**Assessment:** Infographic, poster and Unit 8 test.

**Answer key:** Infographic rubric: two sources summarized accurately (memories form as connections between neurons strengthen; repetition, sleep and meaning help). Poster rubric: catching a ball involves nervous (eyes, brain), muscular, skeletal, circulatory and respiratory systems. Unit 8 test sample: bones/joints/muscle pairs; blood path; diaphragm; digestion path; kidneys; neuron parts; senses.

## World History and Geography: Ancient Civilizations: Life and economy in the Roman Empire

**Prepare:** Gather: images of Roman coins (Smithsonian), map of Roman roads and trade, cardboard and tape for aqueduct. Print Roman coin images and road maps; gather cardboard and tape.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all Romans lived like the rich. Most were poor or enslaved.
- Thinking aqueducts used pumps. They used gravity and a gentle slope.

### Day 1 (Monday): Roads and trade
*Student:* Map Roman roads and sea trade routes and list goods from each province.

*How to teach:* Map roads and sea routes.

- **Q:** Why were Roman roads important?
  **A:** They moved armies, trade and messages quickly.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Roman coins
*Student:* Analyze images of Roman coins and explain how a common currency helped the economy and spread imperial messages.

*How to teach:* Analyze coins.

- **Q:** How did coins spread imperial messages?
  **A:** They showed the emperor's face and slogans everywhere.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Daily life
*Student:* Read about family life, slavery, schools and entertainment and compare a rich and a poor Roman's day.

*How to teach:* Compare rich and poor days.

- **Q:** Where did poor Romans in the city often live?
  **A:** Crowded apartment buildings (insulae).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Build an aqueduct
*Student:* Build a cardboard aqueduct with a gentle slope that carries water 1 meter, measuring the drop.

*How to teach:* Build the aqueduct.

- **Q:** What makes an aqueduct work?
  **A:** A gentle downhill slope so gravity moves water.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on the Roman economy and daily life.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz.

- **Q:** What province supplied much of Rome's grain?
  **A:** Egypt.

**Assessment:** Aqueduct model and quiz.

**Answer key:** Aqueduct rubric: carries water 1 m with a gentle, consistent slope (a few cm), measured drop recorded. Quiz: roads connected the empire; common coins eased trade and showed the emperor's image; goods (grain from Egypt, olive oil, wine, metals); slavery widespread; entertainment at the Colosseum and Circus Maximus.

## Visual and Performing Arts 6: Personal investigation artwork

**Prepare:** Review the sketchbook 'ideas' page; gather the child's chosen media.

**Watch for:**
- Picking a theme that's too broad. Narrow it to a specific idea you can show.
- Waiting until the end for feedback. Mid-process feedback helps.

### Day 1 (Monday): Big idea
*Student:* Review your sketchbook ideas page and choose a personally meaningful theme.

*How to teach:* Review ideas; choose a personally meaningful theme. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** What makes a theme meaningful?
  **A:** It connects to your experiences, interests or values.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plan
*Student:* Make three thumbnail sketches and choose media.

*How to teach:* Make three thumbnails; choose media. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** Why choose media carefully?
  **A:** Different media create different effects suited to the idea.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Create
*Student:* Begin the culminating artwork.

*How to teach:* Begin the culminating artwork. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What is a concept in art?
  **A:** The idea or message behind an artwork.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Create and revise
*Student:* Continue the artwork and get feedback halfway through.

*How to teach:* Continue; get mid-point feedback. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** What feedback question helps most?
  **A:** 'What do you think this artwork is about?' — to check the message.

### Day 5 (Friday): Finish
*Student:* Complete the artwork and reflect on whether it conveys your intended meaning.

*How to teach:* Complete and reflect on whether it conveys intended meaning. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** How do you know if your art conveys meaning?
  **A:** Viewers can describe the intended idea; elements and principles support it.

**Assessment:** Culminating artwork.

**Answer key:** Culminating artwork rubric (1–4 each): clear personal concept, purposeful media and techniques, use of elements/principles, craftsmanship, reflection. Proficient = 3+ each.

## Physical Education 6: Following the plan: week 2

**Prepare:** Gather: fitness log, stopwatch. Fitness log and stopwatch. Check the space for hazards, have water ready, and start every session with the dynamic warm-up.

**Watch for:**
- Not progressing. Add reps or sets slowly.
- Skipping the log.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cardio day
*Student:* Complete your plan's cardio workout and record heart rate.

*How to teach:* Cardio; record heart rate.

- **Q:** Was your heart rate in the target zone?
  **A:** Answer from data; for age 12, about 125–177 bpm.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Strength day
*Student:* Complete your strength workout with progression (add reps or sets).

*How to teach:* Strength with progression.

- **Q:** Give an example of progression.
  **A:** Going from 2 sets of 10 squats to 3 sets of 10.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Active play
*Student:* Play a favorite sport from the year for 30 minutes.

*How to teach:* 30 minutes of active play.

- **Q:** Does playing a sport count as exercise?
  **A:** Yes, if it's moderate-to-vigorous.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Flexibility day
*Student:* Complete your flexibility workout and a sit-and-reach check.

*How to teach:* Flexibility and sit-and-reach.

- **Q:** What improves flexibility?
  **A:** Regular stretching after warming up.

### Day 5 (Friday): Log review
*Student:* Review your log and rate exertion for each day.

*How to teach:* Rate exertion.

- **Q:** What does a 9/10 exertion rating mean?
  **A:** Very hard effort — nearly maximum.

**Assessment:** Fitness log review.

**Answer key:** Log review: 5 days logged with FITT, heart rate on cardio day, progression on strength day, sit-and-reach result, and a rating of perceived exertion (1–10) for each day.

## Health 6: Health care services and providers

**Prepare:** With a parent, look at family health records (immunization record, last check-up). Have a prescription label example. Look up a local clinic, dentist and the county public health department online.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking you only see a doctor when sick. Regular check-ups catch problems early and keep vaccines current.
- Being afraid to ask a doctor or pharmacist questions. Asking questions is your right and keeps you safe.

### Day 1 (Monday): Providers
*Student:* Match 10 health-care providers to what they do.

*How to teach:* Match 10 providers to what they do (pediatrician, dentist, orthodontist, optometrist, dermatologist, pharmacist, school nurse, counselor, allergist, physical therapist).

- **Q:** What does a dermatologist treat?
  **A:** Skin, hair and nail conditions (like acne).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Check-up prep
*Student:* Write questions to ask at your next check-up and learn what a well-child visit includes.

*How to teach:* Write questions for the next check-up; learn what a well-child visit includes (growth, vision, hearing, vaccines, questions).

- **Q:** Name three things checked at a well-child visit.
  **A:** Height and weight, blood pressure, vision and hearing, vaccines, development and questions about health.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pharmacist role
*Student:* Read a prescription label and list questions to ask a pharmacist.

*How to teach:* Read a prescription label; list questions for a pharmacist. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** What should you ask a pharmacist about a new medicine?
  **A:** How and when to take it, side effects, whether to take it with food, and interactions with other medicines.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Community services
*Student:* Locate a local clinic, dentist and public health department online.

*How to teach:* Locate a local clinic, dentist and public health department. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** What does a public health department do?
  **A:** Protects community health: vaccines, disease tracking, food safety, health education.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take the Personal and Consumer Health unit test.

*How to teach:* Take the Personal and Consumer Health unit test. Have your child work independently; afterward, go over any errors together and let them explain the correct answer back to you.

- **Q:** What is a specialist?
  **A:** A doctor with extra training in one area (e.g., allergist, dermatologist).

**Assessment:** Unit test.

**Answer key:** Unit test key: hygiene and gingivitis; sleep needs; 60/60 rule; sunscreen; pathogens; vaccines; antibiotics only for bacteria; chronic disease risk factors; asthma; unit price; quackery; health-care providers. Proficient = 80%.
