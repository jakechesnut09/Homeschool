# Teacher's Guide — 6th Grade, Week 1

## English Language Arts 6: Plot and suspense: 'Rikki-Tikki-Tavi'

**Prepare:** Gather: 'Rikki-Tikki-Tavi' by Rudyard Kipling (from The Jungle Book), reading journal. Print or download 'Rikki-Tikki-Tavi' (in The Jungle Book on Project Gutenberg). Read it yourself first: the snake fights are suspenseful but not graphic.

**Watch for:**
- Writing a 'quote sandwich' with no explanation. After every quote, the child must add a sentence beginning 'This shows…'.
- Using objective pronouns as subjects ('Him and me went'). Test by dropping the other person: 'Me went' sounds wrong, so it's 'He and I went'.

### Day 1 (Monday): Reader's notebook launch
*Student:* Set up a reader's notebook, write a reading-history page, and read the first third of 'Rikki-Tikki-Tavi', jotting predictions about the cobras.

*How to teach:* Help the child set up the notebook (reading-history page, a section for vocabulary). Read the first third aloud together, pausing when Nag appears so the child writes a prediction about the cobras.

- **Q:** What is Rikki-tikki, and how does he come to live with Teddy's family?
  **A:** He is a young mongoose; a summer flood washes him out of his burrow, and Teddy's family finds him and revives him.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Character and evidence
*Student:* Finish the story; list three traits of Rikki with a quoted line of evidence for each, and practice the 'claim + quote + explain' paragraph frame.

*How to teach:* Finish the story. Model one claim + quote + explain sentence set about Rikki ('Rikki is curious. The family motto is "Run and find out." This shows…'), then the child writes three traits with evidence.

- **Q:** Name a trait of Rikki and support it with an event from the story.
  **A:** Brave: he attacks Nag in the bathroom and holds on even as he is thrown about; curious: he follows the motto 'Run and find out'; protective: he kills Karait near Teddy.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Plot mountain
*Student:* Draw a plot diagram of the story and mark where Kipling builds suspense; discuss with a parent which moment is the climax and why.

*How to teach:* Draw the plot mountain with the child labeling exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and resolution. Ask: 'Which moment had you most worried?'

- **Q:** What is the climax of the story? Defend your choice.
  **A:** Most readers choose Rikki following Nagaina down into the rat-hole: we don't know if he will come out alive. (Accept the Nag fight with a reason, but the final Nagaina battle resolves the main conflict.)
- **Q:** Name one way Kipling builds suspense.
  **A:** Examples: Nag and Nagaina plotting in the dark; Darzee's wife distracting Nagaina; Nagaina threatening Teddy at breakfast; the long silence after Rikki goes down the hole.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Pronoun case
*Student:* Lesson on subjective, objective and possessive pronouns; correct 15 sentences (e.g., 'Him and me went') and find 5 pronouns in the story.

*How to teach:* Teach the three cases with a chart (I/me/my, he/him/his, we/us/our, they/them/their). Model the 'drop the other person' test, then the child corrects 15 sentences and finds 5 story pronouns.

- **Q:** Correct: 'Him and me went to the garden.'
  **A:** 'He and I went to the garden.'
- **Q:** Correct: 'Teddy's mother gave the egg to my sister and I.'
  **A:** '…to my sister and me.' (object of the preposition)

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a one-paragraph response explaining how Rikki changes from beginning to end, citing two quotations; take a 10-item pronoun-case quiz.

*How to teach:* The child writes the paragraph independently, then takes the 10-item pronoun quiz.

- **Q:** How is Rikki different at the end of the story than at the beginning?
  **A:** At first he is a curious, inexperienced young mongoose who has never fought a cobra; by the end he has defeated Karait, Nag and Nagaina and is the confident protector of the garden.

**Assessment:** Evidence paragraph on character plus pronoun-case quiz.

**Answer key:** Evidence paragraph (4 = clear claim about how Rikki changes, e.g., from a curious, untested young mongoose to a confident, battle-proven protector; two accurate quotations — e.g., the family motto 'Run and find out' early, and the closing line that he 'had a right to be proud of himself'; each explained; 3 = claim and quotes but thin explanation; 2 = one quote or retelling only; 1 = no evidence). Pronoun quiz sample: 1) He and I 2) her and me 3) between you and me 4) We students 5) us kids 6) whom/who → 'Who is calling?' 7) My brother and I 8) to Teddy and me 9) theirs 10) its.

## Mathematics 6: Area of rectangles and parallelograms

**Prepare:** Gather: graph paper, scissors, tangram set or paper tangrams. Print or cut paper tangrams if you have no set; draw a few tilted parallelograms on graph paper ahead of Day 4.

**Watch for:**
- Using the slanted side as the height. Correct: height is always perpendicular (makes a square corner) to the base; check with the corner of an index card.
- Thinking shapes that look different must have different areas. Show that rearranging pieces never changes area — nothing is added or removed.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tiling the plane
*Student:* Compare areas of tangram shapes by covering them with smaller pieces, then explain in writing why two different-looking shapes can have equal area.

*How to teach:* Let the child cover the square, the medium triangle and the parallelogram tangram pieces with the two small triangles. Ask: 'If both are covered by exactly the same pieces, what do you know about their areas?' Have them write two sentences explaining that area is the amount of surface covered, not the shape.

- **Q:** In a standard tangram set, the square and the medium triangle can each be covered exactly by the two small triangles. Which has more area?
  **A:** Neither — they have equal area, because each is made of the same two small triangles; rearranging pieces does not change area.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Composing and decomposing
*Student:* Find the area of L-shaped and staircase regions on graph paper three different ways (cut apart, enclose and subtract, rearrange) and record each method.

*How to teach:* Draw an L-shape on graph paper: a 6-wide by 8-tall rectangle with a 3-wide by 4-tall piece missing from the top-right. Model one method (cut into two rectangles), then have the child do 'enclose and subtract' and 'rearrange' themselves and compare answers.

- **Q:** Find the area of that L-shape (6 × 8 rectangle with a 3 × 4 corner removed) two ways.
  **A:** 36 square units. Cut apart: 3 × 8 = 24 plus 3 × 4 = 12 → 36. Enclose and subtract: 6 × 8 = 48 − 12 = 36.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Parallelograms to rectangles
*Student:* Cut a paper parallelogram along its height, slide the triangle to form a rectangle, and use this to write A = b x h; solve 8 parallelogram area problems.

*How to teach:* Have the child cut out a paper parallelogram, draw a height line from one top corner straight down to the base, cut along it and slide the triangle to the other side. Ask what shape it makes and what its length and width are; record A = b × h, then assign the 8 problems.

- **Q:** A parallelogram has base 9 cm and height 4 cm. What is its area?
  **A:** 36 sq cm (9 × 4).
- **Q:** Base 7.5 m, height 2 m?
  **A:** 15 sq m.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Bases and heights
*Student:* Identify every base-height pair on tilted parallelograms, including heights that fall outside the shape, and compute areas of 10 drawn figures.

*How to teach:* Show that any side can be the base, and the height is the perpendicular distance to the opposite side — sometimes drawn outside the shape with a dashed extension. Draw one tilted parallelogram and find area with both base-height pairs to show they agree.

- **Q:** A parallelogram has sides 10 and 6. The height to the side of length 10 is 4.8. What is the height to the side of length 6?
  **A:** Area = 10 × 4.8 = 48, so height = 48 ÷ 6 = 8.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a 10-problem quiz on rectangle and parallelogram area and write one sentence correcting any error you made.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-problem quiz (see key) without help. Afterward, have the child find any error and write one sentence naming the mistake (e.g., 'I used the slanted side instead of the height').

- **Q:** Why does A = b × h work for a parallelogram?
  **A:** You can cut off a triangle from one side and move it to the other to form a rectangle with the same base and height, so its area is base × height.

**Assessment:** Quiz: area of composite rectangles and parallelograms; explanation of why A = bh works.

**Answer key:** Sample 10-problem quiz: 1) rectangle 7 × 12 = 84 sq units 2) parallelogram b = 10, h = 6 → 60 3) b = 4.5, h = 8 → 36 4) 10 × 6 rectangle with a 4 × 3 corner removed → 60 − 12 = 48 5) parallelogram area 72, base 9 → height 8 6) b = 15, h = 3.2 → 48 7) square side 9 → 81 8) parallelogram b = 20, h = 2.5 → 50 9) two rectangles 5 × 4 and 3 × 6 joined → 20 + 18 = 38 10) parallelogram area 56, height 7 → base 8. Explanation (full credit): cutting the right triangle off one end and sliding it to the other end makes a rectangle with the same base and height, so area = b × h; the height must be perpendicular to the base.

## Science 6 (Integrated): Scientific investigations and the science notebook

**Prepare:** Gather: paper towels of 3 brands, water, measuring cup, notebook. Use the same measuring cup each trial; label three cups. Have a composition book for the notebook.

**Watch for:**
- Changing more than one variable at once. In a fair test only the independent variable changes; everything else stays constant.
- Thinking one trial is enough. Repeat trials (3) and average to reduce error.

### Day 1 (Monday): Notebook setup
*Student:* Set up a science notebook with table of contents, safety rules and a page explaining what scientists and engineers do differently.

*How to teach:* Set up the notebook together (table of contents, safety rules). Discuss: scientists ask questions about the natural world; engineers design solutions to problems.

- **Q:** What is the difference between what scientists and engineers do?
  **A:** Scientists ask questions and investigate to explain how the world works; engineers define problems and design, test and improve solutions.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Testable questions
*Student:* Turn 10 'wonder' questions into testable questions and identify independent and dependent variables in each.

*How to teach:* Model turning 'Why do plants grow?' into 'Does the amount of water affect how tall bean plants grow in 2 weeks?' The child rewrites 10 and names variables.

- **Q:** In 'Does the temperature of water affect how fast sugar dissolves?', name the independent and dependent variables.
  **A:** Independent: water temperature. Dependent: time for sugar to dissolve.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Paper towel investigation
*Student:* Design and run a fair test of which paper towel absorbs the most water, keeping water amount and soaking time constant.

*How to teach:* The child writes the procedure first (same size sheet, same 100 mL water, same soak time), then runs three trials per brand, measuring water left over or squeezed out.

- **Q:** Name three things that must stay the same for a fair paper-towel test.
  **A:** Towel size, amount of water, soaking time (also how it's squeezed/drained).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Data and graphs
*Student:* Organize results in a data table, calculate averages of three trials and draw a bar graph.

*How to teach:* Model a data table with Trial 1–3 and Average columns; compute averages; draw a labeled bar graph.

- **Q:** Trials of 28, 30 and 32 mL. What is the average?
  **A:** 30 mL.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a claim-evidence-reasoning conclusion for the paper towel test and quiz on variables.

*How to teach:* Model the CER frame. The child writes a conclusion and takes the variables quiz.

- **Q:** What is the 'evidence' part of a CER?
  **A:** Specific data from the investigation (e.g., averages) that supports the claim.

**Assessment:** CER conclusion and variables quiz.

**Answer key:** Variables quiz: for 'Does the amount of sunlight affect plant height?' — independent: amount of sunlight; dependent: plant height; controlled: plant type, water, soil, pot size. For the paper towel test — independent: brand; dependent: amount of water absorbed (mL); constants: towel size, water amount, soaking time, squeezing method. CER rubric — 4: a claim that answers the question, specific data as evidence (numbers from the investigation), and reasoning that connects the evidence to the science idea; 3: claim and evidence correct but reasoning thin; 2: claim with vague evidence; 1: claim only or incorrect. (Example: 'Brand B absorbs the most water — it held an average of 32 mL compared with 25 mL and 20 mL — because its thicker fibers have more space for water.')

## World History and Geography: Ancient Civilizations: Tools of the historian and geographer

**Prepare:** Gather: world map, long strip of paper for timeline, family artifacts. Choose three family artifacts (an old photo, a letter or card, an object). Hang a long paper strip on a wall for the year's timeline.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking BCE dates count up like CE dates. BCE years count down toward year 1: 3000 BCE is earlier than 2000 BCE.
- Thinking secondary sources are less valuable. Each type has uses; primary sources come from the time, secondary sources interpret them.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is history?
*Student:* Examine three family artifacts (photo, letter, object) and write what each reveals and what it cannot tell you.

*How to teach:* Handle each artifact together. The child writes what it reveals and what it can't tell (feelings, the whole story).

- **Q:** What can an old family photo tell you, and what can't it tell you?
  **A:** It can show clothing, places, who was together and roughly when; it can't tell what people were thinking or what happened just before or after.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Primary vs. secondary
*Student:* Sort 12 sources (a diary, an encyclopedia article, a coin, a textbook) as primary or secondary and justify each.

*How to teach:* Define primary (created at the time by participants) vs. secondary (later interpretation). The child sorts 12 sources and justifies.

- **Q:** Is a modern textbook chapter on ancient Egypt primary or secondary? A clay tablet from Sumer?
  **A:** Textbook — secondary; clay tablet — primary.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Timelines and BCE/CE
*Student:* Build a classroom-length timeline from 10,000 BCE to 500 CE that you will add to all year; practice calculating years between BCE and CE dates.

*How to teach:* Mark 10,000 BCE, 1 CE and 500 CE on the strip with equal spacing. Practice: for two BCE dates subtract; across BCE/CE add.

- **Q:** How many years from 3000 BCE to 1000 BCE?
  **A:** 2,000 years.
- **Q:** Approximately how many years from 500 BCE to 500 CE?
  **A:** About 1,000 years (add 500 + 500; strictly 999 because there is no year 0).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Map skills
*Student:* Locate the seven continents, major rivers (Nile, Tigris, Euphrates, Indus, Huang He) and practice latitude/longitude coordinates.

*How to teach:* On a world map, the child labels continents and the five rivers, then finds places by coordinates.

- **Q:** On which continent is the Nile? The Huang He?
  **A:** Africa; Asia (China).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on sources, timelines and map skills.

*How to teach:* Give the skills quiz.

- **Q:** What do lines of latitude measure?
  **A:** Distance north or south of the equator.

**Assessment:** Skills quiz and timeline setup.

**Answer key:** Skills quiz: 1) a diary from 1850 — primary 2) an encyclopedia article — secondary 3) an ancient coin — primary 4) a textbook — secondary 5) 3000 BCE to 1000 BCE = 2,000 years 6) 500 BCE to 500 CE ≈ 1,000 years (add the two numbers; strictly 999 since there is no year 0) 7) Nile — Africa; Tigris and Euphrates — Southwest Asia (Iraq); Indus — Pakistan; Huang He — China 8) latitude lines run east–west and measure north/south of the equator; longitude lines run north–south and measure east/west of the Prime Meridian. Timeline: 10,000 BCE to 500 CE with even spacing (e.g., 10 cm per 500 years).

## Visual and Performing Arts 6: Sketchbook launch and line

**Prepare:** Gather a sketchbook, HB, 2B and 4B pencils (B = softer, darker), an eraser and a fine marker. Set a timer for 30-second gestures and ask a family member to pose.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a 'good' blind contour looks realistic. It's an observation exercise; distorted results are normal and useful.
- Spending too long on gesture drawings. Gesture captures movement and the line of action in seconds, not detail.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sketchbook cover
*Student:* Design a sketchbook cover with your name in a decorative style and an 'ideas I want to explore' page.

*How to teach:* Your child designs a cover with their name in a decorative lettering style, then starts an 'ideas I want to explore' page (subjects, media, questions) to return to all year.

- **Q:** Why keep an 'ideas I want to explore' page?
  **A:** It records interests and inspiration to use for future projects, especially the final personal artwork.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Line types
*Student:* Fill a page with 20 line types (zigzag, hatched, wavy, broken) and label each with a mood word.

*How to teach:* Your child fills a page with 20 line types and labels each with a mood word (e.g., jagged = tense, flowing curve = calm).

- **Q:** How can a line show mood?
  **A:** Its direction, thickness, speed and shape suggest feelings: horizontal = calm, diagonal/zigzag = energy or tension, curvy = gentle.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Contour drawing
*Student:* Make three blind contour and three modified contour drawings of your hand.

*How to teach:* Three blind contours (never look at the paper) and three modified contours (glance occasionally) of the hand.

- **Q:** What skill does blind contour build?
  **A:** Careful observation and eye–hand coordination.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Gesture drawing
*Student:* Do ten 30-second gesture drawings of a family member in different poses.

*How to teach:* Ten 30-second gestures; start each with the line of action (a curve through the spine). Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** What is a 'line of action'?
  **A:** A single flowing line that shows the main direction and movement of a pose.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & reflect
*Student:* Choose your best line drawing and write two sentences on what line choices made it successful.

*How to teach:* Your child picks the best line drawing and writes two sentences about successful line choices.

- **Q:** What is line weight?
  **A:** How thick or dark a line is; varying it adds emphasis and depth.

**Assessment:** Sketchbook check with written reflection.

**Answer key:** Sketchbook check: cover, ideas page, 20 labeled lines, 3 blind + 3 modified contours, 10 gestures. Reflection rubric: 4 = two sentences using specific art vocabulary (contour, line weight, gesture) tied to the drawing; 3 = one term; 2 = general; 1 = missing.

## Physical Education 6: Warm-ups, safety and heart rate

**Prepare:** Gather: stopwatch, cones, water bottle. Clear a safe open space and set up cones. Know the signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps) and stop activity if they appear. Check the space for hazards, have water ready, and start every session with the dynamic warm-up.

**Watch for:**
- Holding static stretches before activity. Warm up with movement (dynamic) first; save held stretches for the cool-down.
- Pressing too hard with the thumb to find a pulse. Use the index and middle fingers lightly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dynamic warm-up
*Student:* Learn a 10-move dynamic warm-up (high knees, butt kicks, carioca, arm circles) and a static-stretch cool-down.

*How to teach:* Model each of the 10 warm-up moves with a cue ('knees to hip height'); the child copies, then do the cool-down stretches, holding each 15–30 seconds.

- **Q:** Why do a dynamic warm-up before exercise?
  **A:** It raises heart rate and muscle temperature and loosens joints, which helps prevent injury.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Finding your pulse
*Student:* Find your carotid and radial pulse, measure resting heart rate for 15 seconds x 4, and record it.

*How to teach:* Show the radial (thumb side of the wrist) and carotid (side of the neck) pulses. The child counts for 15 seconds and multiplies by 4.

- **Q:** You count 19 beats in 15 seconds. What is your heart rate?
  **A:** 76 beats per minute.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Heart rate response
*Student:* Measure heart rate after walking, jogging and sprinting for 1 minute each and graph the results.

*How to teach:* Walk, jog and sprint 1 minute each, measuring pulse right after; graph.

- **Q:** Which activity should produce the highest heart rate, and why?
  **A:** Sprinting — higher intensity means muscles need more oxygen, so the heart pumps faster.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Safety and hydration
*Student:* Play a tag game, then discuss heat safety, hydration and proper shoes; set a daily water goal.

*How to teach:* Play tag, then discuss heat safety, hydration and shoes; the child sets a water goal.

- **Q:** Name two signs of heat illness and what to do.
  **A:** Dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps, heavy sweating or confusion; stop, move to shade, drink water and tell an adult.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Lead the warm-up yourself and complete a short quiz on heart rate and warm-up purpose.

*How to teach:* The child leads the warm-up; give the quiz.

- **Q:** Where do you find the carotid pulse?
  **A:** On the side of the neck, beside the windpipe.

**Assessment:** Student-led warm-up and heart-rate log.

**Answer key:** Student-led warm-up checklist (4 = leads all 10 moves in order with correct form cues and counts, explains warm-up purpose; 3 = minor omissions; 2 = needs prompting; 1 = can't lead). Heart-rate log: resting rate (typically about 60–100 bpm for this age), plus walk/jog/sprint rates rising in order. Quiz: 1) warm-ups raise heart rate and body temperature and prepare muscles to prevent injury 2) count beats for 15 seconds × 4 = beats per minute 3) radial pulse at the wrist, carotid at the neck 4) heart rate rises with intensity.

## Health 6: Dimensions of wellness and health skills

**Prepare:** Get a health journal and draw a wellness wheel with five slices (physical, emotional, social, intellectual, environmental). List the eight California health skills: essential concepts, analyzing influences, accessing valid information, interpersonal communication, decision making, goal setting, practicing health-enhancing behaviors, health promotion/advocacy. Write 12 behavior cards for sorting.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking wellness is only physical. All five dimensions affect each other — poor sleep (physical) can cause irritability (emotional) and arguments (social).
- Thinking a risk factor guarantees a bad outcome. Risk factors raise the chance of problems; protective factors lower it, and you can strengthen them.

### Day 1 (Monday): Wellness wheel
*Student:* Rate yourself 1–5 on a wellness wheel (physical, emotional, social, intellectual, environmental) and shade it in.

*How to teach:* Your child rates each slice 1–5 and shades the wheel. Discuss the highest and lowest areas and how they connect.

- **Q:** Give one example of a habit for intellectual and for environmental wellness.
  **A:** Intellectual: reading, learning a new skill, puzzles. Environmental: keeping your space clean, spending time outside, recycling.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Health skills
*Student:* Match the eight CA health skills to middle-school scenarios and write one example for each.

*How to teach:* Your child matches the eight health skills to middle-school scenarios and writes one example of each.

- **Q:** A student checks whether a supplement ad is trustworthy by looking at the CDC website. Which skill is that?
  **A:** Accessing valid information.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Risk and protective factors
*Student:* Sort 12 behaviors into risk or protective factors and explain two.

*How to teach:* Your child sorts 12 behaviors (e.g., wearing a helmet, skipping breakfast, having a trusted adult, vaping) into risk or protective factors and explains two.

- **Q:** Is 'being involved in a sport or club' a risk or protective factor? Why?
  **A:** Protective — it builds skills, positive friendships and supervised time, lowering the chance of risky behavior.

### Day 4 (Thursday): SMART goal
*Student:* Write a SMART goal to strengthen the lowest section of your wellness wheel and make a tracker.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a SMART goal for the lowest wheel section and makes a tracker. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** Rewrite as a SMART goal: 'I'll be more social.'
  **A:** Example: 'I will call or meet a friend at least twice a week for the next 4 weeks and log it on my tracker.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a quick check on wellness dimensions and share your goal with a parent.

*How to teach:* Give a quick check orally; your child shares the goal with you. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** Name the five dimensions of wellness on the wheel.
  **A:** Physical, emotional, social, intellectual, environmental.

**Assessment:** Quick check and SMART goal.

**Answer key:** Quick check key: 1) five dimensions — physical, emotional, social, intellectual, environmental 2) dimensions affect each other (example) 3) risk factor = raises chance of harm; protective factor = lowers it 4) one example of each 5) SMART = Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. SMART goal rubric: 4 = all SMART parts, targets lowest area, includes tracker; 3 = one part missing; 2 = vague; 1 = missing.
