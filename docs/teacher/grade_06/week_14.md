# Teacher's Guide — 6th Grade, Week 14

## English Language Arts 6: Analyzing arguments

**Prepare:** Gather: CommonLit argument articles (e.g., on school start times, homework, screen time), highlighters. Choose two CommonLit argument articles (e.g., later school start times) and print them; get three highlighter colors.

**Watch for:**
- Treating an opinion as an argument. An argument has a claim supported by reasons and evidence.
- Accepting any evidence. Ask: Is it relevant? Is there enough? Is it from a reliable source?

### Day 1 (Monday): What is an argument?
*Student:* Compare a fact, an opinion and an argument; identify the claim in 6 short advertisements and op-ed excerpts.

*How to teach:* Show 'Pizza is food' (fact), 'Pizza is delicious' (opinion), and a claim with reasons. The child finds claims in 6 ads/excerpts.

- **Q:** Is 'Schools should start at 8:30 because teens need more sleep' a fact, opinion or argument?
  **A:** An argument — a claim supported by a reason.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Claim, reasons, evidence
*Student:* Read an article on whether middle schools should start later and annotate the claim, reasons and evidence in three colors.

*How to teach:* Model annotating claim (one color), reasons (second), evidence (third).

- **Q:** What is the difference between a reason and evidence?
  **A:** A reason explains why the claim is true; evidence (facts, data, expert quotes) proves the reason.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Supported or not?
*Student:* Evaluate each piece of evidence in a second article as relevant, sufficient or unsupported and explain why.

*How to teach:* The child labels each piece of evidence relevant, sufficient or unsupported.

- **Q:** Evidence: 'My cousin is always tired in the morning.' Is it sufficient to prove all teens need later starts?
  **A:** No — one person's experience is not enough evidence for a general claim.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Author's purpose and bias
*Student:* Determine each author's point of view and identify loaded words that reveal bias.

*How to teach:* Find loaded words (e.g., 'cruel', 'ridiculous') and discuss point of view.

- **Q:** What are loaded words?
  **A:** Words with strong emotional connotations that push the reader toward the author's view.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Read a new short argument and complete an argument-analysis organizer independently as a quiz.

*How to teach:* The child analyzes a new argument independently.

- **Q:** What should you identify first when analyzing an argument?
  **A:** The author's claim (main position).

**Assessment:** Independent argument analysis.

**Answer key:** Argument-analysis organizer key (for any short article): Claim — one sentence of the author's position; Reasons — 2–3; Evidence — the facts, statistics, or expert quotes for each; evaluation — whether evidence is relevant and sufficient; Point of view and any loaded words. 4 = all parts accurate; 3 = one part weak; 2 = claim or reasons misidentified; 1 = incomplete.

## Mathematics 6: Dividing fractions by fractions

**Prepare:** Gather: fraction strips, grid paper. Work 2/3 ÷ 1/6 yourself with fraction strips before Day 4 so you can model it.

**Watch for:**
- Flipping the first fraction (dividend) instead of the divisor. Only the divisor is replaced by its reciprocal: a/b ÷ c/d = a/b × d/c.
- Forgetting to convert mixed numbers to improper fractions before using the reciprocal.

### Day 1 (Monday): How much in each group?
*Student:* Solve problems like '3/4 of a pound fills 1/2 a bag; how much fills a whole bag?' with tape diagrams (6 problems).

*How to teach:* Model: '¾ lb fills ½ bag; how much fills the whole bag?' Draw a tape: half the bag = ¾, so the whole = 2 × ¾ = 1½. The child solves 6 problems with tapes.

- **Q:** ¾ pound fills ½ a bag. How much fills a whole bag?
  **A:** 1½ pounds.
- **Q:** 2½ liters fills ⅚ of a tank. How much fills the whole tank?
  **A:** 3 liters (each sixth holds ½ L).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dividing by unit fractions
*Student:* Notice that dividing by 1/3 is the same as multiplying by 3, and test the pattern with 10 examples.

*How to teach:* Compute 2 ÷ ⅓ (6), 5 ÷ ⅓ (15) and compare with 2 × 3 and 5 × 3. The child tests 10 examples and states the pattern.

- **Q:** What is 5 ÷ ⅓? What is the shortcut?
  **A:** 15. Dividing by ⅓ is the same as multiplying by 3.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Algorithm for fraction division
*Student:* Use the reciprocal method to compute 15 quotients including mixed numbers, and check each with multiplication.

*How to teach:* Teach: keep the first fraction, multiply by the reciprocal of the second. Show checking: quotient × divisor = dividend.

- **Q:** Compute ¾ ÷ ⅖.
  **A:** 15/8 = 1⅞. Check: 15/8 × 2/5 = 30/40 = ¾.
- **Q:** Compute 2½ ÷ 1¼.
  **A:** 5/2 × 4/5 = 2.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Explain it
*Student:* Write a short explanation, with a diagram, of why 2/3 / 1/6 = 4, and critique a sample student's incorrect solution.

*How to teach:* Ask how many ⅙ fit in ⅔ using fraction strips; then show a sample wrong answer (3/2 × ⅙ = ¼) and have the child critique it.

- **Q:** Why is ⅔ ÷ ⅙ = 4?
  **A:** ⅔ = 4/6, so four ⅙-pieces fit in ⅔.
- **Q:** A student wrote ⅔ ÷ ⅙ = 3/2 × ⅙ = ¼. What went wrong?
  **A:** They flipped the dividend instead of the divisor. Correct: ⅔ × 6/1 = 4.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on computing fraction quotients and explaining the algorithm.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz, including one written explanation.

- **Q:** Compute 4 ÷ ⅔.
  **A:** 6.

**Assessment:** Fraction division computation quiz with one written explanation.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) ½ ÷ ⅛ = 4 2) ⅗ ÷ 3/10 = 2 3) ⅚ ÷ ⅔ = 5/4 = 1¼ 4) 4 ÷ ⅔ = 6 5) 1¾ ÷ ½ = 3½ 6) 2⅔ ÷ 1⅓ = 2 7) ⅞ ÷ 3 = 7/24 8) 9/10 ÷ ⅗ = 3/2 = 1½. Written explanation: 4 = diagram plus clear reasoning (e.g., ⅔ = 4/6, so four ⅙-pieces), 3 = correct with minor gaps, 2 = computation only, 1 = incorrect.

## Science 6 (Integrated): Ocean currents and regional climate

**Prepare:** Gather: baking dish, warm and cold colored water, hair dryer or straw, climate data for two cities. Gather monthly temperature and precipitation data for San Diego, Sacramento and Death Valley (NOAA/Weather.gov climate data).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking latitude alone determines climate. Ocean currents, elevation and mountains matter.
- Thinking mountains get equal rain on both sides.

### Day 1 (Monday): Wind-driven currents
*Student:* Blow across water in a baking dish with pepper flakes to model wind-driven currents and sketch the pattern.

*How to teach:* Blow across water with pepper; sketch.

- **Q:** What drives surface ocean currents?
  **A:** Wind (plus Earth's rotation and continents shaping their paths).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): California Current
*Student:* Compare San Francisco and Washington, D.C. temperatures at similar latitudes and explain the role of the cold California Current.

*How to teach:* Compare SF and D.C. temperatures.

- **Q:** Why are San Francisco summers cooler than Washington, D.C.'s?
  **A:** The cold California Current flowing south cools the coastal air.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rain shadow
*Student:* Model a rain shadow with a cardboard mountain and spray bottle; compare rainfall on both sides of the Sierra Nevada using data.

*How to teach:* Model a rain shadow; compare Sierra rainfall.

- **Q:** Why is the east side of the Sierra Nevada drier?
  **A:** Moist air rises up the western slope, cools and drops precipitation; air descending the east side is dry (rain shadow).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Climographs
*Student:* Make climographs for San Diego, Sacramento and Death Valley and explain their differences.

*How to teach:* Make three climographs.

- **Q:** Why does Death Valley get so little rain?
  **A:** It sits in rain shadows of mountains to the west.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a CER on why two places at the same latitude have different climates.

*How to teach:* The child writes the CER.

- **Q:** Name two factors besides latitude that affect climate.
  **A:** Ocean currents, elevation, mountains, distance from the ocean.

**Assessment:** Climographs and CER.

**Answer key:** Climograph rubric: bars for monthly precipitation, line for temperature, labeled axes. Expected patterns: San Diego mild year-round, little rain; Sacramento hot dry summers, wet winters; Death Valley extremely hot summers and very little rain (rain shadow). CER rubric — 4: a claim that answers the question, specific data as evidence (numbers from the investigation), and reasoning that connects the evidence to the science idea; 3: claim and evidence correct but reasoning thin; 2: claim with vague evidence; 1: claim only or incorrect. Expected: San Francisco and Washington, D.C. are at similar latitudes, but the cold California Current and ocean winds keep San Francisco cool in summer and mild in winter, while D.C. has hot summers and cold winters.

## World History and Geography: Ancient Civilizations: Kingdom, teachings and ethics

**Prepare:** Gather: Book of Ruth (short reading), excerpts from the prophets (Micah 6:8, Amos 5:24). Read the Book of Ruth (4 short chapters) and Micah 6:8, Amos 5:24.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the prophets predicted the future mainly. They also called for justice and ethical behavior.
- Thinking ethics are separate from law. In Jewish tradition they were linked.

### Day 1 (Monday): Naomi and Ruth
*Student:* Read the Book of Ruth and explain what it teaches about loyalty and kindness to strangers.

*How to teach:* Read Ruth; discuss loyalty.

- **Q:** What does Ruth teach about loyalty and kindness?
  **A:** Ruth stays with her mother-in-law Naomi ('Where you go I will go'), and Boaz treats her, a foreigner, kindly — valuing loyalty and kindness to strangers.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): David and Solomon
*Student:* Read about King David and Solomon's Temple and add the united monarchy to the timeline.

*How to teach:* Read about David and Solomon; add to timeline.

- **Q:** What did Solomon build?
  **A:** The First Temple in Jerusalem.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Prophets and justice
*Student:* Read Micah 6:8 and Amos 5:24 and explain the prophets' teachings on justice and righteousness.

*How to teach:* Read Micah and Amos.

- **Q:** Explain Micah 6:8 in your own words.
  **A:** God wants people to act justly, love kindness/mercy and be humble.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Western traditions
*Student:* Find connections between Jewish ethical teachings (Sabbath, care for the poor, rule of law) and later Western traditions.

*How to teach:* Connect teachings to Western traditions.

- **Q:** Name one Jewish teaching that influenced later Western traditions.
  **A:** A weekly day of rest (Sabbath), care for the poor, the idea that even rulers are under law.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining one key Jewish ethical teaching and its influence, citing a source.

*How to teach:* The child writes the paragraph.

- **Q:** Who was David?
  **A:** A king of Israel who made Jerusalem the capital.

**Assessment:** Evidence paragraph.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric — 4: clear topic sentence answering the question, at least two specific accurate pieces of evidence (named source, artifact or fact), explanation linking each to the claim, concluding sentence; 3: accurate but only one piece of evidence explained; 2: general statements without evidence; 1: inaccurate or off-topic. Expected: e.g., justice — Micah 6:8 ('do justly, love mercy, walk humbly with your God') and Amos 5:24 ('let justice roll down like waters') — influenced Western ideas that leaders must be just and that the poor deserve care; or the Sabbath as weekly rest.

## Visual and Performing Arts 6: Rhythm and meter

**Prepare:** Bookmark musictheory.net (note duration, rest duration, time signatures). Get a bucket and drumsticks or wooden spoons, and a metronome app. Note values in 4/4: whole = 4 beats, half = 2, quarter = 1, eighth = ½, sixteenth = ¼.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing beat and rhythm. Beat is the steady pulse; rhythm is the pattern of durations.
- Rushing sixteenth notes. Count '1-e-and-a' evenly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Beat vs. rhythm
*Student:* Tap the steady beat and then the rhythm of three songs and explain the difference.

*How to teach:* Tap the steady beat, then the rhythm of three songs; explain the difference. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** What is the difference between beat and rhythm?
  **A:** The beat is the steady pulse; rhythm is the pattern of long and short sounds.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Note values
*Student:* Complete musictheory.net lessons on note and rest durations and clap 8 written rhythm patterns.

*How to teach:* Complete note and rest lessons; clap 8 patterns. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** How many sixteenth notes equal one quarter note?
  **A:** Four.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Time signatures
*Student:* Identify 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 in recordings by counting and conducting patterns.

*How to teach:* Identify 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 by counting and conducting. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What does 3/4 mean?
  **A:** Three beats per measure; the quarter note gets one beat.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Bucket drumming
*Student:* Play a 4-measure bucket-drum rhythm with sixteenth notes from notation.

*How to teach:* Play a 4-measure bucket rhythm with sixteenths from notation. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** How do you count four sixteenth notes?
  **A:** '1-e-and-a.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform a rhythm reading test of 4 patterns with a metronome.

*How to teach:* Rhythm reading test of 4 patterns with a metronome. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** How many beats does a dotted half note get in 4/4?
  **A:** Three.

**Assessment:** Rhythm reading performance.

**Answer key:** Rhythm reading: 4 patterns at a steady metronome tempo (4 = all accurate; 3 = 3; 2 = 2; 1 = 0–1).

## Physical Education 6: Basketball: games and officiating

**Prepare:** Gather: basketball, hoop, whistle. Get a whistle; review referee signals. Check the space for hazards, have water ready, and start every session with the dynamic warm-up.

**Watch for:**
- Calling every bump a foul. Fouls are illegal contact that affects play.
- Getting upset in pressure games.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rules
*Student:* Learn traveling, double dribble and foul calls and practice referee signals.

*How to teach:* Learn violations and signals.

- **Q:** What is a double dribble?
  **A:** Dribbling with both hands, or stopping and then starting to dribble again.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): 3v3 game
*Student:* Play 3v3 games, rotating a student referee.

*How to teach:* 3v3 with student referee.

- **Q:** What does a referee signal by rolling the arms?
  **A:** Traveling.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Knockout and HORSE
*Student:* Play shooting games to practice under pressure.

*How to teach:* Knockout and HORSE.

- **Q:** How does pressure shooting help?
  **A:** Practices staying calm and keeping form.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Tournament
*Student:* Hold a mini-tournament with family or co-op, focusing on sportsmanship.

*How to teach:* Mini-tournament with sportsmanship.

- **Q:** Name a sportsmanship behavior.
  **A:** Shaking hands, helping opponents up, accepting calls.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Take a basketball rules quiz and redo the shooting check to compare with week 12.

*How to teach:* Quiz and redo the shooting check.

- **Q:** How many points is a free throw?
  **A:** 1.

**Assessment:** Rules quiz and shooting improvement.

**Answer key:** Rules quiz: 1) traveling = moving the pivot foot or taking steps without dribbling 2) double dribble = dribbling with two hands or dribbling again after stopping 3) personal foul = illegal contact 4) free throws are worth 1 point; regular baskets 2; shots beyond the arc 3 5) the ball goes out of bounds to the team that didn't touch it last. Shooting improvement: compare percentage with Week 12.

## Health 6: Digital citizenship and online safety

**Prepare:** Preview a Common Sense Education digital citizenship lesson (grade 6, free). Sit with your child to review privacy settings on a family device or app. Write 5 sample messages (phishing, prize scam, stranger asking for photos, a real message, a friend request from someone unknown).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking deleted posts disappear. Screenshots, archives and shares can keep them forever.
- Thinking online strangers are always obvious. Predators may pretend to be kids and build trust slowly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Digital footprint
*Student:* Search a made-up name scenario and discuss what a digital footprint reveals and how long it lasts.

*How to teach:* Discuss a made-up name scenario: what a search could reveal (photos, comments, school) and how long it lasts.

- **Q:** What is a digital footprint?
  **A:** The trail of information you leave online, which can be permanent and seen by others.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Privacy settings
*Student:* Review privacy settings on a family device or app with a parent.

*How to teach:* Review privacy settings with a parent: private account, location off, who can message you.

- **Q:** Why turn off location sharing?
  **A:** So strangers can't find where you are or live.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Scams and phishing
*Student:* Identify red flags in 5 sample messages (phishing, prize scams, stranger requests).

*How to teach:* Identify red flags in 5 messages. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** Name three red flags of a phishing or scam message.
  **A:** Urgent demands, requests for passwords or personal info, prizes you didn't enter, misspellings, strange links or senders.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Online strangers
*Student:* Role-play refusing and reporting an online stranger asking for photos or to meet.

*How to teach:* Role-play refusing and reporting an online stranger who asks for photos or to meet. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** What should you do if an online stranger asks for a photo or to meet?
  **A:** Don't respond or share, screenshot, block, and tell a trusted adult right away.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a Common Sense digital citizenship lesson and quiz.

*How to teach:* Complete a Common Sense lesson and quiz. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** What information should you never share online?
  **A:** Full name with address, phone number, school, passwords, location, or private photos.

**Assessment:** Digital citizenship quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: digital footprint is permanent; private settings; scam red flags; never share personal info; stop-block-tell for strangers; think before you post. Proficient = 80%.
