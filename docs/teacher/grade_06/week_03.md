# Teacher's Guide — 6th Grade, Week 3

## English Language Arts 6: Personal narrative: drafting

**Prepare:** Gather: mentor text: opening of 'Rikki-Tikki-Tavi', writing notebook. Model a heart map of your own to show the child.

**Watch for:**
- Choosing a huge topic ('my summer vacation'). Narrow to one small moment lasting minutes.
- Dialogue punctuation errors: commas and periods go inside quotation marks, and a new paragraph starts with each new speaker.

### Day 1 (Monday): Finding a story
*Student:* Brainstorm a heart map of memorable moments, choose one small moment, and tell it aloud to a listener in two minutes.

*How to teach:* Draw a heart map together; the child chooses one small moment and tells it aloud in two minutes while you time and ask one question.

- **Q:** Is 'my trip to Yosemite' a small moment? How could it be narrowed?
  **A:** Too big. Narrow to one moment, e.g., the minute you reached the top of the trail and saw the waterfall.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Strong leads
*Student:* Study leads from the unit's stories and write three possible openings for your narrative (action, dialogue, setting).

*How to teach:* Show Kipling's first sentence ('This is the story of the great war that Rikki-tikki-tavi fought single-handed…') as a lead that hooks us. The child drafts three different leads.

- **Q:** Name three types of leads and give an example of one.
  **A:** Action ('I slipped on the wet rock'), dialogue ('"Don't look down," Dad said'), setting ('The lake was silver and still').

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Drafting with dialogue
*Student:* Draft the middle of your narrative, including at least four lines of correctly punctuated dialogue.

*How to teach:* Review dialogue punctuation with one modeled line. The child drafts the middle with at least four lines.

- **Q:** Punctuate: we're almost there said Mom
  **A:** "We're almost there," said Mom.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Slow-motion moment
*Student:* Expand the most important moment with sensory detail and inner thoughts so it takes up at least a full paragraph.

*How to teach:* Model slowing down a moment: what you saw, heard, felt, thought, second by second. The child expands their key moment.

- **Q:** What can a writer add to slow down an important moment?
  **A:** Sensory details (sight, sound, touch, smell), inner thoughts and feelings, and small actions in sequence.

### Day 5 (Friday): Ending and reflection
*Student:* Draft an ending that reflects on why the moment mattered and read the full draft aloud for a family member's feedback.

*How to teach:* The child drafts a reflective ending and reads the full draft aloud; give one 'glow' and one 'grow'.

- **Q:** What should a reflective ending do?
  **A:** Show why the moment mattered or what the writer learned, not just 'then I went home'.

**Assessment:** Complete draft checked against a narrative checklist.

**Answer key:** Draft checklist (check each): one focused moment; a lead that is action, dialogue or setting; clear sequence with transitions; at least four lines of correctly punctuated dialogue; one slowed-down moment of at least a paragraph with sensory details and thoughts; an ending that reflects on why it mattered. 6/6 = ready to revise; 4–5 = add missing elements; ≤3 = conference and redraft.

## Mathematics 6: Polygons and real-world area

**Prepare:** Gather: graph paper, measuring tape, home floor plan sketch. Sketch your home's floor plan or pick a room/yard to measure; have a measuring tape ready for Days 3–4.

**Watch for:**
- Adding a triangle's full b × h instead of half when decomposing. Have the child label each piece's area before adding.
- Mixing units (feet and inches) in one area calculation. Convert all measurements to feet first (e.g., 10 ft 6 in = 10.5 ft).
- Rounding the number of tile boxes down. You can't buy part of a box — always round up.

### Day 1 (Monday): Decomposing polygons
*Student:* Split a trapezoid, a kite and a pentagon into triangles and rectangles and find each area; compare two different decompositions.

*How to teach:* Draw a trapezoid and a kite on grid paper. Have the child split each into rectangles and triangles, label each piece's area, and add; then try a second decomposition and confirm the total matches.

- **Q:** A trapezoid has parallel sides 6 and 10 and height 4. Find its area by decomposing.
  **A:** 32 square units: a 6 × 4 rectangle (24) plus two triangles whose bases total 4 with height 4 (½ × 4 × 4 = 8).
- **Q:** A kite has diagonals 8 and 6. Area?
  **A:** 24 square units — two triangles each with base 8 and height 3: 12 + 12.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Trapezoid strategies
*Student:* Use two copies of a trapezoid to make a parallelogram and solve 8 trapezoid-area problems with your own reasoning.

*How to teach:* Have the child cut two identical trapezoids and rotate one to form a parallelogram with base (b₁ + b₂). Guide them to discover A = ½(b₁ + b₂)h, then solve the 8 problems.

- **Q:** Trapezoid with bases 5 and 9 and height 6?
  **A:** 42 square units: ½ × (5 + 9) × 6.
- **Q:** Bases 3.5 and 6.5, height 4?
  **A:** 20 square units: ½ × 10 × 4.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Measure a room
*Student:* Measure a room or yard at home, sketch it to scale on graph paper, and calculate its area in square feet.

*How to teach:* Measure a room together, rounding to the nearest half-foot. The child sketches it on graph paper with a scale (1 square = 1 ft), splits any L-shape into rectangles and finds total area.

- **Q:** A room is 12 ft by 14 ft. Area?
  **A:** 168 square feet.
- **Q:** An L-shaped room is a 10 × 12 ft section plus a 4 × 6 ft nook. Area?
  **A:** 120 + 24 = 144 sq ft.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Covering a surface
*Student:* Determine how many 1 ft square tiles and how many boxes of tiles (10 per box) would cover the room you measured, including irregular parts.

*How to teach:* Ask: 'If each tile is 1 sq ft, how many tiles cover the room? How many boxes of 10?' Discuss rounding up for partial boxes and cut tiles for irregular edges.

- **Q:** A 168 sq ft room needs 1-ft tiles sold 10 per box. How many boxes?
  **A:** 17 boxes (168 ÷ 10 = 16.8, round up).
- **Q:** What about 145 sq ft?
  **A:** 15 boxes (14.5 rounds up).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a 6-problem composite-figure assessment and present your floor plan calculation to a family member.

*How to teach:* Give the 6-problem assessment independently. Then the child presents the floor plan to a family member, explaining how they decomposed the shape.

- **Q:** Why might two people decompose the same polygon differently but get the same area?
  **A:** Area doesn't depend on how you cut a shape; as long as pieces don't overlap or leave gaps, the pieces' areas add to the same total.

**Assessment:** Composite polygon quiz plus floor-plan area project.

**Answer key:** Composite quiz: 1) trapezoid bases 8 and 12, height 5 → 50 2) kite with diagonals 10 and 6 → 30 3) pentagon = 6 × 6 square + triangle base 6, height 4 → 36 + 12 = 48 4) 12 × 10 rectangle minus 4 × 5 corner → 100 5) 8 × 3 rectangle with triangle on top (base 8, height 3) → 24 + 12 = 36 6) hexagon made of two trapezoids (bases 4 and 8, height 3) → 18 + 18 = 36. Floor-plan rubric: 4 = accurate measurements, labeled scale drawing, correct area with work shown, tiles/boxes rounded correctly; 3 = one small calculation error; 2 = drawing or area incomplete; 1 = little work shown.

## Science 6 (Integrated): Temperature and particle motion

**Prepare:** Gather: food coloring, cups of hot and cold water, thermometer, PhET States of Matter. Use hot tap water (not boiling) and ice water; bookmark PhET States of Matter.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking temperature and thermal energy are the same. Temperature is average particle motion; thermal energy is the total — a bathtub of warm water has more thermal energy than a cup of hot cocoa.
- Thinking particles stop moving in cold substances. They move slower, but still move.

### Day 1 (Monday): Food coloring in hot and cold water
*Student:* Add a drop of food coloring to cups of hot and cold water at the same time, time how long each takes to mix, and record observations.

*How to teach:* Drop food coloring in both cups at the same moment; time and sketch observations every 30 seconds.

- **Q:** Predict: in which cup will the color spread faster?
  **A:** Hot water — its particles move faster.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Particle model
*Student:* Draw particle models of the two cups and explain the difference using particle motion.

*How to teach:* The child draws particle models (dots with motion lines) of hot vs. cold.

- **Q:** How should particle drawings of hot and cold water differ?
  **A:** Hot: longer motion lines (faster), slightly more spread apart; cold: shorter lines (slower).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Simulation
*Student:* Use the PhET States of Matter simulation to heat and cool a substance and record how particle speed changes with temperature.

*How to teach:* Run PhET; heat and cool; record particle speed.

- **Q:** What happens to particle speed as temperature rises?
  **A:** It increases.

### Day 4 (Thursday): How thermometers work
*Student:* Build a simple thermometer from a bottle, colored water and a straw, and test it in warm and cool water baths.

*How to teach:* Build the bottle thermometer; place it in warm then cool water.

- **Q:** Why does the liquid rise in the straw in warm water?
  **A:** The liquid's particles move faster and spread out, so it expands and climbs the straw.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a CER explaining why food coloring spreads faster in hot water; quiz on temperature vs. thermal energy.

*How to teach:* The child writes the CER and takes the quiz.

- **Q:** Which has more thermal energy: a bathtub of warm water or a cup of hot tea?
  **A:** The bathtub — it has far more particles, so more total energy, even at a lower temperature.

**Assessment:** CER and quiz.

**Answer key:** CER rubric — 4: a claim that answers the question, specific data as evidence (numbers from the investigation), and reasoning that connects the evidence to the science idea; 3: claim and evidence correct but reasoning thin; 2: claim with vague evidence; 1: claim only or incorrect. Expected: food coloring spreads faster in hot water because water particles move faster at higher temperatures and bump the dye particles around more quickly. Quiz: 1) temperature measures average kinetic energy of particles 2) thermal energy is total kinetic energy, so it depends on amount 3) a large pool at 25°C has more thermal energy than a cup at 80°C 4) heating makes particles move faster.

## World History and Geography: Ancient Civilizations: Peopling the world

**Prepare:** Gather: world outline map, colored pencils. Print a world outline map.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking people crossed oceans to reach the Americas first. Most evidence points to Beringia and/or a coastal route along it.
- Thinking migration was a planned journey. It happened gradually over thousands of years as groups followed food.

### Day 1 (Monday): Out of Africa
*Student:* Map the major human migration routes out of Africa with approximate dates.

*How to teach:* Map routes with approximate dates.

- **Q:** Where did modern humans first live?
  **A:** Africa.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ice Age land bridges
*Student:* Explain how lower sea levels during the Ice Age created Beringia and allowed people to reach the Americas.

*How to teach:* Explain Beringia.

- **Q:** What was Beringia, and why did it exist?
  **A:** A land bridge between Siberia and Alaska; during the Ice Age much water was locked in glaciers, lowering sea levels and exposing land.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Adapting to environments
*Student:* Compare how people adapted clothing, shelter and food in the Arctic, desert and rainforest.

*How to teach:* Compare Arctic, desert and rainforest adaptations.

- **Q:** Name one Arctic adaptation.
  **A:** Fur and hide clothing; snow or hide shelters; hunting seals and caribou.

### Day 4 (Thursday): First Californians
*Student:* Read about early evidence of people in California (e.g., Channel Islands sites) and add it to the timeline.

*How to teach:* Read about Channel Islands sites (Arlington Springs on Santa Rosa Island, about 13,000 years old).

- **Q:** What do Channel Islands sites show about early Californians?
  **A:** People lived on islands off California about 13,000 years ago, so they had boats and used ocean resources.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Map quiz on migration routes and a short answer on human adaptation.

*How to teach:* Give the map quiz.

- **Q:** How did lower sea levels help people migrate?
  **A:** They exposed land bridges like Beringia that people could walk across.

**Assessment:** Migration map and quiz.

**Answer key:** Migration map: arrows from East Africa to the Middle East (~70,000–60,000 years ago), to South Asia and Australia (~50,000–65,000 years ago), Europe (~45,000), East Asia, and across Beringia to the Americas (by ~15,000 or more years ago). Quiz short answer (4 = explains adaptation with two environments and specific examples: Arctic — fur clothing, snow/hide shelters, hunting seals; desert — loose clothing, moving between water sources; rainforest — light shelters, gathering fruits, hunting small animals).

## Visual and Performing Arts 6: Color theory

**Prepare:** Tempera or watercolor (red, yellow, blue, white, black), brushes, a paper-plate palette and paper. Use one water cup for rinsing and change it only when muddy (water conservation).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking hue and value are the same. Hue is the color name; value is how light or dark it is.
- Thinking warm and cool colors are only about temperature. They also suggest mood and distance (warm advances, cool recedes).

### Day 1 (Monday): Color wheel
*Student:* Paint a 12-part color wheel by mixing only red, yellow and blue.

*How to teach:* Paint a 12-part wheel mixing only primaries. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What are the three tertiary colors next to orange?
  **A:** Red-orange and yellow-orange are the tertiaries on either side of orange.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tints and shades
*Student:* Paint a tint and shade strip of one color with 7 steps; practice proper brush cleaning and water conservation.

*How to teach:* Paint a 7-step tint and shade strip; practice brush cleaning and saving water. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** How do you make a shade?
  **A:** Add black (a little at a time) to a hue.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Color schemes
*Student:* Paint the same simple landscape twice, once in warm analogous colors and once in cool colors.

*How to teach:* Paint the same simple landscape in warm analogous colors and in cool colors. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What are analogous colors?
  **A:** Colors next to each other on the color wheel (e.g., red, red-orange, orange).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Complementary pop
*Student:* Create a small abstract painting using a complementary color pair to make a focal point.

*How to teach:* Make a small abstract using a complementary pair to create a focal point. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** Why do complementary colors make a strong focal point?
  **A:** Opposites create the highest contrast, so the eye goes there.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & reflect
*Student:* Compare your two landscapes and write how color changed the mood.

*How to teach:* Compare the two landscapes; write how color changed mood. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** What mood might cool colors create?
  **A:** Calm, peaceful, sad, cold or quiet.

**Assessment:** Color wheel and mood landscapes.

**Answer key:** Color wheel: 12 colors accurately mixed and ordered. Mood landscapes: same composition in warm analogous and cool palettes; written comparison names specific colors and resulting moods. Proficient = both complete with accurate explanation.

## Physical Education 6: Fitness testing: flexibility and goal setting

**Prepare:** Gather: box and ruler for sit-and-reach, fitness log. Build a sit-and-reach box (or use a box and yardstick). Have the fitness log. Check the space for hazards, have water ready, and start every session with the dynamic warm-up.

**Watch for:**
- Bouncing in the sit-and-reach. Reach slowly and hold.
- Arching the back hard and fast in the trunk lift. Lift slowly; the test stops at 12 inches.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sit-and-reach
*Student:* Complete the back-saver sit-and-reach test on both legs.

*How to teach:* Back-saver sit-and-reach: one leg straight, other knee bent; reach four times, hold the fourth; both sides.

- **Q:** Why test one leg at a time?
  **A:** It protects the lower back and checks each hamstring separately.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Trunk lift
*Student:* Complete the trunk-lift test and learn safe back-strengthening exercises.

*How to teach:* Trunk lift: lie face down, lift chest slowly, hold, measure chin height (max 12 inches). Teach supermans and bird-dogs.

- **Q:** What does the trunk lift measure?
  **A:** Strength and flexibility of the back (trunk extensor) muscles.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Components of fitness
*Student:* Learn the five components of health-related fitness and match each test to a component; play a circuit game.

*How to teach:* Teach the five components; match tests; play a circuit.

- **Q:** Name the five components of health-related fitness.
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility and body composition.
- **Q:** Which component does the PACER test?
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory (aerobic) endurance.

### Day 4 (Thursday): SMART goals
*Student:* Write two SMART goals based on your baseline results.

*How to teach:* The child writes two SMART goals.

- **Q:** Make this SMART: 'I want to run better.'
  **A:** Example: 'I will improve my PACER score from 20 to 28 laps by January by jogging 20 minutes three times a week.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Share goals with a parent and do a 20-minute circuit targeting your goal areas.

*How to teach:* Share goals; do a 20-minute goal circuit.

- **Q:** What does the 'T' in SMART stand for?
  **A:** Time-bound — there is a deadline.

**Assessment:** Written SMART goals.

**Answer key:** SMART goal rubric — 4: two goals that are Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant (tied to a test below or near HFZ) and Time-bound, with a plan (e.g., 'I will increase my push-ups from 8 to 12 by the mid-year test by doing push-ups 3 days a week'); 3: one element missing; 2: vague; 1: none.

## Health 6: Understanding emotions

**Prepare:** Print a feelings wheel and a body outline; set up two journal pages for trigger tracking. PARENT PREVIEW: read the materials first; if your child shares feelings that worry you (hopelessness, self-harm, not wanting to live), stay calm, listen, and contact your pediatrician or the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking some emotions are 'bad.' All emotions are normal signals; what matters is how we respond.
- Thinking emotions come out of nowhere. Most have triggers and body cues you can learn to notice early.

### Day 1 (Monday): Feelings wheel
*Student:* Use a feelings wheel to identify 15 emotions beyond happy/sad/mad and give a situation for each.

*How to teach:* Your child finds 15 emotions beyond happy/sad/mad (e.g., frustrated, jealous, proud, anxious, lonely) and writes a situation for each.

- **Q:** Why is it helpful to name emotions precisely?
  **A:** Naming a feeling clearly ('frustrated' vs. 'mad') helps you understand it and choose the right way to handle it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Body cues
*Student:* Map where you feel anger, anxiety and excitement in your body on an outline.

*How to teach:* Your child maps where anger, anxiety and excitement show in the body (hot face, tight fists, racing heart, butterflies).

- **Q:** What are body cues?
  **A:** Physical signs of an emotion, like a racing heart, tense muscles or a hot face.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Triggers
*Student:* Track emotional triggers in a journal for two days.

*How to teach:* Track triggers in a journal for two days (what happened, emotion, body cue, response). Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** What is an emotional trigger?
  **A:** An event or situation that sets off a strong emotion.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Healthy expression
*Student:* Sort responses to anger into healthy and unhealthy and role-play two healthy responses.

*How to teach:* Sort responses to anger (yelling, hitting, deep breaths, walking away, talking it out) into healthy and unhealthy; role-play two healthy ones.

- **Q:** Give two healthy ways to express anger.
  **A:** Take deep breaths, walk away to cool down, use an I-message, exercise, write about it.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a journal entry analyzing one emotion, its trigger and your response; complete a check.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a journal entry analyzing one emotion, its trigger and response. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** What is emotional regulation?
  **A:** Managing your emotions so you respond in healthy, helpful ways.

**Assessment:** Journal analysis and check.

**Answer key:** Check key: emotions are normal; triggers set them off; body cues warn us; healthy expression examples; empathy = understanding others' feelings. Journal analysis: 4 = names specific emotion, trigger, body cue, response and a better alternative if needed; 3 = three elements; 2 = two; 1 = one.
