# Teacher's Guide — 6th Grade, Week 4

## English Language Arts 6: Personal narrative: revising and publishing

**Prepare:** Gather: narrative draft, computer for typing. Make sure a computer or tablet is available for typing on Day 4.

**Watch for:**
- Revising means only fixing spelling. Revision changes ideas, words and sentences; editing fixes conventions.
- Starting many sentences with 'I' or 'Then'. Try beginning with a time phrase, an action verb, or a describing word.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sentence variety
*Student:* Lesson on varying sentence beginnings and lengths; revise ten sentences in your draft so no two consecutive sentences start the same way.

*How to teach:* Model rewriting three sentences that all start with 'I'. The child revises ten so no two in a row start the same way.

- **Q:** Revise: 'I ran. I saw the dog. I yelled.'
  **A:** Example: 'Racing down the path, I spotted the dog and yelled.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Word choice
*Student:* Replace weak verbs and vague nouns with precise ones; highlight five improvements in a different color.

*How to teach:* Hunt for weak verbs (went, got, said) and vague nouns (stuff, thing); the child highlights five replacements.

- **Q:** Give a stronger verb for 'went' in 'I went down the hill.'
  **A:** Sprinted, tumbled, crept, slid — depending on meaning.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Editing conference
*Student:* Edit with a checklist for pronoun case, dialogue punctuation, capitalization and spelling, then conference with a parent.

*How to teach:* Use the checklist; the child edits, then you conference for 10 minutes on two things only.

- **Q:** Which is correct: 'Dad and me laughed' or 'Dad and I laughed'?
  **A:** 'Dad and I laughed' (subject).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Publish
*Student:* Type the final narrative (at least 2 pages) and add a title.

*How to teach:* The child types the final copy (2+ pages) with a title.

- **Q:** What makes a good title for a narrative?
  **A:** A short phrase that hints at the moment or its meaning without giving away the ending.

### Day 5 (Friday): Author's chair and check
*Student:* Read your narrative aloud with expression to an audience and score it with the narrative rubric; take a unit vocabulary quiz.

*How to teach:* Hold author's chair; the child reads with expression and self-scores on the rubric; give the vocabulary quiz.

- **Q:** What is foreshadowing?
  **A:** Hints or clues about what will happen later in a story.

**Assessment:** Published personal narrative scored on a 4-point rubric.

**Answer key:** Published narrative — 4-point rubric — 4: focused small moment, engaging lead, well-paced sequence, correctly punctuated dialogue, vivid sensory detail and a reflective ending, few errors; 3: all elements present but uneven detail or several errors; 2: summary-like, little dialogue or detail, errors interfere; 1: incomplete or off-task. Vocabulary quiz: definitions and use-in-sentence for unit words (lead, dialogue, irony, foreshadowing, point of view, climax, sensory detail).

## Mathematics 6: Nets, surface area, squares and cubes

**Prepare:** Gather: cereal box, cardstock, tape, sugar cubes or snap cubes. Preview: a net must fold into the solid with no overlaps or gaps.

**Watch for:**
- Counting only three faces of a rectangular prism. Remind that opposite faces come in pairs: SA = 2(lw + lh + wh).
- Thinking 3³ means 3 × 3 = 9 or 3 × 3. Correct: the exponent tells how many times the base is used as a factor: 3 × 3 × 3 = 27.
- Confusing surface area (square units) with volume (cubic units).

### Day 1 (Monday): Polyhedra and faces
*Student:* Sort household objects into prisms and pyramids, count faces, edges and vertices, and record them in a table.

*How to teach:* Gather 6–8 objects (box, tissue box, toy pyramid). The child sorts them into prisms (two identical parallel bases) and pyramids (one base, triangle faces meeting at a point) and fills a faces/edges/vertices table.

- **Q:** How many faces, edges and vertices does a cube have? A square pyramid?
  **A:** Cube: 6 faces, 12 edges, 8 vertices. Square pyramid: 5 faces, 8 edges, 5 vertices.
- **Q:** Triangular prism?
  **A:** 5 faces, 9 edges, 6 vertices.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Unfold a box
*Student:* Cut open a cereal box along its edges to make a net, measure each face and compute its surface area; compare with a partner's or parent's estimate.

*How to teach:* Cut the cereal box along edges so it lies flat. The child measures each rectangle, writes its area on it, and adds all six.

- **Q:** A box is 12 in × 8 in × 2 in. What is its surface area?
  **A:** 272 sq in: 2(12×8) + 2(12×2) + 2(8×2) = 192 + 48 + 32.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Nets to surface area
*Student:* Draw nets for a cube, a rectangular prism and a square pyramid on grid paper, fold one from cardstock, and calculate surface area of each.

*How to teach:* Model drawing a cube net on grid paper, then the child draws nets for a rectangular prism and a square pyramid, folds one from cardstock, and computes surface area from the net.

- **Q:** A cube has edges of 5 cm. Surface area?
  **A:** 150 sq cm (6 × 25).
- **Q:** A square pyramid has a 4 × 4 base and triangle faces with height 5. Surface area?
  **A:** 56 square units: 16 + 4 × (½ × 4 × 5) = 16 + 40.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Squares and cubes
*Student:* Build cubes with snap cubes to model 2^3, 3^3, 4^3; write area of a square as s^2, volume of a cube as s^3, and surface area as 6s^2; evaluate for 8 side lengths.

*How to teach:* Build 2×2×2, 3×3×3 and 4×4×4 cubes and count the small cubes. Write the results as 2³ = 8, 3³ = 27, 4³ = 64, and connect s² to the area of one face and 6s² to the whole surface.

- **Q:** Evaluate 4³ and find the surface area of a cube with edge 4.
  **A:** 4³ = 64; SA = 6 × 4² = 96 square units.
- **Q:** Volume of a cube with edge 10?
  **A:** 1,000 cubic units.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 1 assessment (area of polygons, nets, surface area, exponent notation) and redo two missed problems with explanation.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 test without help. Afterward, the child picks two missed problems and redoes them with a written explanation.

- **Q:** Which units go with surface area and which with volume?
  **A:** Surface area: square units (cm², in²). Volume: cubic units (cm³, in³).

**Assessment:** Unit 1 end-of-unit assessment.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test sample key: 1) parallelogram b = 12, h = 5 → 60 2) triangle b = 9, h = 4 → 18 3) trapezoid bases 4 and 10, h = 6 → 42 4) rectangular prism 5 × 3 × 2: SA = 2(15 + 10 + 6) = 62 sq units 5) cube with edge 7: SA = 6 × 49 = 294 sq units, V = 343 cubic units 6) 5² = 25; 2⁴ = 16 7) six squares in a cross shape fold into a cube (yes) 8) square pyramid, base 6, triangle heights 5: 36 + 4 × 15 = 96 sq units. Redo: child explains each fix in a sentence.

## Science 6 (Integrated): Changes of state

**Prepare:** Gather: ice cubes, pot, kitchen thermometer, stopwatch, graph paper. Adult must handle the stove. Use a kitchen thermometer that can read 100°C; don't let it touch the pot bottom.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking temperature keeps rising while ice melts. It stays at 0°C until all ice melts.
- Thinking bubbles in boiling water are air. They are water vapor.

### Day 1 (Monday): Heating curve lab
*Student:* With adult help, heat ice in a pot, recording temperature every 30 seconds until the water boils.

*How to teach:* Heat ice in a pot (adult at the stove), stirring gently; the child records temperature every 30 seconds.

- **Q:** What temperature do you expect while ice is still melting?
  **A:** About 0°C (32°F).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Graph the data
*Student:* Graph temperature vs. time and identify the flat sections where the state changes.

*How to teach:* Graph temperature vs. time; mark the flat sections.

- **Q:** What do the two flat sections represent?
  **A:** Melting at 0°C and boiling at about 100°C (212°F).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Why the plateau?
*Student:* Use particle models to explain why temperature stays constant while ice melts and water boils.

*How to teach:* Discuss where the energy goes during plateaus.

- **Q:** Why does temperature stay constant while water boils even though heat is added?
  **A:** The added energy separates particles into gas (overcoming attractions) instead of speeding them up.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Condensation and freezing
*Student:* Observe condensation on a cold glass and freeze water in a cup; model particles during cooling.

*How to teach:* Observe a cold glass sweating; freeze water.

- **Q:** Where do the droplets on a cold glass come from?
  **A:** Water vapor in the air cools on the glass and condenses into liquid.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draw and label a complete particle model of all state changes and take a quiz on heating curves.

*How to teach:* The child makes the poster and takes the quiz.

- **Q:** What is sublimation? Give an example.
  **A:** A solid changing directly to a gas, like dry ice.

**Assessment:** Particle model poster and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) flat section at 0°C = melting 2) flat section at 100°C (at sea level) = boiling 3) during plateaus, energy goes into breaking attractions between particles rather than speeding them up 4) condensation = gas to liquid 5) freezing releases energy to the surroundings. Poster rubric — 4: particle models of solid (packed, vibrating), liquid (close, sliding), gas (far apart, fast), all six changes labeled (melting, freezing, evaporation/boiling, condensation, sublimation, deposition) with energy added/removed arrows; 3: one change missing; 2: several errors; 1: incomplete.

## World History and Geography: Ancient Civilizations: The agricultural revolution

**Prepare:** Gather: seeds (beans or wheat berries), cup of soil, images of Catalhoyuk. Soak bean seeds overnight; find an image of Çatalhöyük's reconstruction.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking farming was easier. Farming meant harder work and new diseases, but more food and settled life.
- Thinking agriculture began in one place. It began independently in several regions.

### Day 1 (Monday): Domestication
*Student:* Read about the domestication of wheat, rice, maize and animals and map where each began.

*How to teach:* Map domestication centers.

- **Q:** Where were wheat, rice and maize first domesticated?
  **A:** Wheat — the Fertile Crescent (Southwest Asia); rice — China; maize — Mexico (from teosinte).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plant an experiment
*Student:* Plant bean seeds and begin a growth log, reflecting on why early farmers chose to settle near their crops.

*How to teach:* Plant beans; start a log; discuss staying near crops.

- **Q:** Why did farmers settle down?
  **A:** Crops needed tending for months, so people stayed near fields to plant, protect and harvest them.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Catalhoyuk
*Student:* Study an artist's reconstruction of Catalhoyuk and list evidence of permanent settlement and specialization.

*How to teach:* List evidence of permanent settlement at Çatalhöyük.

- **Q:** Give two pieces of evidence that Çatalhöyük was permanent.
  **A:** Mud-brick houses built side by side (entered through roofs), storage areas, burials under floors, wall paintings — built and rebuilt over centuries.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Costs and benefits
*Student:* Create a T-chart of the benefits and costs of farming vs. hunting and gathering.

*How to teach:* T-chart costs and benefits.

- **Q:** Name one cost of farming.
  **A:** Harder labor, diseases from crowding and animals, dependence on a few crops, more inequality.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 1 assessment and write a paragraph on how farming changed human life.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 test.

- **Q:** What is a food surplus and why does it matter?
  **A:** Extra food beyond what's needed; it lets some people do other jobs (specialization).

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test with constructed response.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test sample: 1) primary vs. secondary 2) BCE/CE math 3) Oldowan/Acheulean tools 4) uses of fire 5) Beringia 6) wheat — Fertile Crescent (~10,000 years ago); rice — China; maize — Mexico 7) Çatalhöyük — early town in Anatolia (Turkey) 8) a surplus allows specialization. Constructed response: Paragraph rubric — 4: clear topic sentence answering the question, at least two specific accurate pieces of evidence (named source, artifact or fact), explanation linking each to the claim, concluding sentence; 3: accurate but only one piece of evidence explained; 2: general statements without evidence; 1: inaccurate or off-topic. Expected content: farming allowed permanent villages, food surpluses, population growth, specialized jobs and property, but also more work, disease and inequality.

## Visual and Performing Arts 6: Texture, space and principles of design

**Prepare:** Gather crayons, paper, textured household objects (screens, leaves, baskets, coins), magazines, scissors and glue. Choose a chair for the negative-space drawing.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking texture must be real. Implied (visual) texture is drawn to look like a texture.
- Thinking negative space is empty and unimportant. It shapes the subject and balances the composition.

### Day 1 (Monday): Texture rubbings
*Student:* Make crayon rubbings of 10 textures around the house and then draw implied versions of three.

*How to teach:* Make 10 crayon rubbings; draw implied versions of three. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** What is the difference between actual and implied texture?
  **A:** Actual texture can be felt; implied texture only looks like it would feel a certain way.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Negative space
*Student:* Draw only the negative spaces around a chair and see the chair appear.

*How to teach:* Draw only the spaces around and between the chair's legs and rungs. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What is negative space?
  **A:** The empty space around and between objects.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Principles hunt
*Student:* Find magazine images that show balance, emphasis, rhythm, contrast and unity and label them.

*How to teach:* Find and label magazine images showing balance, emphasis, rhythm, contrast and unity. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What does emphasis mean in design?
  **A:** Making one part stand out as the focal point.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Design collage
*Student:* Create a collage that uses at least three principles of design deliberately.

*How to teach:* Create a collage using at least three principles deliberately. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** How can you create rhythm in a collage?
  **A:** Repeat shapes, colors or lines in a regular or flowing pattern.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & critique
*Student:* Write a criteria checklist for a good composition and use it to evaluate your collage.

*How to teach:* Write a criteria checklist for a good composition; evaluate the collage. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** Give one criterion for a strong composition.
  **A:** A clear focal point, balance, unity, varied but related elements.

**Assessment:** Collage and self-made criteria evaluation.

**Answer key:** Collage rubric: 4 = at least three principles clearly used and labeled, strong craftsmanship, self-made criteria applied with evidence; 3 = two principles; 2 = one; 1 = incomplete.

## Physical Education 6: Communication challenges

**Prepare:** Gather: blindfold, cones, hula hoop, rope. Set up a cone maze; use a soft blindfold; stay with the blindfolded child at all times. Check the space for hazards, have water ready, and start every session with the dynamic warm-up.

**Watch for:**
- Giving vague directions ('go there'). Use specific words: 'three small steps forward, turn left.'
- Blaming teammates. Focus on solutions.

### Day 1 (Monday): Blindfold maze
*Student:* Guide a blindfolded partner through a cone maze using only verbal directions, then switch.

*How to teach:* Model precise directions; switch roles.

- **Q:** What makes verbal directions effective?
  **A:** They are specific (distance, direction, number of steps), calm and checked for understanding.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Hula hoop pass
*Student:* Pass a hula hoop around a hand-holding circle without letting go; time and improve.

*How to teach:* Pass the hoop around a hand-holding circle; time and improve.

- **Q:** What strategy speeds the hoop pass?
  **A:** Plan movements together, step through rather than climb, and call out encouragement.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Human knot
*Student:* Untangle a human knot with family or co-op members and discuss communication strategies.

*How to teach:* Untangle a human knot.

- **Q:** What communication strategy helps the knot?
  **A:** One person speaks at a time; describe moves before doing them; move slowly.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Encouragement game
*Student:* Play a relay where teams earn points for encouraging words as well as speed.

*How to teach:* Run the encouragement relay.

- **Q:** Give an example of encouraging words.
  **A:** 'Nice effort!' 'You've got this!' 'Great try — keep going!'

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Write about one way you helped your team and one way to improve.

*How to teach:* The child writes the reflection.

- **Q:** Why does teamwork matter in physical activity?
  **A:** It helps groups solve problems, stay safe and enjoy activity together.

**Assessment:** Teamwork self-assessment.

**Answer key:** Teamwork self-assessment (1–4 on each): listened to others; gave clear directions; encouraged teammates; accepted feedback; plus written examples of one helpful action and one improvement.

## Health 6: Stress and its effects

**Prepare:** Print a fight-or-flight diagram, a stress inventory (list of common middle-school stressors with ratings 0–3), and 10 situation cards. Have a timer for pulse checks.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all stress is bad. Eustress (positive stress) can motivate, like before a game or performance.
- Thinking stress is only in your head. Stress causes real body changes (hormones, heart rate, muscle tension).

### Day 1 (Monday): Fight or flight
*Student:* Learn the body's stress response and label hormone and body effects on a diagram.

*How to teach:* Explain fight-or-flight: adrenaline and cortisol are released; heart rate and breathing speed up, muscles tense, pupils widen, digestion slows. Your child labels the diagram.

- **Q:** Which hormones are released in the stress response?
  **A:** Adrenaline (epinephrine) and cortisol.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Good vs. bad stress
*Student:* Classify 10 situations as eustress or distress.

*How to teach:* Your child classifies 10 situations (big game, test, moving, a fight with a friend, starting a new club) as eustress or distress.

- **Q:** What's the difference between eustress and distress?
  **A:** Eustress is positive stress that motivates (a performance); distress is negative stress that harms or overwhelms (ongoing conflict).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Stress inventory
*Student:* Complete a middle-school stress inventory and identify your top three stressors.

*How to teach:* Complete the stress inventory; identify top three stressors. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** What is a stressor?
  **A:** Anything that causes stress.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Stress and health
*Student:* Read about how long-term stress affects sleep, immunity and concentration.

*How to teach:* Read about long-term stress effects on sleep, immunity and concentration. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** How can long-term stress affect health?
  **A:** It can disrupt sleep, weaken the immune system, cause headaches/stomachaches and make it hard to concentrate.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a stress-concepts quiz.

*How to teach:* Take the stress-concepts quiz. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** Why did the fight-or-flight response develop?
  **A:** To help people react quickly to danger by fighting or running away.

**Assessment:** Stress quiz.

**Answer key:** Stress quiz key: 1) stress = body's response to demands 2) stressor 3) eustress vs distress 4) fight-or-flight effects 5) adrenaline/cortisol 6) long-term effects: sleep, immunity, concentration. Proficient = 80%.
