# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 15

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Frankenstein: the creature's story

**Prepare:** Read chapters 6–17. Prepare seminar questions and norms (cite the text, build on others). Seminar can be parent + student or a co-op group.

**Watch for:**
- Concluding the creature is purely a victim or purely a villain. Push for both: he is shaped by rejection, yet he still chooses to murder William and frame Justine.
- Treating the seminar as a debate to 'win.' Remind the student the goal is shared understanding — they should ask questions and build on others' points.
- Forgetting the creature's own crimes when sympathizing with him. Have the student list his choices (William, Justine) next to the injustices done to him.

### Day 1 (Monday): Justine's trial
*Student:* Read chapters 6–8. Discuss Victor's silence during Justine's trial and what it shows about his sense of responsibility.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 6–8. Discuss Victor's silence at the trial; ask the student whether his reasons justify it and to cite one line.

- **Q:** Why is Justine accused?
  **A:** The picture (miniature) William wore is found in her pocket — the creature planted it.
- **Q:** Why doesn't Victor speak up?
  **A:** He believes no one would believe his story and would think him mad, so he protects himself while Justine is executed.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mont Blanc
*Student:* Read chapters 9–10. Connect the sublime Alpine setting to Romantic poetry from Unit 4.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 9–10. Connect the Alps to Romantic sublime. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Where does Victor meet the creature?
  **A:** On the glacier near Montanvert/Mont Blanc (the Mer de Glace).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): The creature speaks
*Student:* Read chapters 11–13. List what the creature learns from the De Lacey family and from Paradise Lost, Plutarch, and Werther.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 11–13. The student lists what the creature learns. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Which books shape the creature?
  **A:** Paradise Lost, Plutarch's Lives, and The Sorrows of Werther.
- **Q:** Who are the De Laceys?
  **A:** An exiled family — blind old father, Felix, Agatha — joined by Safie; the creature learns language by watching them.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rejection
*Student:* Read chapters 14–17. Hold a seminar discussion: is the creature born good and made monstrous, or responsible for his own choices?

*How to teach:* Read chapters 14–17 and hold the seminar. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What happens when the creature reveals himself to the De Laceys?
  **A:** The blind father is kind, but Felix attacks him; the family flees and the creature burns the cottage.
- **Q:** What does the creature demand?
  **A:** A female companion.

### Day 5 (Friday): Seminar reflection
*Student:* Write a 300-word post-seminar reflection citing one idea you heard and how it changed or confirmed your view.

*How to teach:* The student writes a 300-word reflection. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What makes a strong seminar reflection?
  **A:** It cites a specific idea from someone else and explains how it changed or confirmed the student's view.

**Assessment:** Seminar participation rubric and written reflection.

**Answer key:** Seminar participation rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): 4 = cites text 3+ times, asks questions, builds on others. Reflection: 4 = cites a specific idea, explains its effect on thinking, references text.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Law of Sines, Law of Cosines, and triangle area

**Prepare:** Tape measure, protractor (or a phone inclinometer/compass app), notebook. Preview OpenStax 10.1–10.2. Scout a safe triangular region (three trees, corners of a yard).

**Watch for:**
- Using the Law of Sines on SAS or SSS triangles, where it can't start. Use the Law of Cosines first for those cases.
- Missing the second triangle in the SSA ambiguous case. If sin B < 1, check whether 180° − B also works with the given angle.
- Calculator in radian mode for degree problems.

### Day 1 (Monday): Area formula
*Student:* Derive A = ½ab sin C from base-height area and find areas of 6 triangles.

*How to teach:* Drop an altitude h = b sin C to get A = ½ab sin C. The student finds 6 areas. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Find the area of a triangle with a = 8, b = 11, C = 40°.
  **A:** ½(8)(11) sin 40° ≈ 28.3 square units.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Law of Sines
*Student:* Prove the Law of Sines and solve AAS, ASA, and ambiguous SSA triangles.

*How to teach:* Prove a/sin A = b/sin B using two altitude expressions. Solve AAS and the ambiguous case. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** A = 40°, B = 60°, a = 10. Find b and c.
  **A:** C = 80°; b = 10 sin 60°/sin 40° ≈ 13.47; c = 10 sin 80°/sin 40° ≈ 15.32.
- **Q:** a = 8, b = 10, A = 40°. How many triangles?
  **A:** sin B = 10 sin 40°/8 ≈ 0.8035 → B ≈ 53.5° or 126.5°. Both work (126.5° + 40° < 180°), so two triangles, with C ≈ 86.5° or 13.5°.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Law of Cosines
*Student:* Prove the Law of Cosines and solve SAS and SSS triangles; use Heron's formula.

*How to teach:* Prove c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C by placing the triangle on coordinates. Solve SAS and SSS and use Heron's formula. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** a = 5, b = 7, C = 60°. Find c.
  **A:** c² = 25 + 49 − 70(0.5) = 39 → c ≈ 6.24.
- **Q:** A triangle has sides 7, 8, 9. Find the largest angle and the area.
  **A:** cos C = (49 + 64 − 81)/112 ≈ 0.2857 → C ≈ 73.4°. Heron: s = 12, A = √(12·5·4·3) = √720 ≈ 26.8.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Field measurement
*Student:* Measure two sides and an angle of a triangular region in your yard or a park and compute the unknown side and area; verify with a tape measure.

*How to teach:* Outside, measure two sides and the included angle of a triangle, compute the third side (Law of Cosines) and area, then measure the third side to check. Compute percent error.

- **Q:** Why measure the included angle rather than any angle?
  **A:** SAS determines a unique triangle and lets you use the Law of Cosines directly; SSA may be ambiguous.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Take a quiz on solving oblique triangles including a navigation word problem.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz with a navigation problem; collect the field report. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** A ship sails 30 mi due east, then turns 40° to the north (bearing N50°E) and sails 20 mi. How far is it from the start?
  **A:** The angle between the legs is 180° − 40° = 140°. d² = 900 + 400 − 1200 cos 140° ≈ 1300 + 919.3 = 2219.3 → d ≈ 47.1 mi.

**Assessment:** Oblique triangle quiz and field report.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Area with a = 8, b = 11, C = 40° ≈ 28.3. 2) A = 40°, B = 60°, a = 10: b ≈ 13.47, c ≈ 15.32. 3) SSA a = 8, b = 10, A = 40°: two triangles (B ≈ 53.5°, C ≈ 86.5°; or B ≈ 126.5°, C ≈ 13.5°). 4) SAS 5, 7, 60°: c ≈ 6.24. 5) SSS 7, 8, 9: largest angle ≈ 73.4°, area ≈ 26.8. 6) Navigation: ≈ 47.1 mi. Field report rubric: 4 = diagram, measurements with units, correct Law of Cosines and area, percent error explained; 3 = correct with minor omissions; 2 = method error; 1 = incomplete.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Natural hazards and human activity

**Prepare:** Sand, water, pan, small weights (coins, small toy houses), the Cal OES MyHazards website; CAL FIRE statistics page; readings on the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and the Great Flood of 1862. Family emergency plan template (ready.gov).

**Watch for:**
- Confusing hazard and risk. Hazard is the natural event; risk combines likelihood and vulnerability/exposure.
- Thinking earthquakes can be predicted to the day. Scientists give probabilities, not predictions.
- Believing wildfire is only a climate problem. Fuel buildup, ignition sources, and building in the wildland-urban interface all matter.

### Day 1 (Monday): Earthquake risk
*Student:* Look up your address on Cal OES MyHazards; record earthquake, flood, fire, and tsunami risk.

*How to teach:* Look up your address on MyHazards; record earthquake, flood, fire, and tsunami levels.

- **Q:** Define hazard vs. risk.
  **A:** A hazard is a potentially damaging natural event; risk is the likelihood of harm, depending on the hazard's probability and the exposure and vulnerability of people and property.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Liquefaction lab
*Student:* Fill a pan with saturated sand, place small weights on top, and tap the pan to model liquefaction; relate to the 1989 Loma Prieta Marina District damage.

*How to teach:* Saturate sand in a pan, place weights on top, tap the pan repeatedly; observe sinking and water rising. Relate to the Marina District in 1989 Loma Prieta (M6.9), built on artificial fill.

- **Q:** What causes liquefaction?
  **A:** Shaking increases water pressure between grains in loose, saturated sediment, so grains lose contact and the soil behaves like a liquid.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Wildfire
*Student:* Analyze CAL FIRE data on acres burned over decades and explain links to climate, fuels, and development in the wildland-urban interface.

*How to teach:* Graph CAL FIRE acres burned by decade; explain links to warming/drought, fuels, longer fire seasons, and WUI development.

- **Q:** What is the wildland-urban interface?
  **A:** Areas where homes and development meet or mix with undeveloped wildland vegetation, increasing wildfire risk to people and ignitions from people.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Historical case
*Student:* Research how a hazard (1906 San Francisco earthquake, 1862 Great Flood) changed where and how Californians built.

*How to teach:* Research how the 1906 earthquake (M7.9) or the 1862 flood changed building and settlement.

- **Q:** How did the Great Flood of 1862 change Sacramento?
  **A:** The city was flooded for months; afterward it raised its downtown streets (roughly 10–15 ft) and built stronger levees.

### Day 5 (Friday): Household plan
*Student:* Write a family emergency plan and supply checklist for your top two hazards.

*How to teach:* Student writes a family emergency plan and supply checklist for the top two hazards.

- **Q:** How much water should an emergency kit contain?
  **A:** At least 1 gallon per person per day for several days (ready.gov recommends a minimum of 3 days; 2 weeks for home is better).

**Assessment:** Family hazard plan and case-study summary.

**Answer key:** Family hazard plan rubric. 4 – Uses MyHazards data to choose two hazards, includes meeting places, out-of-area contact, evacuation routes, specific actions (drop-cover-hold on; evacuate early for fire), supply checklist with quantities, and case-study summary linking past events to current codes/land use. 3 (proficient) – Plan with contacts, routes, actions, supplies, and accurate case study. 2 – Generic plan. 1 – Incomplete.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Media and democracy

**Prepare:** Find a summary of NYT v. U.S. (1971). Pick a political story covered by three outlets. Gather 5 viral claims to fact-check.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking prior restraint is always banned. It faces a heavy presumption against it.
- Assuming any bias means a story is false. Framing shapes emphasis; check facts separately from tone.
- Reading vertically (staying on one site) to check credibility. Lateral reading means leaving the site to see what others say about it.

### Day 1 (Monday): Free press
*Student:* Analyze the Pentagon Papers case (New York Times v. U.S.) and explain prior restraint.

*How to teach:* Analyze the Pentagon Papers case. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What did NYT v. U.S. hold?
  **A:** 6–3, the government failed to justify prior restraint; the papers could publish.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Comparing coverage
*Student:* Compare coverage of the same political story in three outlets and identify framing and word-choice differences.

*How to teach:* Compare three outlets' coverage. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is framing?
  **A:** How a story is presented — emphasis, word choice, sources.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Lateral reading
*Student:* Practice lateral reading to fact-check five viral social-media claims.

*How to teach:* Practice lateral reading on five claims. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is lateral reading?
  **A:** Opening new tabs to check what other sources say about a site or claim.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Officials and media
*Student:* Analyze how a public official uses social media to shape opinion; compare to FDR's fireside chats.

*How to teach:* Analyze an official's social media and FDR's fireside chats. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What were fireside chats?
  **A:** FDR's radio addresses (1933–1944) speaking directly to Americans.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a media-literacy guide for voters and take a short quiz.

*How to teach:* The student writes a media-literacy guide; quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Name three steps to verify a claim.
  **A:** Check the source, read laterally, find the original.

**Assessment:** Media-literacy guide.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) NYT v. U.S. (1971), 6–3: government failed to justify prior restraint. 2) Prior restraint = government blocking publication in advance. 3) Framing = choices of emphasis, words, and sources. 4) Lateral reading = leaving the site to check what others say about it. 5) FDR's fireside chats were radio addresses. Media-literacy guide rubric (4 levels): 4 = 5+ practical steps with examples (check the source, read laterally, find the original, check date, look for evidence), explains framing and bias vs. accuracy, clear voter audience.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Yoga and stress management

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Diversification, fees & investor behavior

**Prepare:** Get the NGPF stock market simulation, find historical S&P 500 returns (e.g. 2008: about −37% total return), and open FINRA BrokerCheck and Investor.gov's fraud warning signs. Prepare three sample 'opportunities' (one legitimate index fund, one guaranteed-20%-return pitch, one unregistered crypto scheme).

**Watch for:**
- Putting all money in one stock. Diversification spreads risk; one company can lose most of its value.
- Thinking a 1% fee is small. Over decades fees compound and can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars.
- Selling in a panic during a crash. Loss aversion and herd behavior lead to selling low; historically, diversified markets have recovered over time.

### Day 1 (Monday): Diversification
*Student:* Simulate a single-stock vs. index portfolio through a market crash using historical returns and compare losses.

*How to teach:* Simulate a single-stock vs. index portfolio through a crash using historical returns; compare losses.

- **Q:** Why is an index fund less risky than one stock?
  **A:** It spreads money across hundreds of companies, so one company's failure has a small effect.
- **Q:** About how much did the S&P 500 lose in 2008?
  **A:** About 37% (total return).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Fees matter
*Student:* Calculate the 40-year difference in a $100,000 balance invested at 0.05% vs. 1% annual fees.

*How to teach:* Calculate the 40-year difference on $100,000 at 0.05% vs. 1% fees (assume 7% gross return).

- **Q:** What is the difference?
  **A:** About $1,470,000 vs. $1,029,000 — roughly $441,000 lost to the higher fee.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Behavioral biases
*Student:* Read about loss aversion, herd behavior, and FOMO and connect each to a real event (2008 crash, meme stocks, crypto collapse).

*How to teach:* Read about loss aversion, herd behavior and FOMO; connect each to 2008, meme stocks (2021) and the 2022 crypto collapse.

- **Q:** What is loss aversion?
  **A:** Feeling losses more strongly than equal gains, which can lead to panic selling.
- **Q:** What is dollar-cost averaging?
  **A:** Investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule regardless of price, so you buy more shares when prices are low.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Investment fraud
*Student:* Use FINRA BrokerCheck and the SEC's Investor.gov warning signs to evaluate three sample 'opportunities'.

*How to teach:* Use BrokerCheck and Investor.gov warning signs to evaluate three sample opportunities.

- **Q:** Name three red flags of investment fraud.
  **A:** Guaranteed high returns with little risk, pressure to buy now, unregistered sellers or products, and returns paid from new investors' money (Ponzi scheme).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Create an asset allocation for ages 20, 40, and 60 with a paragraph explaining each.

*How to teach:* Create asset allocations for ages 20, 40 and 60 with explanations.

- **Q:** Why hold more bonds at 60 than at 20?
  **A:** Less time to recover from stock drops before needing the money, so lower volatility matters more.

**Assessment:** Asset allocation project and fraud evaluation.

**Answer key:** Fees calculation: $100,000 for 40 years at 7% gross return — 0.05% fee (6.95% net) → about $1,470,000; 1% fee (6.00% net) → about $1,029,000; difference ≈ $441,000. Asset allocation project rubric: 4 = allocations for ages 20, 40 and 60 that shift from mostly stocks (e.g. 90/10) to more bonds (e.g. 70/30, 50/50 or 60/40), each with a paragraph on time horizon, risk tolerance and diversification; 3 = reasonable allocations, thin reasoning; 2 = allocations not linked to age; 1 = incomplete. Fraud evaluation: guaranteed high returns, pressure to act fast, unregistered sellers (not found on BrokerCheck) and recruiting others are red flags.
