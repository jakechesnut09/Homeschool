# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 5

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Beowulf: fate, age, and legacy

**Prepare:** Finish reading Beowulf. Borrow John Gardner's Grendel (read chapter 1 yourself first; it contains crude language and violence — preview). Prepare the essay prompt.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Beowulf's last fight was simply heroic. Discuss whether he acts wisely as an aging king, leaving his people vulnerable.
- Commas around appositives: essential appositives take no commas; nonessential ones do.
- Organizing an essay by plot. Organize by idea (each paragraph proves a part of the thesis).

### Day 1 (Monday): The dragon
*Student:* Read the dragon episode and Beowulf's death. Annotate how the tone shifts from triumph to elegy.

*How to teach:* Read the dragon episode and death. The student annotates shifts from triumph to elegy. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What wakes the dragon?
  **A:** A thief steals a jeweled cup from its treasure hoard.
- **Q:** How does the tone change at the end?
  **A:** It becomes elegiac: mourning Beowulf, the funeral pyre and barrow, and forebodings that the Geats will be attacked now that their king is gone.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Wiglaf and loyalty
*Student:* Analyze Wiglaf's speech to the cowardly thanes; write a paragraph on how the poem defines loyalty and what happens when the comitatus fails.

*How to teach:* Read Wiglaf's speech. The student writes a paragraph on loyalty and the failed comitatus. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does Wiglaf tell the thanes who fled?
  **A:** He shames them: they took their lord's gifts but deserted him; their families will lose their rights and the Geats will be dishonored and endangered.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Another perspective
*Student:* Read the opening chapter of John Gardner's Grendel and compare how the monster is characterized. Complete a two-column comparison chart.

*How to teach:* Read Gardner's chapter 1; the student fills a two-column chart comparing Grendel's portrayal. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How does Gardner's Grendel differ from the poem's?
  **A:** Gardner's Grendel narrates in first person — lonely, sardonic, intelligent — whereas the poem presents him from outside as a monstrous descendant of Cain.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: appositives
*Student:* Mini-lesson on appositive phrases (Beowulf's epithets are appositives). Write five sentences about the poem using appositives correctly punctuated.

*How to teach:* Teach appositives using Beowulf's epithets. The student writes five punctuated sentences. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Punctuate: Beowulf the son of Ecgtheow sailed to Denmark.
  **A:** Beowulf, the son of Ecgtheow, sailed to Denmark.

### Day 5 (Friday): Explanatory essay
*Student:* Write a 600-word explanatory essay: how does Beowulf define a good leader, and what does the ending suggest about the limits of heroism?

*How to teach:* The student writes a 600-word explanatory essay on good leadership and the limits of heroism. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does the ending suggest about heroism's limits?
  **A:** Even the greatest hero ages and dies; his death leaves his people vulnerable, suggesting individual heroism cannot secure a society forever.

**Assessment:** Explanatory essay scored on thesis, evidence, and organization.

**Answer key:** Explanatory essay rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): Thesis — 4 = defines good leadership (courage, generosity, protecting one's people) and states a clear claim about heroism's limits. Evidence — 4 = at least 3 well-chosen quotations (e.g., Hrothgar's generosity, the dragon fight, Wiglaf's speech). Organization — 4 = idea-based paragraphs with transitions. Proficient: 3 in each.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Polynomial functions

**Prepare:** Gather an 8.5 × 11 in sheet of paper, scissors, tape, and a ruler for the box model. Preview OpenStax 5.3 (Graphs of Polynomial Functions).

**Watch for:**
- Determining end behavior from the first term written rather than the highest-degree term. Rewrite in standard form first: in 3x² − 2x⁵, the leading term is −2x⁵.
- Thinking every zero crosses the axis. Even multiplicity bounces (touches), odd multiplicity crosses; multiplicity 3 crosses with a flattened shape.
- Saying a degree-n polynomial has exactly n − 1 turning points. It has AT MOST n − 1.

### Day 1 (Monday): End behavior
*Student:* Determine end behavior from degree and leading coefficient for 12 polynomials; verify in Desmos.

*How to teach:* Graph y = x², −x², x³, −x³ and generalize: even degree means the ends match, odd means they go opposite ways; the sign of the leading coefficient decides the right end. The student classifies 12 polynomials, then checks in Desmos.

- **Q:** Describe the end behavior of f(x) = −2x⁵ + 3x² + 1.
  **A:** Odd degree, negative leading coefficient: as x → −∞, f(x) → ∞; as x → ∞, f(x) → −∞.
- **Q:** Describe the end behavior of g(x) = 4x⁴ − x.
  **A:** Even degree, positive leading coefficient: both ends go up (→ ∞).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Zeros & multiplicity
*Student:* Investigate how multiplicity 1, 2, and 3 zeros look on a graph (cross, bounce, flatten).

*How to teach:* Graph y = (x − 1), (x − 1)², (x − 1)³ and have the student describe what happens at x = 1. Then they investigate several factored polynomials and write the rule in their notes.

- **Q:** Describe the behavior of f(x) = (x + 2)²(x − 1)³ at each zero.
  **A:** At x = −2 (multiplicity 2) the graph touches and bounces; at x = 1 (multiplicity 3) it crosses and flattens like a cubic.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sketching
*Student:* Sketch 8 polynomials from factored form, labeling intercepts, end behavior, and turning-point counts.

*How to teach:* Model a full sketch for f(x) = x(x − 3)(x + 2): zeros, y-intercept, end behavior, then connect. The student sketches 8 polynomials and labels intercepts, end behavior, and the maximum number of turning points.

- **Q:** Sketch-plan f(x) = −(x − 1)²(x + 3): zeros, y-intercept, end behavior, max turning points.
  **A:** Zeros 1 (bounce) and −3 (cross); y-intercept −(1)(3) = −3; degree 3 with negative leading coefficient: up on the left, down on the right; at most 2 turning points.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Modeling with polynomials
*Student:* Model the volume of an open box made by cutting squares from a sheet of paper; build one and find the maximum volume in Desmos.

*How to teach:* Cut equal squares of side x from each corner of the paper, fold, and tape a box. Derive V(x) = x(8.5 − 2x)(11 − 2x) and find the maximum in Desmos. Discuss why the domain is 0 < x < 4.25.

- **Q:** What size square gives the maximum volume and what is it?
  **A:** x ≈ 1.59 in gives V ≈ 66.1 in³.
- **Q:** Why is the domain 0 < x < 4.25?
  **A:** x must be positive, and 2x must be less than the shorter side 8.5, so x < 4.25.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Complete a polynomial-graphing quiz including writing an equation from a graph.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz below; allow Desmos only to check the last item after writing it by hand. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Write a cubic with a bounce at x = −2, a crossing at x = 1, and y-intercept −8.
  **A:** f(x) = a(x + 2)²(x − 1). f(0) = a(4)(−1) = −8, so a = 2: f(x) = 2(x + 2)²(x − 1).

**Assessment:** Polynomial graphing quiz and box-volume model.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) −2x⁵ + …: up-left, down-right. 2) 4x⁴ − x: both up. 3) (x + 2)²(x − 1)³: bounce at −2, cross/flatten at 1. 4) x(x − 3)(x + 2): zeros 0, 3, −2; y-int 0; down-left, up-right. 5) Equation from graph: f(x) = 2(x + 2)²(x − 1). Box model rubric: correct V(x) = x(8.5 − 2x)(11 − 2x), domain 0 < x < 4.25, max ≈ 66.1 in³ at x ≈ 1.59 in, physical box built and measured. Proficient = all four parts with only minor rounding errors.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Early Earth and the solar system's formation

**Prepare:** Flour, cocoa powder, baking pan, marbles of different sizes, meter stick, ruler. 100 pennies or M&Ms and a cup. NASA images of chondrite meteorites. Background: the solar system is ~4.57 billion years old (from meteorite inclusions); oldest Earth minerals are ~4.4-billion-year-old zircons from Jack Hills, Australia.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Earth's oldest rocks date the planet. Plate tectonics and erosion recycled Earth's earliest crust, so meteorites date the solar system.
- Believing half-life means half the time is left. It's the time for half of the remaining parent atoms to decay.
- Assuming the Moon has more impacts than Earth. Earth was hit as much or more, but erosion, plate tectonics, and the atmosphere erased most craters.

### Day 1 (Monday): Solar nebula
*Student:* Read about the solar nebula hypothesis and draw a storyboard of accretion from dust to planets.

*How to teach:* Read about the solar nebula: a collapsing cloud spun into a disk; dust stuck together (accretion) into planetesimals and planets; rocky planets formed inside the frost line, gas giants beyond. Draw a 6-panel storyboard.

- **Q:** Why are inner planets rocky and outer planets gas giants?
  **A:** Near the Sun it was too hot for ices to condense, so only rock/metal accreted; beyond the frost line ices made larger cores that captured hydrogen and helium gas.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Crater lab
*Student:* Drop marbles from different heights into a pan of flour topped with cocoa and measure crater diameter vs. drop height; relate to the Moon's ancient surface.

*How to teach:* Drop marbles from 25, 50, 100, 150 cm into the flour/cocoa pan; measure crater diameters (3 trials each), graph diameter vs. height.

- **Q:** What happens to crater size as drop height increases, and why?
  **A:** Crater diameter increases (but less than proportionally) because the marble has more kinetic energy at impact (KE = mgh).
- **Q:** Why does the Moon preserve ancient craters?
  **A:** It has no atmosphere, liquid water, or plate tectonics to erode or recycle its surface.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Meteorites
*Student:* Examine NASA images of chondrites and explain why meteorites date the solar system to 4.56 billion years.

*How to teach:* Examine chondrite images; explain that they are unaltered material from the solar nebula, and radiometric dating of their calcium-aluminum-rich inclusions gives ~4.567 billion years.

- **Q:** Why do meteorites give the age of the solar system?
  **A:** Many are primitive leftovers from the solar nebula, never melted or recycled, so their radiometric ages record when solid material first formed.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Radiometric dating
*Student:* Model half-life with 100 coins or M&Ms (remove 'decayed' ones each round) and graph the decay curve; solve dating problems.

*How to teach:* Half-life model: shake 100 coins, remove 'decayed' (heads), repeat; graph remaining vs. round. Solve problems.

- **Q:** A rock has 25% of its original U-235 (half-life 704 million years). How old is it?
  **A:** Two half-lives: 2 × 704 = 1.41 billion years.
- **Q:** A sample has 12.5% of its original parent remaining. How many half-lives have passed?
  **A:** Three (100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5).

### Day 5 (Friday): Account of early Earth
*Student:* Write an evidence-based account of Earth's formation using meteorites, lunar rocks, and zircon crystals.

*How to teach:* Write an evidence-based account of Earth's formation citing meteorites (~4.57 Ga), lunar rocks (up to ~4.46 Ga; Apollo samples), and zircons (~4.4 Ga, suggesting early continental crust and liquid water).

- **Q:** What do 4.4-billion-year-old zircons tell us?
  **A:** That solid crust — and probably liquid water — existed within about 150 million years of Earth's formation.

**Assessment:** Written account of early Earth (HS-ESS1-6).

**Answer key:** Account of early Earth (HS-ESS1-6) rubric. 4 – Coherent timeline from solar nebula to differentiated Earth; cites meteorite ages (~4.57 Ga), lunar samples (~4.4–4.5 Ga, giant-impact origin), and Jack Hills zircons (~4.4 Ga) with explanation of radiometric dating; explains why Earth's own oldest rocks are younger. 3 (proficient) – Uses all three evidence types correctly. 2 – One or two evidence types or errors. 1 – Unsupported.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Congress: structure and powers

**Prepare:** Print Article I. Look up your U.S. Representative (house.gov) and Senators (senate.gov) and their committee assignments in advance. Bookmark congress.gov.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking senators represent districts. Each senator represents the whole state; representatives serve districts.
- Treating the elastic clause as unlimited. 'Necessary and proper' powers must connect to an enumerated power (McCulloch).
- Confusing the Speaker with the Senate's presiding officer. The Vice President presides over the Senate; the majority leader sets its agenda.

### Day 1 (Monday): Article I
*Student:* Read Article I and chart qualifications, terms, and unique powers of the House and Senate.

*How to teach:* Read Article I, Sections 1–3. The student charts qualifications, terms, and unique powers of each chamber; quiz orally. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Give the age, citizenship, and term for House and Senate.
  **A:** House: 25, 7 years a citizen, 2-year term. Senate: 30, 9 years a citizen, 6-year term.
- **Q:** Name a unique power of each chamber.
  **A:** House: originates revenue bills, impeaches. Senate: tries impeachments, confirms appointments, ratifies treaties (two-thirds).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Your representatives
*Student:* Look up your U.S. Representative and both U.S. Senators; summarize their committees and one recent vote each.

*How to teach:* Model looking up a member's committees and votes on congress.gov. The student summarizes all three members. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How many representatives does California have, and why?
  **A:** 52 (after the 2020 census) — House seats are apportioned by population.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Powers of Congress
*Student:* Sort Article I, Section 8 powers into categories (money, commerce, defense) and discuss the elastic clause.

*How to teach:* Read Article I, Section 8 and sort the powers. Discuss the elastic clause. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Give one power in each category: money, commerce, defense.
  **A:** Tax and borrow/coin money; regulate interstate and foreign commerce; declare war and raise armies.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Committees and leadership
*Student:* Diagram congressional leadership (Speaker, majority leaders, whips) and the committee system.

*How to teach:* Diagram congressional leadership and committees. Explain standing, select, joint, and conference committees. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Who leads the House?
  **A:** The Speaker, elected by the House majority.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz on Congress's structure and powers.

*How to teach:* Give the Congress quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How many members are in the House and Senate?
  **A:** 435 and 100.

**Assessment:** Congress quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) House 25/7/2 yrs; Senate 30/9/6 yrs. 2) 435 and 100. 3) House originates revenue bills, impeaches. 4) Senate tries impeachments, confirms, ratifies treaties. 5) Elastic clause = Necessary and Proper. 6) Speaker leads House; VP presides over Senate. 7) California has 52 representatives. 8) Committees do most bill work.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Perspective and space

**Prepare:** Ruler, drawing paper, fineliners; Drawabox.com lessons (free) for the 250-box challenge. Pick a room and a street/building view.

**Watch for:**
- Placing vanishing points too close together in two-point perspective, which distorts boxes. Spread them wide, often off the page.
- Horizon line confusion: it's always at the viewer's eye level.
- Thinking atmospheric perspective is only about size. Distant objects also get lighter, cooler, lower in contrast, and less detailed.

### Day 1 (Monday): Perspective boxes
*Student:* Complete the Drawabox 250-box challenge warm-up (25 boxes today) in two-point perspective.

*How to teach:* Draw 25 boxes in two-point perspective; check that parallel edges converge to the same vanishing point.

- **Q:** In two-point perspective, what happens to vertical lines?
  **A:** They remain vertical (parallel); only the horizontal edges converge to the two vanishing points.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Interior
*Student:* Draw a room in your home in one- or two-point perspective from life.

*How to teach:* Draw a room from life; mark eye level (horizon line) first.

- **Q:** Where is the horizon line in perspective drawing?
  **A:** At the viewer's eye level.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Exterior
*Student:* Draw your street or a building from a dramatic three-point viewpoint.

*How to teach:* Draw your street or a building in three-point perspective (worm's-eye or bird's-eye).

- **Q:** When is three-point perspective used?
  **A:** When looking steeply up or down at tall objects, so vertical lines also converge to a third vanishing point.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Atmospheric perspective
*Student:* Make a landscape study showing depth through value and detail changes.

*How to teach:* Landscape study showing depth through value and detail changes.

- **Q:** List three signs of atmospheric perspective.
  **A:** Distant objects appear lighter, bluer/cooler, lower in contrast, and less detailed.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Select the strongest perspective piece and refine it.

*How to teach:* Select and refine the strongest piece.

- **Q:** What is a vanishing point?
  **A:** A point on the horizon line where parallel lines appear to converge.

**Assessment:** Perspective drawing.

**Answer key:** Perspective drawing rubric. 4 – Accurate horizon line and vanishing points, consistent convergence, convincing space, atmospheric effects or strong value structure, clean line quality, refined composition. 3 (proficient) – Mostly accurate perspective and convincing space. 2 – Inconsistent vanishing points. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Program variables: sets, reps, load

**Prepare:** Weights or bands, training log with columns: exercise, load, sets, reps, volume, RPE. Teach how to estimate a 10-rep max safely (start light; add load until 10 reps is challenging but with good form).

**Watch for:**
- Testing a 1-rep max at home without spotters. Use 10RM estimates instead.
- Thinking more reps always builds strength. Low reps/heavy load emphasize strength; high reps/lighter load emphasize endurance.
- Increasing load too quickly. Progress about 2–10% when all reps are completed with good form.

### Day 1 (Monday): Find working loads
*Student:* Estimate 10-rep max for five lifts and record loads.

*How to teach:* Warm up; estimate 10RM for five lifts (squat, RDL, press, row, lunge) by adding load in small steps; record.

- **Q:** What is a 10-rep max (10RM)?
  **A:** The heaviest load you can lift for exactly 10 reps with good form.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Strength day
*Student:* Do 4×5 of squat and press at heavier load with 2-minute rests.

*How to teach:* Strength day: 4×5 squat and press at heavier load, 2-minute rests.

- **Q:** Why rest 2+ minutes for strength sets?
  **A:** To restore energy (phosphocreatine) so each heavy set can be done with full force and good form.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Endurance day
*Student:* Do 3×15 of lunges, rows, and push-ups with 45-second rests.

*How to teach:* Endurance day: 3×15 lunges, rows, push-ups with 45-second rests.

- **Q:** Which rep range and rest best match muscular endurance?
  **A:** High reps (12–20+) with short rests (30–60 s).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Core
*Student:* Do a core circuit (planks, dead bugs, bird-dogs, side planks) × 3 rounds.

*How to teach:* Core circuit ×3: plank, dead bug, bird-dog, side plank.

- **Q:** What does a bird-dog train?
  **A:** Core stability and anti-rotation/anti-extension while moving opposite arm and leg; also glutes and back extensors.

### Day 5 (Friday): Log review
*Student:* Calculate weekly training volume and plan next week's overload.

*How to teach:* Calculate volume (sets × reps × load) for each lift and plan next week's overload.

- **Q:** Squat: 4 sets × 5 reps × 40 lb. What is the volume?
  **A:** 800 lb.
- **Q:** If volume goes from 800 to 880 lb, what percent increase is that?
  **A:** 10%.

**Assessment:** Training log with volume calculations.

**Answer key:** Training log with volume calculations: proficient = all sessions logged with loads, sets, reps, RPE; volume correctly computed (sets × reps × load) for each lift and summed per week; next-week overload plan is specific and modest (e.g., +1 rep per set or +5%). Sample check: 3×15×20 lb = 900 lb; 4×5×40 = 800 lb.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Banks, credit unions & checking accounts

**Prepare:** Open FDIC Money Smart for Young Adults. Download fee schedules from a local bank and a credit union. Print a sample check register and a sample monthly statement with two planted errors.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking credit unions and banks are identical. Credit unions are member-owned nonprofits (deposits insured by NCUA); banks are for-profit (insured by FDIC). Both are safe up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category.
- Relying on the app's 'available balance' without tracking pending charges. Keep a register so pending transactions don't cause overdrafts.
- Ignoring fees. A $35 overdraft on a $5 coffee is a huge cost.

### Day 1 (Monday): Why bank
*Student:* Complete the FDIC Money Smart module on bank accounts and list the costs of being unbanked (check-cashing and money-order fees).

*How to teach:* Complete the Money Smart module; list costs of being unbanked.

- **Q:** A check-cashing store charges 3% to cash a $600 paycheck every two weeks. Annual cost?
  **A:** $18 per check × 26 = $468 a year.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Compare institutions
*Student:* Compare fee schedules of a local bank and a credit union: minimum balance, monthly fees, overdraft fees, ATM network.

*How to teach:* Compare fee schedules: minimum balance, monthly fees, overdraft fees, ATM network.

- **Q:** How are a bank and a credit union different?
  **A:** A bank is a for-profit company owned by shareholders; a credit union is a nonprofit owned by its members, often with lower fees; both are federally insured.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Account management
*Student:* Practice a month of transactions in a sample check register, including a debit card purchase, direct deposit, and a pending charge.

*How to teach:* Record a month of transactions in a check register, including a debit purchase, a direct deposit and a pending charge.

- **Q:** Balance $500; debit card $42.18; direct deposit $612.50; pending charge $19.99. New balance?
  **A:** 500 − 42.18 + 612.50 − 19.99 = $1,050.33.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Reconcile
*Student:* Reconcile the register with a sample monthly statement and find two errors.

*How to teach:* Reconcile the register with the statement; find two errors.

- **Q:** Why reconcile your account monthly?
  **A:** To catch errors, fraud and forgotten charges and make sure you know your true balance.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a recommendation memo choosing an account for your first job and take a short banking quiz.

*How to teach:* The student writes the account recommendation memo and takes the banking quiz.

- **Q:** What does FDIC insurance protect?
  **A:** Deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category if the bank fails.

**Assessment:** Banking quiz and account recommendation memo.

**Answer key:** Banking quiz: 1) FDIC insures bank deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. 2) NCUA insures credit union deposits similarly. 3) Overdraft = spending more than the account holds; the bank may cover it and charge a fee. 4) Routing number identifies the bank; account number identifies your account. 5) APY = annual percentage yield, the yearly return including compounding. 6) Cost of being unbanked: check-cashing fees (often 1–5% or more of the check) and money order fees. Memo rubric: 4 = recommends a specific account with reasons (no or waivable monthly fee, no/low overdraft fees, ATM network, mobile deposit, FDIC/NCUA insured) and compares at least two options with numbers; 3 = recommendation with limited comparison; 2 = vague; 1 = incomplete. Register reconciliation: student finds both planted errors and the adjusted balance matches.
