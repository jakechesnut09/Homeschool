# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 25

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Research question and preliminary sources

**Prepare:** Get a public library card and learn the database login (Gale, EBSCO). Set up a research notebook. Print a CRAAP test checklist.

**Watch for:**
- Choosing a topic instead of a question ('climate change'). Narrow to an open, arguable question with a place, group, or policy.
- Trusting sources that look professional. Apply the CRAAP test to every source and record the reasons.
- Collecting many sources and reading none deeply. Keep the four strongest and read them closely.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose a topic
*Student:* Brainstorm five topics connected to a career interest or a literature theme; narrow one into an open, arguable research question.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm five topics; model narrowing one into a question. The student writes an arguable question; check it can't be answered with a simple fact.

- **Q:** Narrow 'social media' into a research question.
  **A:** 'Should California high schools restrict student phone use during the school day to improve learning?'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Library databases
*Student:* Use your public library's free databases (e.g., Gale, EBSCO) and Google Scholar to find six candidate sources; record full citation info.

*How to teach:* Model a database search with keywords and Boolean operators. The student finds six sources and records citations. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does AND do in a database search?
  **A:** Narrows results to sources containing both terms.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Evaluate sources
*Student:* Apply the CRAAP test (currency, relevance, authority, accuracy, purpose) to each source and keep the four strongest.

*How to teach:* Explain CRAAP. The student evaluates each source and keeps four. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does CRAAP stand for?
  **A:** Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Proposal
*Student:* Write a one-page research proposal: question, why it matters, working hypothesis, and planned sources.

*How to teach:* The student writes the one-page proposal. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is a working hypothesis?
  **A:** A tentative answer to the research question that the research may revise.

### Day 5 (Friday): Conference
*Student:* Present the proposal in a 10-minute conference with a parent/mentor and revise the question based on feedback.

*How to teach:* Hold a 10-minute conference; the student revises the question. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What should feedback focus on?
  **A:** Whether the question is focused, arguable, and researchable.

**Assessment:** Approved research proposal and source evaluation chart.

**Answer key:** Proposal rubric (4 levels): 4 = focused arguable question, significance, hypothesis, plan with 4+ credible sources. Source chart: CRAAP scores with reasons.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Scatter plots, regression, and correlation

**Prepare:** Download life expectancy vs. GDP per capita from Gapminder (gapminder.org/data) into CODAP or Desmos. Prepare the Unit 8 test.

**Watch for:**
- 'Correlation proves causation.' Require naming a lurking variable and noting only randomized experiments establish cause.
- Thinking r measures any relationship. r measures LINEAR strength only.
- Interpreting the intercept when x = 0 is outside the data. Say whether it is meaningful.

### Day 1 (Monday): Scatter plots
*Student:* Plot life expectancy vs. GDP per capita from public data and describe direction, form, and strength.

*How to teach:* Plot life expectancy vs. GDP per capita. Model describing direction, form, strength, unusual points. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Describe the relationship.
  **A:** Positive, curved (rises steeply at low GDP and levels off), fairly strong, with some outliers.
- **Q:** What transformation might straighten it?
  **A:** Taking log of GDP per capita.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Linear regression
*Student:* Fit a least-squares line in Desmos; interpret slope and intercept in context and compute r.

*How to teach:* Fit least squares in Desmos (y₁ ~ mx₁ + b) and interpret. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** A model is ŷ = 40 + 2.5x (x = hours studied, y = score). Interpret slope and intercept.
  **A:** Each extra hour predicts a 2.5-point higher score; a student who studies 0 hours is predicted to score 40.
- **Q:** If r = 0.8, what is r² and its meaning?
  **A:** 0.64: about 64% of the variation in scores is explained by the linear model.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Residuals
*Student:* Make a residual plot to judge fit; try a logarithmic model and compare residuals.

*How to teach:* Residual = observed − predicted. A residual plot should show no pattern. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Using ŷ = 40 + 2.5x, a student who studied 4 hours scored 55. Residual?
  **A:** Predicted 50, residual 55 − 50 = +5.
- **Q:** What does a curved residual pattern mean?
  **A:** A linear model is not appropriate.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Correlation vs. causation
*Student:* Analyze 5 'spurious correlation' examples and identify lurking variables; explain why an experiment is needed for causation.

*How to teach:* Look at spurious correlations; for each, identify a lurking variable. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Ice cream sales and drownings are correlated. Explain.
  **A:** Lurking variable: hot weather increases both swimming and ice cream sales.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 8 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 8 test on data analysis and regression.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Can r be greater than 1?
  **A:** No. −1 ≤ r ≤ 1.

**Assessment:** Unit 8 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 8 test sample key: 1) SOCS comparison. 2) Mean ≈ 7.29, median 5, IQR 5, outlier 20. 3) z-scores 1.25 vs. 1.4. 4) P(X < 70) ≈ 0.992. 5) Two-way table: P(morning | 9–10) = 0.40, P(morning | 11–12) = 0.20 → association. 6) Slope/intercept interpretation. 7) Residual = +5. 8) r² = 0.64. 9) Lurking variable. Scoring: 90%+ mastery, 75–89% proficient, 60–74% developing.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Waste, plastics, and pollution solutions

**Prepare:** Kitchen scale, gloves, household trash sorted for one week (recycling, organics, landfill), spreadsheet, coffee filters, magnifier, compost bin. Read about SB 1383 (2016): cut organic waste sent to landfills 75% from 2014 levels by 2025 and recover 20% of edible food. Prepare the Unit 7 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking food in landfills just decomposes harmlessly. Without oxygen it produces methane, a potent greenhouse gas.
- Believing everything in the recycling bin gets recycled. Contamination and limited markets mean much is landfilled.
- Assuming technology alone solves pollution. Reduction at the source is usually most effective.

### Day 1 (Monday): Waste audit
*Student:* Weigh and sort your household's trash, recycling, and organics for the week and record in a spreadsheet.

*How to teach:* Weigh and sort the week's trash; record in a spreadsheet; calculate percentages.

- **Q:** A household throws out 12 kg landfill, 6 kg recycling, 8 kg organics. What percent is organic?
  **A:** 8 ÷ 26 ≈ 31%.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Microplastics
*Student:* Filter a sample of beach sand or laundry lint water through a coffee filter and look for microplastic fibers with a magnifier.

*How to teach:* Filter sand or laundry rinse water; look for colored fibers and fragments.

- **Q:** What are microplastics?
  **A:** Plastic pieces smaller than 5 mm, including fibers from synthetic clothing and fragments of larger plastics.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Composting
*Student:* Start a small compost bin and explain how California's SB 1383 organic waste law reduces methane.

*How to teach:* Start compost (browns and greens, moisture, turning); explain how SB 1383 reduces methane.

- **Q:** Why does composting produce less methane than landfilling?
  **A:** Composting is aerobic (with oxygen), so microbes produce mainly CO₂; landfills are anaerobic, producing methane.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Evaluate technology
*Student:* Evaluate a pollution-reduction technology (catalytic converter, scrubbers, Ocean Cleanup) using cost, effectiveness, and side effects.

*How to teach:* Evaluate one technology (catalytic converter: converts CO, NOx, hydrocarbons to CO₂, N₂, H₂O; scrubbers: remove SO₂; Ocean Cleanup: collects floating plastic) on cost, effectiveness, side effects.

- **Q:** What does a catalytic converter do?
  **A:** Uses catalysts (platinum, palladium, rhodium) to convert CO, NOx, and unburned hydrocarbons into CO₂, N₂, and water.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 7 test on water, air, and waste.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test.

- **Q:** Which is more potent as a greenhouse gas over 100 years, CO₂ or methane?
  **A:** Methane — roughly 28–34 times more warming per ton over 100 years (about 80 times over 20 years).

**Assessment:** Waste audit analysis and Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Waste audit analysis: proficient = weights by category, percentages, comparison to SB 1383 goals, three reduction actions with estimated impact. Unit 7 test key: 1) Watershed; point vs. nonpoint. 2) Eutrophication. 3) Nitrate standard. 4) PM2.5 and health. 5) Inversion and ozone formation. 6) AQI categories. 7) Microplastics <5 mm. 8) Methane from landfills; composting aerobic. 9) SB 1383. 10) Catalytic converter chemistry.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Market failures and the role of government

**Prepare:** Print a CARB cap-and-trade overview and the FTC consumer-protection page. Find a short article on California housing permitting delays.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking public goods are anything government provides. A public good is nonexcludable and nonrival.
- Believing externalities are always bad. Positive externalities exist (education, vaccines).
- Assuming regulation always helps. Compare its costs (compliance, delays) with benefits (cleaner air, safety).

### Day 1 (Monday): Public goods
*Student:* Classify 10 goods as private, public, common resource, or club goods and explain the free-rider problem.

*How to teach:* Define the four types of goods by excludability and rivalry. The student classifies 10 goods and explains the free-rider problem.

- **Q:** Classify national defense, a fishery, streaming service, and a sandwich.
  **A:** Public good; common resource; club good; private good.
- **Q:** What is the free-rider problem?
  **A:** People can benefit from a public good without paying, so markets underprovide it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Externalities
*Student:* Graph a negative externality (air pollution) and analyze California's cap-and-trade as a solution.

*How to teach:* Graph a negative externality with private supply below social cost. Discuss cap-and-trade. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Why does pollution cause overproduction?
  **A:** Producers don't pay the external cost, so market output exceeds the socially optimal level.
- **Q:** How does cap-and-trade work?
  **A:** The state caps total emissions and issues tradable allowances; firms that cut cheaply sell extra allowances, putting a price on carbon.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Consumer protection
*Student:* Read about the FTC and a California consumer protection law (e.g., CCPA privacy) and explain the market problem it addresses.

*How to teach:* Read about the FTC and the CCPA. The student explains the market problem each addresses. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What market problem does consumer protection address?
  **A:** Information asymmetry — sellers know more than buyers.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Costs vs. benefits
*Student:* Analyze a case where regulation costs may exceed benefits (permitting delays, housing) and propose improvements.

*How to teach:* Analyze a case where regulation costs may exceed benefits. The student proposes improvements. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How can permitting delays raise housing costs?
  **A:** They increase time and uncertainty costs, reducing supply and raising prices.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz on the government's role.

*How to teach:* Give the government-role quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Give an example of a positive externality.
  **A:** Education or vaccination benefits others besides the person who receives it.

**Assessment:** Government-role quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Defense = public good; fishery = common resource; streaming = club good; sandwich = private. 2) Free rider benefits without paying → underprovision. 3) Negative externality: social cost above private supply, market overproduces. 4) Cap-and-trade sets an emissions cap with tradable allowances. 5) Consumer protection addresses information asymmetry. 6) Regulation is justified when benefits exceed costs. Proficient: 5 of 6.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Investigation: completion

**Prepare:** All investigation pieces laid out; AP scoring guidelines; critique audience.

**Watch for:**
- Calling it done without a gap analysis.
- Adding filler pieces just to reach 15. Each piece should strengthen cohesion.
- Scoring without the official rubric language.

### Day 1 (Monday): Gap analysis
*Student:* Lay out all pieces and identify gaps in the investigation.

*How to teach:* Lay out all pieces; identify gaps (missing experimentation, unresolved ideas, weak skills).

- **Q:** What is 'cohesion' in a body of work?
  **A:** The pieces clearly relate through the inquiry while showing variety and development.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Final pieces
*Student:* Create or revise pieces to fill the gaps.

*How to teach:* Create or revise pieces to fill gaps.

- **Q:** Give an example of a gap.
  **A:** No evidence of revision, or all pieces in one medium so experimentation isn't shown.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Continue
*Student:* Continue final pieces.

*How to teach:* Continue final pieces.

- **Q:** How can process images help fill the 15?
  **A:** They show practice, experimentation, and revision, which AP explicitly scores.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Finish
*Student:* Finish the body of work (12–15 pieces).

*How to teach:* Finish the body of work (12–15 pieces).

- **Q:** What written responses accompany the AP Sustained Investigation?
  **A:** Identify the inquiry and how it guided the work; describe how practice, experimentation, and revision guided it; for each image, materials, processes, and size.

### Day 5 (Friday): Final critique
*Student:* Hold a full critique of the investigation and score it with the AP guidelines.

*How to teach:* Full critique and AP-guideline scoring.

- **Q:** What score range is AP Art and Design reported on?
  **A:** 1–5 (AP scale).

**Assessment:** Completed Sustained Investigation.

**Answer key:** Completed Sustained Investigation rubric (aligned to AP rows). 4 – Clear inquiry guiding all work; strong evidence of practice, experimentation, and revision; materials, processes, and ideas synthesized; advanced 2-D skills; 15 documented images with concise written responses. 3 (proficient) – Inquiry evident, revision shown, good skill, 12–15 pieces documented. 2 – Weak connection or little revision. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Hiking safety and preparation

**Prepare:** Daypack, water, layers, first aid kit, headlamp, map. Research a 4–6 mile hike (state or local park) with weather and closures. Parent accompanies.

**Watch for:**
- Relying on phone only. Carry a paper map and tell someone your plan.
- Underestimating water. Plan about 0.5 liter per hour in moderate temperatures, more in heat.
- Leaving 'natural' trash. Pack out everything.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ten Essentials
*Student:* Pack the Ten Essentials and learn Leave No Trace principles.

*How to teach:* Pack the Ten Essentials; teach the 7 Leave No Trace principles.

- **Q:** List the Ten Essentials.
  **A:** Navigation, headlamp, sun protection, first aid, knife, fire, shelter, extra food, extra water, extra clothes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Conditioning
*Student:* Do stair or hill repeats with a loaded daypack for 30 minutes.

*How to teach:* Stair or hill repeats with a loaded daypack for 30 minutes.

- **Q:** What principle is applied by training with the pack?
  **A:** Specificity.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Plan a hike
*Student:* Plan a 4–6 mile hike in a local or state park, including weather, elevation, and emergency plan.

*How to teach:* Plan a 4–6 mile hike: route, elevation gain, time estimate, weather, turnaround time, emergency plan.

- **Q:** Estimate time for 5 miles with 1,500 ft gain (2 mph + 1 h per 2,000 ft).
  **A:** 2.5 h + 0.75 h ≈ 3.25 hours plus breaks.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Hike
*Student:* Complete the planned hike with a family member, logging time and heart rate.

*How to teach:* Complete the hike, logging time and HR.

- **Q:** Who yields on trails?
  **A:** Cyclists yield to hikers and horses; hikers yield to horses; downhill hikers yield to uphill hikers.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Write a trip report with distance, elevation gain, and lessons learned.

*How to teach:* Trip report: distance, elevation gain, lessons.

- **Q:** Name two Leave No Trace principles.
  **A:** Plan ahead; travel on durable surfaces; dispose of waste properly; leave what you find; minimize campfire impacts; respect wildlife; be considerate of others.

**Assessment:** Hike plan and trip report.

**Answer key:** Hike plan and trip report rubric. 4 – Plan with map, distance, elevation, time estimate, weather, turnaround, emergency contacts; report with data, HR, Leave No Trace actions, lessons. 3 (proficient) – Plan and report with key data. 2 – Missing safety plan or data. 1 – Incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): California Dream Act Application & CSAC WebGrants

**Prepare:** Read CSAC's guidance on which application to file (csac.ca.gov). Read the Cal Grant GPA verification rules for private and homeschool students and the WebGrants student portal (mygrantinfo.csac.ca.gov). Prepare the school's GPA certification or check CSAC's current test-score alternative.

**Watch for:**
- Filing both the FAFSA and the CADAA. Students should file only one; filing both can delay or cancel state aid.
- Assuming a homeschool GPA reaches CSAC automatically. A private school (PSA) must submit GPAs to CSAC or use the approved alternative — confirm before the deadline.
- Ignoring WebGrants messages. Award notices and requests for information appear there.

### Day 1 (Monday): Which application
*Student:* Read CSAC's guidance and explain who files the CADAA (including AB 540 eligibility) versus the FAFSA, and why never to file both.

*How to teach:* Explain who files the CADAA vs. the FAFSA and why not to file both.

- **Q:** Who should file the California Dream Act Application?
  **A:** Students who are undocumented, have DACA, or otherwise can't get federal aid but qualify for California aid (e.g. through AB 540 nonresident tuition exemption eligibility).
- **Q:** Should a student file both FAFSA and CADAA?
  **A:** No — file only one; filing both can cause delays or problems with state aid.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): CADAA walkthrough
*Student:* Walk through the California Dream Act Application sections and compare them to the FAFSA.

*How to teach:* Walk through CADAA sections and compare to FAFSA.

- **Q:** What aid can CADAA filers receive?
  **A:** California state aid such as Cal Grants, the Middle Class Scholarship, the California College Promise Grant and institutional aid — not federal aid.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): GPA verification
*Student:* Learn how Cal Grant GPA verification works for a private homeschool student and prepare the school's GPA certification or test-score alternative.

*How to teach:* Learn how Cal Grant GPA verification works for a private homeschool and prepare the certification.

- **Q:** Why is GPA verification needed for a Cal Grant?
  **A:** Cal Grant awards require a verified GPA meeting the minimum (e.g. 3.0 for Cal Grant A, 2.0 for Cal Grant B) submitted by the deadline.

### Day 4 (Thursday): WebGrants
*Student:* Create a WebGrants student account, find where award status and messages appear, and set reminders to check monthly.

*How to teach:* Create a WebGrants account; find award status and messages; set monthly reminders.

- **Q:** What is WebGrants used for?
  **A:** CSAC's student portal to check Cal Grant and Middle Class Scholarship status, update schools and respond to requests.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a state-aid checklist with deadlines and confirmation of submitted items.

*How to teach:* Complete the state-aid checklist.

- **Q:** What does CSAC stand for?
  **A:** California Student Aid Commission.

**Assessment:** State-aid checklist and WebGrants setup.

**Answer key:** State-aid checklist (pass when complete): correct application chosen and submitted (FAFSA for U.S. citizens and eligible noncitizens; CADAA for undocumented students, DACA recipients, students with U visas or TPS, and others eligible for AB 540 benefits); Cal Grant GPA submitted (school certification or approved alternative) by the deadline; WebGrants account created with school list confirmed; reminders set to check monthly; deadlines and confirmation numbers logged.
