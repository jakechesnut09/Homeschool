# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 34

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Research presentation rehearsal

**Prepare:** Slides, a timer, and a phone to record video. Have the research paper and storyboard ready.

**Watch for:**
- Text-heavy slides. One idea per slide, with images or charts and minimal text.
- Rehearsing silently in one's head. Rehearse out loud, standing, with a timer.
- Avoiding the hardest questions. List them deliberately and prepare evidence.

### Day 1 (Monday): Build slides
*Student:* Build 10–12 slides following the 'one idea per slide' rule with images, charts, and minimal text.

*How to teach:* Show one text-heavy slide and redesign it together. The student builds 10–12 one-idea slides. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is the one-idea-per-slide rule?
  **A:** Each slide makes one point supported by an image or chart and very little text.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Script
*Student:* Write speaker notes and practice transitions so the talk runs 10–12 minutes.

*How to teach:* The student writes speaker notes and rehearses transitions, timing the talk at 10–12 minutes. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What should speaker notes contain?
  **A:** Key points and transitions in brief phrases, not a full script.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Record rehearsal
*Student:* Record a full rehearsal and critique your pacing, filler words, and eye contact.

*How to teach:* Record a full rehearsal and watch it together. The student counts filler words and notes pacing and eye contact. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How can filler words be reduced?
  **A:** Pause silently instead of saying 'um' or 'like'; practice transitions.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Q&A prep
*Student:* List the 8 hardest questions an audience might ask and prepare evidence-based answers.

*How to teach:* The student lists the 8 hardest questions and drafts evidence-based answers. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Give one likely hard question.
  **A:** 'What evidence contradicts your thesis, and why aren't you persuaded?'

### Day 5 (Friday): Dress rehearsal
*Student:* Deliver a dress rehearsal to a family member and revise based on their feedback.

*How to teach:* Hold a dress rehearsal for a family member; the student revises from feedback. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What feedback is most useful?
  **A:** Specific moments where clarity, pacing, or visuals broke down.

**Assessment:** Rehearsal recording self-critique.

**Answer key:** Rehearsal self-critique rubric (4 levels): 4 = specific notes on timing, filler counts, eye contact, and slide clarity with a concrete revision plan; 3 = accurate but general; 2 = superficial; 1 = missing.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Statistics capstone: design and data collection

**Prepare:** Browse data.ca.gov or census tables with the student before Day 1. Set up a capstone folder. Schedule the Day 5 check-in conference.

**Watch for:**
- Questions that aren't statistical ('How old is the mayor?'). A statistical question anticipates variability in data.
- Skipping a data-cleaning log. Record every change so the analysis is reproducible.
- Choosing a question the available data can't answer.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pose a question
*Student:* Write a statistical question about your community using a public data set (data.ca.gov) or your own survey/experiment.

*How to teach:* Model turning a topic into a statistical question naming population and variables. The student drafts three questions and picks one.

- **Q:** Rewrite 'Is air pollution bad in California?' as a statistical question.
  **A:** 'How did average annual PM2.5 levels differ between California counties in 2023?' — it names variables and expects variability.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plan
*Student:* Write a data-collection plan naming variables, sampling method, and potential bias.

*How to teach:* The student writes the plan: variables (type, units), source or sampling method, sample size, and possible bias. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Why identify potential bias before collecting?
  **A:** So you can design around it or acknowledge it as a limitation.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Collect
*Student:* Collect or download and clean the data (remove duplicates, handle missing values) in CODAP or a spreadsheet.

*How to teach:* Download or collect data; clean it (duplicates, missing values, units), logging each step. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How should missing values be handled?
  **A:** Note how many, decide to exclude or mark them, and report the decision.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Explore
*Student:* Create at least three exploratory displays and summary statistics.

*How to teach:* Make at least three displays and summary statistics; write one observation for each. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Which display suits comparing a quantitative variable across 3 groups?
  **A:** Parallel box plots or dot plots.

### Day 5 (Friday): Checkpoint
*Student:* Present your question, plan, and initial graphs in a check-in conference.

*How to teach:* Hold a 15-minute conference: the student presents question, plan, and graphs; give feedback. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What should the student be able to state at the checkpoint?
  **A:** The question, population, variables, data source, cleaning steps, and one preliminary finding.

**Assessment:** Capstone plan and data checkpoint.

**Answer key:** Checkpoint rubric (4 levels): 4 = statistical question with population/variables, detailed plan with bias identified, clean documented data, three appropriate displays with summaries; 3 = one element thin; 2 = question non-statistical or data unusable; 1 = incomplete.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Capstone: data collection

**Prepare:** Thermometer or infrared thermometer, test strips, camera, data sheets, spreadsheet. Plan field days at similar times/conditions.

**Watch for:**
- Recording data without metadata (time, weather), which makes comparisons impossible.
- Rounding inconsistently or mixing units.
- Ignoring outliers without explanation.

### Day 1 (Monday): Field day 1
*Student:* Collect data at your first set of sites, recording date, time, conditions, and photos.

*How to teach:* Field day 1: collect data with date, time, conditions, photos.

- **Q:** What is metadata?
  **A:** Information about the data — when, where, how, and under what conditions it was collected.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Field day 2
*Student:* Collect data at the second set of sites using identical methods.

*How to teach:* Field day 2: identical methods at the second set of sites.

- **Q:** Why keep methods identical?
  **A:** So differences reflect the variable studied, not the method.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Field day 3
*Student:* Collect replicate data to strengthen reliability.

*How to teach:* Field day 3: replicate data.

- **Q:** How does replication improve reliability?
  **A:** It shows whether results are consistent and reduces the influence of random errors.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Organize
*Student:* Enter data in a spreadsheet, calculate means, and note measurement uncertainty.

*How to teach:* Enter data; calculate means; note uncertainty (instrument precision, range).

- **Q:** Readings: 31.2, 30.8, 31.6 °C. Mean and range?
  **A:** Mean 31.2 °C; range 0.8 °C (30.8–31.6).

### Day 5 (Friday): Graph
*Student:* Create graphs that answer your question and draft initial findings.

*How to teach:* Graph results; draft findings.

- **Q:** Which graph suits comparing means at several sites?
  **A:** A bar graph with error bars (range or standard deviation).

**Assessment:** Organized data set and graphs.

**Answer key:** Organized data set: proficient = all field data in a spreadsheet with metadata, units, replicates, correctly calculated means and ranges, uncertainty noted, appropriate graphs with labels, draft findings. 4 adds error bars and outlier discussion; 2 = gaps; 1 = incomplete.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Exchange rates and the global economy

**Prepare:** Look up current exchange rates on a currency converter (rates below are illustrative). Bookmark FRED series DTWEXBGS (nominal broad trade-weighted dollar index). Find a news story about a foreign-owned auto or battery plant in California or the U.S. Prepare the Unit 13 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a 'strong dollar' is always good. It helps importers and Americans traveling abroad but hurts exporters and U.S. firms competing with imports.
- Confusing appreciation and depreciation. If $1 buys MORE euros than before, the dollar appreciated.
- Ignoring capital flows. Exchange rates respond to interest rates and investment demand, not only trade.

### Day 1 (Monday): Exchange rates
*Student:* Convert a travel budget into three currencies and explain what determines exchange rates.

*How to teach:* Convert a $1,000 travel budget at illustrative rates. Explain that exchange rates are set by supply and demand for currencies, influenced by interest rates, inflation, trade, and investor confidence.

- **Q:** At 1 USD = 0.90 EUR, 150 JPY, and 18 MXN, what does $1,000 buy?
  **A:** 900 euros, 150,000 yen, and 18,000 pesos.
- **Q:** If the rate changes to 1 USD = 0.95 EUR, did the dollar appreciate or depreciate?
  **A:** Appreciate — each dollar buys more euros.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Strong vs. weak dollar
*Student:* Analyze who benefits when the dollar appreciates (importers, tourists abroad) vs. depreciates (exporters).

*How to teach:* Make a two-column chart of winners and losers when the dollar appreciates vs. depreciates. The student fills it in with California examples.

- **Q:** Who benefits when the dollar appreciates?
  **A:** U.S. importers, consumers buying imports, and Americans traveling abroad.
- **Q:** Who benefits when it depreciates?
  **A:** U.S. exporters (e.g., California almond growers) and the U.S. tourism industry, since foreign buyers find U.S. goods cheaper.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dollar index
*Student:* Graph the trade-weighted dollar index on FRED over 10 years and connect changes to events.

*How to teach:* Graph the trade-weighted dollar index for 10 years on FRED. The student annotates two big moves with likely causes. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Why might the dollar rise when the Fed raises interest rates?
  **A:** Higher U.S. returns attract foreign investors, increasing demand for dollars.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Global capital
*Student:* Research foreign direct investment in California (e.g., auto or battery plants) and its effects on jobs.

*How to teach:* Read the FDI story. The student summarizes the investment, jobs created, and any concerns. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is foreign direct investment?
  **A:** A foreign company building or buying productive facilities (factories, businesses) in another country, as opposed to buying stocks or bonds.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 13 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 13 test on international economics.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 13 test. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** If the dollar depreciates, what tends to happen to U.S. exports?
  **A:** They rise, because U.S. goods become cheaper for foreign buyers.

**Assessment:** Unit 13 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 13 test key: 1) Comparative advantage from an opportunity-cost table. 2) Terms of trade between the two opportunity costs. 3) Tariff effects: price up, imports down, deadweight loss. 4) Smoot-Hawley 1930 and retaliation. 5) NAFTA 1994 → USMCA 2020 (auto rules of origin). 6) $1,000 → 900 EUR at 0.90. 7) Appreciation helps importers/hurts exporters. 8) FDI definition. Scoring: 90%+ mastery, 75–89% proficient, 60–74% developing.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Senior exhibition

**Prepare:** Artwork, labels, level, measuring tape, hooks/nails, guest book, simple refreshments, lighting (clip lights if needed).

**Watch for:**
- Installing without measuring, leading to uneven heights.
- Artist talks that read the statement word for word.
- Ignoring visitor feedback afterward.

### Day 1 (Monday): Install
*Student:* Install the exhibition, level and light the works, and place labels.

*How to teach:* Install: measure, level, light, place labels.

- **Q:** How do you hang a work centered at 58 inches?
  **A:** Measure 58 in. to the center, add half the frame height, subtract the distance from the hanging wire (pulled taut) to the frame top; place the nail there.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Artist talk prep
*Student:* Prepare a 5-minute artist talk.

*How to teach:* Prepare a 5-minute artist talk: inquiry, process, highlights, what's next.

- **Q:** What makes a good artist talk?
  **A:** A clear story of the inquiry, specific process moments, connection to viewers, and openness to questions.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Opening
*Student:* Host the opening reception and give your artist talk.

*How to teach:* Host the opening; give the talk.

- **Q:** What is a reception?
  **A:** A social event (often the opening) where visitors meet the artist and view the show.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Feedback
*Student:* Review guest-book comments and visitor conversations.

*How to teach:* Review guest-book comments and conversations.

- **Q:** Why review audience feedback?
  **A:** It shows how the work communicates to the public and informs future work.

### Day 5 (Friday): Deinstall
*Student:* Deinstall carefully and write an exhibition report.

*How to teach:* Deinstall carefully; write an exhibition report.

- **Q:** What should an exhibition report include?
  **A:** Attendance, what went well, problems and solutions, feedback, photos, lessons for next time.

**Assessment:** Exhibition and artist talk.

**Answer key:** Exhibition and artist talk rubric. 4 – Professional installation (level, consistent heights, lighting, labels), engaging 5-minute talk, gracious hosting, thorough report with feedback analysis, careful deinstall. 3 (proficient) – Clean installation, clear talk, report. 2 – Installation issues or weak talk. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Final fitness assessment

**Prepare:** Stopwatch, mat, all earlier data (baseline, midyear). Same protocols.

**Watch for:**
- Expecting gains in every measure.
- Comparing to peers instead of self.
- Not noting conditions (sleep, weather, illness).

### Day 1 (Monday): Mile
*Student:* Final timed mile.

*How to teach:* Final timed mile.

- **Q:** Mile: 9:45 → 9:05 → 8:50. Total improvement?
  **A:** 55 seconds.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Strength
*Student:* Final push-up and curl-up tests.

*How to teach:* Final push-ups and curl-ups.

- **Q:** Push-ups 15 → 22 → 25. Percent improvement from baseline?
  **A:** (25 − 15) ÷ 15 ≈ 67%.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Flexibility
*Student:* Final flexibility and resting heart rate tests.

*How to teach:* Final flexibility and resting HR.

- **Q:** Why measure resting HR at the same time of day?
  **A:** Resting HR varies with time, activity, caffeine, and stress; consistency makes comparisons valid.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Graph
*Student:* Graph year-long data across three test points.

*How to teach:* Graph data across three test points.

- **Q:** What graph best shows change over time?
  **A:** A line graph with test date on the x-axis.

### Day 5 (Friday): Analyze
*Student:* Write an analysis of your year's progress.

*How to teach:* Write an analysis of the year's progress.

- **Q:** What makes a progress analysis strong?
  **A:** It cites data, explains causes (training, habits), acknowledges limitations, and sets next goals.

**Assessment:** Final fitness assessment.

**Answer key:** Final fitness assessment rubric. 4 – All tests completed with consistent protocols; line graphs for three test points; changes calculated; HFZ comparison; analysis with causes and next goals. 3 (proficient) – Complete data, graphs, explanation. 2 – Partial. 1 – Incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Workplace skills & workers' rights

**Prepare:** Open the California Labor Commissioner (dir.ca.gov/dlse) and Cal/OSHA pages. Check the current state minimum wage ($16.90/hour in 2026; some cities and industries, such as fast food at $20, are higher). Prepare the Unit 11 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking breaks are optional favors. California law requires a 30-minute unpaid meal break for shifts over 5 hours (can be waived by mutual consent if the shift is no more than 6 hours) and a paid 10-minute rest break per 4 hours worked or major fraction.
- Fear of retaliation. Retaliating against workers for reporting wage or safety violations is illegal; complaints can be filed with the Labor Commissioner or Cal/OSHA.
- Overly casual work emails. Use a clear subject, greeting, short body and professional sign-off.

### Day 1 (Monday): Professional communication
*Student:* Write three professional emails: requesting time off, following up on an application, and asking a supervisor for feedback.

*How to teach:* Write three professional emails: requesting time off, following up on an application, asking a supervisor for feedback.

- **Q:** What makes an email professional?
  **A:** Clear subject line, proper greeting, concise purpose, polite tone, correct grammar and a signature with your name.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Workers' rights
*Student:* Research California minimum wage, overtime, meal and rest breaks, and paid sick leave and make a rights card.

*How to teach:* Research California minimum wage, overtime, meal and rest breaks and paid sick leave; make a rights card.

- **Q:** How much paid sick leave must California employers provide?
  **A:** At least 40 hours or 5 days per year (since 2024).
- **Q:** When is a meal break required?
  **A:** For shifts over 5 hours: a 30-minute meal break (waivable by mutual consent if the shift is 6 hours or less).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Workplace safety and harassment
*Student:* Learn how to report unsafe conditions and harassment and the role of Cal/OSHA and the Labor Commissioner.

*How to teach:* Learn how to report unsafe conditions and harassment; roles of Cal/OSHA and the Labor Commissioner.

- **Q:** Where do you report unpaid wages in California?
  **A:** The Labor Commissioner's Office (Division of Labor Standards Enforcement).
- **Q:** What should you do first if you experience harassment at work?
  **A:** Document what happened (dates, details, witnesses) and report it to a supervisor or HR per the company's policy; you can also file with the California Civil Rights Department.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Job offer
*Student:* Evaluate two sample job offers by total compensation, schedule, growth, and commute cost.

*How to teach:* Evaluate two sample job offers by total compensation, schedule, growth and commute.

- **Q:** Why include commute costs in comparing jobs?
  **A:** Gas, transit, parking and time reduce the real value of the pay.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Unit 11 test on career readiness and employment law.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 11 test in the key.

- **Q:** Is it legal for an employer to fire you for reporting a safety hazard?
  **A:** No — retaliation is illegal; you can file a complaint with the Labor Commissioner or Cal/OSHA.

**Assessment:** Unit 11 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 11 test answers: 1) Resume accomplishment statements use action verb + result. 2) ATS definition. 3) STAR method. 4) Minor work permit = B1-1 request, B1-4 permit. 5) I-9 verifies identity and work authorization. 6) California daily overtime after 8 hours. 7) Meal break: 30 minutes for shifts over 5 hours. 8) Rest break: 10 minutes per 4 hours. 9) Paid sick leave: at least 40 hours (5 days) per year. 10) Report wage theft to the Labor Commissioner; unsafe conditions to Cal/OSHA. 11) Comparing offers: total compensation = pay + benefits − commute costs. Mastery 9+/11. Job-offer evaluation example: Job A $18/hour, 30 hrs/week, 20-minute commute; Job B $20/hour, 30 hrs/week, 40-mile round-trip commute at $0.20/mile cost → A = $540/week; B = $600 − (5 days × 40 × $0.20 = $40) = $560/week before time cost.
