# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 21

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: The Importance of Being Earnest

**Prepare:** Download The Importance of Being Earnest (Project Gutenberg) and choose a filmed production (e.g., 2002 film or 1952 film). Prepare the timed essay prompt.

**Watch for:**
- Dismissing the comedy as frivolous. Show how each joke exposes Victorian hypocrisy about marriage, class, and respectability.
- Explaining epigrams literally. Have the student name the expected saying or value and show how Wilde inverts it.
- Comparing Ibsen and Wilde only by plot. Ask how each mode — realism vs. comedy — makes the critique land differently.

### Day 1 (Monday): Act I
*Student:* Read Act I. Collect five epigrams and explain the paradox in each (e.g., 'The truth is rarely pure and never simple').

*How to teach:* Read Act I. The student collects five epigrams and explains each paradox. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Explain 'The truth is rarely pure and never simple.'
  **A:** It reverses the cliché 'the plain and simple truth' and hints at the lies both men tell.
- **Q:** Explain Lady Bracknell's line about losing parents.
  **A:** 'To lose one parent… may be regarded as a misfortune; to lose both looks like carelessness' — absurdly treating tragedy as social failing.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Act II
*Student:* Read Act II. Analyze 'Bunburying' as a satire of the double life demanded by Victorian respectability.

*How to teach:* Read Act II. Analyze Bunburying. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is Bunburying?
  **A:** Algernon's invented invalid friend, Bunbury, used as an excuse to escape social obligations — a satire of double lives.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Act III
*Student:* Read Act III. Discuss how the absurd ending mocks the obsession with names and social class.

*How to teach:* Read Act III. Discuss the absurd ending. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How is Jack's identity resolved?
  **A:** Miss Prism had left baby Jack in a handbag at Victoria Station; he is Algernon's older brother and was christened Ernest.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Stage vs. screen
*Student:* Watch one scene from a filmed production and evaluate how performance choices heighten the comedy.

*How to teach:* Watch a scene and evaluate performance choices. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How can performance heighten Wilde's comedy?
  **A:** Deadpan delivery and exaggerated reactions emphasize absurdity.

### Day 5 (Friday): Comparison essay
*Student:* Write a 45-minute comparative essay on how Ibsen and Wilde use different modes (realism vs. comedy) to critique marriage and respectability.

*How to teach:* Give the 45-minute comparison essay. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What modes do Ibsen and Wilde use?
  **A:** Ibsen uses realism; Wilde uses comedy and satire.

**Assessment:** Timed comparison essay (RL.11-12.9).

**Answer key:** Timed essay rubric (4 levels): 4 = thesis on how realism and comedy each critique marriage and respectability; evidence from both plays; analysis of how mode affects the critique. 3 = clear comparison; 2 = summary; 1 = one play only.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Conic sections

**Prepare:** String, two pushpins, cardboard, pencil, flashlight. Preview OpenStax 12.1–12.4. Prepare the Unit 7 test.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up a and c: for an ellipse c² = a² − b²; for a hyperbola c² = a² + b².
- Forgetting to balance both sides when completing the square with a coefficient: 4(x² − 4x + 4) adds 16, not 4.
- Identifying the conic from x² terms alone. Use the signs/coefficients of both squared terms: same coefficient → circle; same sign different → ellipse; opposite signs → hyperbola; only one squared → parabola.

### Day 1 (Monday): Slicing a cone
*Student:* Shine a flashlight at a wall at different angles to see circle, ellipse, parabola, and hyperbola; derive a parabola from focus and directrix.

*How to teach:* Shine a flashlight at a wall at different tilts to see the conics. Then derive the parabola with focus (0, p) and directrix y = −p: x² = 4py.

- **Q:** Find the equation of the parabola with focus (0, 2) and directrix y = −2.
  **A:** x² = 8y

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ellipses
*Student:* Draw an ellipse with string and two pins; derive its equation and graph ellipses from standard form.

*How to teach:* Loop string around two pins and trace an ellipse: the sum of distances to the foci is constant (= 2a). Graph from standard form.

- **Q:** For x²/25 + y²/9 = 1, give a, b, c, vertices, and foci.
  **A:** a = 5, b = 3, c = 4; vertices (±5, 0); foci (±4, 0).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hyperbolas
*Student:* Graph hyperbolas, find foci and asymptotes, and discuss LORAN-style navigation.

*How to teach:* Graph x²/16 − y²/9 = 1 using the box and asymptotes. Discuss LORAN: a constant difference in signal arrival time places a ship on a hyperbola.

- **Q:** Foci and asymptotes of x²/16 − y²/9 = 1?
  **A:** c = 5, foci (±5, 0); asymptotes y = ±(3/4)x.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Completing the square
*Student:* Convert general-form conic equations to standard form and identify the conic type.

*How to teach:* Model completing the square for a circle and an ellipse. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Write x² + y² − 6x + 4y − 3 = 0 in standard form.
  **A:** (x − 3)² + (y + 2)² = 16: circle, center (3, −2), radius 4.
- **Q:** Identify 4x² + 9y² − 16x + 18y − 11 = 0.
  **A:** 4(x − 2)² + 9(y + 1)² = 36 → (x − 2)²/9 + (y + 1)²/4 = 1, an ellipse centered at (2, −1).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 7 test on sequences, series, and conics.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Identify y² − x² = 1.
  **A:** A hyperbola opening up and down.

**Assessment:** Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 7 test sample key: 1) 3n + 2 and S₂₀ = 670. 2) hₙ = 10(0.8)ⁿ. 3) 2 + 6 + … + 2·3⁹ = 59,048. 4) 0.2727… = 3/11. 5) Focus (0, 2), directrix y = −2: x² = 8y. 6) x²/25 + y²/9 = 1: foci (±4, 0). 7) x²/16 − y²/9 = 1: foci (±5, 0), asymptotes y = ±3x/4. 8) x² + y² − 6x + 4y − 3 = 0: circle (3, −2), r = 4. 9) 4x² + 9y² − 16x + 18y − 11 = 0: ellipse (x − 2)²/9 + (y + 1)²/4 = 1. Scoring: 90%+ mastery, 75–89% proficient, 60–74% developing.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Simulating resources, population, and biodiversity

**Prepare:** Goldfish crackers or dried beans (a large bowl as the 'ocean'), spreadsheet. Logistic model: N_next = N + rN(1 − N/K) − H.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a fixed quota is always safe. When populations drop, a fixed quota takes a larger share and can cause collapse.
- Assuming maximum harvest happens at maximum population. Maximum sustainable yield occurs near K/2 in the logistic model.
- Treating model output as exact predictions. Models depend on assumptions (parameters).

### Day 1 (Monday): Fishing game
*Student:* Play a 'tragedy of the commons' fishing game with crackers (fish reproduce each round) and record the population collapse.

*How to teach:* Fishing game: 4 players fish from a shared pool; remaining fish double each round (up to a limit). Record collapse.

- **Q:** What is the tragedy of the commons?
  **A:** When individuals acting in self-interest overuse a shared resource, depleting it for everyone.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Build the model
*Student:* Build a spreadsheet model of a fish population with growth rate, carrying capacity, and harvest.

*How to teach:* Build the logistic spreadsheet with r, K, harvest H.

- **Q:** With r = 0.5, K = 1,000, and N = 500, what is growth before harvest?
  **A:** 0.5 × 500 × (1 − 0.5) = 125 fish per year.
- **Q:** What is the maximum sustainable yield for r = 0.5, K = 1,000?
  **A:** rK/4 = 125 fish per year, at N = K/2 = 500.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Test strategies
*Student:* Test three harvest policies (fixed quota, fixed percentage, no-take zones) and graph population over 50 years.

*How to teach:* Test three policies (fixed quota, fixed percentage, no-take zones) over 50 years; graph.

- **Q:** Why is a fixed-percentage harvest more stable than a fixed quota?
  **A:** Harvest automatically falls as the population falls, reducing pressure when the stock is low.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Biodiversity solution
*Student:* Revise the simulation to add a habitat corridor or marine protected area and measure its effect.

*How to teach:* Add a marine protected area (portion of the population not harvested, which spills over) and measure effect.

- **Q:** How do marine protected areas help fisheries?
  **A:** Protected populations grow larger and older, produce more young, and spill over into fished areas.

### Day 5 (Friday): Report
*Student:* Write a short report recommending a management strategy with simulation graphs as evidence.

*How to teach:* Report recommending a strategy with graphs.

- **Q:** What is a parameter in a model?
  **A:** A fixed input value (like growth rate or carrying capacity) that sets the model's behavior.

**Assessment:** Simulation and recommendation report.

**Answer key:** Simulation and recommendation report rubric. 4 – Correct logistic spreadsheet, three policies compared over 50 years with graphs, MPA added, sensitivity to r or K noted, recommendation justified with data and limitations. 3 (proficient) – Working model, graphs, justified recommendation. 2 – Model errors or no comparison. 1 – Incomplete.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Demand

**Prepare:** Graph paper; have grocery receipts handy. Bookmark the EIA California gasoline price series.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing a change in quantity demanded (movement along the curve, caused by price) with a change in demand (shift, caused by other factors).
- Mixing up substitutes and complements. Substitutes: price of one rises, demand for the other rises. Complements: price rises, demand for the other falls.
- Thinking elastic means 'stretches a lot in price.' Elasticity measures how much quantity responds to price.

### Day 1 (Monday): Law of demand
*Student:* Survey 10 people on how many coffees they'd buy at different prices and graph a market demand curve.

*How to teach:* Survey 10 people at prices $1–$6. The student graphs the market demand curve by summing quantities. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** State the law of demand.
  **A:** Other things equal, as price rises, quantity demanded falls.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Shifts
*Student:* Graph demand shifts caused by income, tastes, substitutes, complements, and expectations for 8 scenarios.

*How to teach:* Model the five shifters. The student graphs 8 scenarios. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** The price of peanut butter rises. What happens to demand for jelly?
  **A:** Decreases (shifts left) — complements.
- **Q:** Coffee prices rise. Demand for tea?
  **A:** Increases (shifts right) — substitutes.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Elasticity
*Student:* Calculate price elasticity for three goods and classify each as elastic or inelastic.

*How to teach:* Teach the midpoint formula. The student classifies three goods. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Price rises $2 → $3; quantity falls 100 → 60. Elasticity?
  **A:** %ΔQ = 40/80 = 50%; %ΔP = 1/2.5 = 40%; E = 1.25, elastic.
- **Q:** Gas $4 → $5, quantity 50 → 48 gallons/month. Elasticity?
  **A:** %ΔQ ≈ 4.1%, %ΔP ≈ 22.2%; E ≈ 0.18, inelastic.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Real data
*Student:* Analyze how gasoline demand responded to price spikes using EIA California gas price data.

*How to teach:* Analyze EIA price data. Discuss why gasoline demand is inelastic short-run. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Why is short-run gas demand inelastic?
  **A:** Few substitutes; people need to commute and can't quickly change cars or homes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a demand quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the demand quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** If a good is elastic, what happens to total revenue when price rises?
  **A:** Revenue falls.

**Assessment:** Demand quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Law of demand: price up → quantity demanded down. 2) Shifters: income, tastes, prices of related goods, expectations, number of buyers. 3) Peanut butter price up → jelly demand down (complements); coffee price up → tea demand up (substitutes). 4) $2→$3, 100→60: E = 1.25, elastic. 5) Gas $4→$5, 50→48: E ≈ 0.18, inelastic. 6) For elastic goods a price increase lowers total revenue; for inelastic goods it raises revenue. Proficient: 5 of 6.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Investigation: piece 8

**Prepare:** A material new to the student (beeswax with supervision, thread/embroidery, air-dry clay, coffee or tea staining). SAFETY: heat encaustic wax only with an adult, in a ventilated area, below smoking point.

**Watch for:**
- Expecting a new material to work on the first try. Test first.
- Using unusual media as a gimmick. Choose materials whose qualities support the idea.
- Not recording experiments.

### Day 1 (Monday): Material tests
*Student:* Test a new material in five sketchbook experiments.

*How to teach:* Five sketchbook experiments with the new material; record results.

- **Q:** Why test a new material before the final piece?
  **A:** To learn its behavior (drying time, layering, durability) and avoid ruining the final work.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Piece 8
*Student:* Begin piece 8 using the new material.

*How to teach:* Begin piece 8 with the new material.

- **Q:** Give an example of a material carrying meaning.
  **A:** Thread and stitching can suggest care, repair, or domestic labor.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Continue
*Student:* Continue piece 8.

*How to teach:* Continue piece 8.

- **Q:** What is encaustic?
  **A:** Painting with heated, pigmented beeswax.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Finish
*Student:* Finish piece 8.

*How to teach:* Finish piece 8.

- **Q:** What makes media 'unconventional'?
  **A:** Materials not traditionally used in fine art (coffee, thread, wax, found objects).

### Day 5 (Friday): Document
*Student:* Photograph and caption piece 8.

*How to teach:* Photograph and caption piece 8.

- **Q:** How do you photograph textured work?
  **A:** Use raking (low-angle) side light to reveal texture, plus an evenly lit straight-on shot.

**Assessment:** Piece 8.

**Answer key:** Piece 8 rubric. 4 – Five documented experiments, completed piece where the new material deepens the inquiry, skilled handling, clear caption. 3 (proficient) – Experiments and a completed piece connected to inquiry. 2 – Few experiments or weak connection. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Tennis fundamentals

**Prepare:** Tennis racket and balls, public court or a smooth wall. Review tennis scoring.

**Watch for:**
- Arm-only swings. Rotate shoulders and hips; use the kinetic chain.
- Late contact. Contact the ball in front of the body.
- Serving with a frying-pan grip. Use a continental grip as skill develops.

### Day 1 (Monday): Forehand
*Student:* Practice forehand grip and swing; hit 50 balls against a wall.

*How to teach:* Eastern or semi-western forehand grip; turn, swing low to high, contact in front, follow through. 50 balls against a wall.

- **Q:** How does a low-to-high swing affect the ball?
  **A:** It creates topspin, which makes the ball dip and bounce higher.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Backhand
*Student:* Practice one- or two-handed backhand against a wall.

*How to teach:* Backhand (one- or two-handed) against the wall.

- **Q:** Give an advantage of a two-handed backhand.
  **A:** More stability and power for beginners, easier on high balls.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Serve
*Student:* Practice the serve motion using kinetic chain cues.

*How to teach:* Serve motion with kinetic chain cues: toss, trophy position, legs drive, trunk rotates, arm reaches up and through.

- **Q:** Describe the kinetic chain in a serve.
  **A:** Force flows from legs to hips to trunk to shoulder to elbow to wrist to racket in sequence.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rally
*Student:* Rally with a partner, counting consecutive shots.

*How to teach:* Rally with a partner, counting consecutive shots.

- **Q:** What is the baseline?
  **A:** The back boundary line of the court.

### Day 5 (Friday): Scoring
*Student:* Learn tennis scoring and play mini-tennis games.

*How to teach:* Teach scoring (love, 15, 30, 40, deuce, advantage; 6 games a set, win by 2; tiebreak at 6-6) and play mini-tennis.

- **Q:** Score is 30-40, server wins the point. New score?
  **A:** Deuce.

**Assessment:** Rally count and serve test.

**Answer key:** Rally count and serve test: proficient = cooperative rally of 10+ shots and 6/10 serves in the correct box. 4 = 20+ rally and 8/10 serves; 2 = 5–9 rally, 3–5 serves; 1 = fewer. Scoring check: love = 0; deuce = 40-40; advantage then game.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Homeschool applicants: a–g, testing & records

**Prepare:** Read the UC 'Homeschooled students' admissions page and the CSU homeschool guidance (check the current wording; requirements change, and UC no longer uses SAT/ACT scores in admission). Gather the student's records: courses, texts used, grades, AP/test scores, college classes.

**Watch for:**
- Assuming homeschool courses automatically count as a–g. UC/CSU have specific ways for homeschoolers to show a–g (e.g. UC-approved online courses, AP exams, transferable college courses); check the current options on each system's page.
- Vague course descriptions. Admissions readers need texts, topics, labs, assessments and how grades were determined.
- Inflated or inconsistent grades. The transcript, descriptions and test scores should tell a consistent story.

### Day 1 (Monday): UC and homeschoolers
*Student:* Read the UC admissions page for homeschooled students and summarize how a–g can be met (AP exams, college courses, a–g approved online courses, admission by examination).

*How to teach:* Read UC's homeschool page and summarize how a–g can be met.

- **Q:** Name two ways a homeschooled student can demonstrate a–g subject work for UC.
  **A:** Examples: courses from UC-approved a–g online providers, qualifying AP exam scores, and UC-transferable community college courses (check UC's current page for all options).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): CSU and homeschoolers
*Student:* Read CSU's homeschool guidance and list required documents and how to report courses in Cal State Apply.

*How to teach:* Read CSU's homeschool guidance; list required documents and how to report courses in Cal State Apply.

- **Q:** What does Cal State Apply ask applicants to enter?
  **A:** A self-reported academic record of courses and grades, plus test scores (if any), activities and personal information.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Course list
*Student:* Build a four-year course list from your homeschool records with a–g letter, grade, and semester credits for each course.

*How to teach:* Build the four-year course list with a–g letters, grades and credits.

- **Q:** List the a–g subject areas and minimum years.
  **A:** a) history 2, b) English 4, c) math 3, d) lab science 2, e) language other than English 2, f) visual and performing arts 1, g) college-prep elective 1.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Course descriptions
*Student:* Write course descriptions (texts, labs, assessments) for three courses that will appear in the counselor packet.

*How to teach:* Write three course descriptions for the counselor packet.

- **Q:** What should a course description include?
  **A:** Course title, level, curriculum/texts, topics covered, labs or projects, assessments and how the grade was determined.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Parent reviews the draft course list and three descriptions for accuracy.

*How to teach:* Parent reviews the list and descriptions for accuracy.

- **Q:** Why must the course list match the transcript exactly?
  **A:** Discrepancies between self-reported records and the final transcript can cause admission offers to be withdrawn.

**Assessment:** Draft four-year course list and course descriptions.

**Answer key:** Draft four-year course list and descriptions rubric: 4 = every course listed by year with subject, a–g letter where applicable, grade, and semester credits (10 per year-long course); at least the a–g minimums (2 years history, 4 English, 3 math, 2 lab science, 2 language other than English, 1 visual/performing arts, 1 elective); three course descriptions each naming the curriculum and texts, major topics, labs/projects, assessments and grading method; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = missing a–g mapping or descriptions thin; 1 = incomplete.
