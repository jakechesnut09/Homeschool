# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 27

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Thesis, outline, and primary research

**Prepare:** Create a synthesis-matrix template (subtopics × sources). Prepare a consent script for interviews or surveys.

**Watch for:**
- Outlining source by source. Organize by subtopic so each section synthesizes several sources.
- A thesis that is too broad or merely factual. Test it: could a reasonable person disagree? Is it specific about who, what, and why?
- Leading survey questions. Rewrite each question neutrally and pilot it with one person first.

### Day 1 (Monday): Synthesis matrix
*Student:* Build a synthesis matrix mapping each subtopic to what each source says; identify a gap or debate.

*How to teach:* Model a synthesis matrix. The student fills it in and identifies a gap. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does a synthesis matrix reveal?
  **A:** Where sources agree, disagree, or are silent.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Thesis
*Student:* Draft three versions of a thesis and choose the most specific, arguable one.

*How to teach:* Draft three thesis versions and choose the most specific. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Which is stronger: 'Phones affect learning' or 'California high schools should restrict phones during class because research links them to lower attention'?
  **A:** The second — it is specific and arguable.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Primary research
*Student:* Conduct a short interview or survey (5+ responses) or analyze a primary document related to your question; record results.

*How to teach:* The student conducts an interview or survey (5+ responses). Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Why is primary research valuable?
  **A:** It adds original evidence and local perspective.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Outline
*Student:* Write a full sentence outline with topic sentences, evidence assignments, and a counterargument section.

*How to teach:* The student writes a full-sentence outline including counterargument. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What goes in a counterargument section?
  **A:** The strongest opposing view and a rebuttal.

### Day 5 (Friday): Outline check
*Student:* Submit outline; meet to confirm every section has at least two sources.

*How to teach:* Meet to confirm each section has at least two sources. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Why two sources per section?
  **A:** It shows synthesis rather than relying on one voice.

**Assessment:** Thesis and outline checkpoint.

**Answer key:** Checkpoint rubric (4 levels): 4 = specific arguable thesis; full-sentence outline; each section with 2+ sources; counterargument; primary research recorded.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Conditional probability and independence

**Prepare:** Bring back the Week 24 two-way table (example used below: grades 9–10 morning 8, evening 12; grades 11–12 morning 4, evening 16; total 40). Deck of cards. Preview Intro Stats 3.2–3.4.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A). Underline the 'given' condition — that group's total is the denominator.
- Confusing independent with mutually exclusive. Mutually exclusive events (with positive probabilities) are always dependent: if one happens the other can't.
- Ignoring base rates in medical testing. Translate to counts in 10,000 people.

### Day 1 (Monday): Conditional probability
*Student:* Compute P(A|B) from two-way tables and Venn diagrams; explain in everyday language.

*How to teach:* Say: 'Given B means we shrink the world to just B.' Compute P(A|B) = P(A and B)/P(B) from the table and from a Venn diagram, then have the student say each result in an everyday sentence.

- **Q:** Find P(11–12 | evening).
  **A:** 16/28 ≈ 0.571: of students who prefer evening, about 57% are 11th–12th graders.
- **Q:** Find P(evening | 11–12).
  **A:** 16/20 = 0.80.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Independence
*Student:* Test whether events are independent using P(A and B) = P(A)P(B) and P(A|B) = P(A) with your survey data.

*How to teach:* Model both tests for independence: P(A and B) = P(A)P(B), or P(A|B) = P(A). The student applies them to their own survey data and states a conclusion.

- **Q:** Are 'morning' and 'grade 9–10' independent in the example?
  **A:** P(morning) = 12/40 = 0.30 but P(morning | 9–10) = 0.40. Not equal, so not independent.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tree diagrams
*Student:* Use tree diagrams and the general multiplication rule for drawing cards without replacement.

*How to teach:* Draw a tree for drawing two cards without replacement, writing conditional probabilities on the second branches. Multiply along branches.

- **Q:** P(two aces) drawing without replacement?
  **A:** (4/52)(3/51) = 12/2652 = 1/221 ≈ 0.0045.
- **Q:** P(two aces) with replacement?
  **A:** (4/52)² = 1/169 ≈ 0.0059.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Medical testing
*Student:* Compute the probability of disease given a positive test (false positives) using a tree and a table; discuss why base rates matter.

*How to teach:* Present: a disease affects 1% of people; the test catches 90% of cases but gives 9% false positives. Build a tree and a table for 10,000 people and find P(disease | positive). Discuss why base rates matter.

- **Q:** Compute P(disease | positive).
  **A:** 100 sick → 90 test positive; 9900 healthy → 891 false positives. 90/(90 + 891) = 90/981 ≈ 0.092, about 9%.
- **Q:** Why is the answer so low?
  **A:** The disease is rare, so false positives from the large healthy group outnumber true positives.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a conditional probability quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz below. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** A and B are independent, P(A) = 0.4, P(B) = 0.5. Find P(A and B).
  **A:** 0.20

**Assessment:** Conditional probability quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) P(11–12 | evening) = 16/28 ≈ 0.571. 2) Not independent: P(morning | 9–10) = 0.40 ≠ P(morning) = 0.30. 3) Two aces without replacement = 1/221. 4) Medical test P(disease | +) ≈ 0.092. 5) Independent P(A and B) = 0.20. 6) Short answer: a positive result for a rare disease is often false because false positives from the large healthy group outnumber true positives. Proficient: 5 of 6.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Climate models and forecasts

**Prepare:** Cal-Adapt (cal-adapt.org) and En-ROADS (en-roads.climateinteractive.org). Read a short explainer on how climate models work (NASA or NOAA). IPCC AR6 scenario summary: by 2081–2100, about 1.8 °C (very low emissions, SSP1-2.6) to about 4.4 °C (very high, SSP5-8.5) warming above 1850–1900.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking projections are predictions of exact weather. They project ranges of long-term climate under assumed emission scenarios.
- Believing uncertainty means models are unreliable. Models successfully hindcast past climate; uncertainty is quantified in ranges.
- Assuming one policy solves everything. En-ROADS shows many combined actions are needed.

### Day 1 (Monday): How models work
*Student:* Read how climate models divide Earth into grid cells and test them by hindcasting the past.

*How to teach:* Read how models divide Earth into 3-D grid cells and solve physics equations; tested by hindcasting.

- **Q:** What is hindcasting?
  **A:** Running a model for past periods and comparing results with observed data to test its accuracy.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Scenarios
*Student:* Compare low and high emission scenario projections for 2100 temperature and sea level.

*How to teach:* Compare low vs. high scenario projections for 2100 temperature and sea level.

- **Q:** Approximately how much global sea-level rise is projected by 2100 under low vs. very high emissions (IPCC AR6, likely range)?
  **A:** Low (SSP1-2.6): about 0.3–0.6 m; very high (SSP5-8.5): about 0.6–1.0 m (higher if ice sheets respond faster).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cal-Adapt
*Student:* Use Cal-Adapt to project extreme heat days and snowpack for your county under two scenarios.

*How to teach:* Use Cal-Adapt for extreme heat days and snowpack for your county under two scenarios.

- **Q:** Why do projections show more extreme heat days in inland California?
  **A:** Warmer average temperatures shift the whole distribution, so days above the historical extreme threshold become much more frequent.

### Day 4 (Thursday): En-ROADS
*Student:* Use the En-ROADS climate simulator to test policies and record which changes most reduce warming.

*How to teach:* En-ROADS: test policies; record the most effective changes.

- **Q:** In En-ROADS, which kinds of actions typically have large effects?
  **A:** Carbon pricing, rapid shift from coal and fossil fuels to renewables, electrification, and reducing methane and other gases; single actions alone rarely reach 1.5–2 °C.

### Day 5 (Friday): Forecast
*Student:* Write an evidence-based forecast of climate impacts for your region in 2050 with uncertainty noted.

*How to teach:* Student writes a forecast of regional 2050 impacts with uncertainty.

- **Q:** What is an emission scenario?
  **A:** A plausible future pathway of greenhouse gas emissions based on assumptions about population, economy, technology, and policy.

**Assessment:** Regional climate forecast.

**Answer key:** Regional climate forecast rubric. 4 – Uses Cal-Adapt data for at least three variables (heat days, precipitation/snowpack, sea level or fire) under two scenarios with numbers, explains uncertainty (scenario, model, natural variability), and impacts on people and ecosystems. 3 (proficient) – Data-based forecast for two variables with uncertainty noted. 2 – General. 1 – Unsupported.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Labor markets and wages

**Prepare:** Bookmark the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook and the BLS chart 'Education pays.' Find California EDD long-term occupational projections for your region. Pick one current article on AI and work.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking wages are set by fairness or effort alone. Wages reflect the supply of and demand for particular skills.
- Assuming education guarantees high income. It raises median earnings and lowers unemployment on average.
- Believing automation eliminates jobs overall. It shifts demand among tasks and skills.

### Day 1 (Monday): Wages as prices
*Student:* Graph labor supply and demand for two occupations and explain wage differences.

*How to teach:* Graph labor supply and demand for two occupations (e.g., surgeons and cashiers). The student explains the wage difference.

- **Q:** Why do surgeons earn more than cashiers?
  **A:** High demand for scarce, highly trained skills; long training limits supply.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Human capital
*Student:* Use BLS data to graph median earnings and unemployment by education level.

*How to teach:* Graph BLS median weekly earnings and unemployment by education. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does the BLS 'Education pays' data show?
  **A:** Median earnings rise and unemployment falls with each level of education; bachelor's holders earn roughly 1.6–1.7 times high-school graduates' median weekly earnings.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Career research
*Student:* Research two careers in the Occupational Outlook Handbook and California EDD projections for your region.

*How to teach:* The student researches two careers: duties, education, median pay, outlook, regional projections. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What four facts should career research record?
  **A:** Duties, required education, median pay, and job outlook.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Technology and work
*Student:* Analyze how automation and AI affect demand for different skills; read one current article.

*How to teach:* Read the AI article; discuss which tasks are most and least exposed. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Which skills tend to be complements to automation?
  **A:** Complex problem-solving, interpersonal and creative skills, and skilled trades requiring physical dexterity in varied settings.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a labor market quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the labor market quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is human capital?
  **A:** Skills, knowledge, and experience that increase a worker's productivity.

**Assessment:** Career research chart and quiz.

**Answer key:** Career chart rubric (4 levels): 4 = two careers with duties, education, median pay, outlook, regional projection, sources. Quiz: labor supply/demand, human capital, education pays, automation.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Sculpture and assemblage

**Prepare:** Cardboard, wire, air-dry clay, found objects, hot glue (low-temp, supervise), paint. Images of Betye Saar's The Liberation of Aunt Jemima (1972) and Louise Nevelson's black wood wall assemblages.

**Watch for:**
- Building without an armature, so pieces sag or collapse.
- Designing only from the front. Sculpture in the round must work from all sides.
- Found objects chosen only for looks; their history adds meaning.

### Day 1 (Monday): Assemblage artists
*Student:* Study Betye Saar's assemblages and Louise Nevelson; note how found objects carry meaning.

*How to teach:* Study Saar (reclaimed a racist stereotype image, arming the Aunt Jemima figure to transform its meaning) and Nevelson (unified found wood scraps by painting them one color, usually black).

- **Q:** How does Betye Saar's The Liberation of Aunt Jemima transform its found image?
  **A:** She placed a racist mammy figure holding a broom alongside a rifle and pistol with a raised Black Power fist, turning a stereotype into a symbol of resistance.
- **Q:** Why did Louise Nevelson paint her assemblages a single color?
  **A:** To unify varied found wood pieces into one composition, emphasizing shape, shadow, and rhythm over the objects' original identities.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Maquette
*Student:* Build a small cardboard or clay maquette.

*How to teach:* Build a small maquette.

- **Q:** What is a maquette?
  **A:** A small preliminary model of a sculpture.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Build
*Student:* Build the sculpture with an armature or assemblage.

*How to teach:* Build the sculpture with armature or assemblage.

- **Q:** What is an armature?
  **A:** An internal support framework (wire, wood, foil) that holds the sculpture's form.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Finish
*Student:* Finish surfaces and paint or patina.

*How to teach:* Finish surfaces; paint or patina.

- **Q:** What does 'in the round' mean?
  **A:** Sculpture meant to be viewed from all sides.

### Day 5 (Friday): Photograph
*Student:* Photograph the sculpture from four angles.

*How to teach:* Photograph from four angles.

- **Q:** Why photograph sculpture from multiple angles?
  **A:** A single view can't show the full three-dimensional form.

**Assessment:** Sculpture or assemblage.

**Answer key:** Sculpture or assemblage rubric. 4 – Strong 3-D composition from all angles, stable construction, meaningful found objects or forms tied to inquiry, finished surfaces, four quality photos. 3 (proficient) – Stable, finished sculpture with clear intent and documentation. 2 – Unstable or unfinished. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Cycling for fitness and transport

**Prepare:** Bicycle, helmet, repair kit (tire levers, spare tube or patch kit, pump). Route-planning app with bike lanes. Parent rides along on new routes.

**Watch for:**
- Removing a tire with a screwdriver, which pinches tubes. Use tire levers.
- Not finding the cause of a flat. Check inside the tire for the thorn/glass before installing a new tube.
- Choosing the shortest route rather than the safest.

### Day 1 (Monday): Maintenance
*Student:* Learn the ABC check (air, brakes, chain) and fix a flat tire.

*How to teach:* ABC check (air, brakes, chain) and flat repair: release brake, remove wheel, lever off one side, remove tube, find the cause, insert slightly inflated new tube, reseat tire by hand, inflate to sidewall PSI.

- **Q:** Why should you inspect the inside of the tire after a flat?
  **A:** To find and remove the object (thorn, glass) that caused it, or the new tube will puncture too.
- **Q:** Where do you find the correct tire pressure?
  **A:** Printed on the tire's sidewall.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Route
*Student:* Plan a safe 10–15 mile route using bike lanes or trails.

*How to teach:* Plan a safe 10–15 mile route using bike lanes/trails.

- **Q:** Name two features of a safe bike route.
  **A:** Bike lanes or paths, low traffic speeds, fewer complex intersections, good visibility.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ride
*Student:* Complete the ride, logging time, distance, and heart rate.

*How to teach:* Complete the ride; log time, distance, HR.

- **Q:** You rode 12 miles in 60 minutes. Average speed?
  **A:** 12 mph.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Commute
*Student:* Plan a bike or walk route to a future job or campus and time it.

*How to teach:* Plan and time a route to a future job or campus.

- **Q:** Why plan an active commute?
  **A:** It builds daily activity into routine, saving money and time spent exercising separately.

### Day 5 (Friday): Strength
*Student:* Complete a full-body strength workout.

*How to teach:* Full-body strength workout.

- **Q:** Which muscles do cyclists rely on most?
  **A:** Quadriceps, glutes, hamstrings, and calves.

**Assessment:** Ride log and route plan.

**Answer key:** Ride log and route plan rubric. 4 – Correct ABC check and flat fix demonstrated; route map with distance, safety features, and alternatives; ride log with time, distance, speed, HR; commute plan with timing. 3 (proficient) – ABC, route, log, commute plan. 2 – Missing elements. 1 – Incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Scholarships

**Prepare:** Bookmark scholarship search sites (Fastweb, Bold.org, local community foundations, employers, churches, clubs) and the FTC's scholarship scam page. Set up a tracker (spreadsheet).

**Watch for:**
- Paying for scholarship searches. Legitimate scholarships don't charge fees; 'guaranteed' scholarships are scams.
- Skipping small local scholarships. Smaller, local awards have fewer applicants and better odds.
- Not reporting outside scholarships. Colleges must be told; award displacement may reduce other aid (ask whether loans or grants are reduced first).

### Day 1 (Monday): Finding scholarships
*Student:* Search local, community, employer, and online scholarships and list 10 with deadlines and requirements.

*How to teach:* Search local, community, employer and online scholarships; list 10 with deadlines and requirements.

- **Q:** Why prioritize local scholarships?
  **A:** Fewer applicants compete, so odds are better.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Scam spotting
*Student:* Use FTC guidance to identify red flags of scholarship scams (fees, guaranteed winnings).

*How to teach:* Identify red flags of scholarship scams using FTC guidance.

- **Q:** Name two red flags of a scholarship scam.
  **A:** Requiring a fee, guaranteeing you'll win, or asking for bank or credit card information.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Essay draft
*Student:* Draft a 500-word scholarship essay that can be adapted to multiple prompts.

*How to teach:* Draft a 500-word essay adaptable to multiple prompts.

- **Q:** How can one essay be reused for different prompts?
  **A:** Keep a strong core story and rewrite the opening and conclusion to answer each prompt directly.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Applications
*Student:* Complete and submit (or fully prepare) two scholarship applications.

*How to teach:* Complete and submit (or fully prepare) two applications.

- **Q:** What is award displacement?
  **A:** When a college reduces other financial aid because you received an outside scholarship.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Revise the essay with parent feedback and log applications in a tracker.

*How to teach:* Revise with parent feedback and log applications in the tracker.

- **Q:** What should the tracker include?
  **A:** Name, amount, deadline, requirements, submission date and status.

**Assessment:** Two completed scholarship applications.

**Answer key:** Scholarship applications rubric: 4 = two complete applications (or fully prepared) meeting all requirements; essay adapted to each prompt with a specific story and clear connection to goals; recommendation and transcript requests sent on time; tracker with 10 scholarships, deadlines, requirements and status; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = only one application or generic essay; 1 = incomplete. Scam quiz: red flags = application or 'processing' fees, guaranteed winnings, requests for bank/credit card numbers, 'you've won' a scholarship you never applied for, high-pressure deadlines.
