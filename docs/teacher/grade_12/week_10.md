# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 10

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Macbeth Acts IV–V – downfall

**Prepare:** Read Acts IV–V. Choose two filmed productions and identify the same scene (e.g., V.1 or V.5) in each.

**Watch for:**
- Taking the apparitions literally like Macbeth. Each prophecy is technically true but misleading.
- Thinking Lady Macbeth was always strong. Track her collapse in V.1.
- Comparing films only on 'which is better.' Compare specific choices.

### Day 1 (Monday): The apparitions
*Student:* Read IV.1. Explain how each apparition equivocates and how Macbeth's misreading drives his overconfidence.

*How to teach:* Read IV.1. The student explains each apparition. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What do the three apparitions say?
  **A:** An armed head: beware Macduff. A bloody child: none of woman born shall harm Macbeth. A crowned child with a tree: Macbeth is safe until Birnam Wood comes to Dunsinane.
- **Q:** How do two of these come true?
  **A:** Malcolm's soldiers carry Birnam Wood branches as camouflage; Macduff was 'untimely ripped' from his mother's womb (born by Caesarean).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Macduff's grief
*Student:* Read IV.2–IV.3. Compare Macduff's grief with Macbeth's response to loss; discuss what the play defines as true manhood.

*How to teach:* Read IV.2–IV.3. Compare responses to loss. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does Macduff say when told to 'dispute it like a man'?
  **A:** 'I shall do so; / But I must also feel it as a man.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sleepwalking
*Student:* Read V.1. Analyze Lady Macbeth's prose speech and how her guilt reverses Act II's 'a little water clears us of this deed.'

*How to teach:* Read V.1. Analyze prose and reversal of 'a little water clears us.' Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does Lady Macbeth say about her hand?
  **A:** 'Out, damned spot!' and 'All the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Tomorrow and tomorrow
*Student:* Read V.2–V.8. Perform and analyze the 'Tomorrow, and tomorrow' soliloquy as Macbeth's final worldview.

*How to teach:* Read V.2–V.8. The student performs and analyzes 'Tomorrow.' Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What view of life does Macbeth express?
  **A:** Life is meaningless — 'a tale / Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, / Signifying nothing.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Compare productions
*Student:* Watch the same scene in two filmed versions and complete a comparison chart on setting, casting, cuts, and tone; discuss which is more faithful.

*How to teach:* Watch the same scene in two versions and fill in the chart. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Name one dimension for comparing productions.
  **A:** Setting, casting, cuts, or tone.

**Assessment:** Production comparison chart and discussion (RL.11-12.7).

**Answer key:** Production chart rubric (4 levels): 4 = chart compares setting, casting, cuts, and tone with specific details from both versions, and a claim about faithfulness supported by the text; 3 = all categories with general notes; 2 = only one production detailed; 1 = incomplete.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Radian measure and the unit circle

**Prepare:** Paper plate, string, scissors, protractor, marker. Preview OpenStax 7.1–7.3. Print a blank unit circle for daily practice.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a radian is a fixed number of degrees 'like 1°.' One radian ≈ 57.3°; it is the angle whose arc equals the radius.
- Using degrees in s = rθ. The formula only works with θ in radians.
- Sign errors outside Quadrant I. Use 'All Students Take Calculus': QI all positive, QII sine, QIII tangent, QIV cosine.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is a radian?
*Student:* Wrap string the length of a paper plate's radius around its edge to see one radian; convert 15 angles between degrees and radians.

*How to teach:* Cut string equal to the radius and wrap it along the plate's edge, marking off radians (about 6.28 fit). Teach degrees × π/180 = radians. The student converts 15 angles.

- **Q:** Convert 150° to radians and 7π/4 to degrees.
  **A:** 150° = 5π/6; 7π/4 = 315°.
- **Q:** About how many degrees is 1 radian?
  **A:** 180/π ≈ 57.3°.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Arc length & sector area
*Student:* Solve arc-length and sector-area problems (s = rθ, A = ½r²θ), including a bicycle-wheel application.

*How to teach:* Model s = rθ and A = ½r²θ. Then a bicycle wheel: distance per rotation equals circumference. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** r = 10 cm, θ = π/3. Find arc length and sector area.
  **A:** s = 10π/3 ≈ 10.47 cm; A = ½(100)(π/3) = 50π/3 ≈ 52.36 cm².
- **Q:** A 26-inch-diameter bike wheel turns once. How far does the bike travel?
  **A:** 26π ≈ 81.7 inches.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Building the unit circle
*Student:* Use 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles to build the unit circle coordinates for all special angles.

*How to teach:* Draw 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles with hypotenuse 1 to get (√3/2, 1/2) and (√2/2, √2/2), then reflect into all quadrants. The student builds a full unit circle.

- **Q:** Give the coordinates for π/3 and 3π/4 on the unit circle.
  **A:** π/3: (1/2, √3/2). 3π/4: (−√2/2, √2/2).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Reference angles
*Student:* Evaluate sine, cosine, and tangent of angles in all quadrants using reference angles and the unit circle.

*How to teach:* Model finding the reference angle and sign for 4π/3 and 7π/4. The student evaluates sin, cos, tan in all quadrants. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Evaluate sin(5π/6), cos(4π/3), tan(7π/4).
  **A:** 1/2, −1/2, −1.
- **Q:** Evaluate sin 225°.
  **A:** −√2/2 (reference angle 45°, Quadrant III).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit circle quiz
*Student:* Fill in a blank unit circle from memory in under 5 minutes and take an evaluation quiz.

*How to teach:* Time the student filling a blank unit circle (goal: under 5 minutes), then give the evaluation quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Evaluate cos(π/2) and tan(π/2).
  **A:** cos(π/2) = 0; tan(π/2) is undefined.

**Assessment:** Timed unit circle and evaluation quiz.

**Answer key:** Timed unit circle: all 16 special angles (0, π/6, π/4, π/3, π/2 … 11π/6) with correct coordinates in under 5 minutes = mastery. Evaluation quiz: 1) 150° = 5π/6. 2) 7π/4 = 315°. 3) Arc length r = 10, θ = π/3: 10π/3. 4) sin(5π/6) = 1/2. 5) cos(4π/3) = −1/2. 6) tan(7π/4) = −1. 7) sin 225° = −√2/2. 8) sec(π/3) = 2. Proficient: 7 of 8.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Water as a geologic agent

**Prepare:** Two identical containers, water, sand, lamp, 2 thermometers; chalk, white vinegar, kitchen scale (0.1 g if possible); plaster of Paris and a straw (to mold a hole), freezer; baking pan, sand, watering can, books for slope. Prepare a lab report template.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking weathering and erosion are the same. Weathering breaks rock down in place; erosion moves the pieces.
- Believing ice shrinks. Water expands about 9% when it freezes, which wedges cracks open.
- Changing multiple variables in the stream table at once.

### Day 1 (Monday): Properties of water
*Student:* Test water's high heat capacity by heating equal masses of water and sand under a lamp and recording temperature every 2 minutes.

*How to teach:* Heat equal masses of water and sand under a lamp, recording temperature every 2 minutes for 20 minutes. Graph.

- **Q:** Why does the sand warm faster than the water?
  **A:** Water's specific heat (4.18 J/g·°C) is much higher than sand's (~0.8 J/g·°C), so water needs more energy to change temperature.
- **Q:** How does water's high heat capacity affect coastal climates?
  **A:** Oceans warm and cool slowly, giving coastal areas milder temperatures than inland areas.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Chemical weathering
*Student:* Place chalk in water vs. vinegar and measure mass loss over 24 hours to model acid weathering of limestone.

*How to teach:* Weigh chalk pieces; place one in water and one in vinegar for 24 hours; dry and reweigh; calculate percent mass loss.

- **Q:** Chalk lost 3.2 g of 8.0 g in vinegar. Percent loss?
  **A:** 40%.
- **Q:** Write the reaction responsible.
  **A:** CaCO₃ + 2CH₃COOH → Ca(CH₃COO)₂ + H₂O + CO₂ — acid dissolves calcium carbonate, as carbonic acid in rain dissolves limestone.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Frost wedging
*Student:* Fill a small plaster block's drilled hole with water, freeze/thaw it several cycles, and record cracking.

*How to teach:* Fill the plaster block's hole with water; freeze/thaw for several cycles (one per day over the week, continuing the observations), recording cracks.

- **Q:** Explain frost wedging.
  **A:** Water in cracks freezes and expands ~9%, widening the cracks; repeated freeze-thaw cycles break rock apart.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Stream table
*Student:* Build a sand stream table in a baking pan; vary slope and flow rate and measure erosion and deposition.

*How to teach:* Stream table: vary slope (1 vs. 3 books) at constant flow; measure distance of erosion and deposition (delta) size.

- **Q:** Predict how increasing slope affects erosion.
  **A:** Steeper slope → faster water → more erosion and sediment carried farther.

### Day 5 (Friday): Lab report
*Student:* Write a full lab report on one investigation with variables, data table, graph, and conclusion.

*How to teach:* Student writes a full lab report on one investigation.

- **Q:** In the chalk experiment, name the independent, dependent, and controlled variables.
  **A:** Independent: liquid (water vs. vinegar). Dependent: mass lost. Controlled: chalk size/type, volume of liquid, time, temperature.

**Assessment:** Water-investigation lab report (HS-ESS2-5).

**Answer key:** Lab report rubric (HS-ESS2-5). 4 – Testable question, hypothesis, variables identified, procedure, data table with units and trials, appropriate graph, conclusion that connects results to water's properties and Earth processes, error analysis. 3 (proficient) – All sections with correct variables, graph, and evidence-based conclusion. 2 – Missing sections or weak conclusion. 1 – Incomplete.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Civil rights and civil liberties cases

**Prepare:** Print excerpts of Plessy (with Harlan's dissent), Brown, Tinker, Miranda, Gideon, Bakke. Find a summary of Prop 209 (1996). Prepare moot court roles and Unit 4 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Brown ended segregation immediately. Implementation was slow ('all deliberate speed,' Brown II 1955) and met massive resistance.
- Thinking students have no free-speech rights at school or unlimited rights. Tinker protects non-disruptive expression; later cases allow limits.
- Confusing civil rights (equal treatment) with civil liberties (freedoms from government).

### Day 1 (Monday): Plessy to Brown
*Student:* Compare excerpts from Plessy v. Ferguson (including Harlan's dissent) and Brown v. Board; explain the reversal.

*How to teach:* Compare Plessy and Brown, including Harlan's dissent. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What did Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) hold?
  **A:** 'Separate but equal' segregation laws were constitutional.
- **Q:** What did Brown (1954) hold and why?
  **A:** Unanimously, that 'separate educational facilities are inherently unequal,' violating equal protection; it cited harm to Black children's sense of status.
- **Q:** What did Harlan write?
  **A:** 'Our Constitution is color-blind.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Student rights
*Student:* Analyze Tinker v. Des Moines and a later student speech case; discuss limits on student speech.

*How to teach:* Analyze Tinker and a later case (e.g., Morse v. Frederick or Mahanoy v. B.L.). Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is the Tinker test?
  **A:** Schools may restrict student speech only if it causes or forecasts substantial disruption or invades others' rights.
- **Q:** What did Mahanoy v. B.L. (2021) decide?
  **A:** A school violated a cheerleader's free speech by punishing her off-campus Snapchat post.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rights of the accused
*Student:* Analyze Miranda v. Arizona and Gideon v. Wainwright and their effect on policing and courts.

*How to teach:* Analyze Miranda and Gideon. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does Miranda require?
  **A:** Police must warn suspects in custody of the right to remain silent and to an attorney before interrogation.
- **Q:** What did Gideon v. Wainwright (1963) hold?
  **A:** States must provide lawyers to defendants in felony cases who can't afford one.

### Day 4 (Thursday): California cases
*Student:* Analyze Regents of UC v. Bakke and California's Proposition 209 and how admissions policy changed.

*How to teach:* Analyze Bakke and Prop 209. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What did Bakke (1978) decide?
  **A:** Racial quotas in UC Davis medical school admissions were unconstitutional, but race could be one factor.
- **Q:** What did Prop 209 do?
  **A:** Banned race and sex preferences in California public education, employment, and contracting (1996).

### Day 5 (Friday): Moot court
*Student:* Prepare and argue one side of a landmark case for 5 minutes before a family 'bench'; take the Unit 4 test.

*How to teach:* The student argues one side for 5 minutes; take the Unit 4 test. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What makes a strong moot court argument?
  **A:** Clear legal question, precedent, and answers to judges' questions.

**Assessment:** Moot court argument and Unit 4 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 4 test key: 1) Plessy (1896) upheld 'separate but equal'; Harlan: 'Our Constitution is color-blind.' 2) Brown (1954): segregated schools 'inherently unequal,' 14th Amendment equal protection. 3) Tinker (1969): substantial-disruption test. 4) Mahanoy (2021) protected off-campus speech. 5) Miranda (1966): warnings before custodial interrogation. 6) Gideon (1963): right to appointed counsel in state felony cases. 7) Bakke (1978): quotas unconstitutional, race may be one factor. 8) Prop 209 (1996) banned preferences in CA public institutions. Moot court rubric (4 = exemplary … 1 = beginning): 4 = states the legal question, cites the Constitution and 2+ precedents accurately, answers 'bench' questions directly, clear 5-minute delivery.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Plein air and landscape

**Prepare:** Portable kit (small panels, limited palette, water, rags, a viewfinder), sun hat and water. Images of Granville Redmond's poppy fields and Guy Rose's coastal paintings (e.g., from the Irvine Museum or Laguna Art Museum) and works by Monet.

**Watch for:**
- Trying to paint every detail outdoors. Plein air studies capture light and big shapes quickly before light changes.
- Thinking California Impressionism simply copied the French. California painters adapted Impressionism to the intense light, landscapes, and color of California.
- Interpretations without evidence from the painting.

### Day 1 (Monday): California Impressionists
*Student:* Study California plein air painters (e.g., Granville Redmond, Guy Rose) and compare to French Impressionists.

*How to teach:* Compare California Impressionists (Redmond, a deaf painter known for poppy-covered hillsides; Rose, who lived near Monet at Giverny) with French Impressionists (Monet, Pissarro).

- **Q:** What is plein air painting?
  **A:** Painting outdoors directly from the subject, capturing natural light and atmosphere.
- **Q:** How did California Impressionism differ from French Impressionism?
  **A:** It focused on California's landscapes — golden hills, poppies, coast — with brighter, high-key light, and often painted wilder, less urban scenes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Morning study
*Student:* Paint a 1-hour plein air study in morning light.

*How to teach:* 1-hour morning study: thumbnail first, then big shapes and light.

- **Q:** Why work quickly in plein air?
  **A:** Light and shadows change as the sun moves, so the effect lasts only about 1–2 hours.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Afternoon study
*Student:* Paint the same scene in afternoon light and compare.

*How to teach:* Paint the same scene in afternoon light; compare color and shadows.

- **Q:** How does morning light differ from afternoon light?
  **A:** Morning light is often cooler and clearer; late afternoon light is warmer and more golden, with long shadows from the opposite direction.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Studio painting
*Student:* Use your studies to begin a larger studio landscape.

*How to teach:* Begin a larger studio landscape from the studies.

- **Q:** How should plein air studies be used in the studio?
  **A:** As references for true color and light, combined with photos for detail and a refined composition.

### Day 5 (Friday): Interpretation
*Student:* Write two different interpretations of a California landscape painting and defend one.

*How to teach:* Write two interpretations of a California landscape painting; defend one with visual evidence.

- **Q:** What makes an interpretation defensible?
  **A:** It cites visible evidence (color, composition, subject, light) and context, rather than personal opinion alone.

**Assessment:** Plein air studies and interpretation.

**Answer key:** Plein air studies and interpretation rubric. 4 – Two completed studies capturing distinct light conditions with accurate value and color temperature; written interpretations supported by specific visual evidence and context; one defended convincingly. 3 (proficient) – Two studies and two supported interpretations. 2 – Studies lack light contrast or interpretations unsupported. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: 5K training: building base

**Prepare:** Running shoes and log (date, duration, distance, pace, HR, RPE). Choose a runner's yoga video for Day 4.

**Watch for:**
- Overstriding to go faster. Increase cadence with shorter steps.
- Starting the long run too fast. Keep it conversational.
- Skipping strength work. Single-leg strength helps prevent running injuries.

### Day 1 (Monday): Easy run
*Student:* Run 25–30 minutes easy and log pace and heart rate.

*How to teach:* 25–30 minutes easy; log pace and HR.

- **Q:** You ran 3 miles in 30:45. What is your pace?
  **A:** 10:15 per mile.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Strength
*Student:* Do a runner's strength circuit (single-leg squats, calf raises, glute bridges).

*How to teach:* Runner's strength circuit: single-leg (box/assisted) squats, calf raises, glute bridges, 3 rounds.

- **Q:** Why do runners need single-leg strength?
  **A:** Running is a series of single-leg landings; strength and stability reduce injury and improve efficiency.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fartlek
*Student:* Do a 30-minute fartlek run with 8 surges.

*How to teach:* 30-minute fartlek with 8 surges (20–60 s faster).

- **Q:** What does 'fartlek' mean?
  **A:** Swedish for 'speed play' — unstructured mixed-pace running.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mobility
*Student:* Complete a 30-minute yoga flow for runners.

*How to teach:* 30-minute yoga flow for runners (hips, hamstrings, calves).

- **Q:** Which muscles do runners commonly stretch?
  **A:** Calves, hamstrings, hip flexors, quadriceps, glutes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Long run
*Student:* Run or run/walk 35–40 minutes at easy pace.

*How to teach:* Long run 35–40 minutes easy; run/walk allowed.

- **Q:** What is a negative split?
  **A:** Running the second half faster than the first.

**Assessment:** Running log.

**Answer key:** Running log rubric. 4 – All 5 sessions logged with duration, distance, pace (correctly calculated), HR and RPE; easy runs in easy range; reflection on trends. 3 (proficient) – Complete log with correct paces. 2 – Missing data. 1 – Incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): How taxes work

**Prepare:** Print the current IRS federal bracket table and the CA FTB tax rate schedule (ftb.ca.gov). The worked examples below use the 2025 federal single-filer brackets on TAXABLE income: 10% up to $11,925; 12% $11,926–$48,475; 22% $48,476–$103,350; 24% $103,351–$197,300 — swap in the current year's table if it has changed. Look up your county's sales tax rate (cdtfa.ca.gov).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking your whole income is taxed at your top bracket rate. Only the dollars inside each bracket are taxed at that bracket's rate (marginal rates).
- Confusing marginal and effective rates. Marginal = rate on the next dollar; effective = total tax ÷ income.
- Thinking a raise can lower take-home pay because of brackets. Only the extra dollars are taxed at the higher rate.

### Day 1 (Monday): Why taxes
*Student:* List public goods funded by federal and California taxes and compare the share of revenue from income, payroll, and sales taxes.

*How to teach:* List public goods funded by taxes (roads, schools, military, Social Security, Medi-Cal, parks, fire services). Compare revenue shares: federal revenue comes mostly from individual income and payroll taxes; California's General Fund relies mostly on personal income tax, then sales and corporation taxes.

- **Q:** What is the largest source of federal revenue?
  **A:** Individual income taxes (about half), followed by payroll taxes (Social Security and Medicare).
- **Q:** What is the largest source of California's General Fund revenue?
  **A:** The personal income tax.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Brackets
*Student:* Calculate federal income tax for three incomes using the current bracket table and find each marginal and effective rate.

*How to teach:* Calculate federal tax for taxable incomes of $20,000, $40,000 and $80,000 with the bracket table; find marginal and effective rates.

- **Q:** Tax on $40,000 taxable income?
  **A:** 10% × $11,925 = $1,192.50; 12% × $28,075 = $3,369.00; total $4,561.50. Marginal 12%; effective ≈ 11.4%.
- **Q:** What does 'progressive tax' mean?
  **A:** Higher incomes pay higher rates on the portions of income in higher brackets.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): California taxes
*Student:* Use the CA FTB rate schedule to compute state income tax for the same incomes and add local sales tax on a $1,000 purchase in your county.

*How to teach:* Use the FTB schedule to compute California tax for the same incomes; compute sales tax on a $1,000 purchase in your county.

- **Q:** What is California's statewide base sales tax rate, and the tax on $1,000?
  **A:** 7.25% → $72.50 (local district taxes add more in most counties).
- **Q:** What agency administers California income tax?
  **A:** The Franchise Tax Board (FTB).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Tax myths
*Student:* Debunk 'a raise can put me in a higher bracket and lower my take-home pay' with a worked example.

*How to teach:* Debunk the 'raise into a higher bracket' myth with a worked example.

- **Q:** Taxable income rises from $48,000 to $49,000. How much more federal tax is owed?
  **A:** $475 × 12% = $57.00 plus $525 × 22% = $115.50 → $172.50 more; you keep $827.50 of the $1,000 raise (before payroll/state taxes).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Solve a five-problem tax-calculation quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the five-problem tax quiz in the key.

- **Q:** Define marginal and effective tax rate.
  **A:** Marginal: the rate on your last (next) dollar of income. Effective: total tax divided by total income.

**Assessment:** Tax-calculation quiz.

**Answer key:** Tax quiz (2025 federal single brackets on taxable income): 1) $20,000 → $1,192.50 + 12% × $8,075 = $969.00 → $2,161.50; marginal 12%, effective 10.8%. 2) $40,000 → $1,192.50 + 12% × $28,075 = $3,369.00 → $4,561.50; marginal 12%, effective 11.4%. 3) $80,000 → $1,192.50 + $4,386.00 + 22% × $31,525 = $6,935.50 → $12,514.00; marginal 22%, effective 15.6%. 4) Sales tax on $1,000 at California's 7.25% statewide base = $72.50 (add district taxes for your county, e.g. at 9.5% → $95.00). 5) Raise from $48,000 to $49,000 taxable: only the $525 above $48,475 is taxed at 22%; tax rises by 12% × $475 + 22% × $525 = $57.00 + $115.50 = $172.50, so take-home still rises by $827.50 (before payroll and state taxes). California tax answers: check with the FTB rate schedule — tax = sum of each bracket's rate × income in that bracket, minus the personal exemption credit.
