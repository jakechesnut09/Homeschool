## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Empire: context and Things Fall Apart Part One

**Prepare:** Borrow Things Fall Apart. Read Part One. Print Kipling's 'The White Man's Burden.' Parental preview: the novel includes violence, wife-beating, and the killing of a child.

**Watch for:**
- Taking Kipling's poem as neutral information. Treat it as a primary source of imperialist ideology and analyze its assumptions and audience.
- Seeing Okonkwo as representative of all Igbo values. Point to characters such as Obierika and Nwoye and to community judgments that question him.
- Treating proverbs as decoration. Ask what each proverb accomplishes in its conversation (persuading, softening criticism, showing respect).

### Day 1 (Monday): The empire's voice
*Student:* Read Kipling's 'The White Man's Burden' as a primary source. Analyze its rhetoric and assumptions about colonized peoples.

*How to teach:* Read Kipling as a primary source. The student analyzes its assumptions. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How does Kipling describe colonized peoples?
  **A:** 'Half-devil and half-child' — a racist assumption that they need Western rule.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Chapters 1–5
*Student:* Read chapters 1–5. Build a character profile of Okonkwo including his fear of being like his father, Unoka.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 1–5. Build Okonkwo's profile. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does Okonkwo fear?
  **A:** Being like his father, Unoka — lazy, in debt, and considered weak.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Chapters 6–9
*Student:* Read chapters 6–9. Analyze the death of Ikemefuna and its effect on Okonkwo and Nwoye.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 6–9. Analyze Ikemefuna's death. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Why does Okonkwo strike Ikemefuna?
  **A:** He is afraid of being thought weak.
- **Q:** How does it affect Nwoye?
  **A:** Something 'gave way inside him'; it alienates him from his father.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Proverbs
*Student:* Collect 8 proverbs from Part One and explain how Achebe uses them to show the sophistication of Igbo oral culture.

*How to teach:* Collect 8 proverbs and explain their function. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Explain 'Proverbs are the palm-oil with which words are eaten.'
  **A:** Proverbs make speech persuasive and graceful.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reading check
*Student:* Take a reading quiz and write a paragraph on how Achebe's portrayal of Umuofia answers Kipling's poem.

*How to teach:* Give reading quiz and paired-text paragraph. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How does Achebe's Umuofia answer Kipling?
  **A:** By showing a complex society with law, religion, and art — refuting the idea that Africans were uncivilized.

**Assessment:** Reading quiz and paired-text paragraph.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: Unoka; Amalinze the Cat; Ikemefuna from Mbaino; Ezinma; Week of Peace violation. Paragraph rubric (4 levels): 4 = claim with evidence from both texts.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Describing distributions

**Prepare:** Ruler (30 cm), a helper, CODAP (codap.concord.org). Ruler-drop conversion: time t = √(2d/980) seconds with d in cm. Preview OpenStax Introductory Statistics ch. 2.

**Watch for:**
- Describing a distribution with numbers only. Always address shape, outliers, center, and spread (SOCS) in context.
- Using mean and standard deviation for strongly skewed data. Use median and IQR when the data are skewed or have outliers.
- Confusing histograms (quantitative, touching bars) with bar graphs (categorical).

### Day 1 (Monday): Collect data
*Student:* Collect reaction-time data (ruler-drop test, 30 trials each for dominant and non-dominant hand).

*How to teach:* Model the ruler drop: helper holds the ruler at 0 cm between the student's open fingers, drops without warning, and the catch distance is recorded. Collect 30 trials per hand; convert to seconds.

- **Q:** A catch at 15 cm corresponds to what reaction time?
  **A:** t = √(2·15/980) ≈ 0.175 s.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Displays
*Student:* Create dot plots, histograms, and box plots of your data in CODAP; describe shape, center, spread, outliers (SOCS).

*How to teach:* Enter data in CODAP and make dot plot, histogram, box plot. Model a SOCS description. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does a long right tail on a histogram indicate?
  **A:** The distribution is skewed right — a few unusually large values; the mean will be pulled above the median.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Center and spread
*Student:* Compute mean, median, standard deviation, and IQR; explain which are appropriate for skewed data.

*How to teach:* Compute mean, median, standard deviation, IQR, and check outliers with the 1.5·IQR rule. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** For 2, 4, 4, 5, 7, 9, 20: mean, median, IQR, and outliers?
  **A:** Mean ≈ 7.29, median 5, Q1 = 4, Q3 = 9, IQR = 5; upper fence 9 + 7.5 = 16.5, so 20 is an outlier.
- **Q:** Which center is better for these data?
  **A:** The median — the outlier 20 pulls the mean up.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing groups
*Student:* Compare dominant vs. non-dominant reaction times with parallel box plots and write a comparison paragraph.

*How to teach:* Make parallel box plots for dominant vs. non-dominant hand. The student writes a paragraph comparing all four SOCS features with numbers.

- **Q:** What makes a comparison statement complete?
  **A:** It compares shape, center, spread, and outliers using values from both groups and in context (e.g., 'the dominant hand's median was 0.02 s faster').

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Take a quiz on describing and comparing distributions.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz; collect the write-up. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** If every value increases by 5, what happens to the mean and SD?
  **A:** Mean increases by 5; SD is unchanged.

**Assessment:** Reaction-time comparison write-up and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) SOCS description of a given histogram. 2) For 2, 4, 4, 5, 7, 9, 20: mean ≈ 7.29, median 5, IQR 5, outlier 20. 3) Median/IQR for skewed data. 4) Adding 5: mean +5, SD same. 5) Multiplying by 2: mean and SD both double. Write-up rubric: 4 = graphs, all SOCS features compared with numbers, context, and variability discussed; 3 = complete but one feature missing; 2 = numbers without comparison; 1 = incomplete.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Designing solutions for biodiversity

**Prepare:** Native plant seeds or a pollinator plant (check Calscape.org for local natives), optional wood scraps and drill for a bee hotel, window decals. Read about the Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing (Liberty Canyon, Agoura Hills; groundbreaking April 2022) built so mountain lions and other wildlife can cross the 101 Freeway. Prepare the Unit 6 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking any plant helps pollinators. Native plants support far more native insects.
- Designing without criteria and constraints.
- Believing small actions don't matter. Many backyard habitats together create corridors.

### Day 1 (Monday): Case study
*Student:* Study the Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing over the 101 Freeway and the criteria used to design it.

*How to teach:* Study the crossing: problem (freeway isolates Santa Monica Mountains lions, causing inbreeding), criteria (width, vegetation, noise/light shielding), constraints (cost, traffic).

- **Q:** Why do mountain lions in the Santa Monica Mountains need a wildlife crossing?
  **A:** Freeways isolate the population, leading to inbreeding, low genetic diversity, and road deaths; a crossing reconnects habitat.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Define the problem
*Student:* Choose a local biodiversity problem and write criteria and constraints for a solution.

*How to teach:* Choose a local problem; write criteria and constraints.

- **Q:** Distinguish criteria from constraints.
  **A:** Criteria are what a successful solution must achieve; constraints are limits (budget, space, time, rules).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Design
*Student:* Design a solution (pollinator garden, bird-safe windows, native lawn replacement) with a sketch and budget.

*How to teach:* Design a solution with sketch and budget.

- **Q:** Give an example of a bird-safe window solution.
  **A:** Decals or patterns spaced ≤2 inches apart horizontally / 4 inches vertically (the '2×4 rule'), external screens, or UV-patterned glass.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Build/plant
*Student:* Implement a small-scale version (plant native seeds, build a bee hotel, apply window decals) and document it with photos.

*How to teach:* Implement a small version and document with photos.

- **Q:** How can you measure your solution's success?
  **A:** Count pollinator visits per 10 minutes before and after, track bird strikes, or record species observed via iNaturalist.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 6 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 6 test on ecosystems and biodiversity.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test.

- **Q:** What is a wildlife corridor?
  **A:** A connected strip of habitat allowing animals to move between otherwise isolated areas.

**Assessment:** Solution design portfolio and Unit 6 test.

**Answer key:** Solution design portfolio: proficient = defined problem, criteria/constraints, sketch and budget, implemented small-scale version with photos, plan to measure success. Unit 6 test key: 1) Three levels of biodiversity. 2) Simpson's index calculation. 3) Keystone species (sea otter). 4) Primary vs. secondary succession. 5) HIPPO. 6) Hotspot definition. 7) Invasive species example. 8) Condor recovery and lead. 9) Tragedy of the commons; MSY = rK/4. 10) Wildlife corridors.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Supply and market equilibrium

**Prepare:** Graph paper; make 20 index cards for a pit market (10 buyer cards with maximum prices, 10 seller cards with minimum costs).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a rise in price causes supply to increase (shift). Price changes cause movement along the supply curve.
- Assuming both price and quantity are always predictable when both curves shift. One is indeterminate.
- Calling a surplus a shortage. A price above equilibrium creates a surplus (Qs > Qd); below creates a shortage.

### Day 1 (Monday): Law of supply
*Student:* Build a supply schedule for a lemonade stand and graph it; identify supply shifters.

*How to teach:* Build a lemonade supply schedule and graph; list shifters. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** State the law of supply.
  **A:** As price rises, quantity supplied rises.
- **Q:** Name three supply shifters.
  **A:** Input costs, technology, number of sellers.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Market simulation
*Student:* Run a pit-market simulation with family using buyer/seller index cards and record trade prices across rounds.

*How to teach:* Run the pit market for 3–4 rounds and record prices. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What happens to trade prices over rounds?
  **A:** They converge toward the equilibrium price.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Equilibrium
*Student:* Find equilibrium from schedules and graphs; identify shortages and surpluses.

*How to teach:* Find equilibrium from a schedule. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** P $1: Qd 50, Qs 10; $2: 40, 20; $3: 30, 30; $4: 20, 40. Equilibrium? At $2? At $4?
  **A:** Equilibrium $3, 30 units. At $2, shortage of 20; at $4, surplus of 20.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Double shifts
*Student:* Analyze 6 scenarios where supply and demand shift (e.g., avocado prices after a drought) and predict price and quantity.

*How to teach:* Analyze 6 double-shift scenarios. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** A drought cuts avocado supply. Effect?
  **A:** Price rises, quantity falls.
- **Q:** Supply falls and demand rises at once. Effect?
  **A:** Price rises; quantity indeterminate.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a supply and equilibrium quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Define equilibrium.
  **A:** Where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

**Assessment:** Equilibrium quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Law of supply: price up → quantity supplied up. 2) Shifters: input costs, technology, number of sellers, expectations, taxes/subsidies. 3) Equilibrium $3, 30 units. 4) At $2 shortage of 20; at $4 surplus of 20. 5) Drought: supply falls → price up, quantity down. 6) Supply down + demand up: price up, quantity indeterminate. Proficient: 5 of 6.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Mid-investigation critique

**Prepare:** All investigation pieces; three viewers who haven't heard the student explain the work; recording sheet.

**Watch for:**
- Explaining the work before viewers respond. Silent viewing tests whether the art communicates.
- Dismissing unexpected readings. They can reveal strengths to build on.
- Revising everything at once.

*Student:* Show your pieces to three people without explanation and record their interpretations.

*How to teach:* Show pieces to three viewers without explanation; record interpretations.

*How to teach:* Compare interpretations with intent; decide which readings to strengthen.

*How to teach:* Revise one earlier piece based on critique.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Piece 9
*Student:* Begin piece 9.

*How to teach:* Begin piece 9.

*Student:* Write a process reflection on the critique.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Tennis play

**Prepare:** Tennis gear, phone for slow-motion video, and a pro forehand slow-motion clip. Prepare the racket sports quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Rushing the net without an approach shot. Approach deep, then close in.
- Doubles partners standing one behind the other. Use side-by-side or one-up-one-back formations deliberately.
- Poor line-call etiquette. Call your own side honestly; if unsure, the ball is in.

### Day 1 (Monday): Net play
*Student:* Practice volleys and approach shots.

*How to teach:* Volleys (short punch, no swing) and approach shots.

- **Q:** Why don't you take a big swing on a volley?
  **A:** The ball is coming fast and close; a short punch gives control.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Singles
*Student:* Play a singles set.

*How to teach:* Play a singles set.

- **Q:** How many games are needed to win a set?
  **A:** 6, by a margin of 2 (or a tiebreak at 6-6).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Doubles
*Student:* Play doubles focusing on positioning.

*How to teach:* Play doubles focusing on positioning.

- **Q:** What is a lob?
  **A:** A high shot over an opponent at the net.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Film review
*Student:* Film your forehand and compare with a pro's slow-motion swing.

*How to teach:* Film the forehand; compare with a pro's slow-motion clip (preparation, contact point, follow-through).

- **Q:** What is one checkpoint at contact in a good forehand?
  **A:** Contact in front of the body with a stable wrist and the body rotating forward.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Take a racket-sports rules and strategy quiz and play a match.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and play a match.

- **Q:** In pickleball, who can score? In tennis?
  **A:** Pickleball: only the serving team (traditional scoring). Tennis: either player can win points/games.

**Assessment:** Racket sports quiz and match.

**Answer key:** Racket sports quiz key: 1) Pickleball kitchen = 7 ft non-volley zone. 2) Two-bounce rule. 3) Pickleball game to 11, win by 2. 4) Tennis: love, 15, 30, 40, deuce, advantage. 5) Set = 6 games by 2 or tiebreak. 6) Let serve replayed. 7) Topspin from low-to-high swing. 8) Ready position/split step. 9) Etiquette: honest calls, wait to cross courts. Match: proficient = correct scoring, legal serves, sportsmanship.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): UC & CSU applications

**Prepare:** Have the transcript draft, UC Application and Cal State Apply logins (or practice access). Check current application fees and fee-waiver rules (each system grants waivers for up to four campuses to eligible students).

**Watch for:**
- Estimating grades from memory. Enter the self-reported record exactly from the transcript.
- Listing activities without impact. Use specific numbers and results ('tutored 6 children weekly for 2 years').
- Missing confirmation. Save confirmation numbers and portal logins; check email and portals regularly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Application anatomy
*Student:* Tour every section of the UC Application and Cal State Apply and note required information and fee-waiver eligibility.

*How to teach:* Tour each section of the UC Application and Cal State Apply; note required information and fee-waiver eligibility.

- **Q:** How many UC campuses can an eligible student apply to with a fee waiver?
  **A:** Up to four.
- **Q:** What sections does the UC Application include?
  **A:** Personal information, academic history, test scores (optional/if applicable), activities and awards, Personal Insight Questions, and review/submit.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Academic history
*Student:* Enter or practice entering your self-reported academic record exactly from your transcript.

*How to teach:* Enter or practice entering the self-reported academic record exactly from the transcript.

- **Q:** Why is the UC academic record called 'self-reported'?
  **A:** Applicants enter their own courses and grades; transcripts are verified later if admitted.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Activities and awards
*Student:* Write up to 20 activity entries with concise, quantified 350-character descriptions.

*How to teach:* Write up to 20 activity entries with concise, quantified descriptions (350 characters).

- **Q:** Improve: 'Volunteered at food bank.'
  **A:** 'Sorted and packed 400+ lbs of food weekly at the Sacramento Food Bank; trained 5 new volunteers (2023–present, 4 hrs/week).'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Check against deadlines
*Student:* Verify submitted applications (or the plan for a future term/transfer cycle) and log confirmation numbers and portal logins.

*How to teach:* Verify submissions (or a future cycle plan) and log confirmation numbers and logins.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Peer or parent audit of the application for errors and consistency.

*How to teach:* Parent or peer audits the application with the checklist.

- **Q:** Why check consistency across applications?
  **A:** Inconsistencies in names, grades or dates can raise questions or delay processing.

**Assessment:** Application section audit checklist.

**Answer key:** Application audit checklist: [ ] personal info matches legal documents; [ ] every course and grade matches the transcript; [ ] a–g categories correct; [ ] up to 20 activities/awards, each ≤ 350 characters, specific and quantified; [ ] PIQs (UC) complete; [ ] fee or fee waiver; [ ] submitted with confirmation numbers logged; [ ] portal logins saved; [ ] consistent names/dates across applications. Pass = all checked or documented plan for a future cycle.
