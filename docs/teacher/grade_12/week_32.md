# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 32

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: The Metamorphosis

**Prepare:** Download The Metamorphosis (Project Gutenberg). Read it. Find two short critical interpretations (e.g., in a library database or SparkNotes-style critical overviews). Parental note: the story is bleak and ends in Gregor's death.

**Watch for:**
- Asking 'What insect is he really?' Redirect to how realistically everyone responds to the absurd premise.
- Reading the ending as purely happy. Point out that the family's relief comes immediately after Gregor's lonely death.
- Picking an interpretation by preference. Require at least three pieces of evidence from different parts of the story.

### Day 1 (Monday): Part I
*Student:* Read Part I. Discuss why Gregor worries more about his job than his transformation; mark deadpan narration.

*How to teach:* Read Part I. Ask why Gregor worries about his job; the student marks deadpan narration. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does Gregor worry about first?
  **A:** Missing his train and losing his job as a traveling salesman — he supports his family and their debts.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Part II
*Student:* Read Part II. Track how each family member's treatment of Gregor changes and what the apple symbolizes.

*How to teach:* Read Part II. The student tracks each family member's treatment of Gregor. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What happens with the apple?
  **A:** His father pelts him with apples; one lodges in his back and rots, a lasting wound.
- **Q:** How does Grete's role change?
  **A:** She first feeds him with care, then becomes impatient and dominant over his care.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Part III
*Student:* Read Part III. Analyze the ending's tone and the family's 'new dreams.'

*How to teach:* Read Part III. Analyze the ending's tone. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does Grete say about Gregor?
  **A:** That they must get rid of 'it' — she no longer considers it her brother.
- **Q:** What happens after Gregor dies?
  **A:** The family takes a tram to the countryside, feeling hopeful; the parents notice Grete has become a young woman and think of finding her a husband.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Interpretations
*Student:* Read two short critical interpretations (economic, family, existential) and decide which is most convincing with evidence.

*How to teach:* Read two interpretations; the student chooses the more convincing one with evidence. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Give an economic reading in one sentence.
  **A:** Gregor is valued only as a breadwinner; when he can't work, his family sees him as a burden.

### Day 5 (Friday): Essay
*Student:* Write a 700-word essay on how Kafka uses an absurd premise to explore a realistic theme.

*How to teach:* The student writes a 700-word essay. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Give a sample thesis.
  **A:** Kafka's absurd transformation exposes a realistic truth: Gregor's family loves him only as long as he is useful.

**Assessment:** Metamorphosis analytical essay.

**Answer key:** Essay rubric (4 levels): 4 = thesis connecting absurd premise to realistic theme (alienation, economic worth, family duty), evidence from all three parts, analysis of narration and ending. 3 = clear with thinner evidence; 2 = summary; 1 = incomplete.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Decisions with probability

**Prepare:** Find two sample insurance quotes (or use the numbers below) and a phone-warranty price. Spreadsheet. Prepare the Unit 10 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the lowest expected cost is always the best choice. Expected value is a long-run average; risk tolerance and ability to pay a large bill matter too.
- Confusing false positives with false negatives. False positive: test says yes, truth is no.
- Assuming 'random' means fair. Fair means each outcome has the intended equal chance.

### Day 1 (Monday): Insurance decisions
*Student:* Compare expected costs of low vs. high deductible car or health insurance plans.

*How to teach:* Compare Plan A: $1,200/yr premium, $500 deductible; Plan B: $800/yr, $1,500 deductible, with a 10% chance of a claim exceeding the deductible. Compute expected annual cost.

- **Q:** Expected annual cost of each plan?
  **A:** A: 1200 + 0.10(500) = $1,250. B: 800 + 0.10(1500) = $950. B is cheaper on average.
- **Q:** When might someone still choose Plan A?
  **A:** If they couldn't afford a $1,500 surprise bill or expect frequent claims.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Warranties
*Student:* Decide whether an extended phone warranty is worth it using expected value and failure probabilities.

*How to teach:* A 2-year warranty costs $150; the chance the phone needs a $300 repair is 15%. Compute expected repair cost and decide. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Is the warranty worth it on expected value?
  **A:** Expected repair cost = 0.15 × 300 = $45 < $150, so no on average.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fair decisions
*Student:* Design a fair method to assign chores or choose a winner using random numbers and justify its fairness.

*How to teach:* Design a fair chore-assignment method with random numbers (e.g., assign each person a die face, reroll extras). Justify why each person has equal probability.

- **Q:** Four people; roll a die and reroll 5 or 6. Is it fair?
  **A:** Yes — each of 1–4 is equally likely, 1/4 each.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Product testing
*Student:* Analyze a drug-trial or product-testing strategy for false positive/negative tradeoffs.

*How to teach:* Analyze a drug-test scenario: which error is worse (false positive vs. negative) and how a threshold trades them off. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** For an airport security screen, which error is more serious?
  **A:** A false negative (missing a real threat), so the threshold is set to catch nearly all threats even at the cost of more false positives.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 10 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 10 test on inference and decisions.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 10 test. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does a randomization test tell you?
  **A:** How often a difference as large as observed would happen by chance alone if the treatment had no effect.

**Assessment:** Unit 10 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 10 test sample key: 1) Parameter/statistic identification. 2) Voluntary response bias example. 3) Experiment design with control, randomization, replication. 4) 54%, n = 1000: ME ≈ 3.1%, CI 50.9%–57.1%. 5) Shuffle distribution: 1.5% as extreme → significant. 6) Insurance: A $1,250 vs. B $950. 7) Warranty $45 expected < $150. 8) Fair die method. Scoring: 90%+ mastery, 75–89% proficient, 60–74% developing.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Evaluating trade-offs and simulating impacts

**Prepare:** Spreadsheet; En-ROADS or a simple spreadsheet model; design brief template.

**Watch for:**
- Assuming impact scales perfectly. Adoption, maintenance, and behavior vary.
- Ignoring negative side effects in trade-off analysis.
- Presenting only positive results; good engineers report limitations.

### Day 1 (Monday): Trade-offs
*Student:* Score your solution on cost, safety, reliability, aesthetics, and social/environmental impact.

*How to teach:* Score the solution on cost, safety, reliability, aesthetics, social/environmental impact.

- **Q:** What does life-cycle assessment consider?
  **A:** Environmental impacts from raw materials, manufacturing, use, and disposal.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Scale it up
*Student:* Build a spreadsheet simulation of the impact if 10,000 households adopted your solution.

*How to teach:* Simulate 10,000 households adopting it.

- **Q:** A solar oven saves 0.5 kg of firewood per day per household. How much for 10,000 households in a year?
  **A:** 0.5 × 10,000 × 365 = 1,825,000 kg ≈ 1,825 metric tons per year.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sensitivity
*Student:* Change key assumptions in the simulation and report how results change.

*How to teach:* Sensitivity: change assumptions (adoption rate, days of use) and report.

- **Q:** If only 60% of households use it on 200 days/year, what are the savings?
  **A:** 0.5 × 6,000 × 200 = 600,000 kg = 600 metric tons.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Design brief
*Student:* Write a 2-page design brief: problem, criteria, design, test results, trade-offs, and simulated impact.

*How to teach:* Write a 2-page design brief.

- **Q:** What sections belong in a design brief?
  **A:** Problem, criteria and constraints, design description, test results, trade-offs, simulated impact, recommendations.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 9 presentation
*Student:* Present your design in a 5-minute pitch and answer questions.

*How to teach:* 5-minute pitch and Q&A.

- **Q:** What is scalability?
  **A:** The ability of a solution to be expanded to many more users while staying effective and affordable.

**Assessment:** Design brief and pitch.

**Answer key:** Design brief and pitch rubric. 4 – Brief covers all sections with data, trade-off scoring, simulation with sensitivity analysis, honest limitations; pitch clear within 5 minutes with confident answers. 3 (proficient) – Complete brief with data and simulation; clear pitch. 2 – Missing simulation or trade-offs. 1 – Incomplete.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Comparative advantage and trade

**Prepare:** Find Census trade data for California (census.gov/foreign-trade/statistics/state) and Port of Los Angeles/Long Beach statistics. Graph paper.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing absolute and comparative advantage. Comparative advantage depends on lower opportunity cost.
- Thinking trade is zero-sum. Show with the PPF example that both countries can consume more after specializing.
- Assuming trade benefits everyone equally. Overall gains can coexist with losses for workers in import-competing industries.

### Day 1 (Monday): Comparative advantage
*Student:* Calculate opportunity costs for two countries producing two goods and determine who should specialize in what.

*How to teach:* Model opportunity costs for two countries. Country A: 6 wheat or 3 cloth per worker; Country B: 2 wheat or 2 cloth. The student determines specialization.

- **Q:** Which country has comparative advantage in cloth?
  **A:** B: 1 cloth costs B 1 wheat but costs A 2 wheat.
- **Q:** Who specializes in wheat?
  **A:** A — its wheat opportunity cost is 0.5 cloth vs. B's 1 cloth.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Gains from trade
*Student:* Show consumption gains from trade on a PPF graph.

*How to teach:* Graph PPFs and show consumption beyond the PPF after trade. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Give a mutually beneficial terms of trade.
  **A:** Between 1 and 2 wheat per cloth, e.g., 1.5 wheat per cloth.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): California trade
*Student:* Analyze California's top exports and imports and trading partners using Census data and port statistics.

*How to teach:* Analyze California's top exports, imports, partners. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Name two of California's top trading partners.
  **A:** Mexico and China (also Canada, Japan, South Korea).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Western Hemisphere
*Student:* Trace changing trade patterns with Mexico and Canada over the 20th century.

*How to teach:* Trace trade with Mexico and Canada over the 20th century. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** When did NAFTA take effect?
  **A:** 1994.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a comparative advantage quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the comparative advantage quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Can a country with no absolute advantage still gain from trade?
  **A:** Yes — it still has comparative advantage in something.

**Assessment:** Comparative advantage quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) A: 6 wheat or 3 cloth; B: 2 wheat or 2 cloth. A's cost of 1 cloth = 2 wheat; B's = 1 wheat. 2) B specializes in cloth, A in wheat. 3) Terms of trade between 1 and 2 wheat per cloth (e.g., 1.5). 4) Trade lets both consume beyond their PPFs. 5) Top CA partners: Mexico, China, Canada (also Japan, South Korea). 6) NAFTA 1994; USMCA 2020. Proficient: 5 of 6.

**Watch for:**
- Waiting too long to book a venue.
- Hanging work at random heights. Center artworks around 57–60 inches from the floor (museum standard).
- Labels missing information.

### Day 1 (Monday): Venue
*Student:* Secure a venue (library community room, co-op, café) and set a date.

*How to teach:* Secure venue and date.

- **Q:** What questions should you ask a venue?
  **A:** Dates/hours, wall hanging methods allowed, lighting, insurance/liability, cost, setup times, accessibility.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Theme
*Student:* Choose an exhibition title and theme that frames the viewer's understanding.

*How to teach:* Choose an exhibition title and theme.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Layout
*Student:* Draw an installation plan placing each work on the walls.

*How to teach:* Draw an installation plan placing each work.

- **Q:** At what height are artworks typically centered?
  **A:** About 57–60 inches from the floor to the center of the work.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Labels
*Student:* Write and print wall labels and an exhibition intro panel.

*How to teach:* Write and print wall labels and an intro panel.

- **Q:** What goes on a wall label?

**Answer key:** Exhibition plan rubric. 4 – Confirmed venue and date, meaningful title and theme statement, scaled installation plan with heights and spacing, complete labels and intro panel, professional flyer distributed. 3 (proficient) – Venue, plan, labels, flyer. 2 – Missing plan or labels. 1 – Incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Job applications & work permits

**Prepare:** Download California form B1-1 (Statement of Intent to Employ a Minor and Request for Work Permit) from the CDE website. Find two real job postings. Download Form I-9 and its List of Acceptable Documents, a W-4, and a sample direct-deposit form. Note: students who are 18 or older don't need a work permit.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking homeschoolers can't get work permits. Minors in a private homeschool can get a permit; for a private school, it is typically issued by the local public school district (or the school's authorized administrator, per district practice).
- Ignoring minor hour limits. Ages 16–17: during school weeks up to 4 hours on a school day, 8 hours on a non-school day, 28 hours per week; during vacation up to 8 hours a day and 48 hours a week.
- Bringing the wrong I-9 documents. Bring one List A document (e.g. U.S. passport) or one List B (e.g. driver's license/state ID) plus one List C (e.g. Social Security card or birth certificate).

### Day 1 (Monday): Job search
*Student:* Find five local jobs on employer sites and job boards and evaluate pay, schedule, and requirements.

*How to teach:* Find five local jobs; evaluate pay, schedule and requirements.

- **Q:** What should you check in a job posting besides pay?
  **A:** Schedule and hours, location/commute, requirements, age limits, benefits and how to apply.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Online application
*Student:* Complete a full online job application, including availability, work history, and screening questions.

*How to teach:* Complete a full online application including availability, work history and screening questions.

- **Q:** Why must availability on an application be accurate?
  **A:** Employers schedule based on it; overstating availability leads to conflicts or losing the job.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Work permits
*Student:* Read California's minor work permit rules and complete form B1-1; learn where a private-school student obtains the permit (typically the local district) and teen hour limits.

*How to teach:* Read California's minor work permit rules and complete form B1-1; learn where to obtain the permit and hour limits.

- **Q:** During a school week, how many hours may a 16- or 17-year-old work?
  **A:** Up to 4 hours on a school day, 8 on a non-school day, and 28 hours per week.
- **Q:** Which form does the student fill out to request a work permit?
  **A:** Form B1-1, Statement of Intent to Employ a Minor and Request for Work Permit; the issued permit is form B1-4.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Hiring paperwork
*Student:* Practice Form I-9 identity documents, a W-4, and direct-deposit forms.

*How to teach:* Practice I-9 documents, W-4 and direct-deposit forms.

- **Q:** What does Form I-9 verify?
  **A:** Identity and authorization to work in the United States.
- **Q:** Give a valid I-9 document combination.
  **A:** A U.S. passport alone (List A), or a state ID card (List B) plus a Social Security card (List C).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Submit one real application (or a complete practice packet) and log it in a job tracker.

*How to teach:* Submit one real application (or complete practice packet) and log it.

- **Q:** When should you follow up on an application?
  **A:** About one week after applying, with a short, polite email or call.

**Assessment:** Completed application packet and work permit form.

**Answer key:** Application packet check (pass when complete): one real or complete practice online application with accurate availability, work history and screening answers; resume attached; B1-1 completed (student, parent and employer sections as applicable) with the correct district identified; I-9 Section 1 practice completed and documents identified; W-4 and direct-deposit form practiced; job tracker entry with date, position, company, contact and follow-up date.
