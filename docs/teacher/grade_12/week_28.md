# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 28

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Drafting the research paper

**Prepare:** Have the outline, notes, and synthesis matrix at hand. Block 60–90 minutes daily for drafting. Print a list of signal phrases.

**Watch for:**
- 'Quote-dumping' — quotations with no commentary. Require at least two sentences of commentary per quotation.
- Conclusions that only repeat the introduction. Ask 'So what?' — implications, recommendations, or open questions.
- A straw-man counterargument. Represent the strongest opposing source accurately before rebutting.

### Day 1 (Monday): Introduction
*Student:* Draft the introduction with a hook, context, and thesis; learn to use signal phrases ('According to…,' 'X argues…').

*How to teach:* Model an introduction: hook, context, and thesis as the last sentence. The student drafts their introduction using at least two signal phrases; check that the thesis matches the approved outline.

- **Q:** Give three signal phrases.
  **A:** 'According to…,' 'X argues that…,' 'As Y points out,…'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Body section 1
*Student:* Draft the first major section (2–3 pages), pairing every quotation with at least two sentences of commentary.

*How to teach:* The student drafts the first major section (2–3 pages) following the outline. Check a page: each quotation should be introduced, cited, and followed by two sentences of commentary.

- **Q:** What is commentary?
  **A:** The writer's explanation of how the evidence supports the claim — not a restatement of the quotation.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Body section 2
*Student:* Draft the second major section, including your primary research findings.

*How to teach:* The student drafts the second section, integrating primary research. Show how to report survey or interview results with numbers and context.

- **Q:** How should survey results be reported?
  **A:** With counts or percentages and the sample: 'Seven of ten parents surveyed (70%) said…' plus a note on limits.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Counterargument
*Student:* Draft the counterargument and rebuttal section, fairly representing the strongest opposing source.

*How to teach:* The student drafts the counterargument section. Ask them to read the opposing source's strongest point aloud first. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What makes a fair counterargument?
  **A:** It states the opposing view accurately and at its strongest, with a source, before rebutting with evidence.

### Day 5 (Friday): Conclusion & check
*Student:* Draft the conclusion (implications, not repetition) and submit the complete rough draft.

*How to teach:* The student drafts the conclusion and submits the complete rough draft. Skim for missing sections or citations. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What should a conclusion do?
  **A:** Explain the implications of the findings and why they matter, without simply repeating the introduction.

**Assessment:** Complete rough draft checkpoint.

**Answer key:** Rough-draft checkpoint (4 levels): 4 = complete draft with introduction and thesis, two body sections with commentary, primary research, fair counterargument, conclusion with implications, in-text citations throughout; 3 = complete with thin commentary in places; 2 = one section missing or uncited; 1 = substantially incomplete.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Counting methods

**Prepare:** Calculator with nPr and nCr keys (or Desmos nPr/nCr). Look up California SuperLotto Plus rules: pick 5 of 47 plus a Mega number 1 of 27.

**Watch for:**
- Using permutations when order doesn't matter. Ask: 'Would swapping two choices make a different outcome?' If not, use combinations.
- Forgetting to divide by repeated letters' factorials when arranging words.
- Treating with-repetition and without-repetition situations alike (passwords allow repeats; race finishes don't).

### Day 1 (Monday): Counting principle
*Student:* Count license plates, passwords, and outfits with the fundamental counting principle.

*How to teach:* Model the fundamental counting principle with a tree for outfits, then generalize to license plates and passwords. The student completes the set; check that each answer multiplies the number of choices per position.

- **Q:** How many plates of 3 letters followed by 3 digits?
  **A:** 26³ · 10³ = 17,576,000.
- **Q:** How many 4-character PINs using digits 0–9 with repeats allowed?
  **A:** 10⁴ = 10,000.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Permutations
*Student:* Compute nPr for race finishes and seating arrangements; handle repeated letters.

*How to teach:* Define n! and nPr = n!/(n − r)!, and model with a race. Then arrangements with repeated letters: divide by the factorial of each repeat count.

- **Q:** In how many ways can 8 runners finish 1st, 2nd, 3rd?
  **A:** 8P3 = 8·7·6 = 336.
- **Q:** How many distinct arrangements of MISSISSIPPI?
  **A:** 11!/(4!·4!·2!) = 34,650.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Combinations
*Student:* Compute nCr for committees and pizza toppings; distinguish when order matters.

*How to teach:* Define nCr = n!/(r!(n − r)!) and contrast committees (order doesn't matter) with officer slates (order matters). Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Choose a 3-person committee from 10 people.
  **A:** 10C3 = 120.
- **Q:** Choose president, VP, and secretary from 10 people.
  **A:** 10P3 = 720 (6 times as many, since each committee can be ordered 3! = 6 ways).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Lottery math
*Student:* Compute the probability of winning the California SuperLotto Plus jackpot and compare to everyday risks.

*How to teach:* Compute the SuperLotto Plus jackpot probability: C(47, 5) ways for the five numbers times 27 Mega numbers. Compare to everyday risks (e.g., lightning strike lifetime odds are commonly cited around 1 in 15,000).

- **Q:** What is the probability of winning the jackpot?
  **A:** C(47, 5) = 1,533,939; × 27 = 41,416,353. Probability 1 in 41,416,353.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Take a counting and probability quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the counting and probability quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** P(a 5-card hand is all hearts)?
  **A:** C(13, 5)/C(52, 5) = 1287/2,598,960 ≈ 0.000495.

**Assessment:** Counting quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Plates: 17,576,000. 2) 8P3 = 336. 3) MISSISSIPPI: 34,650. 4) 10C3 = 120. 5) 10P3 = 720. 6) Jackpot: 1 in 41,416,353. 7) All-hearts hand ≈ 0.000495. Proficient: 6 of 7.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Climate impacts on California

**Prepare:** DWR snow survey data (April 1 statewide snowpack, percent of average), ice cubes, pan with sand beach model, ruler. Facts: April 1 snowpack was 5% of average in 2015 and about 237% in 2023; a series of atmospheric rivers hit California from late December 2022 to March 2023 after severe drought.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking more winter rain makes up for less snow. Snow acts as a natural reservoir releasing water in spring/summer; rain runs off quickly.
- Believing melting sea ice raises sea level. Floating ice doesn't; land ice and thermal expansion do.
- Assuming seawalls protect beaches. They protect property but can increase beach loss in front of them.

### Day 1 (Monday): Snowpack
*Student:* Graph April 1 Sierra snowpack data and explain why it matters for California's water supply.

*How to teach:* Graph April 1 snowpack; explain it typically supplies about 30% of California's water.

- **Q:** Why does Sierra snowpack matter for water supply?
  **A:** It stores winter precipitation and melts gradually in spring/summer, filling reservoirs when demand is highest.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Weather whiplash
*Student:* Analyze the swing from drought to atmospheric-river floods (e.g., 2022–2023) and its effects.

*How to teach:* Analyze drought-to-flood whiplash (2020–2022 drought → winter 2022–23 atmospheric rivers).

- **Q:** What is an atmospheric river?
  **A:** A long, narrow band of concentrated water vapor in the atmosphere that can deliver extreme rain and snow when it makes landfall.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sea-level model
*Student:* Build a sand beach in a pan, add water, and model sea-level rise with melting ice to measure shoreline retreat.

*How to teach:* Sand beach model: add water, then ice (a) floating in water and (b) on the 'land'; measure shoreline retreat as ice melts.

- **Q:** In your model, which ice raised water level when melting: floating or on land?
  **A:** Ice on land; floating ice already displaces its own mass of water.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Adaptation
*Student:* Compare adaptation strategies (managed retreat, seawalls, groundwater recharge) for a coastal or valley community.

*How to teach:* Compare adaptation strategies (managed retreat, seawalls, living shorelines, groundwater recharge with floodwater 'Flood-MAR').

- **Q:** Give one benefit and one drawback of seawalls.
  **A:** Benefit: protect buildings/infrastructure. Drawback: expensive and can cause the beach in front to erode away ('coastal squeeze').

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz and write a paragraph on the climate impact you consider most urgent for your region.

*How to teach:* Quiz and a paragraph on the most urgent impact.

- **Q:** Name two causes of sea-level rise.
  **A:** Thermal expansion of warming ocean water and melting land ice (glaciers and ice sheets).

**Assessment:** Impact quiz and paragraph.

**Answer key:** Impact quiz key: 1) Snowpack as reservoir (~30% of supply). 2) 2015 ~5% vs. 2023 ~237% of average. 3) Atmospheric rivers. 4) Sea-level rise from thermal expansion and land ice. 5) Floating ice doesn't raise sea level. 6) Adaptation vs. mitigation. 7) Managed retreat definition. Paragraph rubric: proficient = names a regional impact, uses data, explains mechanism, suggests adaptation.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Unions, minimum wage, and global labor

**Prepare:** Find a Cesar Chavez speech (UFW website; e.g., his 1984 Commonwealth Club address) and the California Department of Industrial Relations minimum wage page. Prepare union and employer role cards. Prepare Unit 11 test.

**Watch for:**
- Crediting the UFW alone for the Delano grape strike. Filipino workers of the AWOC led by Larry Itliong started it in 1965; Chavez's NFWA joined, and they merged into the UFW.
- Thinking minimum wage is uniform. California's is higher than the federal $7.25, and some cities and industries are higher still.
- Assuming globalization only hurts workers. It can displace some workers while lowering prices and creating export jobs; discuss both.

### Day 1 (Monday): Union history
*Student:* Read about the AFL, CIO, and the United Farm Workers; analyze a Cesar Chavez speech.

*How to teach:* Read about the AFL, CIO, and UFW; analyze a Chavez speech. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How did the AFL and CIO differ?
  **A:** AFL organized skilled craft workers; CIO organized whole industries. They merged in 1955.
- **Q:** What tactics did the UFW use?
  **A:** Strikes, a national grape boycott, marches, and Chavez's fasts; it led to California's 1975 Agricultural Labor Relations Act.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Collective bargaining
*Student:* Simulate a contract negotiation between a union and employer with family playing roles.

*How to teach:* Simulate a contract negotiation. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is collective bargaining?
  **A:** Negotiation between a union and employer over wages, hours, and conditions.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Minimum wage
*Student:* Compare California's current minimum wage with the federal rate and evaluate research on its effects.

*How to teach:* Compare California and federal minimum wages; evaluate research. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is the federal minimum wage?
  **A:** $7.25 per hour (since 2009).
- **Q:** What is California's statewide minimum wage?
  **A:** $16.90 per hour in 2026 (it was $16.50 in 2025); it adjusts yearly for inflation — confirm on the DIR site.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Global mobility
*Student:* Analyze how capital mobility (factories moving abroad) and immigration affect U.S. workers and consumers.

*How to teach:* Analyze capital mobility and immigration effects. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How do consumers benefit from offshoring?
  **A:** Lower prices on goods.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 11 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 11 test on labor markets.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 11 test. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Who co-founded the UFW with Chavez?
  **A:** Dolores Huerta.

**Assessment:** Unit 11 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 11 test key: 1) Wages = labor supply and demand. 2) Human capital raises productivity and pay. 3) AFL (crafts) and CIO (industries) merged 1955. 4) Delano grape strike 1965 (AWOC with Itliong; NFWA with Chavez and Huerta) → UFW; 1975 ALRA. 5) Collective bargaining definition. 6) Federal minimum $7.25; California $16.90 (2026). 7) Offshoring lowers consumer prices but can displace workers. Scoring: 90%+ mastery, 75–89% proficient.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Public art proposal

**Prepare:** Photos of a local site (park wall, library, underpass — public view, safe access), sketchbook, graph paper for a scaled rendering. Read about Judy Baca's The Great Wall of Los Angeles (begun 1976 in the Tujunga Wash flood-control channel; about 2,754 feet long; painted with hundreds of young people through SPARC; depicts California history from an ethnically diverse perspective).

**Watch for:**
- Designing for yourself rather than the site's community.
- Ignoring practical constraints (weather, maintenance, permits, budget).
- Thinking public art is only murals. It includes sculpture, installation, and temporary works.

### Day 1 (Monday): Public art
*Student:* Study California public art (e.g., Judy Baca's Great Wall of Los Angeles) and its community process.

*How to teach:* Study The Great Wall of Los Angeles and its community process.

- **Q:** What made The Great Wall of Los Angeles a community project?
  **A:** Judy Baca worked with hundreds of youth from diverse backgrounds and consulted historians and community members to depict histories often left out of textbooks.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Site
*Student:* Photograph a local site and analyze its audience and context.

*How to teach:* Photograph and analyze a site: audience, traffic, history, light.

- **Q:** What does 'site-specific' mean?
  **A:** Art created for a particular location, responding to its physical, social, or historical context.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Design
*Student:* Design a mural or installation for the site with a scaled rendering.

*How to teach:* Design a mural or installation with a scaled rendering (e.g., 1 inch = 2 feet).

- **Q:** A wall is 30 ft × 10 ft. At 1 inch = 2 ft, how big is the rendering?
  **A:** 15 inches × 5 inches.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Proposal
*Student:* Write a proposal with budget, materials, and community benefit.

*How to teach:* Write a proposal with budget, materials, and community benefit.

- **Q:** Name three budget items for a mural.
  **A:** Exterior paint and primer, anti-graffiti/UV clear coat, scaffolding or lifts, brushes/rollers, permits, insurance, artist fees.

### Day 5 (Friday): Present
*Student:* Present the proposal as if to a city arts commission.

*How to teach:* Present as if to a city arts commission.

- **Q:** What do arts commissions usually look for?
  **A:** Artistic quality, community relevance and input, feasibility, safety, durability, and budget.

**Assessment:** Public art proposal.

**Answer key:** Public art proposal rubric. 4 – Site analysis with photos, scaled rendering, design responsive to community and history, realistic itemized budget and timeline, maintenance plan, persuasive presentation. 3 (proficient) – Site analysis, rendering, budget, community benefit, clear presentation. 2 – Missing budget or site response. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Outdoor adventure challenge

**Prepare:** Outdoor gear for the chosen outing (hike, bike, kayak). For water activities: life jackets (PFDs) for all and an approved, supervised rental location. Risk-management plan template.

**Watch for:**
- Leading without a safety briefing.
- Ignoring weather or participants' abilities.
- Debriefs that skip honest feedback.

### Day 1 (Monday): Plan
*Student:* Plan an outdoor outing for family or friends (hike, bike, kayak) with a risk-management plan.

*How to teach:* Plan the outing: route, participants' abilities, gear, weather, risk-management plan (hazards, prevention, emergency response, contacts).

- **Q:** What are the parts of a risk-management plan?
  **A:** Identify hazards, assess risk, prevention strategies, emergency plan (first aid, communication, evacuation), and contacts.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Conditioning
*Student:* Do a 40-minute conditioning session.

*How to teach:* 40-minute conditioning session.

- **Q:** Why condition before leading?
  **A:** Leaders need reserve energy to watch others and respond to emergencies.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Lead outing
*Student:* Lead the outing, briefing safety and Leave No Trace.

*How to teach:* Lead the outing: brief safety and Leave No Trace, keep group together, check in.

- **Q:** What should a leader say in a pre-trip briefing?
  **A:** Route and timing, hazards, buddy system, signals, water/food reminders, Leave No Trace, emergency plan.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Debrief
*Student:* Debrief participants and record feedback.

*How to teach:* Debrief participants; record feedback.

- **Q:** Why debrief after an outing?
  **A:** To learn what went well and what to improve for safety and enjoyment.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Take an outdoor-safety quiz and write about how outdoor activity fits your adult life.

*How to teach:* Outdoor-safety quiz; write how outdoor activity fits adult life.

- **Q:** What is the first thing to do in a wilderness emergency?
  **A:** Ensure your own and the group's safety (stop, assess), then give first aid and call or send for help.

**Assessment:** Leadership outing and quiz.

**Answer key:** Leadership outing: proficient = written plan with hazards/prevention/emergency response, clear briefing, group kept safe, debrief with feedback. Quiz key: 1) Ten Essentials. 2) Leave No Trace principles. 3) Ride with traffic; CA helmet under 18. 4) Reach-throw-don't-go. 5) Contour line reading. 6) Heat illness signs. 7) Lightning: get off ridges, avoid tall isolated objects. 8) PFD required for kayaking. 90%+ advanced, 75% proficient.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Comparing financial aid offers

**Prepare:** Gather three sample aid offers in different formats (NGPF and many colleges post examples) or the student's real offers. Open a spreadsheet, College Scorecard and the StudentAid.gov Loan Simulator. Prepare the Unit 9 test.

**Watch for:**
- Treating loans as part of the 'award'. Some offers list loans alongside grants; subtract only grants and scholarships to find net price.
- Comparing only one year. Multiply by four (or the expected years to graduate) and check whether grants are renewable.
- Not appealing. Colleges can adjust aid for changed circumstances (job loss, medical costs); a polite, documented appeal can help.

### Day 1 (Monday): Decoding offers
*Student:* Label grants, loans, and work-study on three sample aid offers that use different formats.

*How to teach:* Label grants, loans and work-study on three offers in different formats.

- **Q:** An offer lists a 'Direct Unsubsidized Loan $2,000'. Is that free money?
  **A:** No — it's a loan that must be repaid with interest (and interest accrues while in school).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Net cost spreadsheet
*Student:* Build a spreadsheet comparing four-year net cost and expected loan debt across offers.

*How to teach:* Build a spreadsheet comparing four-year net cost and expected debt across offers.

- **Q:** COA $30,000/year; grants $12,000/year; scholarship $1,000/year (renewable). Four-year net price?
  **A:** ($30,000 − $13,000) × 4 = $68,000 (assuming costs stay the same).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Debt-to-income
*Student:* Compare projected loan payments to starting salaries for your intended major using College Scorecard data.

*How to teach:* Compare projected loan payments to starting salaries for the intended major using College Scorecard.

- **Q:** $27,000 in loans at 6.39% on a 10-year plan is about $305/month. Is that manageable on a $45,000 salary?
  **A:** Yes by common guidelines — $305 is about 8% of gross monthly income ($3,750), under the 10% rule of thumb, and total debt is below first-year salary.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Appeals
*Student:* Write a professional financial-aid appeal letter for a sample changed circumstance.

*How to teach:* Write a professional financial-aid appeal letter for a sample changed circumstance.

- **Q:** What should an aid appeal include?
  **A:** Specific change in circumstances, documentation, the amount of help requested, and a polite, professional tone addressed to the financial aid office.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Unit 9 test and a written decision memo choosing a school based on fit and cost.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 9 test and have the student write the decision memo.

- **Q:** Why compare debt to expected salary?
  **A:** It shows whether loan payments will be affordable after graduation.

**Assessment:** Unit 9 test and decision memo.

**Answer key:** Unit 9 test answers: 1) COA components. 2) Grants/scholarships vs. loans vs. work-study. 3) SAI meaning; need = COA − SAI. 4) FAFSA vs. CADAA — file only one. 5) Cal Grant A (3.0 GPA, tuition at UC/CSU), B (2.0 GPA, access award + tuition after year one), C (career/technical). 6) Middle Class Scholarship for UC/CSU students under income/asset ceilings. 7) California College Promise Grant waives the $46/unit CCC fee. 8) Scholarship scam red flags. 9) Net price = COA − grants − scholarships; example: COA $34,000, grants $18,000, scholarship $2,000, loans $5,500 → net price $14,000 (loans are a way to pay it, not a reduction). 10) Rule of thumb: total student debt at or below expected first-year salary. Mastery 8+/10. Decision memo rubric: 4 = compares four-year net cost, expected debt and monthly payment vs. starting salary for each option, plus fit factors (program, graduation rate, location), and makes a justified choice; 3 = mostly complete; 2 = cost only or fit only; 1 = incomplete.
