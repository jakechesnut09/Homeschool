## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Things Fall Apart Parts Two and Three

**Prepare:** Read Parts Two and Three. Print 'The Second Coming.' Prepare debate format. Parental note: Okonkwo's suicide occurs at the end.

**Watch for:**
- Blaming only colonialism or only Okonkwo. Require evidence for both forces before the student takes a side in the debate.
- Missing the irony of the Commissioner's book title. Ask what 'pacification' and 'primitive' mean after reading a whole novel about Umuofia's complex society.
- Debating without rebuttal. Each speaker must answer the other side's strongest evidence, not just repeat their own.

### Day 1 (Monday): Exile in Mbanta
*Student:* Read chapters 14–19. Discuss how exile and the missionaries' arrival change Okonkwo's status and family.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 14–19. Discuss exile. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Why is Okonkwo exiled?
  **A:** His gun accidentally explodes at Ezeudu's funeral and kills Ezeudu's son; he must leave for seven years to his mother's village, Mbanta.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Nwoye's choice
*Student:* Analyze why Nwoye converts, citing the hymn passage; compare father-son conflicts here and in Part One.

*How to teach:* Analyze Nwoye's conversion. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What draws Nwoye to Christianity?
  **A:** The hymn and the questions about twins thrown away and Ikemefuna's death.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Part Three
*Student:* Read chapters 20–25. Annotate the final paragraph and the Commissioner's planned book title.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 20–25. Annotate the ending. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What happens to Okonkwo?
  **A:** He kills a court messenger; the others let the remaining messengers escape; he hangs himself.
- **Q:** What is the Commissioner's planned title?
  **A:** The Pacification of the Primitive Tribes of the Lower Niger.

### Day 4 (Thursday): The Second Coming
*Student:* Read Yeats's 'The Second Coming,' the source of Achebe's title, and discuss how Achebe repurposes 'things fall apart.'

*How to teach:* Read 'The Second Coming.' Discuss Achebe's title. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Quote the source line.
  **A:** 'Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Debate
*Student:* Hold a structured debate: Is Okonkwo destroyed more by colonialism or by his own rigidity? Evaluate the other side's evidence and reasoning.

*How to teach:* Hold the debate. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Normal distributions

**Prepare:** CODAP and a calculator with normalcdf/invNorm (or Desmos 'normaldist'). Preview Intro Stats ch. 6.

**Watch for:**
- Applying the 68-95-99.7 rule to any data. It only applies to approximately normal distributions.
- Sign of z: values below the mean have negative z-scores.
- Reporting a z-score as a probability. Convert with a normal table or technology.

### Day 1 (Monday): The normal curve
*Student:* Explore normal curves in CODAP; apply the 68-95-99.7 rule to heights and test scores.

*How to teach:* Draw a normal curve with ticks at μ ± σ, 2σ, 3σ. Apply 68-95-99.7 to heights (μ = 64 in, σ = 2.5 in). Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Between which heights are the middle 95% of women (μ = 64, σ = 2.5)?
  **A:** 59 to 69 inches.
- **Q:** About what percent are taller than 66.5 in?
  **A:** 16% (half of the 32% outside ±1σ).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): z-scores
*Student:* Compute and compare z-scores (e.g., SAT vs. ACT scores) to decide which performance is more unusual.

*How to teach:* Teach z = (x − μ)/σ and compare scores on different scales. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** SAT: 1300 (μ = 1050, σ = 200). ACT: 28 (μ = 21, σ = 5). Which is more unusual?
  **A:** SAT z = 1.25, ACT z = 1.4; the ACT score is relatively higher.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Normal calculations
*Student:* Use technology (normalcdf or Desmos) to find areas and percentiles for 10 problems.

*How to teach:* Use normalcdf(lower, upper, μ, σ) and invNorm(area, μ, σ) in Desmos or a calculator. The student does 10 problems. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** P(height < 70) with μ = 64, σ = 2.5?
  **A:** z = 2.4 → ≈ 0.992.
- **Q:** Find the 90th percentile height.
  **A:** 64 + 1.2816·2.5 ≈ 67.2 in.

### Day 4 (Thursday): When it's not normal
*Student:* Check your reaction-time data for normality with a histogram and discuss when the normal model is inappropriate.

*How to teach:* Look at the reaction-time histogram and a normal probability plot. Discuss right skew and when a normal model isn't appropriate.

- **Q:** Why might reaction times be right-skewed?
  **A:** There's a physical lower limit to how fast you can react, but occasional slow reactions (distraction) create a long right tail.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a normal-distribution quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the normal-distribution quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** P(60 < X < 68) for μ = 64, σ = 2.5?
  **A:** z from −1.6 to 1.6 → ≈ 0.890.

**Assessment:** Normal distribution quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Middle 95% for μ = 64, σ = 2.5: 59–69. 2) Percent above 66.5: 16%. 3) SAT z = 1.25, ACT z = 1.4 → ACT more unusual. 4) P(X < 70) ≈ 0.992. 5) P(60 < X < 68) ≈ 0.890. 6) 90th percentile ≈ 67.2 in. 7) z of 59 = −2. Proficient: 6 of 7 with notation shown.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Water quality

**Prepare:** Water test strips (pH, nitrate, hardness), clear jars, small amount of garden fertilizer, coffee filters, sand, gravel, activated charcoal (aquarium store), 2-liter bottle cut in half. EPA 'How's My Waterway.' SAFETY: wear gloves for stream samples; don't drink filtered water.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking clear water is clean water. Dissolved pollutants and microbes can be invisible.
- Believing the homemade filter makes water safe to drink. It reduces turbidity but doesn't remove microbes or dissolved chemicals reliably.
- Assuming only factories pollute. Nonpoint sources (runoff from lawns, farms, streets) are the leading cause of water-quality problems.

### Day 1 (Monday): Your watershed
*Student:* Use the EPA 'How's My Waterway' tool to identify your watershed and its listed impairments.

*How to teach:* Find your watershed and listed impairments.

- **Q:** Define watershed.
  **A:** The land area that drains to a common water body, such as a river, lake, or bay.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Water testing
*Student:* Test tap water, a local stream or pond, and rainwater with test strips; record pH, nitrate, and hardness.

*How to teach:* Test tap, stream/pond, and rain water; record pH, nitrate, hardness.

- **Q:** Why is normal rain slightly acidic (pH ~5.6)?
  **A:** CO₂ dissolves in rain to form carbonic acid.
- **Q:** What is the EPA drinking-water limit for nitrate?
  **A:** 10 mg/L as nitrogen.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Eutrophication
*Student:* Add a pinch of fertilizer to one of two jars of pond water and observe algae growth over the week (continue observations daily).

*How to teach:* Two jars of pond water; add a pinch of fertilizer to one; observe daily for algae.

- **Q:** Explain eutrophication.
  **A:** Excess nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus) cause algae blooms; when algae die, decomposers use up dissolved oxygen, causing hypoxia and fish kills.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Filtration
*Student:* Build a layered sand-gravel-charcoal filter and measure turbidity before and after filtering muddy water.

*How to teach:* Build a gravel-sand-charcoal filter; measure turbidity before and after (Secchi-style: view a printed mark through the water, or rate clarity 1–5).

- **Q:** What does activated charcoal do in a filter?
  **A:** Adsorbs some dissolved organic chemicals, odors, and colors onto its huge surface area.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a watershed report connecting test results to land use; take a quiz.

*How to teach:* Write a watershed report connecting results to land use; quiz.

- **Q:** Point vs. nonpoint source: storm drain runoff from a city?
  **A:** Nonpoint source (though storm drains discharge at points, the pollution originates diffusely across the landscape).

**Assessment:** Watershed report.

**Answer key:** Watershed report rubric. 4 – Identifies watershed and impairments, data table of tests with units, compares to standards, connects to land use (agriculture, urban runoff), eutrophication observations, filter results, recommendations. 3 (proficient) – Data, comparisons, and land-use connections. 2 – Data without interpretation. 1 – Incomplete. Quiz key: 1) Watershed. 2) Point vs. nonpoint. 3) Eutrophication steps. 4) Nitrate limit 10 mg/L N. 5) Turbidity = cloudiness.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Price controls and market structures

**Prepare:** Find two short articles on rent control in California (one pro, one con) and a summary of the Costa-Hawkins Rental Housing Act. Print Sherman Antitrust Act Sections 1–2. Read a summary of United States v. Google (2024 search ruling).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a price ceiling must be above equilibrium to matter. A binding ceiling is set BELOW equilibrium; a binding floor is ABOVE it.
- Believing being a monopoly is itself illegal. The Sherman Act targets monopolizing through anticompetitive conduct and agreements that restrain trade.
- Ignoring evidence that complicates a model. Minimum-wage research finds mixed effects; teach the student to weigh studies.

### Day 1 (Monday): Price ceilings
*Student:* Graph the effect of rent control and analyze California's Costa-Hawkins Act debates.

*How to teach:* Draw supply and demand for apartments and add a ceiling below equilibrium; label the shortage. Read the articles and discuss Costa-Hawkins; the student writes the strongest argument on each side.

- **Q:** What does a binding rent ceiling cause?
  **A:** A shortage (quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied), plus effects like less maintenance and less new construction.
- **Q:** What does Costa-Hawkins (1995) do?
  **A:** Limits local rent control in California: it exempts single-family homes and condos and newer construction, and allows landlords to reset rent to market when a unit becomes vacant (vacancy decontrol).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Price floors
*Student:* Graph a minimum wage as a price floor and discuss predicted vs. observed effects.

*How to teach:* Graph the labor market with a minimum wage above equilibrium and label the surplus. Discuss what the model predicts and what research finds.

- **Q:** What does the simple model predict from a binding minimum wage?
  **A:** Quantity of labor supplied exceeds quantity demanded — some unemployment — while employed workers earn more.
- **Q:** Why might observed job losses be smaller than predicted?
  **A:** Employers may have wage-setting power (monopsony), turnover costs fall, or prices adjust; studies differ by size of the increase and region.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Market structures
*Student:* Compare perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and monopoly in a chart with California examples.

*How to teach:* Build a chart of the four market structures (number of firms, product type, barriers, price control) with California examples.

- **Q:** Give a California example of each structure.
  **A:** Perfect competition: almond or strawberry farmers (near-competitive); monopolistic competition: restaurants; oligopoly: wireless carriers or gasoline refiners; monopoly: a local water utility.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Antitrust
*Student:* Read excerpts of the Sherman Antitrust Act and analyze a recent tech antitrust case.

*How to teach:* Read Sherman Act Sections 1 and 2. The student summarizes the Google case: market, conduct, ruling. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What do Sections 1 and 2 prohibit?
  **A:** Section 1: contracts or conspiracies in restraint of trade; Section 2: monopolizing or attempting to monopolize.
- **Q:** What did the court rule in U.S. v. Google (2024)?
  **A:** That Google illegally maintained its monopoly in general search, partly through payments to be the default search engine on phones and browsers.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a policy brief on one price control and take a quiz.

*How to teach:* The student writes a one-page policy brief on rent control or minimum wage; then give a short quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What sections belong in a policy brief?
  **A:** Problem, background, analysis (with a graph), options with trade-offs, and a recommendation.

**Assessment:** Price-control policy brief.

**Answer key:** Policy brief rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): 4 = correct graph of the ceiling/floor with shortage or surplus labeled, evidence from both sides, trade-offs for affected groups, clear recommendation. Quiz: binding ceiling below equilibrium → shortage; floor above → surplus; four market structures; Sherman Act §1/§2; Google ruling.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Golf fundamentals

**Prepare:** Borrow or rent clubs (putter, wedge/9-iron, 7-iron); find a putting green and driving range; budget for one small bucket. Bring tees.

**Watch for:**
- Gripping too tightly. Light, consistent grip pressure.
- Trying to 'lift' the ball. Hit down and through; the loft lifts the ball.
- Using a full swing for chips. Chips use a short putting-like stroke.

### Day 1 (Monday): Putting
*Student:* Practice putting from 3, 6, and 10 feet; record make percentage.

*How to teach:* Putt from 3, 6, and 10 feet, 10 each; record make percentage. Teach pendulum stroke from shoulders, eyes over the ball.

- **Q:** You made 7 of 10 from 6 feet. Make percentage?
  **A:** 70%.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Chipping
*Student:* Practice chipping to a target from 10–20 yards.

*How to teach:* Chip to a target from 10–20 yards: narrow stance, weight forward, hands ahead, short stroke.

- **Q:** Where should your weight and hands be in a chip?
  **A:** Weight on the lead foot, hands slightly ahead of the ball.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Full swing
*Student:* Learn grip, stance, and full swing at a driving range (one small bucket).

*How to teach:* Full swing: neutral grip (V's point to trail shoulder), athletic stance, ball position; small bucket.

- **Q:** Describe a neutral golf grip.
  **A:** Lead hand on top, the 'V' between thumb and forefinger of both hands pointing roughly toward the trail shoulder; light pressure.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Biomechanics
*Student:* Analyze how leverage and rotation produce club-head speed.

*How to teach:* Biomechanics: club-head speed comes from rotation of hips and torso, a long lever (arm + club), and wrist hinge release.

- **Q:** Why does a longer club usually hit the ball farther?
  **A:** A longer lever moves the club head faster at the same rotational speed, and longer clubs have less loft.

### Day 5 (Friday): Skill check
*Student:* Complete a putting and chipping skills test.

*How to teach:* Skills test: putting (3/6/10 ft) and chipping (balls within 6 feet of target).

- **Q:** What does 'par' mean?
  **A:** The expected number of strokes for a skilled golfer to complete a hole.

**Assessment:** Short-game skills test.

**Answer key:** Short-game skills test: proficient = putting 8/10 at 3 ft, 5/10 at 6 ft, 2/10 at 10 ft; chipping 5/10 within 6 ft of the target from 15 yards. 4 = clearly exceeds; 2 = about half of proficient; 1 = below. Written: par definition; chip vs. putt technique.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Personal Insight Questions

**Prepare:** Print the eight UC Personal Insight Questions and UC's PIQ guide (admission.universityofcalifornia.edu). Students answer four, up to 350 words each.

**Watch for:**
- Writing a résumé in paragraph form. PIQs need a specific example and reflection on what it shows about you.
- Trying to sound impressive instead of genuine. Simple, honest language and real detail work best.
- Ignoring the question asked. Each PIQ should clearly answer its prompt.

### Day 1 (Monday): Prompts and planning
*Student:* Read all eight UC PIQ prompts and UC's guide, then brainstorm two stories for each and pick four.

*How to teach:* Read all eight prompts and UC's guide; brainstorm two stories for each; pick four.

- **Q:** How many PIQs must you answer, and what's the word limit?
  **A:** Four of the eight, up to 350 words each.
- **Q:** Which prompt fits writing about homeschooling as an educational experience?
  **A:** Prompt 4 (taking advantage of an educational opportunity or overcoming an educational barrier).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Draft 1 and 2
*Student:* Draft two PIQs of up to 350 words each using specific examples and reflection.

*How to teach:* Draft two PIQs with specific examples and reflection.

- **Q:** What's the difference between telling and showing in a PIQ?
  **A:** Telling: 'I am a leader.' Showing: a specific story of what you did as a leader and the result.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft 3 and 4
*Student:* Draft the remaining two PIQs, including one about homeschooling as an educational experience if relevant.

*How to teach:* Draft the remaining two PIQs.

- **Q:** What two parts should each PIQ include?
  **A:** A specific example (what happened and what you did) and reflection (what it shows or how you grew).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revision
*Student:* Revise all four for specificity, active voice, and word count after reading them aloud.

*How to teach:* Revise for specificity, active voice and word count after reading aloud.

- **Q:** Rewrite in active voice: 'The fundraiser was organized by me.'
  **A:** 'I organized the fundraiser.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Submit the four polished PIQs with a reflection on what each shows admissions readers.

*How to teach:* Submit four polished PIQs with a reflection; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Why avoid repeating your activities list in PIQs?
  **A:** PIQs should add new insight — your thinking, motivations and growth — not repeat information readers already have.

**Assessment:** Four polished PIQs scored with a rubric.

**Answer key:** PIQ rubric (each of four, 4 levels): 4 = directly answers the prompt; one focused, specific example (who, what, when, actions taken); reflection on what the student learned or how they grew; shows qualities not visible elsewhere in the application; clear, active, error-free writing; ≤ 350 words; 3 = specific but reflection thin; 2 = general claims, few details; 1 = off-topic or over the limit. Reflection paragraph: proficient answers name what each PIQ shows readers (e.g. leadership, curiosity, resilience).
