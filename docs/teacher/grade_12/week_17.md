# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 17

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Frankenstein literary argument

**Prepare:** Find a contemporary article on AI or gene editing (CommonLit, a library database, or a reputable newspaper) and read it first. Print the argument rubric and the Purdue OWL MLA page.

**Watch for:**
- A thesis that just names a 'monster.' A nuanced thesis explains why and under what conditions — e.g., what Victor does or fails to do.
- Forcing the modern connection. The paragraph must name specific parallels (a creator's responsibility, consent, unintended consequences).
- MLA errors: citing the novel without page or chapter, or listing the article without its container. Check each citation against OWL.

### Day 1 (Monday): Paired nonfiction
*Student:* Read a contemporary article on AI or gene editing. List three ways it echoes Victor's choices.

*How to teach:* Read the article together. The student lists three specific ways it echoes Victor's choices; check that each names a behavior, not just a topic.

- **Q:** Give one way current technology debates echo Victor.
  **A:** Developers creating powerful systems before thinking through responsibility for how they affect people — as Victor creates life and then abandons it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Thesis & outline
*Student:* Write a nuanced thesis on 'Who is the real monster?' or 'What does Frankenstein warn about creators?' and outline with evidence.

*How to teach:* Model turning a simple claim into a nuanced thesis. The student writes one and outlines three body paragraphs with evidence assigned.

- **Q:** Give a nuanced thesis on 'Who is the real monster?'
  **A:** Victor becomes the true monster not by creating life but by abandoning it; his refusal of responsibility turns a benevolent being toward violence.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft
*Student:* Draft a 1,200-word literary argument that includes one paragraph connecting the novel to the modern article.

*How to teach:* The student drafts the 1,200-word essay, including one paragraph that connects the novel to the article. Check that quotations are introduced and explained.

- **Q:** Where should the modern connection go?
  **A:** In a later body paragraph, after the literary argument is established, so the novel stays the focus.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revise & edit
*Student:* Revise using a parent's feedback; check MLA in-text citations and Works Cited for the novel and article.

*How to teach:* Read the draft and give two strengths and two priorities. The student revises and checks every in-text citation and the Works Cited.

- **Q:** Write an MLA Works Cited entry for a Project Gutenberg Frankenstein.
  **A:** Shelley, Mary. Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus. 1818. Project Gutenberg, www.gutenberg.org.

### Day 5 (Friday): Submit
*Student:* Submit the final essay and complete a self-assessment against the argument rubric.

*How to teach:* The student submits the final essay and scores it against the rubric with a sentence of evidence per criterion. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What makes a self-assessment useful?
  **A:** Specific evidence for each score and one goal for the next essay.

**Assessment:** Literary argument essay (major grade).

**Answer key:** Literary argument rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): Thesis — nuanced and arguable. Evidence — 6+ well-chosen quotations from across the novel. Analysis — explains how evidence proves the claim. Modern connection — specific and integrated. MLA — correct in-text citations and Works Cited. Proficient: 3 in each.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Vectors

**Prepare:** Ruler, protractor, graph paper. Preview OpenStax 10.8 (Vectors). Clarify heading/bearing conventions before Day 3.

**Watch for:**
- Adding magnitudes of vectors that point in different directions. Add components, then find the magnitude.
- Using arctan(y/x) for direction without checking the quadrant.
- In wind problems, confusing 'wind from the west' with 'wind toward the west.' Wind FROM the west blows TOWARD the east.

### Day 1 (Monday): Vector basics
*Student:* Draw vectors, find magnitude and direction, and write them in component and i/j form.

*How to teach:* Draw ⟨3, 4⟩ from the origin; compute |v| = 5 and direction θ = arctan(4/3) ≈ 53.1°. Convert between ⟨a, b⟩ and ai + bj. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Find the magnitude and direction of v = ⟨3, 4⟩.
  **A:** |v| = 5; direction ≈ 53.1° above the positive x-axis.
- **Q:** Write a vector of magnitude 10 at 30° in component form.
  **A:** ⟨10 cos 30°, 10 sin 30°⟩ = ⟨5√3, 5⟩ ≈ ⟨8.66, 5⟩.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Adding vectors
*Student:* Add and subtract vectors tip-to-tail, by parallelogram, and by components; scalar-multiply vectors.

*How to teach:* Show tip-to-tail and parallelogram methods on graph paper, then components. Scalar multiples stretch or reverse. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Compute ⟨2, −1⟩ + ⟨−3, 5⟩ and 3⟨2, −1⟩.
  **A:** ⟨−1, 4⟩ and ⟨6, −3⟩.
- **Q:** Find u − v for u = ⟨4, 1⟩, v = ⟨1, 3⟩.
  **A:** ⟨3, −2⟩

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Velocity
*Student:* Solve airplane-with-crosswind and boat-across-river problems for ground speed and heading.

*How to teach:* Model a plane heading due north at 200 mph with a 30 mph wind from the west. Find ground speed and direction; then a boat crossing a river.

- **Q:** Find ground speed and direction for the plane.
  **A:** Velocity ⟨30, 200⟩: speed √40900 ≈ 202.2 mph, about 8.5° east of north.
- **Q:** A boat heads straight across at 4 mph; the current is 3 mph downstream. Resultant?
  **A:** 5 mph at about 36.9° downstream from straight across.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Forces
*Student:* Find the resultant of two people pulling a wagon at different angles; check equilibrium of three forces.

*How to teach:* Two people pull a wagon: 50 N at 20° and 40 N at −30°. Add components to find the resultant; then find the third force for equilibrium.

- **Q:** Find the resultant force.
  **A:** x: 50cos20° + 40cos30° ≈ 46.98 + 34.64 = 81.6; y: 50sin20° − 40sin30° ≈ 17.10 − 20 = −2.90. Magnitude ≈ 81.7 N at about −2.0°.
- **Q:** What third force puts the wagon in equilibrium?
  **A:** The opposite of the resultant: ≈ ⟨−81.6, 2.9⟩, about 81.7 N.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Take a vectors quiz including one real-world navigation problem.

*How to teach:* Give the vectors quiz with a navigation problem. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is a unit vector in the direction of ⟨3, 4⟩?
  **A:** ⟨3/5, 4/5⟩

**Assessment:** Vectors quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) |⟨3, 4⟩| = 5, direction ≈ 53.1°. 2) ⟨2, −1⟩ + ⟨−3, 5⟩ = ⟨−1, 4⟩. 3) 3⟨2, −1⟩ = ⟨6, −3⟩. 4) 10 at 30° = ⟨8.66, 5⟩. 5) Unit vector of ⟨3, 4⟩ = ⟨0.6, 0.8⟩. 6) Plane north 200 mph, wind from west 30 mph: ≈ 202.2 mph at ≈ 8.5° east of north. Proficient: 5 of 6 with diagrams.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Energy and mineral resource design solutions

**Prepare:** Household utility bill (kWh and cost), spreadsheet, California Energy Commission 'Total System Electric Generation' page (current and ~10 years ago). Emission factor: use the utility's stated factor or about 0.2 kg CO₂ per kWh for the California grid.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking renewables have zero environmental impact. All sources have life-cycle impacts (mining, land use).
- Comparing sources by installed capacity alone. Capacity factor (how much they actually produce) matters.
- Ignoring payback time in cost-benefit.

### Day 1 (Monday): Energy mix
*Student:* Analyze California's in-state electricity mix from the California Energy Commission and compare to 10 years ago.

*How to teach:* Analyze the in-state mix now vs. 10 years ago; note growth of solar and decline of others.

- **Q:** What does capacity factor mean?
  **A:** The ratio of actual energy produced over a period to the maximum possible if running at full capacity all the time.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Compare sources
*Student:* Build a spreadsheet comparing solar, wind, natural gas, geothermal, and nuclear on cost, emissions, land use, and reliability.

*How to teach:* Spreadsheet comparing solar, wind, gas, geothermal, nuclear: cost (levelized cost), emissions, land use, reliability (capacity factor).

- **Q:** A 100 MW solar farm has a 25% capacity factor. How much energy per year?
  **A:** 100 MW × 8,760 h × 0.25 = 219,000 MWh (219 GWh).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Lithium
*Student:* Evaluate lithium extraction from the Salton Sea (geothermal brine) vs. hard-rock mining for EV batteries.

*How to teach:* Evaluate Salton Sea geothermal-brine lithium (lower land disturbance, uses existing geothermal plants; technology still scaling) vs. hard-rock mining (proven, larger land and water impacts).

- **Q:** Why is the Salton Sea area called 'Lithium Valley'?
  **A:** Its hot geothermal brines contain dissolved lithium that can be extracted at geothermal power plants.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Home audit
*Student:* Use your household utility bill to calculate monthly kWh and CO₂ and test one solution (LED swap, solar panel) with a cost-benefit ratio.

*How to teach:* From the bill: monthly kWh × emission factor = CO₂. Test an LED swap: 60 W → 9 W bulb used 3 h/day.

- **Q:** Your household uses 500 kWh/month. Estimate CO₂ at 0.2 kg/kWh.
  **A:** 100 kg CO₂ per month.
- **Q:** Swapping a 60 W bulb for a 9 W LED used 3 h/day saves how much energy per month, and at $0.30/kWh what is the payback on a $3 bulb?
  **A:** 51 W × 3 h × 30 = 4.59 kWh/month ≈ $1.38/month; payback ≈ 2.2 months.

### Day 5 (Friday): Decision memo
*Student:* Write a one-page memo recommending an energy solution for a California town with justified cost-benefit reasoning.

*How to teach:* Student writes a one-page decision memo for a California town with cost-benefit reasoning.

- **Q:** What is a cost-benefit ratio?
  **A:** Benefits divided by costs (or the reverse), used to compare options; a benefit/cost ratio above 1 means benefits exceed costs.

**Assessment:** Energy decision memo.

**Answer key:** Energy decision memo rubric. 4 – Clear recommendation for a specific town; compares at least three options with data (cost, emissions, capacity factor, land/water use); quantified cost-benefit; addresses trade-offs and equity; cites CEC data. 3 (proficient) – Recommendation with data comparison and cost-benefit reasoning. 2 – Little data. 1 – Unsupported.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Enduring tensions and civic action project

**Prepare:** Print excerpts of King's 'Letter from Birmingham Jail' (1963; King Institute at Stanford has the text). Help the student brainstorm local or state issues in advance and find the relevant decision-makers (city council, school board, legislators).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking civic action means protest only. Letters, public comment, petitions, testimony, and volunteering are all civic action.
- Choosing an issue too big to influence ('end climate change'). Narrow to a local decision with a clear decision-maker.
- Not documenting the action. Keep copies, dates, photos, and any replies for the portfolio.

### Day 1 (Monday): Enduring tensions
*Student:* Read excerpts of King's 'Letter from Birmingham Jail' and identify tensions between law, order, liberty, and equality.

*How to teach:* Read the excerpts aloud, stopping at the just/unjust law passage. The student lists the tensions King addresses (law vs. justice, order vs. liberty, patience vs. urgency) with a quotation for each.

- **Q:** How does King distinguish just from unjust laws?
  **A:** A just law squares with the moral law; an unjust law degrades human personality (e.g., segregation laws). One who breaks an unjust law must do so openly, lovingly, and accept the penalty.
- **Q:** Whom does King criticize as a 'great stumbling block'?
  **A:** The 'white moderate' who prefers order to justice and tells activists to wait.
- **Q:** Quote King's line about interconnectedness.
  **A:** 'Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Choose an issue
*Student:* Choose a local or state issue, research multiple perspectives, and identify the decision-makers.

*How to teach:* Model researching an issue from three perspectives (e.g., a local housing project). The student chooses an issue, summarizes at least three perspectives with sources, and names who decides.

- **Q:** Why identify decision-makers before acting?
  **A:** So the action reaches the people with authority over the decision, making it effective.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Plan action
*Student:* Plan one civic action (letter to a legislator, public comment, petition, volunteer project) with a timeline.

*How to teach:* The student writes a plan: the action, audience, message, timeline, and how they'll document it. Review it for feasibility this week.

- **Q:** Name four forms of civic action.
  **A:** A letter to a legislator, public comment at a meeting, a petition, and a volunteer project.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Take action
*Student:* Carry out the action: send the letter, deliver public comment, or complete the volunteer hours, and document it.

*How to teach:* The student carries out the action (sends the letter, delivers comment, completes volunteer hours) and documents it. Accompany them to any meeting.

- **Q:** What counts as documentation?
  **A:** A copy of the letter or comment, dates, photos or meeting minutes, and any response received.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Write a reflection on what you learned about how change happens in a democracy.

*How to teach:* The student writes a reflection on how change happens in a democracy. Discuss it together. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What should the reflection address?
  **A:** What the student did, how decision-makers responded, what they learned about the process, and what they would do next.

**Assessment:** Civic action project portfolio.

**Answer key:** Civic action portfolio rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): Research — multiple perspectives with sources; Plan — clear audience, message, timeline; Action — completed and documented; Reflection — thoughtful analysis of how change happens, connected to King or course concepts. Proficient: 3 in each.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Photography and visual culture

**Prepare:** Phone camera and a free editing app (Snapseed, Lightroom mobile). Plan shoots at golden hour (about the hour after sunrise / before sunset), midday, and by a window. Get permission before photographing people or private property.

**Watch for:**
- Centering everything. Rule of thirds and leading lines create more dynamic compositions.
- Heavy filters that hide weak photos. Edit for clarity and consistency.
- Photographing people without consent.

### Day 1 (Monday): Composition
*Student:* Shoot 50 photos practicing rule of thirds, leading lines, and framing.

*How to teach:* Shoot 50 photos practicing rule of thirds, leading lines, framing.

- **Q:** What is the rule of thirds?
  **A:** Dividing the frame into a 3×3 grid and placing key subjects along the lines or at intersections for balance and interest.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Light
*Student:* Shoot the same subject in golden hour, midday, and window light.

*How to teach:* Shoot one subject in golden hour, midday, and window light; compare.

- **Q:** Why is golden hour light popular?
  **A:** Low sun produces warm, soft, directional light with long shadows and less harsh contrast.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Photo essay
*Student:* Create a 6-image photo essay about your community's visual culture (signs, murals, advertising).

*How to teach:* Create a 6-image photo essay on community visual culture.

- **Q:** What is visual culture?
  **A:** The images and visual objects in everyday life — signs, ads, murals, media — and how they shape meaning and identity.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Edit
*Student:* Edit and sequence the photo essay.

*How to teach:* Edit and sequence the essay (opening image, variety of distance, closing image).

- **Q:** How does aperture affect depth of field?
  **A:** A wider aperture (smaller f-number, e.g., f/2) gives shallow depth of field; a narrower aperture (e.g., f/16) keeps more in focus.

### Day 5 (Friday): Present
*Student:* Present the photo essay with a statement on how images shape community life.

*How to teach:* Present with a statement on how images shape community life.

- **Q:** What are the three parts of the exposure triangle?
  **A:** Aperture, shutter speed, ISO.

**Assessment:** Photo essay.

**Answer key:** Photo essay rubric. 4 – Six strong, varied images with deliberate composition and light, coherent sequence, consistent editing, insightful statement on visual culture, ethical consent. 3 (proficient) – Six well-composed images in a logical sequence with a clear statement. 2 – Weak composition or sequencing. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Semester fitness reassessment

**Prepare:** Stopwatch, mat, fitness log with baseline data. Schedule tests after rest days.

**Watch for:**
- Comparing only to HFZ, not to personal baseline.
- Concluding a goal 'failed' without analyzing why.
- Inconsistent test protocols.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mile retest
*Student:* Retest the mile run.

*How to teach:* Retest the mile.

- **Q:** Mile went from 9:45 to 9:05. Improvement in seconds?
  **A:** 40 seconds.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Strength retest
*Student:* Retest push-ups and curl-ups.

*How to teach:* Retest push-ups and curl-ups to cadence.

- **Q:** Why use cadence in these tests?
  **A:** It standardizes speed so results are comparable between tests and people.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Flexibility retest
*Student:* Retest flexibility and resting heart rate.

*How to teach:* Retest flexibility and resting HR.

- **Q:** Resting HR fell from 72 to 64. What does that suggest?
  **A:** Improved cardiovascular fitness — greater stroke volume means fewer beats needed at rest.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compare
*Student:* Graph baseline vs. current scores.

*How to teach:* Graph baseline vs. current.

- **Q:** What type of graph shows baseline vs. current for several tests?
  **A:** A clustered (side-by-side) bar graph.

### Day 5 (Friday): Goal review
*Student:* Review SMART goals and mark each as met or not.

*How to teach:* Review each SMART goal: met or not, with evidence.

- **Q:** If a goal wasn't met, what should you do?
  **A:** Analyze why (training, time, goal difficulty) and revise the plan or goal.

**Assessment:** Reassessment results.

**Answer key:** Reassessment results: proficient = all retests recorded with protocol matching baseline, changes and percent changes computed, graph labeled, each SMART goal marked met/not met with evidence. 4 adds causes and HFZ comparison; 2 = partial; 1 = incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Consumer protection, scams & identity theft

**Prepare:** Open FDIC Money Smart's fraud module and IdentityTheft.gov. Collect 10 real-looking scam texts/emails (FTC and bank phishing examples). Bookmark the CFPB complaint portal and California DFPI (dfpi.ca.gov).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking scammers only target older adults. Young adults report losing money to fraud often, especially through online shopping, social media and fake job offers.
- Believing a credit freeze hurts your credit. A freeze is free and doesn't affect your score; you temporarily lift it to apply for credit.
- Trusting caller ID or official-looking logos. Both can be faked; contact the organization using a number you look up yourself.

### Day 1 (Monday): Scam spotting
*Student:* Analyze 10 real phishing texts, emails, and job-offer scams and label the red flags in each.

*How to teach:* Analyze 10 messages and label red flags.

- **Q:** A 'job' offers $500/week to reship packages and sends a check first. Red flags?
  **A:** Pay for little work, sending money before work, reshipping (often stolen goods), and a check to deposit and return part of — a classic fake-check scam.
- **Q:** Why do scammers ask for gift cards?
  **A:** Gift card payments are fast, hard to trace and nearly impossible to reverse.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Identity theft
*Student:* Complete the FDIC Money Smart identity-theft lesson and walk through the IdentityTheft.gov recovery plan steps.

*How to teach:* Complete the identity-theft lesson and walk through IdentityTheft.gov recovery steps.

- **Q:** What are the first steps after identity theft?
  **A:** Contact the affected companies, place a fraud alert or credit freeze, report at IdentityTheft.gov, and change passwords.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Credit freezes
*Student:* Explain how to place free credit freezes at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion and why it matters before age 18.

*How to teach:* Explain how to freeze credit at all three bureaus and why it matters before 18.

- **Q:** Why freeze a minor's credit?
  **A:** Children's Social Security numbers are targeted because misuse may go unnoticed for years; a freeze blocks new accounts. Parents can request freezes for children under 16.
- **Q:** Does a freeze affect your credit score?
  **A:** No.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Your rights
*Student:* Research consumer rights under the Fair Credit Reporting Act and California law, and draft a CFPB or DFPI complaint for a sample problem.

*How to teach:* Research FCRA and California consumer rights; draft a CFPB or DFPI complaint for a sample problem.

- **Q:** What right does the FCRA give you?
  **A:** To see your credit report and dispute inaccurate information, which must be investigated, usually within 30 days.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Make a one-page scam-prevention guide for a younger sibling or grandparent; short quiz.

*How to teach:* The student makes the scam-prevention guide and takes the quiz.

- **Q:** What should you do if a 'bank' calls asking for a code sent to your phone?
  **A:** Hang up — never share the code; call the bank using the number on your card.

**Assessment:** Scam-prevention guide and quiz.

**Answer key:** Scam-prevention guide rubric: 4 = clear, one page, written for the chosen audience; lists at least five red flags (urgency, unusual payment methods like gift cards/wire/crypto, requests for codes or passwords, too-good-to-be-true offers, unexpected contact); gives steps if scammed (contact bank, change passwords, report to IdentityTheft.gov/FTC, freeze credit); 3 = mostly complete; 2 = generic; 1 = incomplete. Quiz: 1) Phishing definition. 2) Credit freeze is free at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. 3) IdentityTheft.gov creates a recovery plan. 4) Fair Credit Reporting Act gives the right to dispute inaccurate information. 5) CFPB and California DFPI accept consumer financial complaints. 6) A job that sends you a check and asks you to send part back is a fake-check scam.
