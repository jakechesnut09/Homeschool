# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 7

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: The Pardoner's Tale and the Wife of Bath

**Prepare:** Read the Pardoner's Tale, the Wife of Bath's Tale, and the Gawain beheading-game and girdle sections first. Parental preview: the Wife of Bath's Tale begins with a knight raping a maiden; plan to discuss it directly and seriously. Prepare the unit test.

**Watch for:**
- Missing the Pardoner's double irony: he preaches against greed while openly admitting he preaches for profit — then tries to sell to the pilgrims anyway.
- Reading the Wife of Bath's Tale as simply feminist or simply anti-woman. It both challenges (women's desire for sovereignty) and reinforces (the hag's ideal wife ending) medieval views.
- Vague pronouns ('this shows'). Always follow 'this' with a noun: 'this irony shows…'

### Day 1 (Monday): The Pardoner's Tale
*Student:* Read the Pardoner's Prologue and Tale. Identify the moral ('greed is the root of evil') and explain the double irony of who is telling it.

*How to teach:* Read the Pardoner's Prologue and Tale aloud. Ask the student to state the moral and then explain why the teller makes it ironic; check that they mention both the tale and the teller.

- **Q:** Summarize the plot of the Pardoner's Tale.
  **A:** Three rioters set out to kill Death; an old man directs them to a tree where they find gold. The youngest goes to town and buys poison; the other two plot to kill him. They stab him on his return, then drink the poisoned wine and all die.
- **Q:** What is the double irony?
  **A:** The Pardoner's theme is 'greed is the root of evil' (Radix malorum est cupiditas), yet he admits he preaches it only to make money — and then tries to sell relics to the pilgrims.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Wife of Bath's Tale
*Student:* Read the Wife of Bath's Tale and answer: what does the knight learn women most desire, and how does the tale both challenge and reinforce medieval views of women?

*How to teach:* Read the Wife of Bath's Tale; address the opening crime plainly. The student answers both parts of the question with evidence.

- **Q:** What does the knight learn women most desire?
  **A:** Sovereignty — mastery over their husbands.
- **Q:** How does the tale end, and how does it both challenge and reinforce medieval views?
  **A:** Given the choice between an old, faithful wife or a young, possibly unfaithful one, the knight lets her choose; she becomes beautiful and faithful. It challenges male authority by valuing women's sovereignty, but reinforces ideals by ending with an obedient, pleasing wife.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Gawain's test
*Student:* Read the beheading game and the girdle episode from Sir Gawain and the Green Knight; list how chivalric ideals are tested.

*How to teach:* Read the beheading game and girdle episode. The student lists how chivalric ideals (courage, honesty, courtesy, chastity) are tested.

- **Q:** What is Gawain's one failing?
  **A:** He keeps the green girdle from the lady instead of giving it to his host under their exchange-of-winnings agreement, because he believes it will save his life.
- **Q:** How is he punished?
  **A:** At the Green Chapel the Green Knight feints twice and then gives him only a small nick on the neck for hiding the girdle; Gawain wears the girdle afterward as a sign of his fault.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: pronoun clarity
*Student:* Mini-lesson on vague and ambiguous pronoun references. Revise a paragraph about the Tales to eliminate every unclear 'it,' 'this,' and 'they.'

*How to teach:* Model fixing vague pronouns in a sample paragraph. The student revises a paragraph so every 'it/this/they' has a clear antecedent.

- **Q:** Revise: 'The Pardoner and the Host argue, and he gets angry.'
  **A:** 'The Pardoner and the Host argue, and the Host gets angry.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 test
*Student:* Take a unit test on Beowulf, Chaucer, and Gawain including a 40-minute comparative essay: how do two medieval texts test the ideals of their society?

*How to teach:* Give the unit test (identification, short answer, 40-minute comparative essay). Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Which two texts pair well for 'testing society's ideals'?
  **A:** E.g., Gawain (chivalry tested by temptation) and Beowulf (heroic code tested by age and failed loyalty).

**Assessment:** Unit test with comparative essay (RL.11-12.9).

**Answer key:** Unit test: identification (Hrothgar, Grendel's mother, Wiglaf, the Pardoner, the Wife of Bath, the Green Knight, Bertilak) and short answers per the questions above. Comparative essay rubric (4 levels): 4 = thesis naming two texts and the ideals tested, balanced evidence from both, analysis of how each text judges its society, organized by idea; 3 = solid comparison with uneven evidence; 2 = summary of two texts with little comparison; 1 = one text only.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Rational functions

**Prepare:** Preview OpenStax 5.6 (Rational Functions). Have Desmos ready. Write the Unit 2 test from the key below (or use OpenStax chapter 5 review).

**Watch for:**
- Cancelling a common factor and forgetting it leaves a HOLE, not a vertical asymptote. Factor fully; a cancelled factor gives a hole, a remaining denominator factor gives a VA.
- Thinking graphs never cross a horizontal asymptote. A horizontal asymptote describes end behavior only; the graph may cross it in the middle.
- Not checking rational-equation solutions in the original equation. Any solution that makes a denominator zero is extraneous.

### Day 1 (Monday): Asymptotes
*Student:* Find vertical, horizontal, and slant asymptotes and holes for 10 rational functions.

*How to teach:* Model the three horizontal-asymptote cases by comparing degrees, and find a slant asymptote by long division. The student finds all asymptotes and holes for 10 functions.

- **Q:** Find holes and asymptotes of f(x) = (x² − 1)/(x² − x − 2).
  **A:** f = (x − 1)(x + 1)/((x − 2)(x + 1)). Hole at x = −1 (point (−1, 2/3)); VA x = 2; HA y = 1.
- **Q:** Find the slant asymptote of (x² + 1)/(x − 1).
  **A:** (x² + 1)/(x − 1) = x + 1 + 2/(x − 1), so y = x + 1.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Graphing
*Student:* Sketch 6 rational functions by hand using intercepts, asymptotes, and sign charts; check in Desmos.

*How to teach:* Sketch f(x) = (x + 1)/(x − 2) using intercepts, asymptotes, and a sign chart. The student sketches 6, then overlays in Desmos.

- **Q:** Give intercepts and asymptotes of f(x) = (x + 1)/(x − 2).
  **A:** x-intercept (−1, 0); y-intercept (0, −1/2); VA x = 2; HA y = 1.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rational expressions
*Student:* Add, subtract, multiply, and divide rational expressions; solve rational equations and check for extraneous solutions.

*How to teach:* Review operations by analogy to fractions: factor, find a common denominator, simplify. Solve a rational equation and check for extraneous roots.

- **Q:** Solve x/(x − 3) = 3/(x − 3) + 2.
  **A:** Multiply by (x − 3): x = 3 + 2(x − 3) → x = 3. But x = 3 makes a denominator 0, so there is no solution.
- **Q:** Simplify 1/x + 1/(x + 1).
  **A:** (2x + 1)/(x(x + 1))

### Day 4 (Thursday): Applications
*Student:* Model average cost per item C(x) = (fixed + variable·x)/x and interpret the horizontal asymptote.

*How to teach:* Model a business: fixed cost $5000, $12 per item, average cost C(x) = (5000 + 12x)/x. Graph it, find the horizontal asymptote, and interpret.

- **Q:** Find C(1000) and the horizontal asymptote; interpret.
  **A:** C(1000) = (5000 + 12000)/1000 = $17 per item. HA y = 12: as production grows, average cost per item approaches the $12 variable cost.
- **Q:** How many items must be produced for average cost to fall to $14?
  **A:** (5000 + 12x)/x = 14 → 5000 = 2x → x = 2500 items.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 2 test on polynomial and rational functions.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test (about 55 minutes). Grade with the key and have the student correct every error. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Which function has a hole: (x − 2)/(x² − 4) or (x + 2)/(x² − 9)?
  **A:** (x − 2)/(x² − 4) = 1/(x + 2) for x ≠ 2, so it has a hole at x = 2.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test sample key: 1) End behavior of −x⁴ + x: both ends down. 2) Sketch (x + 1)²(x − 2): bounce at −1, cross at 2, y-int −2. 3) Zeros of x³ − 2x² − 5x + 6: 1, 3, −2. 4) Zeros of x⁴ − 1: ±1, ±i. 5) (4 − 2i)/(1 + i) = 1 − 3i. 6) (x² − 1)/(x² − x − 2): hole (−1, 2/3), VA x = 2, HA y = 1. 7) Slant asymptote of (x² + 1)/(x − 1): y = x + 1. 8) x/(x − 3) = 3/(x − 3) + 2: no solution. 9) Average cost (5000 + 12x)/x → $12. Scoring: 90%+ mastery, 75–89% proficient, 60–74% developing, below 60% reteach.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Geologic time

**Prepare:** Adding-machine tape or 4.6 m string, meter stick, clear cup, colored sand or paper layers. Prepare 4 rock-layer diagrams. Find a reading on California's geologic assembly (e.g., California Geological Survey or USGS).

**Watch for:**
- Assuming the oldest layer is always at the bottom. Folding, overturning, or faults can change order; use cross-cutting and inclusions too.
- Thinking an unconformity is a layer. It's a surface representing missing time (erosion or nondeposition).
- Underestimating how recent humans are. On a 4.6 m timeline, Homo sapiens occupy the last ~0.3 mm.

### Day 1 (Monday): Relative dating
*Student:* Apply superposition, cross-cutting, and inclusions to sequence events in 4 rock-layer diagrams.

*How to teach:* Teach superposition, original horizontality, cross-cutting relationships, and inclusions; sequence events in 4 diagrams.

- **Q:** A fault cuts layers A–C but not layer D above it. What is the order?
  **A:** A, B, C deposited (A oldest at bottom), then faulting, then D deposited (after the fault).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Layer model
*Student:* Build layered sand/paper strata in a clear cup, add a 'fault' and 'intrusion,' and have a family member determine the sequence.

*How to teach:* Build layered strata in a clear cup; add a 'fault' (cut and shift) and 'intrusion' (vertical colored band); a family member determines the sequence.

- **Q:** What does the law of inclusions say?
  **A:** Fragments of one rock included in another must be older than the rock containing them.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Timeline
*Student:* Build a 4.6-meter scaled timeline (1 mm = 1 million years) marking major events from Earth's formation to humans.

*How to teach:* Build the timeline (1 mm = 1 million years): Earth forms (4,600 mm), oldest zircons (4,400), first life evidence (~3,700–3,500), Great Oxidation (~2,400), Cambrian explosion (~539), first land plants (~470), Permian extinction (252), dinosaurs' end (66), Homo sapiens (~0.3 mm).

- **Q:** Where does the dinosaur extinction fall on a 4.6 m timeline at 1 mm = 1 Myr?
  **A:** 66 mm (6.6 cm) from the present end.
- **Q:** What fraction of Earth history has had animals visible to the naked eye (since ~540 Ma)?
  **A:** About 540/4,600 ≈ 12%.

### Day 4 (Thursday): California's story
*Student:* Read about how California was assembled (accreted terranes, Sierra batholith, San Andreas) and add its events to the timeline.

*How to teach:* Read how California was assembled: accreted terranes added to the continent's edge; Sierra Nevada batholith formed from subduction-related magma (~140–80 Ma, Cretaceous peak); San Andreas transform began ~30 Ma when the Pacific Plate met North America.

- **Q:** What formed the granite of the Sierra Nevada?
  **A:** Magma generated by subduction of an ocean plate beneath North America cooled underground into a huge batholith (mostly Cretaceous), later uplifted and exposed by erosion.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 2 test on Earth's formation and history.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test.

- **Q:** What is an index fossil?
  **A:** A fossil of an organism that was widespread but lived for a short time, used to date and correlate rock layers.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 test and scaled timeline.

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test key (sample): 1) Solar nebula and accretion; frost line. 2) Solar system age ~4.57 Ga from meteorites. 3) Half-life problems (e.g., 1/8 remaining = 3 half-lives). 4) Zircons ~4.4 Ga. 5) Wegener's evidence; lacked mechanism. 6) Magnetic stripes symmetric. 7) Spreading rate calc. 8) Superposition, cross-cutting, inclusions sequence. 9) Unconformity = gap in record. 10) California: terranes, Sierra batholith from subduction, San Andreas transform ~30 Ma. Timeline rubric: proficient = correct scale, at least 12 events placed accurately (±5%), labeled.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): The executive branch

**Prepare:** Print Article II. Pick a recent executive order on federalregister.gov. Gather Cuban Missile Crisis documents (DocsTeach, JFK Library). Set up iCivics 'Executive Command.'

**Watch for:**
- Believing executive orders are laws. They direct the executive branch and must rest on the Constitution or a statute; courts can strike them.
- Thinking the president can declare war. Only Congress declares war; the president is commander in chief.
- Treating the bureaucracy as separate from the president. Departments are part of the executive branch.

### Day 1 (Monday): Article II
*Student:* Read Article II and list formal powers; compare to informal powers like 'bully pulpit.'

*How to teach:* Read Article II; list formal powers. Discuss informal powers. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Name four formal presidential powers.
  **A:** Commander in chief, make treaties (with Senate consent), appoint officials (with Senate consent), veto, pardon.
- **Q:** What is the 'bully pulpit'?
  **A:** Theodore Roosevelt's term for using the presidency's visibility to persuade the public.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Executive orders
*Student:* Read a recent executive order from the Federal Register and analyze its legal basis and limits.

*How to teach:* Read the executive order; identify the legal basis and limits. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Where does an executive order's authority come from?
  **A:** The Constitution (Article II) or powers granted by Congress through statute.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Bureaucracy
*Student:* Diagram the 15 executive departments and research one independent agency (EPA, FCC) and what it regulates.

*How to teach:* Diagram the 15 executive departments; the student researches one independent agency. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Name four executive departments.
  **A:** State, Treasury, Defense, Justice (also Interior, Agriculture, Commerce, Labor, HHS, HUD, Transportation, Energy, Education, Veterans Affairs, Homeland Security).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Case study
*Student:* Analyze primary sources from the Cuban Missile Crisis and evaluate Kennedy's decision-making options.

*How to teach:* Analyze Cuban Missile Crisis documents; evaluate Kennedy's options. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What options did Kennedy consider and choose?
  **A:** Airstrike, invasion, diplomacy, or a naval blockade; he chose a 'quarantine' (blockade) plus negotiation; the USSR removed missiles and the U.S. pledged not to invade Cuba (and privately agreed to remove missiles from Turkey).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz and play iCivics 'Executive Command.'

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and play the game. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How many executive departments are there?
  **A:** 15.

**Assessment:** Executive branch quiz and case-study analysis.

**Answer key:** Quiz: formal powers; executive orders; 15 departments; Cuban Missile Crisis 1962 blockade. Case study (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): 4 = options, risks, decision, evaluation with documents.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Portraiture and expressive drawing

**Prepare:** Charcoal, ink, brushes, mirror or photo references (own photos). Images of Alice Neel portraits and Kehinde Wiley portraits (e.g., his 2018 official portrait of Barack Obama). Print the four-step critique (describe, analyze, interpret, evaluate).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking likeness is the only goal. Expressive portraits use distortion, color, and mark-making to convey personality and mood.
- Placing features too high. The eyes sit at the halfway line; the nose bottom is about halfway between eyes and chin.
- Critique as judgment only. Follow the four steps in order.

### Day 1 (Monday): Facial proportions
*Student:* Draw a portrait using measured proportions from a family member or photo.

*How to teach:* Measured portrait: eyes at halfway; nose about halfway from eyes to chin; mouth about one-third from nose to chin; ears between brow and nose bottom; one eye-width between eyes.

- **Q:** Where do the ears line up on a face in front view?
  **A:** Roughly between the brow line and the bottom of the nose.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Expressive approaches
*Student:* Study expressive portraits by artists such as Alice Neel and Kehinde Wiley; note choices of line and color.

*How to teach:* Study Neel (direct, psychologically intense portraits with blue outlines, expressive color) and Wiley (contemporary sitters in heroic Old Master poses against ornate patterned backgrounds).

- **Q:** How does Kehinde Wiley challenge traditional portraiture?
  **A:** He paints contemporary Black sitters in the grand poses and settings of European portraits, questioning who has historically been shown as powerful.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Expressive portrait
*Student:* Create a portrait that conveys mood through mark-making and composition.

*How to teach:* Create an expressive portrait conveying mood through marks and composition.

- **Q:** Name two ways to convey mood in a portrait without changing the expression.
  **A:** Line quality (jagged vs. smooth), color/value key, cropping and composition, background treatment, lighting.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Refine
*Student:* Refine the portrait using feedback from a family member.

*How to teach:* Refine using a family member's feedback.

- **Q:** What is 'likeness'?
  **A:** How recognizably the portrait resembles the specific person.

### Day 5 (Friday): Critique
*Student:* Hold a formal critique (describe, analyze, interpret, evaluate) of your unit drawings.

*How to teach:* Formal critique of the unit's drawings using describe, analyze, interpret, evaluate.

- **Q:** List the four steps of formal critique in order.
  **A:** Describe, analyze, interpret, evaluate.

**Assessment:** Expressive portrait and critique notes.

**Answer key:** Expressive portrait rubric. 4 – Strong likeness or purposeful distortion, deliberate mark-making and composition conveying a clear mood, refined with feedback; critique notes complete all four steps with evidence. 3 (proficient) – Recognizable portrait with intentional expressive choices; critique uses four steps. 2 – Little expressive intent or incomplete critique. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Program in progress

**Prepare:** Weights or bands, training log. Review the signs of overtraining and DOMS.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking soreness means a good workout. DOMS is common with new exercises, but progress is measured in performance, not soreness.
- Pushing through joint pain. Joint pain or sharp pain means stop and modify.
- Giving up at a plateau. Adjust variables or deload.

### Day 1 (Monday): Workout B
*Student:* Complete Workout B with added load or reps.

*How to teach:* Workout B with added load or reps.

- **Q:** What is DOMS?
  **A:** Delayed-onset muscle soreness — muscle soreness peaking 24–72 hours after unaccustomed or eccentric exercise.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cardio
*Student:* Do 25 minutes of moderate cardio of choice.

*How to teach:* 25 minutes of moderate cardio (60–70% HRR).

- **Q:** Why include cardio in a strength program?
  **A:** For heart health, recovery, and overall fitness balance (meeting aerobic guidelines).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Workout A
*Student:* Complete Workout A with progression.

*How to teach:* Workout A with progression.

- **Q:** Define plateau.
  **A:** A period with no measurable progress despite training.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mobility
*Student:* Do a 30-minute mobility and foam-rolling session.

*How to teach:* 30-minute mobility and foam rolling.

- **Q:** When is foam rolling most useful?
  **A:** During warm-up or recovery to reduce perceived tightness and temporarily improve range of motion.

### Day 5 (Friday): Workout B
*Student:* Complete Workout B and review progress.

*How to teach:* Workout B; review progress in the log.

- **Q:** What is a deload?
  **A:** A planned lighter training period (reduced load or volume) to allow recovery and adaptation.

**Assessment:** Training log progression.

**Answer key:** Training log progression rubric. 4 – Every session logged; load or reps increased appropriately on each lift over two weeks; RPE recorded; comments on soreness/recovery; adjustments justified. 3 (proficient) – Complete log with visible progression. 2 – Gaps or no progression. 1 – Incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Credit reports & credit scores

**Prepare:** Get a sample credit report (NGPF or CFPB). Watch Khan Academy's credit score video. Note that everyone can get free credit reports weekly from all three bureaus at AnnualCreditReport.com (the only official site).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking checking your own credit hurts your score. Checking your own report is a soft inquiry and does not affect your score.
- Thinking a debit card builds credit. Only credit accounts reported to bureaus (cards, loans) build credit history.
- Believing income is in the credit score. Scores don't include income; they're based on borrowing and repayment behavior.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is credit
*Student:* Watch the Khan Academy credit score video and diagram the five FICO factors with their approximate weights.

*How to teach:* Diagram the five FICO factors: payment history 35%, amounts owed 30%, length of credit history 15%, new credit 10%, credit mix 10%.

- **Q:** What is the biggest factor in a FICO score?
  **A:** Payment history (about 35%).
- **Q:** You have a $1,500 limit and a $450 balance. What is your utilization?
  **A:** 30%.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Reading a report
*Student:* Annotate a sample credit report: personal info, accounts, payment history, inquiries, and collections.

*How to teach:* Annotate a sample report: personal info, accounts, payment history, inquiries, collections, public records.

- **Q:** How long do most negative items stay on a credit report?
  **A:** Generally up to 7 years (Chapter 7 bankruptcy up to 10).
- **Q:** Where can you get free credit reports?
  **A:** AnnualCreditReport.com — free weekly from Equifax, Experian and TransUnion.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Who checks your credit
*Student:* Research how landlords, employers, insurers, and lenders use credit and write a one-paragraph summary for each.

*How to teach:* Research how landlords, employers, insurers and lenders use credit; write a paragraph each.

- **Q:** Can an employer in California check your credit report?
  **A:** Only for certain positions (e.g. managerial, law enforcement, access to large sums of money or confidential data) under California Labor Code 1024.5, and with notice — most employers can't.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Disputes
*Student:* Find two errors in a sample report and draft a dispute letter following CFPB guidance.

*How to teach:* Find two errors in the sample report and draft a dispute letter following CFPB guidance.

- **Q:** Who should you dispute an error with?
  **A:** The credit bureau reporting it and the company that furnished the information.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Rank six credit profiles from best to worst score and justify your order; short quiz.

*How to teach:* Rank six credit profiles and give the short quiz.

- **Q:** Does checking your own credit score lower it?
  **A:** No — it's a soft inquiry.

**Assessment:** Credit profile ranking and dispute letter.

**Answer key:** Credit profile ranking (sample): best to worst — the profile with long history, on-time payments and low (under 10–30%) utilization ranks highest; a profile with a recent collection or 90-day late payments ranks lowest; justify by payment history (35%) and amounts owed/utilization (30%) first. Dispute letter rubric: 4 = includes name, address, the specific error(s) with account number, why it's wrong, request to correct/delete, supporting documents listed, sent to the credit bureau (and the furnisher), following CFPB sample; 3 = missing one element; 2 = vague; 1 = incomplete. Quiz: FICO factors and weights; utilization $600 of $2,000 = 30%; hard vs. soft inquiry.
