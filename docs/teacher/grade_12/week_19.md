# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 19

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Victorian poetry

**Prepare:** Print 'Dover Beach,' 'My Last Duchess,' 'Ulysses,' and a 'Goblin Market' excerpt. Choose an unseen dramatic monologue for the quiz (e.g., Browning's 'Porphyria's Lover' — preview: it depicts a murder — or Tennyson's 'Tithonus').

**Watch for:**
- Confusing the poet with the speaker. In a dramatic monologue the speaker is a character, often unreliable.
- Missing the silent listener (auditor). Ask: who is being spoken to, and why?
- Reading 'Dover Beach' as only about the sea. The sea is an image of retreating religious faith.

### Day 1 (Monday): Victorian age
*Student:* Read an overview of industrialization, empire, and Darwin's impact on faith. Read 'Dover Beach' and annotate imagery of retreating faith.

*How to teach:* Give a short overview of industrialization, empire, and Darwin. Read 'Dover Beach' aloud; the student annotates images of retreating faith and the final plea.

- **Q:** What is the 'Sea of Faith' and what is happening to it?
  **A:** Religious faith, once full; now the speaker hears only its 'melancholy, long, withdrawing roar.'
- **Q:** What does the speaker ask at the end?
  **A:** 'Ah, love, let us be true / To one another!' — personal fidelity in a world with no certainty, where 'ignorant armies clash by night.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): My Last Duchess
*Student:* Read Browning's 'My Last Duchess' aloud. Infer what happened to the Duchess and what the Duke unknowingly reveals about himself.

*How to teach:* Read 'My Last Duchess' aloud twice. Ask the student to infer what happened and cite lines; check that they notice what the Duke reveals.

- **Q:** What happened to the Duchess?
  **A:** The Duke implies he had her killed: 'I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together.'
- **Q:** Who is the listener and why is it ironic?
  **A:** An envoy arranging the Duke's next marriage — the Duke reveals his jealous, controlling nature to the person negotiating his new bride.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ulysses
*Student:* Read Tennyson's 'Ulysses' and compare its ideal of striving with Beowulf's heroism.

*How to teach:* Read 'Ulysses.' Compare its striving ideal with Beowulf's heroism in a two-column chart. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is the poem's final resolution?
  **A:** 'To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield.'
- **Q:** How is Ulysses like and unlike Beowulf?
  **A:** Both are aging heroes who seek one last challenge; Ulysses seeks experience and knowledge, while Beowulf fights to protect his people.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Write a monologue
*Student:* Write a 30-line dramatic monologue in which a speaker reveals more than they intend to an implied listener.

*How to teach:* Review features: speaker, auditor, occasion, revealing more than intended. The student drafts a 30-line monologue; check that the listener is implied, not addressed by stage directions.

- **Q:** What makes a dramatic monologue 'dramatic'?
  **A:** A single speaker addresses a silent listener in a specific situation, revealing character, often unintentionally.

### Day 5 (Friday): Poetry quiz
*Student:* Take a quiz identifying speaker, auditor, and irony in an unseen dramatic monologue; share your monologue aloud.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz on an unseen monologue; the student shares their monologue aloud. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** In 'My Last Duchess,' what does the Duke's comment about his 'nine-hundred-years-old name' reveal?
  **A:** His pride; he resented that the Duchess valued simple pleasures as much as his ancient family name.

**Assessment:** Original dramatic monologue and poetry quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key (for the chosen poem): identify the speaker, the silent auditor, the situation, and at least one example of irony where the speaker reveals more than intended. Monologue rubric (4 levels): 4 = 30 lines, distinct speaker voice, implied listener and occasion, the speaker unintentionally reveals a flaw, vivid diction; 3 = clear monologue, revelation less subtle; 2 = speech without listener; 1 = incomplete.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Matrices and systems

**Prepare:** Open the Desmos Matrix Calculator (desmos.com/matrix). Preview OpenStax 11.5–11.8. Prepare the Unit 6 test on vectors and matrices.

**Watch for:**
- Multiplying matrices entry-by-entry. Teach row-times-column, and check dimensions: (m × n)(n × p) gives m × p.
- Assuming AB = BA. Show a quick counterexample; matrix multiplication is generally not commutative.
- Thinking every square matrix has an inverse. If det A = 0, A has no inverse (the system has no unique solution).

### Day 1 (Monday): Matrices as data
*Student:* Organize store inventory and price data in matrices; add, subtract, and scalar-multiply them.

*How to teach:* Put two stores' inventories into 2 × 3 matrices and add/subtract them; scalar-multiply by 1.1 for a 10% increase. Label rows and columns.

- **Q:** A = [[4, 2], [1, 3]], B = [[1, 0], [5, 2]]. Find A + B and 2A.
  **A:** A + B = [[5, 2], [6, 5]]; 2A = [[8, 4], [2, 6]].

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Matrix multiplication
*Student:* Multiply matrices by hand and compute total revenue by multiplying a sales matrix by a price matrix.

*How to teach:* Model row-by-column multiplication. A sales matrix [[10, 5], [8, 12]] (rows = days, columns = items) times prices [[3], [4]] gives revenue per day.

- **Q:** Compute [[10, 5], [8, 12]]·[[3], [4]] and interpret.
  **A:** [[50], [72]]: $50 revenue on day 1 and $72 on day 2.
- **Q:** Can a 2 × 3 matrix be multiplied by a 2 × 3 matrix?
  **A:** No — the inner dimensions (3 and 2) don't match.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Determinants & inverses
*Student:* Compute 2×2 determinants and inverses; verify AA⁻¹ = I; discuss why det = 0 means no inverse.

*How to teach:* Teach det [[a, b], [c, d]] = ad − bc and A⁻¹ = (1/det)[[d, −b], [−c, a]]. Verify AA⁻¹ = I. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Find det and inverse of A = [[2, 1], [5, 3]].
  **A:** det = 6 − 5 = 1; A⁻¹ = [[3, −1], [−5, 2]]. Check: AA⁻¹ = [[1, 0], [0, 1]].
- **Q:** Does [[2, 4], [1, 2]] have an inverse?
  **A:** No: det = 4 − 4 = 0.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Solving systems
*Student:* Write 3×3 systems as AX = B and solve with inverse matrices using technology; solve a nutrition-mix problem.

*How to teach:* Write x + y + z = 6, 2x − y + z = 3, x + 2y − z = 2 as AX = B and solve X = A⁻¹B in Desmos. Then set up a nutrition-mix problem the same way.

- **Q:** Solve the system above.
  **A:** x = 1, y = 2, z = 3 (check: 1+2+3 = 6, 2−2+3 = 3, 1+4−3 = 2).
- **Q:** Write 2x + 3y = 8, x − y = −1 as a matrix equation and solve.
  **A:** [[2, 3], [1, −1]][x, y]ᵀ = [8, −1]ᵀ; det = −5; x = 1, y = 2.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 6 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 6 test on vectors and matrices.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does the identity matrix do in multiplication?
  **A:** AI = IA = A, like multiplying a number by 1.

**Assessment:** Unit 6 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 6 test sample key: 1) |⟨3, 4⟩| = 5, ≈ 53.1°. 2) ⟨2, −1⟩ + ⟨−3, 5⟩ = ⟨−1, 4⟩. 3) Plane: ≈ 202.2 mph, ≈ 8.5° E of N. 4) Wagon resultant ≈ 81.7 N. 5) [[10, 5], [8, 12]]·[[3], [4]] = [[50], [72]]. 6) Inverse of [[2, 1], [5, 3]] = [[3, −1], [−5, 2]]. 7) det [[2, 4], [1, 2]] = 0, no inverse. 8) 3 × 3 system: (1, 2, 3). Scoring: 90%+ mastery, 75–89% proficient, 60–74% developing.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Ecosystem stability and change

**Prepare:** Meter stick and string to make a 1 m² quadrat, field notebook, iNaturalist app (with parent account settings for privacy). Sea otter–urchin–kelp data (e.g., from Monterey Bay Aquarium or NOAA). Note: since 2013–14, sea star wasting disease killed sunflower sea stars (urchin predators), and northern California bull kelp declined by more than 90%.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking stable ecosystems have constant populations. Populations fluctuate within a range around carrying capacity.
- Equating biodiversity with number of species only. It includes genetic and ecosystem diversity and evenness.
- Believing keystone species are the most abundant. They have effects disproportionate to their abundance.

### Day 1 (Monday): Biodiversity
*Student:* Define genetic, species, and ecosystem diversity and calculate a simple diversity index from sample data.

*How to teach:* Define genetic, species, ecosystem diversity. Calculate Simpson's diversity index D = 1 − Σn(n−1)/[N(N−1)] from sample counts.

- **Q:** A quadrat has species counts A = 10, B = 6, C = 3, D = 1. Calculate Simpson's index.
  **A:** N = 20; Σn(n−1) = 90 + 30 + 6 + 0 = 126; N(N−1) = 380; D = 1 − 126/380 ≈ 0.67.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Quadrat survey
*Student:* Mark a 1 m² quadrat in a yard, park, or vacant lot and identify and count all organisms using iNaturalist.

*How to teach:* Survey a 1 m² quadrat; identify and count organisms using iNaturalist.

- **Q:** Why use a quadrat instead of counting a whole area?
  **A:** It gives a manageable, repeatable sample that can be scaled up to estimate the whole area.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Keystone species
*Student:* Analyze sea otter–urchin–kelp data from the California coast as an example of a keystone species.

*How to teach:* Analyze otter–urchin–kelp data: otters eat urchins; when otters were removed by the fur trade, urchins overgrazed kelp ('urchin barrens'); where otters recovered, kelp returned.

- **Q:** Why is the sea otter a keystone species?
  **A:** By eating sea urchins, otters keep urchins from overgrazing kelp, maintaining entire kelp forest ecosystems — a much larger effect than their numbers suggest.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Succession
*Student:* Compare succession after a California wildfire with succession on new volcanic rock; draw a timeline.

*How to teach:* Compare secondary succession after a chaparral fire (soil remains; crown-sprouting shrubs and fire-following annuals return quickly) with primary succession on new lava (lichens and mosses build soil first).

- **Q:** Which succession is faster, after a fire or on new lava, and why?
  **A:** After a fire (secondary) — soil, seeds, and roots remain; on lava (primary) soil must first form.

### Day 5 (Friday): CER
*Student:* Write a CER evaluating the claim that stable ecosystems maintain consistent numbers of organisms, using kelp forest data.

*How to teach:* Student writes a CER evaluating 'stable ecosystems maintain consistent numbers' using kelp data.

- **Q:** What is carrying capacity?
  **A:** The maximum population size an environment can support sustainably given its resources.

**Assessment:** Quadrat data and CER on ecosystem stability.

**Answer key:** Quadrat data: proficient = species list with counts, photos/iNaturalist IDs, correctly calculated Simpson's index. CER rubric: Claim – stable ecosystems maintain populations within a range but can shift dramatically after large disturbances (the 'consistent numbers' claim holds only for modest disturbances). Evidence – kelp/urchin/otter data, including urchin barrens after otter loss and after sea-star die-off. Reasoning – carrying capacity, keystone effects, resilience thresholds. 4 = nuanced and data-rich; 3 = accurate with data; 2 = partial; 1 = unsupported.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Scarcity, choice, and opportunity cost

**Prepare:** Graph paper and Khan Academy Microeconomics (Basic economic concepts). Have the student gather a rough weekly schedule and spending list for Day 1.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking opportunity cost is the money spent. It is the value of the next-best alternative given up (including time and forgone income).
- Counting sunk costs in decisions. Past, unrecoverable costs shouldn't affect choices at the margin.
- Treating points inside the PPF as impossible. Inside = inefficient (unused resources); outside = unattainable.

### Day 1 (Monday): Scarcity
*Student:* List 10 scarce resources in your life (time, money, space) and identify a choice each forces.

*How to teach:* Define scarcity (unlimited wants, limited resources). The student lists 10 scarce resources in their life and the trade-off each forces; check that each names a specific choice.

- **Q:** Why is time a scarce resource?
  **A:** Everyone has only 24 hours a day, so every activity means giving up another.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Opportunity cost
*Student:* Calculate the opportunity cost of attending college vs. working full-time for four years.

*How to teach:* Model opportunity cost of college vs. working: tuition and fees plus forgone wages (not room and board, which you'd pay either way). The student computes with real numbers.

- **Q:** Four years of wages at $35,000/yr forgone plus $14,000/yr tuition and fees. Opportunity cost?
  **A:** 4 × 35,000 + 4 × 14,000 = $196,000.
- **Q:** Why might college still be worth it?
  **A:** Higher lifetime earnings and lower unemployment for degree holders typically exceed the cost, plus nonmonetary benefits.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): PPF
*Student:* Graph a production possibilities frontier for a household (meals vs. cleaning) and identify efficient, inefficient, and unattainable points.

*How to teach:* Graph a household PPF (meals vs. cleaning hours). The student labels efficient, inefficient, and unattainable points and explains the bowed shape.

- **Q:** Why is a PPF usually bowed outward?
  **A:** Resources aren't equally suited to both tasks, so opportunity cost increases as you specialize more in one.
- **Q:** What shifts a PPF outward?
  **A:** More resources, better technology, or improved skills.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Marginal thinking
*Student:* Use a marginal benefit/marginal cost table (slices of pizza, hours of study) to find the optimal choice.

*How to teach:* Build an MB/MC table for pizza slices. The student finds the optimal quantity where MB ≥ MC for the last unit. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** MB of slices: $5, $4, $3, $2, $1; each costs $2.50. How many slices?
  **A:** 3 — the third slice's MB ($3) exceeds $2.50, but the fourth's ($2) does not.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz on scarcity, PPF, and marginal analysis.

*How to teach:* Give the fundamentals quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Define marginal analysis.
  **A:** Comparing the additional benefit and additional cost of one more unit of an activity.

**Assessment:** Fundamentals quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Scarcity = limited resources vs. unlimited wants. 2) Opportunity cost = next-best alternative. 3) College example = $196,000. 4) PPF points: on = efficient, inside = inefficient, outside = unattainable. 5) Bowed shape = increasing opportunity cost. 6) Pizza: 3 slices. 7) Sunk costs ignored. Proficient: 6 of 7.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Pickleball fundamentals

**Prepare:** Pickleball paddles and balls (outdoor balls for courts), a public court or a driveway taped to 20 × 44 ft with a 7-ft kitchen; portable net if needed. Review USA Pickleball rules.

**Watch for:**
- Volleying from the kitchen (non-volley zone). Not allowed, even if momentum carries you in after the volley.
- Forgetting the two-bounce rule. The return of serve and the third shot must both bounce.
- Overhitting at the kitchen line. Dinks should be soft and low.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rules and serve
*Student:* Learn rules and scoring; practice underhand serves (20 per side).

*How to teach:* Teach court, scoring (only serving team scores; to 11, win by 2; doubles call: server score, receiver score, server number — starting 0-0-2), and serve (underhand, contact below waist, diagonal, past the kitchen). 20 serves per side.

- **Q:** The score is called '5-3-1.' What does it mean?
  **A:** The serving team has 5, the receivers have 3, and it is the first server's turn.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dinks
*Student:* Practice dinking at the kitchen line with a partner for 20 minutes.

*How to teach:* Dink at the kitchen line with a partner for 20 minutes.

- **Q:** What is a dink?
  **A:** A soft shot from near the kitchen line that arcs over the net and lands in the opponent's kitchen.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Volleys
*Student:* Practice volleys and blocks; play a short game.

*How to teach:* Practice volleys and blocks (paddle up, short punch); play a short game.

- **Q:** Where must your feet be to volley?
  **A:** Outside the non-volley zone (behind the kitchen line), and you can't step in from the momentum of the volley.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Third-shot drop
*Student:* Practice third-shot drops and play doubles.

*How to teach:* Practice third-shot drops (soft arc into the opponent's kitchen) and play doubles.

- **Q:** Why is the third-shot drop used?
  **A:** It lets the serving team advance to the kitchen line while the opponents must hit up a low ball.

### Day 5 (Friday): Skill check
*Student:* Complete a skill test (serves in, dink rally length) and play a game.

*How to teach:* Skill test: 10 serves (target 8 in), dink rally length; play a game.

- **Q:** State the two-bounce rule.
  **A:** The serve must bounce before the return, and the return must bounce before the third shot; after that, volleys are allowed.

**Assessment:** Pickleball skill test.

**Answer key:** Pickleball skill test: proficient = 8/10 legal serves, a cooperative dink rally of 15+, correct scoring called aloud. 4 = 9–10 serves and 25+ dink rally; 2 = 5–7 serves and short rallies; 1 = below. Rules check answers: kitchen 7 ft; two-bounce rule; only serving team scores; game to 11 win by 2.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Self-assessment & career exploration

**Prepare:** Make sure the student's CaliforniaColleges.edu account works; open the O*NET Interest Profiler (mynextmove.org) and the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook. Help line up a professional for a 20-minute informational interview (in person, phone or video).

**Watch for:**
- Treating an interest inventory as a verdict. It suggests areas to explore; it doesn't decide a career.
- Choosing a career by salary alone. Consider interests, values, training time and cost, working conditions and job outlook.
- Asking for a job in an informational interview. The purpose is to learn; asking for advice and referrals is fine, asking for a job is not.

### Day 1 (Monday): Interest inventory
*Student:* Take the career interest assessment on CaliforniaColleges.edu and the O*NET Interest Profiler, then compare results.

*How to teach:* Take the CaliforniaColleges.edu career assessment and the O*NET Interest Profiler; compare results.

- **Q:** What are the six RIASEC interest areas used by O*NET?
  **A:** Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Strengths and values
*Student:* Complete a work-values ranking and list five strengths with evidence from homeschool projects, jobs, or activities.

*How to teach:* Rank work values (pay, security, creativity, helping others, independence, work-life balance) and list five strengths with evidence.

- **Q:** Why give evidence for a strength?
  **A:** Employers and colleges believe examples (a project you led, a skill you used) more than claims.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Career research
*Student:* Research three careers in the Occupational Outlook Handbook: duties, education, pay, outlook, and California openings.

*How to teach:* Research three careers in the OOH: duties, education, pay, outlook, California openings.

- **Q:** What does 'job outlook' mean in the OOH?
  **A:** The projected percent change in employment over the next ten years compared with the average for all occupations.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Informational interview
*Student:* Prepare 10 questions and conduct a 20-minute informational interview with a professional in one target career.

*How to teach:* Prepare 10 questions and conduct the informational interview; send a thank-you.

- **Q:** Give two good informational interview questions.
  **A:** 'What does a typical day look like?' and 'What do you wish you'd known before entering this field?' (also: how did you get started, what skills matter most).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a two-page career exploration report with a five-year goal.

*How to teach:* The student writes the career exploration report; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Make 'have a good career' a SMART five-year goal.
  **A:** Example: 'By June 2031, complete an AS in Nursing and pass the NCLEX-RN to work as a registered nurse in Sacramento.'

**Assessment:** Career exploration report.

**Answer key:** Career exploration report rubric (two pages, 4 levels): 4 = summarizes both interest inventory results (e.g. Holland/RIASEC codes) and how they compare; five strengths with evidence; values ranking; three researched careers with duties, education, median pay, outlook and California openings with sources; key takeaways from the informational interview; a SMART five-year goal; 3 = complete with thin evidence; 2 = missing a section; 1 = incomplete.
