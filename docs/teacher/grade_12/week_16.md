# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 16

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Frankenstein: pursuit and ending

**Prepare:** Read chapters 18–24 and Walton's final letters before the week. Prepare a theme-map template and a 10-question final reading quiz (see key).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the creature kills Victor. Victor dies of illness and exhaustion aboard Walton's ship; have the student find the passage.
- Confusing semicolons and colons. A semicolon joins two complete, related sentences; a colon introduces a list, explanation, or quotation after a complete sentence.
- Listing themes without showing how they interact. Ask: 'How does theme A cause or intensify theme B?'

### Day 1 (Monday): The broken promise
*Student:* Read chapters 18–20. Analyze Victor's reasons for destroying the female creature and the creature's threat 'I shall be with you on your wedding-night.'

*How to teach:* Read chapters 18–20 together, pausing at the destruction of the female creature. The student writes Victor's reasons in his own words and analyzes how Victor misreads the 'wedding-night' threat as aimed at himself.

- **Q:** Why does Victor destroy the female creature?
  **A:** He fears she might be even more malignant, might refuse the creature's promise to leave humanity, and that the pair could produce a 'race of devils.'
- **Q:** What does the creature threaten, and how does Victor misunderstand it?
  **A:** 'I shall be with you on your wedding-night.' Victor assumes the creature means to kill him, not Elizabeth.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Losses
*Student:* Read chapters 21–23. Chart every death in the novel and who Victor blames for each.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 21–23. The student builds a chart of every death, its cause, and whom Victor blames; discuss where Victor accepts responsibility and where he deflects it.

- **Q:** List the deaths and causes.
  **A:** William (strangled by the creature), Justine (executed after being framed), Clerval (strangled by the creature), Elizabeth (strangled on the wedding night), Alphonse (dies of grief), and later Victor (illness/exhaustion). Earlier, Caroline died of scarlet fever.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Arctic ending
*Student:* Read chapter 24 and Walton's final letters. Discuss why Walton turns back and how the creature's final speech reframes the novel.

*How to teach:* Read chapter 24 and Walton's final letters. Discuss why Walton turns back and how the creature's last speech reframes the story.

- **Q:** Why does Walton turn back?
  **A:** His crew threatens mutiny and demands to return home if the ice frees them; he reluctantly agrees, unlike Victor choosing others' lives over ambition.
- **Q:** What does the creature plan at the end?
  **A:** To go to the northernmost extremity, build a funeral pyre, and destroy himself; he disappears 'borne away by the waves.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: semicolons and colons
*Student:* Mini-lesson on semicolons, colons, and dashes in Shelley's long sentences. Write six sentences about the novel using each correctly.

*How to teach:* Show three long Shelley sentences and label the semicolon, colon, and dash in each. The student writes six original sentences about the novel (two each) and reads them aloud.

- **Q:** Punctuate: Victor had one goal to create life
  **A:** Victor had one goal: to create life.
- **Q:** Join correctly: The creature asked for a companion Victor refused in the end
  **A:** The creature asked for a companion; Victor refused in the end.

### Day 5 (Friday): Theme chart
*Student:* Build a theme map showing how two themes (e.g., responsibility and isolation) interact across the novel, with six quotations; take a final reading quiz.

*How to teach:* The student builds the theme map with six quotations and arrows showing interaction, then takes the final reading quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Give two interacting themes with an example.
  **A:** Responsibility and isolation: Victor's refusal to care for the creature isolates it, and its isolation drives the murders that isolate Victor.

**Assessment:** Theme map and reading quiz.

**Answer key:** Final reading quiz: 1) Female creature destroyed in the Orkneys. 2) Threat: 'I shall be with you on your wedding-night.' 3) Clerval murdered; Victor jailed in Ireland. 4) Elizabeth killed on wedding night. 5) Alphonse dies of grief. 6) Victor pursues creature to the Arctic. 7) Walton's crew demands return. 8) Victor dies aboard ship. 9) Creature mourns over Victor. 10) Creature plans his own funeral pyre. Theme map rubric (4 levels): 4 = two themes, six accurate quotations from across the novel, clear explanation of how they interact; 3 = accurate but interaction thin; 2 = quotations without analysis; 1 = incomplete.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Polar coordinates and complex numbers in polar form

**Prepare:** Polar graph paper (print free), Desmos. Preview OpenStax 10.3–10.5. Prepare the Unit 5 test.

**Watch for:**
- Using θ = arctan(y/x) without checking the quadrant. For (−1, 1), arctan(−1) = −π/4, but the point is in QII, so θ = 3π/4.
- Adding moduli when multiplying complex numbers. Multiply moduli, ADD arguments.
- Miscounting petals: r = a sin(nθ) has n petals if n is odd and 2n if n is even.

### Day 1 (Monday): Polar coordinates
*Student:* Plot points in polar form and convert 12 points between rectangular and polar coordinates.

*How to teach:* Plot (r, θ) by rotating θ then moving r. Convert with x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, r = √(x² + y²), and a quadrant check. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Convert (3, π/3) to rectangular.
  **A:** (1.5, 3√3/2) ≈ (1.5, 2.60).
- **Q:** Convert (−1, 1) to polar.
  **A:** (√2, 3π/4)

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Polar graphs
*Student:* Graph roses, cardioids, and circles in Desmos and by hand; describe symmetry.

*How to teach:* Graph r = 2cos θ, r = 1 + cos θ, r = 3 sin 2θ in Desmos, predicting shapes first; discuss symmetry. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How many petals does r = 3 sin 2θ have? r = cos 3θ?
  **A:** 4 petals; 3 petals.
- **Q:** Describe r = 2 cos θ.
  **A:** A circle with center (1, 0) and radius 1.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Complex plane
*Student:* Plot complex numbers; represent addition and conjugation geometrically; find distance as the modulus of a difference.

*How to teach:* Plot complex numbers as points; show addition as vector addition and the conjugate as reflection over the real axis. Distance is |z₁ − z₂|.

- **Q:** Find the distance between 2 + 3i and 5 − i.
  **A:** |(2 + 3i) − (5 − i)| = |−3 + 4i| = 5.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Polar form operations
*Student:* Write complex numbers in r(cos θ + i sin θ) form; multiply by multiplying moduli and adding arguments; apply De Moivre's Theorem.

*How to teach:* Write 1 + i = √2(cos π/4 + i sin π/4). Multiply in polar form and apply De Moivre's Theorem. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Compute (1 + i)⁸ with De Moivre's Theorem.
  **A:** (√2)⁸(cos 2π + i sin 2π) = 16.
- **Q:** Multiply 2(cos 30° + i sin 30°) · 3(cos 60° + i sin 60°).
  **A:** 6(cos 90° + i sin 90°) = 6i.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 5 test on identities, oblique triangles, and polar/complex forms.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Write −2 in polar form.
  **A:** 2(cos π + i sin π)

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test sample key: 1) cos θ given sin θ = 3/5 in QII: −4/5. 2) Verify sec²θ − tan²θ = 1. 3) sin 75° = (√6 + √2)/4. 4) sin 2x = cos x: π/6, π/2, 5π/6, 3π/2. 5) A = 40°, B = 60°, a = 10: b ≈ 13.47. 6) SAS 5, 7, 60°: c ≈ 6.24. 7) (−1, 1) → (√2, 3π/4). 8) |(2 + 3i) − (5 − i)| = 5. 9) (1 + i)⁸ = 16. 10) r = 3 sin 2θ has 4 petals. Scoring: 90%+ mastery, 75–89% proficient, 60–74% developing.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Natural resources and civilization

**Prepare:** Chocolate chip cookies (2 types), toothpicks, graph paper, 'mining' budget sheet (land cost per square, tool rental, reclamation cost). California water map (State Water Project and Central Valley Project). USGS/DWR Central Valley subsidence data.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking groundwater is an underground lake. It fills pores and fractures in rock and sediment (aquifers).
- Confusing reserve and resource. A reserve is the portion economically recoverable now.
- Believing subsidence reverses when wells refill. Compaction of clay layers is largely permanent, reducing aquifer storage.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cookie mining
*Student:* 'Mine' chocolate chips from cookies with toothpicks under a budget and calculate profit, land damage, and reclamation cost.

*How to teach:* Cookie mining: buy a cookie, trace it on graph paper, mine chips with toothpicks within 5 minutes, then reclaim. Calculate profit = chip value − costs (purchase, tools, time, reclamation per damaged square).

- **Q:** A miner earns $2 per chip and gets 8 chips; costs are cookie $3, tool $2, time $1/min × 5 min, and reclamation $1 × 6 damaged squares. What is the profit?
  **A:** Revenue $16; costs 3 + 2 + 5 + 6 = $16; profit = $0.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): California gold and oil
*Student:* Read about the Gold Rush and Kern County oil fields; explain how these resources shaped settlement.

*How to teach:* Read about the Gold Rush (Coloma, 1848) and Kern County oil; explain settlement and environmental effects (hydraulic mining debris, ban in 1884).

- **Q:** Why was hydraulic mining banned in California in 1884?
  **A:** It washed massive amounts of sediment into rivers, flooding farms and clogging waterways (Woodruff v. North Bloomfield ruling).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Water resources
*Student:* Map the State Water Project and Central Valley Project; explain how water availability shapes agriculture.

*How to teach:* Map SWP (Oroville Dam, California Aqueduct) and CVP (Shasta Dam, federal) — water from wetter north to drier south and the San Joaquin Valley.

- **Q:** Why does California move water north to south?
  **A:** Most precipitation falls in the north and Sierra, while much farmland and population are in the drier south and San Joaquin Valley.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Groundwater
*Student:* Analyze Central Valley subsidence data and explain the link to groundwater pumping during droughts.

*How to teach:* Analyze subsidence (San Joaquin Valley land sank nearly 30 ft near Mendota from 1925–1977; renewed rapid subsidence during recent droughts).

- **Q:** Explain the link between drought and subsidence.
  **A:** During drought, less surface water means more groundwater pumping; as water is removed, clay layers compact and the land surface sinks.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write an explanation of how one resource shaped your region and take a quiz.

*How to teach:* Student explains how one resource shaped their region; quiz.

- **Q:** Renewable or nonrenewable: groundwater in the Central Valley?
  **A:** Technically renewable, but when pumped faster than it recharges (overdraft) it behaves as nonrenewable on human time scales.

**Assessment:** Regional resource explanation and quiz.

**Answer key:** Regional resource explanation rubric. 4 – Specific resource with data/dates, explains how it shaped settlement, economy, and environment, and current sustainability issues. 3 (proficient) – Accurate explanation with evidence. 2 – General. 1 – Inaccurate. Quiz key: 1) Renewable vs. nonrenewable. 2) Aquifer = permeable rock/sediment holding groundwater. 3) Reserve vs. resource. 4) Gold Rush 1848; hydraulic mining ban 1884. 5) SWP state, CVP federal. 6) Subsidence from groundwater overdraft.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Comparative political systems

**Prepare:** Print Freedom House reports for two countries. Find Mandela's 1964 Rivonia Trial speech excerpt.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing federal and unitary systems. In a federal system power is constitutionally divided between national and state governments; in a unitary system the national government holds power and can create or abolish local units.
- Thinking dictatorships arise only by force. Hitler gained power legally before dismantling democracy.
- Treating Freedom House scores as precise facts. They are expert ratings.

### Day 1 (Monday): System types
*Student:* Compare federal, confederal, and unitary systems and presidential vs. parliamentary democracy (U.S. vs. UK).

*How to teach:* Compare systems and U.S. vs UK. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How is the head of government chosen in the UK?
  **A:** The Prime Minister is the leader of the majority in Parliament.
- **Q:** Example of a unitary state?
  **A:** France or the UK.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rise of tyranny
*Student:* Analyze conditions that gave rise to dictatorship in Germany (1933) and one Latin American case (e.g., Chile 1973).

*How to teach:* Analyze Germany 1933 and Chile 1973. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How did Hitler consolidate power?
  **A:** Appointed chancellor January 1933; Reichstag fire decree; Enabling Act (March 1933).
- **Q:** What happened in Chile in 1973?
  **A:** A military coup led by Pinochet overthrew elected president Allende.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Revolutions
*Student:* Analyze the causes and outcomes of the Mexican Revolution (1910) and its constitution of 1917.

*How to teach:* Analyze the Mexican Revolution and 1917 constitution. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Whom did the revolution overthrow?
  **A:** Porfirio Díaz.
- **Q:** Name two 1917 constitution provisions.
  **A:** Land reform (Article 27) and labor rights (Article 123).

### Day 4 (Thursday): New democracies
*Student:* Read Mandela's 'I Am Prepared to Die' excerpt and analyze South Africa's democratic transition; compare one other new democracy.

*How to teach:* Read Mandela's excerpt. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What ideal did Mandela say he was prepared to die for?
  **A:** A democratic and free society with equal opportunities.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a comparison of two countries using Freedom House scores and take a quiz.

*How to teach:* Write a country comparison; quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What are Freedom House's two categories?
  **A:** Political rights and civil liberties.

**Assessment:** Country comparison and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Unitary (UK, France) vs. federal (U.S., Mexico) vs. confederal (Articles of Confederation). 2) Parliamentary PM is chosen by the legislature's majority. 3) Germany 1933: Hitler appointed chancellor, Reichstag fire decree, Enabling Act. 4) Chile 1973: Pinochet's coup overthrew Allende. 5) Mexico: revolution against Díaz (1910); 1917 Constitution — land reform (art. 27), labor rights (art. 123). 6) South Africa's first multiracial election 1994, Mandela president. Comparison rubric (4 levels): 4 = Freedom House scores for political rights and civil liberties, specific evidence for each, explanation of differences.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Digital art and ethics

**Prepare:** Free software: Krita (painting), GIMP or Photopea (photo editing). Phone or tablet. Background on fair use (four factors) and cases: Rogers v. Koons (1992, Koons lost), Cariou v. Prince (2013, most of Prince's works found transformative), Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (2023, Supreme Court ruled against the Warhol Foundation's licensing use). Creative Commons license types; CC0 public domain dedication.

**Watch for:**
- Believing anything online is free to use. Most images are copyrighted by default.
- Thinking changing an image 10% makes it legal. There is no such rule; fair use is a case-by-case four-factor analysis.
- Assuming 'AI-generated' means no ethical questions. Training data, consent, and authorship are debated; AP rules require the work be the student's own.

### Day 1 (Monday): Copyright
*Student:* Learn copyright, fair use, and Creative Commons; analyze two appropriation-art court cases.

*How to teach:* Teach copyright (automatic on creation), fair use (purpose/character, nature of work, amount used, market effect), Creative Commons. Analyze two appropriation cases.

- **Q:** Name the four fair-use factors.
  **A:** 1) Purpose and character of the use (including transformative or commercial), 2) nature of the copyrighted work, 3) amount and substantiality used, 4) effect on the market for the original.
- **Q:** Why did Jeff Koons lose Rogers v. Koons?
  **A:** He made sculptures closely copying Art Rogers's photograph 'Puppies' for commercial sale; the court found it wasn't fair use (it didn't parody the photo itself and harmed its market).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Digital tools
*Student:* Learn layers, masks, and brushes in Krita or Photopea through a guided exercise.

*How to teach:* Guided exercise in layers, masks, brushes.

- **Q:** What is a layer mask?
  **A:** A grayscale mask on a layer that hides (black) or reveals (white) parts of the layer without deleting pixels — non-destructive editing.
- **Q:** Raster vs. vector?
  **A:** Raster images are made of pixels (lose quality when enlarged); vector images use mathematical paths (scale without loss).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): AI and art
*Student:* Discuss ethical questions about AI image generation and write your personal policy.

*How to teach:* Discuss AI image generation ethics; student writes a personal policy.

- **Q:** Give one ethical concern about AI image generators.
  **A:** They may be trained on artists' work without consent or compensation; authorship and originality are unclear; they can imitate living artists' styles.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Digital piece
*Student:* Create a digital artwork related to your inquiry using only your own photos or CC0 sources.

*How to teach:* Create a digital artwork using only own photos or CC0 sources.

- **Q:** What does CC0 mean?
  **A:** The creator has waived copyright, placing the work in the public domain for any use without attribution required.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Finish and document the piece, citing any sources used.

*How to teach:* Finish and document; cite sources.

- **Q:** How should you cite a CC BY image?
  **A:** Give title, creator, source link, and license (e.g., 'Photo by X, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0').

**Assessment:** Digital artwork and ethics statement.

**Answer key:** Digital artwork and ethics statement rubric. 4 – Skilled use of layers/masks/brushes, connects to inquiry, all sources own or CC0 with citations; ethics statement explains copyright, fair use factors, and a thoughtful AI policy. 3 (proficient) – Complete digital work with correct sourcing and clear policy. 2 – Sourcing problems or thin policy. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Nutrition, hydration, sleep, recovery

**Prepare:** Food log app or notebook; MyPlate (myplate.gov). Scale for sweat test. Sleep log. Parental note: emphasize fueling and health, not dieting; watch for signs of disordered eating.

**Watch for:**
- Believing carbs make you unhealthy. Carbohydrate is the main exercise fuel.
- Waiting until thirsty to drink.
- Assuming you can 'catch up' on sleep on weekends.

### Day 1 (Monday): Food log
*Student:* Log your food for three days; do a 30-minute workout of choice.

*How to teach:* Start a 3-day food log; 30-minute workout.

- **Q:** Name the three macronutrients and their calories per gram.
  **A:** Carbohydrate 4, protein 4, fat 9 kcal/g.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Fueling
*Student:* Learn pre- and post-workout fueling; plan a sample day of meals.

*How to teach:* Teach pre-workout (1–3 h, carbs + some protein, low fat/fiber) and post-workout (carbs + protein within ~1–2 h). Plan a sample day.

- **Q:** Give a good post-workout snack and why.
  **A:** Chocolate milk or yogurt with fruit — carbohydrate to refill glycogen and protein to repair muscle.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hydration
*Student:* Calculate sweat loss by weighing before and after a 30-minute run.

*How to teach:* Weigh before and after a 30-minute run (no bathroom break, note fluid drunk). 1 lb lost ≈ 16 oz sweat; plan to replace 16–24 oz per lb.

- **Q:** You weighed 150.0 lb before and 148.5 lb after, drinking nothing. How much sweat did you lose and how much should you drink?
  **A:** 1.5 lb ≈ 24 oz of sweat; drink about 24–36 oz to rehydrate.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sleep
*Student:* Track sleep for the week and write a sleep-hygiene plan.

*How to teach:* Track sleep; write a sleep-hygiene plan.

- **Q:** How much sleep do 17–18-year-olds need?
  **A:** Teens (13–18) need 8–10 hours; adults 18+ need 7 or more.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluate
*Student:* Write an evaluation of your nutrition, hydration, and sleep with two changes.

*How to teach:* Write an evaluation with two changes.

- **Q:** Name three signs of overtraining.
  **A:** Persistent fatigue, declining performance, elevated resting HR, mood changes, poor sleep, frequent illness.

**Assessment:** Recovery habits evaluation.

**Answer key:** Recovery habits evaluation rubric. 4 – 3-day food log compared with MyPlate, correct sweat-loss calculation, sleep averages, specific strengths/gaps, two realistic measurable changes. 3 (proficient) – All three areas evaluated with data and two changes. 2 – Missing an area. 1 – Incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Retirement accounts

**Prepare:** Watch Khan Academy 401(k) and IRA videos. Look up the current IRA contribution limit (irs.gov; $7,000 for 2025). Read CalSavers.com and ssa.gov 'my Social Security' examples. Prepare the Unit 6 test.

**Watch for:**
- Not taking the full employer match. It's an instant return — essentially free money.
- Thinking a teen can't open a Roth IRA. Anyone with earned income can contribute (up to the lesser of earned income or the annual limit); minors use a custodial account.
- Confusing traditional and Roth. Traditional = tax deduction now, taxed in retirement; Roth = no deduction now, qualified withdrawals tax-free.

### Day 1 (Monday): Why retirement now
*Student:* Watch the Khan Academy 401(k) and IRA videos and chart tax treatment of traditional vs. Roth accounts.

*How to teach:* Chart tax treatment: traditional 401(k)/IRA (pre-tax contributions, taxed withdrawals) vs. Roth (after-tax contributions, tax-free qualified withdrawals).

- **Q:** If you expect to be in a higher tax bracket later, which is better, Roth or traditional?
  **A:** Roth — pay tax now at the lower rate; withdrawals are tax-free later.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Employer match
*Student:* Calculate the free money from a 50% match up to 6% on a $50,000 salary over 10 years with growth.

*How to teach:* Calculate the match: 50% of contributions up to 6% of a $50,000 salary, over 10 years with 7% growth.

- **Q:** Annual employer match?
  **A:** 6% of $50,000 = $3,000; 50% of that = $1,500 per year.
- **Q:** Value after 10 years at 7%?
  **A:** About $20,725 (=FV(7%,10,-1500)).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Roth for teens
*Student:* Explain how a teen with earned income can open a custodial Roth IRA and project a $1,000 contribution to age 65.

*How to teach:* Explain custodial Roth IRAs for teens with earned income; project $1,000 to age 65.

- **Q:** $1,000 at 7% from age 17 to 65?
  **A:** $1,000 × 1.07^48 ≈ $25,700.
- **Q:** Can a teen contribute $7,000 to a Roth IRA if they earned $3,000?
  **A:** No — contributions can't exceed earned income, so $3,000 maximum.

### Day 4 (Thursday): CalSavers and Social Security
*Student:* Research California's CalSavers program and read your projected Social Security benefits at ssa.gov examples.

*How to teach:* Research CalSavers and Social Security examples.

- **Q:** Who is CalSavers for?
  **A:** California workers whose employers don't offer a retirement plan; employers must offer a plan or register for CalSavers, and workers are auto-enrolled into a Roth IRA (they can opt out).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Unit 6 test on investing and retirement accounts.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test in the key.

- **Q:** What is vesting?
  **A:** The schedule by which you gain full ownership of employer contributions in a retirement plan.

**Assessment:** Unit 6 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 6 test answers: 1) Saver A vs. B lesson: start early. 2) Stock/bond/fund definitions. 3) Diversification reduces risk. 4) Expense ratio meaning; 1% vs. 0.05% over decades costs hundreds of thousands. 5) Traditional vs. Roth tax treatment. 6) Match: 50% up to 6% on $50,000 → employee contributes $3,000, employer adds $1,500 per year. 7) $1,500/year for 10 years at 7% → ≈ $20,725. 8) $1,000 at 7% from 17 to 65 (48 years) → ≈ $25,700. 9) Vesting = how long you must work to own employer contributions. 10) CalSavers = California's state-run retirement savings program (Roth IRA by default) for workers whose employers don't offer a plan. Mastery 8+/10.
