# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 12

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Satire: Swift and Pope

**Prepare:** Read 'A Modest Proposal' (Project Gutenberg) and Canto 1 of 'The Rape of the Lock.' Pick an appropriate modern satire (e.g., a news-satire article) and preview it. Parental note: Swift's subject (eating babies) is deliberately shocking.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Swift really proposed eating children. The persona's proposal is ironic; Swift's targets are English landlords and Irish indifference.
- Confusing Horatian (gentle) and Juvenalian (bitter) satire. Pope is Horatian; Swift is Juvenalian.
- Writing satire without a target. Every satire needs a real problem and implied solution.

### Day 1 (Monday): Age of Reason
*Student:* Read a short overview of the Enlightenment and the Irish famine context. Read 'A Modest Proposal' paragraphs 1–10 and mark the persona's calm, logical tone.

*How to teach:* Explain the Irish famine context. Read paragraphs 1–10; mark the calm, logical tone. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does the proposer suggest?
  **A:** That poor Irish families sell their one-year-old children to be eaten by the wealthy.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): The proposal
*Student:* Finish 'A Modest Proposal.' Chart each satirical technique (statistics, understatement, mock-logic) and identify Swift's real proposals in paragraph 29.

*How to teach:* Finish the essay. The student charts techniques and identifies Swift's real proposals. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What are Swift's real proposals?
  **A:** In the paragraph beginning 'Therefore let no man talk to me of other expedients,' he lists them: taxing absentee landlords, using only Irish goods, rejecting foreign luxuries, and landlords showing mercy to tenants.
- **Q:** Why does the proposer claim no personal profit?
  **A:** His youngest child is nine and his wife is past childbearing.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mock epic
*Student:* Read an excerpt from Pope's 'The Rape of the Lock' and explain how epic conventions applied to a trivial event create Horatian satire.

*How to teach:* Read Pope excerpt. Explain mock epic. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What trivial event does Pope treat as epic?
  **A:** A baron cutting a lock of Belinda's hair.
- **Q:** Explain 'Puffs, Powders, Patches, Bibles, Billet-doux.'
  **A:** The list puts Bibles alongside cosmetics and love letters, satirizing a society that treats religion as just another vanity.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Modern satire
*Student:* Analyze one modern satirical article or sketch: identify the target, technique, and real argument; compare with Swift.

*How to teach:* Analyze a modern satire: target, technique, real argument; compare to Swift. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What three things should be identified in any satire?
  **A:** The target, the technique (irony, exaggeration, understatement), and the real argument.

### Day 5 (Friday): Write satire
*Student:* Write a 500-word 'modest proposal' on a current issue using a deadpan persona; include a hidden real solution.

*How to teach:* The student writes a 500-word modest proposal with a deadpan persona. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does 'deadpan' mean?
  **A:** Delivering absurd ideas with a completely serious tone.

**Assessment:** Original satire scored on persona, technique, and clear target.

**Answer key:** Satire rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): Persona — consistent deadpan voice. Technique — 3+ satirical devices. Target — clear real issue and hidden real solution. Conventions. Proficient: 3 in each.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Other trig graphs and modeling

**Prepare:** Desmos; print NOAA tide predictions (tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov) for a California station such as San Francisco or La Jolla for 2–3 days.

**Watch for:**
- Treating tangent like sine with amplitude and a period of 2π. tan x has period π and no amplitude.
- Placing secant asymptotes at the zeros of sine. sec x = 1/cos x has asymptotes where cos x = 0.
- Ferris-wheel models that start at the wrong place. If the rider boards at the bottom at t = 0, use h = center − radius·cos(2πt/P).

### Day 1 (Monday): Tangent graphs
*Student:* Graph y = tan x and transformations; identify asymptotes and period.

*How to teach:* Graph y = tan x, noting asymptotes at x = π/2 + kπ and zeros at kπ. Transform to y = tan(2x) and y = tan(x) + 1. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Period and asymptotes of y = tan(2x)?
  **A:** Period π/2; asymptotes x = π/4 + kπ/2.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Reciprocal functions
*Student:* Graph secant and cosecant as reciprocals of cosine and sine and identify their asymptotes.

*How to teach:* Graph cos x lightly, then draw sec x as its reciprocal: asymptotes where cos x = 0, U-shapes touching at the max/min. Repeat for csc and sin.

- **Q:** Where are the asymptotes of y = csc x?
  **A:** Where sin x = 0: x = kπ.
- **Q:** What is the range of y = sec x?
  **A:** (−∞, −1] ∪ [1, ∞).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ferris wheel
*Student:* Write a height-versus-time function for a rider on a Ferris wheel with given diameter, center height, and rotation period.

*How to teach:* Model: diameter 50 m, center 30 m above the ground, one rotation every 10 min, boarding at the bottom: h(t) = 30 − 25 cos(πt/5). The student writes and checks values at quarter turns.

- **Q:** Using h(t) = 30 − 25 cos(πt/5), find h(0), h(2.5), and h(5).
  **A:** h(0) = 5 m (bottom), h(2.5) = 30 m (center height), h(5) = 55 m (top).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Tides
*Student:* Use NOAA tide predictions for a California station to build and test a sinusoidal model.

*How to teach:* Use high/low tide times and heights to estimate amplitude, midline, and period (about 12.4 hours for a semidiurnal tide). Fit and compare predictions with NOAA's.

- **Q:** High tide 5.8 ft and low tide 0.2 ft. Amplitude and midline?
  **A:** Amplitude 2.8 ft; midline 3.0 ft.
- **Q:** Why might a single sinusoid fit California tides only roughly?
  **A:** California tides are mixed semidiurnal — the two daily highs (and lows) differ in height — so a single sinusoid can't capture both.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Complete a mixed graphing review and correct errors.

*How to teach:* Mixed graphing review; the student corrects errors with the key and explains one correction aloud. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Write a sine model with amplitude 4, period 6, midline y = −1.
  **A:** y = 4 sin(πx/3) − 1

**Assessment:** Ferris wheel and tide modeling tasks.

**Answer key:** Ferris wheel rubric: correct center (midline), radius (amplitude), period → b = 2π/P, correct starting position; e.g. h(t) = 30 − 25cos(πt/5) with h(0) = 5, h(5) = 55. Tide rubric: amplitude = (high − low)/2, midline = (high + low)/2, period ≈ 12.4 h, model compared to at least 3 NOAA predictions with errors stated. 4 = all parts correct and explained; 3 = small errors; 2 = one major parameter wrong; 1 = incomplete.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Earth's energy budget and climate drivers

**Prepare:** Two jars, plastic wrap, 2 thermometers, lamp, timer; PhET 'Greenhouse Effect' simulation; NASA Earth energy budget diagram (incoming ~340 W/m²); Vostok or EPICA ice-core temperature/CO₂ graph.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the greenhouse effect is the same as the ozone hole. They are separate issues.
- Believing greenhouse gases trap heat like a glass greenhouse. A real greenhouse mainly blocks convection; greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit infrared radiation.
- Assuming Milankovitch cycles explain current warming. They act over tens of thousands of years; current warming is far faster and tracks CO₂.

### Day 1 (Monday): Energy budget
*Student:* Label NASA's Earth energy budget diagram and confirm incoming and outgoing energy balance.

*How to teach:* Label NASA's budget: ~340 W/m² incoming; ~100 reflected (≈29–30%); ~240 absorbed by atmosphere and surface; ~240 emitted to space as infrared. Confirm balance.

- **Q:** If incoming is 340 W/m² and 100 W/m² is reflected, what must be emitted as infrared for balance?
  **A:** 240 W/m².
- **Q:** What is Earth's albedo from these numbers?
  **A:** 100/340 ≈ 0.29 (29%).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Greenhouse jar
*Student:* Compare temperatures in a covered vs. uncovered jar under a lamp for 30 minutes and graph results; discuss limits of the model.

*How to teach:* Covered vs. uncovered jar under a lamp, 30 minutes, readings every 3 minutes. Graph; discuss limits (wrap blocks convection — not identical to the atmospheric greenhouse effect).

- **Q:** What is a limitation of the jar model?
  **A:** The plastic wrap mainly traps warm air (stops convection) rather than absorbing and re-emitting infrared like greenhouse gases.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Greenhouse simulation
*Student:* Use PhET Greenhouse Effect to vary greenhouse gas concentration and record equilibrium temperature.

*How to teach:* In PhET, vary greenhouse gas concentration and record equilibrium temperature.

- **Q:** Why does more greenhouse gas raise the equilibrium temperature?
  **A:** More IR is absorbed and re-radiated back toward the surface, so the surface must warm until outgoing energy again balances incoming.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Long-term drivers
*Student:* Model Milankovitch cycles (eccentricity, tilt, precession) and match them to ice-age cycles in Vostok ice-core data.

*How to teach:* Milankovitch: eccentricity (~100,000 yr), obliquity/tilt (22.1°–24.5°, ~41,000 yr), precession (~23,000 yr). Match to ice-age cycles (~100,000-year glacial cycles over the last ~800,000 years) in ice-core data.

- **Q:** Name the three Milankovitch cycles and their approximate periods.
  **A:** Eccentricity ~100,000 years; obliquity (tilt) ~41,000 years; precession ~23,000 years (19,000–26,000).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write an explanation of how changes in energy flow at different time scales change climate; take a quiz.

*How to teach:* Student explains how energy-flow changes at different time scales change climate; quiz.

- **Q:** Give one climate driver for each time scale: years, millennia, millions of years.
  **A:** Years: volcanic aerosols, El Niño, solar cycle. Tens of thousands of years: Milankovitch orbital cycles. Millions of years: plate tectonics (continent positions, mountain building and weathering), long-term CO₂ changes, gradual brightening of the Sun.

**Assessment:** Energy flow explanation and quiz.

**Answer key:** Energy flow explanation rubric. 4 – Uses the energy budget to explain balance; explains greenhouse mechanism (IR absorption/re-emission); describes drivers at short (volcanoes, solar), intermediate (Milankovitch), and long (tectonic, solar evolution) scales with evidence; discusses human CO₂ as current driver. 3 (proficient) – Accurate budget, mechanism, and three time scales. 2 – Partial. 1 – Inaccurate. Quiz key: 1) 340 in, ~100 reflected, ~240 out. 2) Albedo ~0.3. 3) Greenhouse gases: CO₂, CH₄, H₂O, N₂O. 4) Milankovitch periods. 5) Jar model limitation.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Voting, direct democracy, and the Electoral College

**Prepare:** Visit registertovote.ca.gov. Get a recent Official Voter Information Guide. Explore Citizens Redistricting Commission maps. Prepare Unit 5 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 16-year-olds can vote in California. They can pre-register; registration activates at 18.
- Thinking the popular vote elects the president directly.
- Confusing gerrymandering with apportionment.

### Day 1 (Monday): Register
*Student:* Learn California's voter pre-registration at 16–17 and registration at 18; complete or walk through the registertovote.ca.gov process.

*How to teach:* Walk through registration. Explain pre-registration. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** At what age can Californians pre-register?
  **A:** 16 or 17; they're automatically registered at 18.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ballot measures
*Student:* Read a recent California ballot proposition in the Official Voter Guide, including LAO analysis and pro/con arguments, and decide how you would vote.

*How to teach:* Read a proposition in the guide with LAO analysis and pro/con. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does the LAO provide?
  **A:** Nonpartisan analysis of a measure's fiscal effects.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Recall and referendum
*Student:* Compare the 2003 and 2021 gubernatorial recall elections and the process for qualifying measures.

*How to teach:* Compare the 2003 and 2021 recalls. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What happened in 2003?
  **A:** Gov. Gray Davis was recalled and Arnold Schwarzenegger elected.
- **Q:** What happened in 2021?
  **A:** Gov. Gavin Newsom survived, with about 62% voting no.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Redistricting
*Student:* Explore maps from California's Citizens Redistricting Commission and explain how gerrymandering works.

*How to teach:* Explore redistricting maps; explain gerrymandering. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What are 'packing' and 'cracking'?
  **A:** Packing concentrates opposing voters into few districts; cracking splits them across many.
- **Q:** Who draws California's districts?
  **A:** The 14-member Citizens Redistricting Commission (Props 11 and 20) draws legislative maps; note that voters approved Prop 50 in 2025 to use legislature-drawn congressional maps temporarily — check current status.

### Day 5 (Friday): Electoral College
*Student:* Calculate electoral votes for a sample election and debate keeping vs. abolishing the Electoral College; take the Unit 5 test.

*How to teach:* Calculate electoral votes and debate; take the Unit 5 test. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How many electoral votes are there and how many to win?
  **A:** 538; 270.
- **Q:** How many electoral votes does California have (2024–2030)?
  **A:** 54.

**Assessment:** Ballot-measure analysis and Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Ballot-measure analysis rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): summary, fiscal effect, both arguments, reasoned decision. Unit 5 test: parties, primaries, Citizens United, registration, recall, redistricting, Electoral College.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Defining an inquiry

**Prepare:** Sketchbook; AP 2-D Art and Design sample Sustained Investigations and scoring guidelines (AP Central, free). Note the written prompts: identify the inquiry and describe how it guided the work; describe ways practice, experimentation, and revision guided the investigation.

**Watch for:**
- Picking a theme instead of an inquiry. An inquiry is a question to explore ('How can I show memory fading using layered transparent media?'), not just a topic ('nature').
- Planning 15 versions of the same picture. Each piece should push the question forward.
- Choosing an inquiry for what judges might like rather than personal meaning.

### Day 1 (Monday): Study examples
*Student:* Review three AP sample Sustained Investigations and note how each inquiry guided the work.

*How to teach:* Review three AP sample Sustained Investigations and note how each inquiry guided the work and how pieces evolved.

- **Q:** What is a Sustained Investigation?
  **A:** A body of work (15 images, including process) exploring a single inquiry through practice, experimentation, and revision.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Brainstorm
*Student:* Brainstorm 10 inquiry questions rooted in your life, culture, or community.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm 10 inquiry questions from life, culture, or community.

- **Q:** Turn the topic 'my grandmother's kitchen' into an inquiry question.
  **A:** Example: 'How can recipes, objects, and handwriting from my grandmother's kitchen be combined to show how family memory is passed down?'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Narrow
*Student:* Narrow to one question and write why it matters to you.

*How to teach:* Narrow to one and write why it matters.

- **Q:** What makes an inquiry strong?
  **A:** It is open-ended, personally meaningful, and visually explorable in many ways and materials.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Planning
*Student:* Make 15 thumbnail ideas responding to your question.

*How to teach:* 15 thumbnails responding to the question.

- **Q:** Why make many thumbnails before starting?

### Day 5 (Friday): Proposal
*Student:* Write a one-page proposal: inquiry, materials, artists to research, and a 15-piece plan.

*How to teach:* One-page proposal: inquiry, materials, artists to research, a 15-piece plan.

  **A:** A clear inquiry; practice, experimentation, and revision; and synthesis of materials, processes, and ideas (with 2-D skills).

**Assessment:** Sustained Investigation proposal.

**Answer key:** Sustained Investigation proposal rubric. 4 – Open-ended, personal inquiry question; explains significance; varied materials and processes; three relevant artists; 15-piece plan showing development (experimentation and revision); 15 thumbnails. 3 (proficient) – Clear inquiry, materials, artists, and plan. 2 – Topic rather than inquiry or thin plan. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: 5K peak and taper

**Prepare:** Running shoes; measure 1 km (2.5 laps of a 400 m track). Plan race-day logistics.

**Watch for:**
- Doing a hard workout in race week. Taper reduces volume but keeps short race-pace work.
- Trying new foods or shoes on race day. Use only what's been tested in training.
- Skipping the warm-up because the race is 'long.' Warm-up still matters.

### Day 1 (Monday): Race pace
*Student:* Run 3 × 1 km at goal race pace.

*How to teach:* 3 × 1 km at goal race pace with 2–3 minutes recovery.

- **Q:** Goal 5K time is 25:00. What is goal pace per km?
  **A:** 5:00 per km.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Easy
*Student:* Run 20 minutes easy plus strides.

*How to teach:* 20 minutes easy plus 4–6 strides.

- **Q:** What are strides?
  **A:** Short (15–20 s) relaxed accelerations to near fast pace, used to stay sharp without fatigue.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cross-train
*Student:* Do 30 minutes of easy swimming or cycling.

*How to teach:* 30 minutes easy swimming or cycling.

- **Q:** Why reduce volume during a taper?
  **A:** To shed fatigue while maintaining fitness so you're fresh on race day.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Race prep
*Student:* Plan race-day nutrition, gear, and warm-up; do a 15-minute shakeout.

*How to teach:* Plan race-day nutrition (familiar carb-rich meal 2–3 hours before), gear, warm-up; 15-minute shakeout.

- **Q:** What is a good pre-race meal timing and type?
  **A:** 2–3 hours before: familiar, carbohydrate-rich, low in fat and fiber (e.g., oatmeal with banana, toast with honey).

### Day 5 (Friday): Rest
*Student:* Do a light mobility routine and visualization.

*How to teach:* Light mobility and visualization of the race.

- **Q:** How does visualization help?
  **A:** Mentally rehearsing the race builds confidence and prepares responses to challenges.

**Assessment:** Race plan.

**Answer key:** Race plan rubric. 4 – Goal time and splits computed correctly from training data, pacing strategy, warm-up, nutrition/hydration timing, gear checklist, contingency plan, mental strategy. 3 (proficient) – Goal pace, warm-up, nutrition, gear. 2 – Vague. 1 – Incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Insurance basics & health insurance

**Prepare:** Find an NGPF risk-pooling activity and Khan Academy insurance videos. Prepare two sample health plans (below) and open Covered California's 'Shop and Compare' tool.

**Watch for:**
- Choosing the plan with the lowest premium automatically. Total cost = premiums + out-of-pocket costs; a high-deductible plan can cost more if you need care.
- Thinking the deductible is the most you can pay. The out-of-pocket maximum is the cap on cost-sharing for covered in-network care in a year (premiums don't count toward it).
- Confusing copay and coinsurance. Copay is a fixed dollar amount; coinsurance is a percentage.

### Day 1 (Monday): How insurance works
*Student:* Play an NGPF risk-pooling simulation and explain in writing why insurers can pay large claims.

*How to teach:* Run the risk-pooling simulation; the student explains why insurers can pay large claims.

- **Q:** How does risk pooling work?
  **A:** Many people pay premiums into a pool; only a few have large losses in a given year, so the pool covers them.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Insurance math
*Student:* Calculate what you pay for three medical scenarios under a high-deductible vs. low-deductible plan.

*How to teach:* Calculate costs for three scenarios under Plan A and Plan B (see key).

- **Q:** Under Plan A, what do you pay in a year with $5,000 of care?
  **A:** Premiums $4,200 + $500 deductible + 20% × $4,500 = $900 → $5,600.
- **Q:** Under Plan B, same year?
  **A:** Premiums $2,400 + $3,000 deductible + 20% × $2,000 = $400 → $5,800.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Health coverage options
*Student:* Compare staying on a parent's plan to age 26, student health plans, employer plans, Covered California, and Medi-Cal.

*How to teach:* Compare coverage options: parent's plan to 26, student health plans, employer plans, Covered California (with subsidies), Medi-Cal (low income).

- **Q:** Until what age can a young adult stay on a parent's health plan?
  **A:** Age 26.
- **Q:** What is Covered California?
  **A:** California's health insurance marketplace, where people can buy plans and get federal and state financial help.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Choosing a plan
*Student:* Use Covered California's shop-and-compare tool for a sample 19-year-old and choose a plan with a written justification.

*How to teach:* Use Covered California's tool for a sample 19-year-old and choose a plan with a written justification.

- **Q:** What do the metal tiers (Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum) mean?
  **A:** How costs are shared: Bronze has the lowest premiums but highest out-of-pocket costs; Platinum the highest premiums and lowest out-of-pocket costs.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on insurance vocabulary and a health-plan cost comparison problem.

*How to teach:* Give the insurance quiz and cost-comparison problem in the key.

- **Q:** What's the difference between a copay and coinsurance?
  **A:** A copay is a fixed amount (e.g. $30 per visit); coinsurance is a percentage of the cost (e.g. 20%).

**Assessment:** Insurance quiz and plan-choice justification.

**Answer key:** Insurance quiz: 1) Premium = regular payment to keep coverage. 2) Deductible = amount you pay before insurance starts sharing costs. 3) Copay = fixed fee per service. 4) Coinsurance = your percentage share after the deductible. 5) Out-of-pocket maximum = yearly cap on what you pay for covered care. 6) Young adults can stay on a parent's plan until age 26. Cost comparison (Plan A: $350/month premium, $500 deductible, 20% coinsurance, $3,000 OOP max; Plan B: $200/month, $3,000 deductible, 20% coinsurance, $6,000 OOP max): $300 of care → A $4,500, B $2,700; $5,000 of care → A $4,200 + $1,400 = $5,600, B $2,400 + $3,400 = $5,800; $50,000 of care → A $4,200 + $3,000 = $7,200, B $2,400 + $6,000 = $8,400. Plan-choice justification: proficient answers compare total annual cost under likely and worst-case scenarios and consider doctors in network.
