# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 1

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Reading personal essays as models

**Prepare:** Find Brian Doyle's 'Joyas Voladoras' (widely anthologized; originally in The American Scholar) and read it first. Print the UC Personal Insight Question list (admission.universityofcalifornia.edu). Set up a writing notebook.

**Watch for:**
- Students think a college essay must describe a dramatic achievement. Ordinary moments told with specific detail and honest reflection are usually stronger.
- Telling instead of showing ('I am hardworking'). Ask: 'What would a camera have seen?' and have them write that scene.
- All story, no reflection (or the reverse). Aim for roughly half narrative, half insight.

### Day 1 (Monday): What makes a voice
*Student:* Read Brian Doyle's short essay 'Joyas Voladoras' (or any short personal essay from the library) and annotate three moments where a concrete detail carries emotion. Write a paragraph on the essay's tone.

*How to teach:* Read 'Joyas Voladoras' aloud together. Model one annotation (e.g., the hummingbird heart detail) and say how the concrete fact carries feeling; the student finds three more and writes a tone paragraph. Check that each annotation names both the detail and the emotion it creates.

- **Q:** How does Doyle move through the essay, and where does it end up?
  **A:** From the tiny, fast-beating hearts of hummingbirds to the enormous heart of the blue whale, and finally to the human heart — the essay turns scientific facts into a meditation on how fragile and vulnerable our emotional hearts are.
- **Q:** Describe the essay's tone in two words with evidence.
  **A:** Wondering and tender — the precise, awed facts about animal hearts build to a gentle, aching close about human love and pain.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Life inventory
*Student:* Complete a 'life inventory' brainstorm: 10 places, 10 people, 10 challenges, 10 things you do without being asked. Star the three stories with the most specific memories.

*How to teach:* Explain that the best essay stories come from specific memories. The student completes all four lists of 10, then stars the three with the most sensory detail. Check: can they tell each starred story with a specific time and place?

- **Q:** What makes a memory a strong candidate for an essay?
  **A:** It is specific (a particular moment, place, people), shows the student acting or changing, and leads to genuine reflection.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Show, then reflect
*Student:* Study the narrative-to-reflection ratio by highlighting story sentences vs. insight sentences in a model essay. Freewrite 300 words on one starred story, ending with what it taught you.

*How to teach:* With two highlighters, mark story sentences and insight sentences in a model essay and discuss the ratio. The student freewrites 300 words on one starred story ending with what it taught them.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a narrative sentence and a reflective sentence?
  **A:** A narrative sentence reports what happened ('I dropped the tray'); a reflective sentence explains meaning ('I learned that asking for help is a skill, not a weakness').

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: sentence variety
*Student:* Mini-lesson on varying sentence openings (participial phrase, subordinate clause, prepositional phrase). Revise your freewrite so no two consecutive sentences begin the same way.

*How to teach:* Model three openings: participial phrase ('Gripping the rail, I…'), subordinate clause ('When the power failed, …'), prepositional phrase ('Behind the barn, …'). The student revises the freewrite so no two consecutive sentences start the same way; check by listing first words.

- **Q:** Rewrite 'I walked into the kitchen. I saw my grandmother.' with varied openings.
  **A:** Sample: 'Walking into the kitchen, I saw my grandmother at the stove.' or 'When I walked into the kitchen, my grandmother was already at the stove.'
- **Q:** Identify the opening type: 'After the game ended, we sat in silence.'
  **A:** Subordinate (adverb) clause.

### Day 5 (Friday): Read-aloud & check
*Student:* Read your freewrite aloud to a parent and get two 'I want to know more about…' questions. Submit the freewrite with a 3-sentence self-reflection on voice.

*How to teach:* The student reads aloud; you ask two 'I want to know more about…' questions. They submit the freewrite with a 3-sentence voice reflection.

- **Q:** What is 'voice' in a personal essay?
  **A:** The writer's distinct personality on the page — word choice, rhythm, humor, and perspective that sound like that specific person.

**Assessment:** Freewrite scored on specific detail and reflection; brainstorm inventory complete.

**Answer key:** Inventory: complete if all 40 items listed and 3 starred. Freewrite rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): Detail — 4 = concrete sensory details place the reader in a specific moment; 2 = mostly general statements. Reflection — 4 = ending offers a genuine, specific insight, not a cliché; 2 = moral tacked on ('it made me stronger'). Proficient: 3 in both.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Functions, domain, and key features

**Prepare:** Set up a free Desmos account and graph paper. Preview OpenStax Precalculus 2e sections 1.1–1.2 (functions, domain and range). Prepare 12 relations (mappings, tables, graphs) for Day 1 — include at least 3 that fail the vertical-line test.

**Watch for:**
- Students think a function cannot repeat outputs. Correct: inputs may not repeat with different outputs; two inputs can share an output (y = x² gives f(2) = f(−2) = 4).
- Forgetting domain restrictions: an even root needs a nonnegative radicand and a denominator cannot be 0. Have them ask 'What breaks this formula?' before writing the domain.
- Mixing brackets and parentheses in interval notation. Remind them ∞ always gets a parenthesis and an endpoint gets a bracket only if it is included.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is a function?
*Student:* Review function definition with mappings, tables, and the vertical-line test; classify 12 relations as functions or not and explain each.

*How to teach:* Show a mapping diagram, a table, and a graph; say 'a function gives each input exactly one output.' Model the vertical-line test on a circle (fails) and a parabola (passes). The student classifies the 12 relations and writes a one-sentence reason for each; check that each 'not a function' names the input with two outputs.

- **Q:** Is {(1, 2), (2, 2), (1, 3)} a function? Why?
  **A:** No — the input 1 is paired with two different outputs (2 and 3). The repeated output 2 is fine.
- **Q:** Does x² + y² = 25 define y as a function of x?
  **A:** No. A vertical line such as x = 3 hits the circle twice, at (3, 4) and (3, −4).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Domain and range
*Student:* Find domain and range in interval notation for 15 functions including square-root, rational, and piecewise examples.

*How to teach:* Model finding domain by asking what inputs are illegal, then write it in interval notation; find range from the graph in Desmos. The student completes the 15 functions; check each answer by graphing in Desmos and tracing the leftmost/rightmost and lowest/highest points.

- **Q:** Find the domain of f(x) = √(x − 3) and of g(x) = 1/(x + 2) in interval notation.
  **A:** f: [3, ∞). g: (−∞, −2) ∪ (−2, ∞).
- **Q:** Find the range of y = x² − 4.
  **A:** [−4, ∞) — the vertex (0, −4) is the minimum.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Reading graphs
*Student:* Analyze 6 real-context graphs (e.g., temperature over a day) for intercepts, increasing/decreasing intervals, and relative extrema.

*How to teach:* Display a temperature-vs-time graph and narrate it: where it crosses axes, where it rises and falls, and its highs and lows, with units. The student analyzes the 6 graphs and writes intervals of increase/decrease using x-values only. Check that intervals are written in terms of x (time), not y.

- **Q:** A ball's height is h(t) = −16t² + 64t (feet, seconds). Give the t-intercepts, the interval where h increases, and the maximum.
  **A:** Intercepts t = 0 and t = 4; increasing on (0, 2); maximum height 64 ft at t = 2 s.
- **Q:** What is the difference between a relative maximum and the maximum value?
  **A:** A relative maximum is the highest point in its neighborhood; the (absolute) maximum is the highest output over the whole domain. A graph can have several relative maxima but only one maximum value.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Piecewise functions
*Student:* Graph piecewise functions by hand, then model a cell-phone or tax-bracket plan as a piecewise function in Desmos.

*How to teach:* Model graphing f(x) = {x + 2 if x < 1; 3 if x ≥ 1}, using an open circle at (1, 3) on the first piece and a closed dot at (1, 3) on the second. The student graphs the practice set by hand, then enters a phone plan in Desmos using braces, e.g. y = {x ≤ 5: 30, 30 + 10(x − 5)}. Check endpoints: each x must have only one dot filled in.

- **Q:** A plan costs $30 for up to 5 GB and $10 per GB beyond 5. Write C(g) as a piecewise function and find C(8).
  **A:** C(g) = 30 for 0 ≤ g ≤ 5; C(g) = 30 + 10(g − 5) for g > 5. C(8) = 30 + 30 = $60.
- **Q:** Evaluate f(1) for f(x) = {x² if x < 1; 2x + 1 if x ≥ 1}.
  **A:** f(1) = 2(1) + 1 = 3 (use the piece whose condition includes 1).

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Take a quiz on domain, range, interval notation, and key features.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz below closed-notes (30 minutes), then grade it together and have the student redo missed items. Note any interval-notation errors for next week's warm-ups.

- **Q:** Write 'all real numbers greater than −1 and at most 4' in interval notation.
  **A:** (−1, 4]

**Assessment:** Quiz on domain, range, and key features.

**Answer key:** Quiz (6 items, use these): 1) Is {(1,2),(2,2),(1,3)} a function? No — 1 has two outputs. 2) Domain of f(x) = √(x − 3): [3, ∞). 3) Domain of g(x) = 1/(x + 2): (−∞, −2) ∪ (−2, ∞). 4) Range of y = |x| + 1: [1, ∞). 5) For h(t) = −16t² + 64t: zeros t = 0 and 4, increasing on (0, 2), decreasing on (2, 4), max 64 at t = 2. 6) C(g) = 30 if 0 ≤ g ≤ 5, 30 + 10(g − 5) if g > 5; C(8) = 60. Score 2 points each (1 for method, 1 for correct notation); 10–12 proficient.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: The Sun: fusion and energy

**Prepare:** Gather two colors of clay, a flashlight, and cardboard and foil for a pinhole projector. Preview NASA's Sun pages and the live Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO) image site. Useful constants: c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s; 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; H atom 1.00783 u; He-4 atom 4.00260 u; Sun's luminosity 3.83 × 10²⁶ W; mass 1.99 × 10³⁰ kg. SAFETY: never look at the Sun directly or through binoculars/telescopes; view only the projected image.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Sun 'burns' like a fire. It is powered by nuclear fusion, not chemical combustion (no oxygen involved).
- Believing light from the core reaches us in 8 minutes. Energy takes on the order of 100,000+ years to random-walk out of the radiative zone; only the trip from the surface to Earth takes about 8.3 minutes.
- Expecting the Sun to explode as a supernova. It is too low-mass; it will become a red giant, then a white dwarf.

### Day 1 (Monday): The Sun's layers
*Student:* Read about the Sun's structure and build a cross-section clay model labeling core, radiative zone, convection zone, photosphere, and corona.

*How to teach:* Read about the Sun's layers. The student builds a clay cross-section labeling core (fusion, ~15 million K), radiative zone, convection zone, photosphere (visible surface, ~5,800 K), chromosphere, and corona. Check the order from center outward.

- **Q:** List the Sun's layers from the center outward.
  **A:** Core, radiative zone, convection zone, photosphere, chromosphere, corona.
- **Q:** How is energy transported in the radiative zone vs. the convection zone?
  **A:** Radiative zone: by photons slowly absorbed and re-emitted (radiation). Convection zone: by rising hot plasma and sinking cooler plasma (convection).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Fusion model
*Student:* Model the proton-proton chain with clay balls (protons/neutrons) showing 4 H → 1 He; calculate the mass lost and energy released with E = mc².

*How to teach:* Model 4 ¹H → ¹He with clay (two protons become neutrons, releasing positrons and neutrinos). Then compute the mass defect and energy with E = mc².

- **Q:** Calculate the mass lost and energy released when 4 hydrogen atoms (1.00783 u each) form one helium-4 atom (4.00260 u).
  **A:** 4 × 1.00783 = 4.03132 u; Δm = 0.02872 u (≈0.7%). Δm = 0.02872 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg ≈ 4.77 × 10⁻²⁹ kg; E = mc² ≈ 4.3 × 10⁻¹² J (≈26.7 MeV).
- **Q:** The Sun's luminosity is 3.83 × 10²⁶ W. How much mass does it convert to energy each second?
  **A:** m = E/c² = 3.83 × 10²⁶ ÷ (9.0 × 10¹⁶) ≈ 4.3 × 10⁹ kg per second (about 4 million metric tons).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Observe sunspots
*Student:* Build a pinhole projector and safely observe the Sun's disk; compare with today's NASA SDO image and sketch sunspots.

*How to teach:* Build the pinhole projector (pinhole in foil, image on white card 1 m away) and observe the solar disk safely; compare with today's SDO HMI image and sketch sunspots.

- **Q:** Why do sunspots appear dark?
  **A:** They are cooler (about 3,500–4,500 K) than the surrounding photosphere because strong magnetic fields inhibit convection; they're still very bright, just darker by contrast.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sun's life span
*Student:* Use the Sun's mass, luminosity, and fusion rate to estimate its main-sequence life (~10 billion years) and draw a model of its future (red giant, white dwarf).

*How to teach:* Estimate main-sequence life: about 10% of the Sun's mass (core hydrogen) can fuse, converting 0.7% of it to energy. Lifetime = 0.1 × 0.007 × Mc² ÷ L. Draw its future.

- **Q:** Estimate the Sun's main-sequence lifetime with the formula above.
  **A:** 0.1 × 0.007 × 1.99 × 10³⁰ × 9.0 × 10¹⁶ ≈ 1.25 × 10⁴⁴ J; ÷ 3.83 × 10²⁶ W ≈ 3.3 × 10¹⁷ s ≈ 1.0 × 10¹⁰ years (~10 billion).
- **Q:** What will happen to the Sun at the end of its main-sequence life?
  **A:** In about 5 billion years it will exhaust core hydrogen, swell into a red giant, shed its outer layers as a planetary nebula, and leave a white dwarf.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a CER explaining how energy from fusion in the core reaches Earth and take a short quiz.

*How to teach:* Student writes a CER on how fusion energy reaches Earth; give a short quiz.

- **Q:** Trace energy from the core to Earth.
  **A:** Fusion in the core releases energy (gamma rays/neutrinos) → photons slowly diffuse through the radiative zone → convection carries energy to the photosphere → it radiates as electromagnetic radiation (mostly visible, IR, UV) across space in about 8.3 minutes to Earth.

**Assessment:** Fusion model and CER on solar energy transfer.

**Answer key:** Fusion model: proficient = shows 4 H → He-4 with conservation of charge noted (2 protons → neutrons, positrons, neutrinos) and correct Δm ≈ 0.029 u, E ≈ 4.3 × 10⁻¹² J. CER rubric: Claim – fusion energy reaches Earth as electromagnetic radiation; Evidence – E = mc² calculation, layer structure (radiative/convective), sunlight takes ~8.3 min; Reasoning – explains radiation, convection, and radiation through space. 4 = all parts accurate with numbers; 3 = accurate with minor gaps; 2 = missing a transfer step; 1 = inaccurate. Quiz key: 1) Fusion of H to He in the core. 2) Layers in order. 3) Sunspots cooler due to magnetic fields. 4) ~10-billion-year lifetime, now ~4.6 billion years old. 5) Future: red giant → white dwarf.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Roots of American government

**Prepare:** Print the Declaration of Independence (archives.gov), short excerpts of Hobbes's Leviathan (ch. 13) and Locke's Second Treatise (chs. 2, 9), Magna Carta clause 39, and the English Bill of Rights (1689). Skim them yourself first.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Hobbes and Locke agree because both use the 'state of nature.' Hobbes concludes people need an absolute sovereign; Locke concludes government is limited and exists to protect natural rights.
- Believing the Declaration created the government. It justified independence; the Articles and later the Constitution created governments.
- Assuming the Framers copied Athens. They feared direct democracy and borrowed more from the Roman Republic and English practice.

### Day 1 (Monday): Why government?
*Student:* Read Hobbes and Locke excerpts on the state of nature and write a paragraph comparing their views on why people form governments.

*How to teach:* Read the Hobbes and Locke excerpts aloud and make a two-column chart (state of nature, why form government, limits on government). The student writes a comparison paragraph; check that it names each thinker's conclusion.

- **Q:** How does Hobbes describe life without government?
  **A:** 'Solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short' — a war of all against all, so people surrender rights to an absolute sovereign for security.
- **Q:** Why do people form government according to Locke?
  **A:** To protect natural rights to life, liberty, and property; government rests on consent and can be replaced if it violates those rights.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ancient roots
*Student:* Compare Athenian direct democracy and the Roman Republic; chart which features the Framers adopted or rejected.

*How to teach:* Explain Athenian direct democracy and the Roman Republic's elected representatives, Senate, and checks. The student charts features the Framers adopted or rejected.

- **Q:** Which feature of the Roman Republic did the Framers adopt?
  **A:** Representative government (elected officials, a Senate) and a mix of checks among offices.
- **Q:** Why did the Framers reject Athenian direct democracy?
  **A:** They feared it was unstable and open to majority passion; it also wasn't practical for a large country.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): English roots
*Student:* Read excerpts of Magna Carta (1215) and the English Bill of Rights (1689); highlight limits on the monarch that reappear in the Constitution.

*How to teach:* Read Magna Carta clause 39 and the English Bill of Rights. The student highlights limits on the monarch and finds the matching U.S. provisions.

- **Q:** What does Magna Carta (1215) clause 39 guarantee?
  **A:** No free man may be imprisoned or punished except by the lawful judgment of his peers or the law of the land — the root of due process.
- **Q:** Name two English Bill of Rights provisions that reappear in the U.S. Constitution.
  **A:** No excessive bail or cruel and unusual punishments (8th Amendment); right to petition (1st Amendment); no taxes without Parliament's consent (Congress's power to tax).

### Day 4 (Thursday): The Declaration
*Student:* Annotate the Declaration's second paragraph, identifying Locke's ideas, and categorize the grievances by type.

*How to teach:* Read the Declaration's second paragraph aloud and underline Locke's ideas. The student sorts the grievances into categories (taxation, courts, military, self-government).

- **Q:** Which Lockean ideas appear in the second paragraph?
  **A:** Unalienable rights (life, liberty, pursuit of happiness), government by consent of the governed, and the right to alter or abolish a destructive government.
- **Q:** Give one grievance about taxation and one about the military.
  **A:** 'Imposing Taxes on us without our Consent'; 'keeping among us, in times of peace, Standing Armies without the Consent of our legislatures.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz and write a 300-word response: which thinker most influenced the founding, with evidence?

*How to teach:* Give the quiz, then the 300-word response. Check that the response uses evidence from at least two documents. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** In what year was the Declaration adopted and who was its principal author?
  **A:** 1776; Thomas Jefferson.

**Assessment:** Quiz and short response.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Hobbes — absolute sovereign for security. 2) Locke — life, liberty, property; consent; right of revolution. 3) Magna Carta 1215, limits king, due process. 4) English Bill of Rights 1689. 5) Declaration 1776, Jefferson. 6) Rome — representative republic. Response rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): 4 = clear claim (usually Locke), evidence from the Declaration and one other document, explanation of influence, acknowledges other influences.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Setting up a studio practice

**Prepare:** Gather a mixed-media sketchbook, graphite pencils 2H–6B, kneaded eraser, fineliners, a mirror, and a lamp. Download the College Board AP 2-D Art and Design course overview (portfolio = Sustained Investigation, 15 images, ~60% of score; Selected Works, 5 works, ~40%). Clear a studio corner with good light (a north-facing window or a daylight-balanced bulb).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the baseline portrait should be perfect. It's a record of where you start; honesty makes growth visible in June.
- Believing talent matters more than practice. Skill grows through daily, deliberate practice; the sketchbook is the engine.
- Treating the artist statement as an afterthought. Writing about intent clarifies the work throughout the year.

### Day 1 (Monday): Studio setup
*Student:* Organize a home studio corner with good light and storage; photograph it for your process journal.

*How to teach:* Help the student organize supplies by type, set up lighting from one side to avoid glare, and photograph the space for the process journal.

- **Q:** Why set up a consistent light source in a studio?
  **A:** Consistent, directional light makes values and color judgments reliable from session to session and reduces glare.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Baseline drawing
*Student:* Draw a self-portrait from a mirror in 45 minutes as a baseline to compare at year's end.

*How to teach:* Student draws a 45-minute mirror self-portrait. Remind them to block in the overall head shape first, then features; don't erase obsessively. Date and save it.

- **Q:** Where are the eyes located on an adult head (front view)?
  **A:** About halfway between the top of the skull and the chin.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Skills inventory
*Student:* Fill two sketchbook pages with quick tests of each medium you own and rate your comfort level.

*How to teach:* Student fills two pages testing every medium (pencil grades, pen, marker, watercolor, etc.) and rates comfort 1–5.

- **Q:** What's the difference between a 2H and a 6B pencil?
  **A:** 2H is harder and lighter (good for light guidelines); 6B is softer and darker (good for deep shadows).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Portfolio goals
*Student:* Review the AP 2-D Art and Design portfolio structure and write three goals for your senior portfolio.

*How to teach:* Review the AP portfolio structure together; student writes three specific goals.

- **Q:** What are the two sections of the AP 2-D Art and Design portfolio?
  **A:** Sustained Investigation (15 images of works and process guided by an inquiry, with written responses) and Selected Works (5 works demonstrating skill and synthesis of materials, processes, and ideas).

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Write a half-page reflection on what kind of artist you want to be by June.

*How to teach:* Student writes a half-page reflection on the artist they want to become.

- **Q:** What is an artist statement?
  **A:** A short written explanation of an artist's ideas, intentions, and process that helps viewers understand the work.

**Assessment:** Baseline self-portrait and goal sheet.

**Answer key:** Baseline self-portrait and goal sheet: proficient = complete 45-minute self-portrait from observation (dated), two-page media inventory with ratings, three specific measurable goals tied to the portfolio (e.g., 'complete 15 investigation pieces by March'), and a thoughtful reflection. 4 = goals include timeline and skills to build; 2 = vague goals or incomplete portrait; 1 = missing.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Baseline fitness testing

**Prepare:** Gather a stopwatch, measuring tape, yardstick and box for sit-and-reach, a mat, and a fitness log (notebook or spreadsheet). Measure a 1-mile route (4 laps of a standard track). Print current Fitnessgram Healthy Fitness Zone (HFZ) charts for 17+ year-olds. Parental note: take body measurements privately and keep the focus on health, not appearance.

**Watch for:**
- Treating BMI as a measure of body fat or health. BMI is a weight-for-height screen; it can't distinguish muscle from fat or show fat distribution.
- Racing through cadence tests. Reps that break the 3-second cadence or form standards don't count.
- Skipping the warm-up before a max test, which lowers scores and raises injury risk.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mile run
*Student:* Warm up, then run/walk a timed mile; record time and heart rate.

*How to teach:* Lead a 5–10 minute dynamic warm-up, then time the mile. Take heart rate immediately after (15 s × 4). Record time, HR, and how it felt.

- **Q:** Which health-related fitness component does the mile test measure?
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory (aerobic) endurance.
- **Q:** You count 40 beats in 15 seconds right after the mile. What is your heart rate?
  **A:** 160 bpm.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Strength tests
*Student:* Do max 90° push-ups and curl-ups to cadence; record results.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate 90° push-ups and curl-ups (one rep per 3 seconds; stop at the second form error). Record max reps.

- **Q:** Are push-ups to cadence a test of strength or endurance?
  **A:** Mostly muscular endurance (repeated submaximal contractions), with some strength component.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Flexibility
*Student:* Perform sit-and-reach and shoulder stretch tests; record results.

*How to teach:* Back-saver sit-and-reach (each leg, best of four reaches, hold 1 s) and shoulder stretch (hands touch behind back, each side). Record.

- **Q:** What is the shoulder stretch test scored as?
  **A:** Pass/fail on each side — pass if the fingertips touch behind the back.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Body measures
*Student:* Measure resting heart rate, waist circumference, and height/weight privately; learn limits of BMI.

*How to teach:* Measure resting HR (after 5 min seated), waist circumference at the navel, and height/weight privately. Compute BMI and discuss its limits.

- **Q:** Compute BMI for 150 lb and 68 in.
  **A:** 703 × 150 ÷ 68² = 105,450 ÷ 4,624 ≈ 22.8.
- **Q:** Give two limits of BMI.
  **A:** It doesn't distinguish muscle from fat (muscular athletes may be labeled 'overweight') and doesn't show where fat is stored; it's a screening tool only.

### Day 5 (Friday): Compare to standards
*Student:* Compare results with Healthy Fitness Zone standards and record in a fitness log.

*How to teach:* Compare each score to HFZ charts and record in the fitness log with 'HFZ' or 'Needs Improvement.'

- **Q:** What is the purpose of a baseline test?
  **A:** To establish a starting point so you can set goals and measure progress.

**Assessment:** Completed baseline fitness log.

**Answer key:** Completed baseline fitness log: proficient = mile time and post-mile HR, push-ups, curl-ups, sit-and-reach (L/R), shoulder stretch (L/R), resting HR, waist circumference recorded with units and dates, each compared to HFZ, and one sentence explaining BMI's limits. 4 adds a written summary of strengths and needs; 2 = missing tests; 1 = mostly incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Income, careers & senior-year money calendar

**Prepare:** Create a free NGPF teacher account (ngpf.org) to unlock the pre-assessment and answer keys. Pull up the BLS chart 'Education pays' (bls.gov/emp/chart-unemployment-earnings-education.htm) and CaliforniaColleges.edu. IMPORTANT: confirm this year's real dates on the official sites — FAFSA and the California Dream Act Application open October 1 for the next school year; check the UC application filing period (recent cycles closed Nov 30 or Dec 1), the Cal State Apply priority window (typically October 1 to early December), and the Cal Grant deadline (historically March 2; CSAC has extended it in some recent years — check csac.ca.gov).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking salary and wage are the same. A wage is paid per hour; a salary is a fixed yearly amount regardless of hours.
- Believing the education-earnings link guarantees any individual's income. The data show averages (medians); field of study, skills and work experience also matter.
- Treating college/aid deadlines as 'spring things'. Many key applications (UC, CSU, FAFSA/CADAA) happen in the fall of senior year.

### Day 1 (Monday): Why personal finance
*Student:* Take the NGPF pre-assessment and write a money autobiography (one page) on how your family talked about money and one financial goal for age 25.

*How to teach:* Have the student take the NGPF pre-assessment (don't grade it — it's a baseline), then write a one-page money autobiography: how money was discussed at home, a money memory, and one financial goal for age 25.

- **Q:** What is a SMART financial goal? Make 'save money' SMART.
  **A:** Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound — e.g. 'Save $3,000 for an emergency fund by June 2028 by depositing $125 a month.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Earnings and education
*Student:* Use BLS median-earnings-by-education data to graph weekly earnings and unemployment rates for six education levels, then write three conclusions.

*How to teach:* Use the BLS 'Education pays' chart: graph median weekly earnings and unemployment rate for six education levels (less than high school through doctoral/professional). The student writes three conclusions.

- **Q:** What two patterns appear in the BLS education data?
  **A:** As education increases, median weekly earnings go up and unemployment rates go down.
- **Q:** Does this guarantee that a bachelor's degree means higher pay for you?
  **A:** No — these are medians; individual results depend on field, skills, location and experience.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Career salary research
*Student:* Pick three careers on CaliforniaColleges.edu and record typical California salary, required training, and projected growth for each.

*How to teach:* On CaliforniaColleges.edu, pick three careers and record California salary, training required and projected growth.

- **Q:** Convert $24/hour to an annual salary for full-time work.
  **A:** $24 × 40 × 52 = $49,920.
- **Q:** What is human capital?
