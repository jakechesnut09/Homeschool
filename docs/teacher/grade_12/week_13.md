# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 13

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Romantic poetry

**Prepare:** Print the Romantic poems. Read Wordsworth's 'The World Is Too Much With Us' for the timed explication. Prepare an AP-style 6-point rubric.

**Watch for:**
- Treating explication as summary. Explication analyzes how figurative language, sound, and structure create meaning.
- Reading 'The Tyger' and 'The Lamb' as children's poems. They are paired poems of innocence and experience.
- Misreading Keats's urn as a living scene. The urn freezes moments permanently.

### Day 1 (Monday): Blake's contraries
*Student:* Read 'The Lamb,' 'The Tyger,' and 'London.' Compare innocence and experience and annotate how 'London' critiques industrial society.

*How to teach:* Read the three Blake poems aloud. The student compares innocence and experience and annotates 'London.' Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Who made the Lamb, according to the speaker?
  **A:** God, who 'calls himself a Lamb' — Christ.
- **Q:** What are 'mind-forg'd manacles' in 'London'?
  **A:** Mental chains — limits on thought imposed by society, institutions, and people's own minds.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Wordsworth
*Student:* Read the preface definition of poetry as 'spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings' and lines from 'Tintern Abbey.' Write about a place that has changed for you.

*How to teach:* Read Wordsworth's definition and 'Tintern Abbey' lines. The student writes about a changed place. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Complete Wordsworth's definition: 'spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings…'
  **A:** '…it takes its origin from emotion recollected in tranquillity.'
- **Q:** How long has the speaker been away in 'Tintern Abbey'?
  **A:** Five years.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Shelley and Keats
*Student:* Read 'Ozymandias' and 'Ode on a Grecian Urn.' Discuss what each poem says about time, art, and permanence.

*How to teach:* Read 'Ozymandias' and 'Ode on a Grecian Urn.' Discuss permanence. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is the irony in 'Ozymandias'?
  **A:** The king commands 'Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!' but nothing remains except a broken statue and empty sand.
- **Q:** What does the urn say?
  **A:** 'Beauty is truth, truth beauty.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Explication method
*Student:* Learn the explication method (literal, figurative, sound, structure, theme). Explicate 'Ozymandias' collaboratively, line by line.

*How to teach:* Teach explication (literal, figurative, sound, structure, theme). Explicate 'Ozymandias' together. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What sonnet form does 'Ozymandias' use?
  **A:** A sonnet with an unusual rhyme scheme blending Petrarchan and Shakespearean patterns.

### Day 5 (Friday): Timed explication
*Student:* Write a 40-minute timed explication of an unseen Romantic poem (e.g., Wordsworth's 'The World Is Too Much With Us').

*How to teach:* Give the 40-minute timed explication of 'The World Is Too Much With Us.' Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does the speaker criticize?
  **A:** Materialism — 'Getting and spending, we lay waste our powers'; we are out of tune with nature.

**Assessment:** Timed poetry explication scored on an AP-style 6-point rubric.

**Answer key:** AP-style 6-point rubric: Thesis (0–1): defensible interpretation of how the poem's devices create meaning (e.g., Wordsworth contrasts commercial life with nature). Evidence and commentary (0–4): specific references — 'Getting and spending,' the Petrarchan octave/sestet turn at 'Great God! I'd rather be / A Pagan,' allusions to Proteus and Triton. Sophistication (0–1). Proficient: 4/6.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Inverse trig functions and trig equations

**Prepare:** Calculator in RADIAN mode (check!). Preview OpenStax 7.5 and 8.3. Have last week's Ferris wheel and daylight models handy. Prepare the Unit 4 test.

**Watch for:**
- Expecting arcsin to return all solutions. Inverse trig functions return one principal value (arcsin, arctan in [−π/2, π/2], arccos in [0, π]); use the unit circle to find the others.
- Dividing both sides by a trig function and losing solutions (e.g. dividing sin 2x = cos x by cos x). Factor instead.
- Calculator in degree mode. Check by computing sin(π/2) — it should be 1.

### Day 1 (Monday): Restricted domains
*Student:* Explain why sine, cosine, and tangent must be restricted to be invertible; graph arcsin, arccos, arctan.

*How to teach:* Show y = sin x fails the horizontal-line test, then restrict to [−π/2, π/2] and reflect over y = x. Repeat for cos ([0, π]) and tan ((−π/2, π/2)).

- **Q:** What is the range of y = arccos x and why?
  **A:** [0, π] — cosine is restricted to [0, π] where it is one-to-one and covers all values −1 to 1.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Evaluate inverses
*Student:* Evaluate 15 inverse trig expressions exactly and with a calculator, including compositions like sin(arccos(3/5)).

*How to teach:* Evaluate exact values with the unit circle, then compositions with a right triangle sketch. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Evaluate arcsin(1/2), arccos(−1/2), arctan(−1).
  **A:** π/6, 2π/3, −π/4.
- **Q:** Evaluate sin(arccos(3/5)).
  **A:** Draw a triangle with adjacent 3, hypotenuse 5, so opposite 4: sin = 4/5.
- **Q:** Evaluate tan(arcsin(5/13)).
  **A:** Opposite 5, hypotenuse 13, adjacent 12: 5/12.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Solve equations
*Student:* Solve linear and quadratic trig equations on [0, 2π) and write general solutions.

*How to teach:* Model 2 sin x − 1 = 0 and a quadratic 2cos²x − cos x − 1 = 0 by factoring. Write general solutions with +2πk. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Solve 2 sin x − 1 = 0 on [0, 2π) and give the general solution.
  **A:** x = π/6, 5π/6; general x = π/6 + 2πk, 5π/6 + 2πk.
- **Q:** Solve 2cos²x − cos x − 1 = 0 on [0, 2π).
  **A:** (2cos x + 1)(cos x − 1) = 0 → x = 0, 2π/3, 4π/3.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Modeling equations
*Student:* Use your Ferris wheel and daylight models to find when the height or daylight reaches a target value.

*How to teach:* Set the Ferris wheel model equal to a target height and solve with arccos, finding both times in a rotation. Repeat with the daylight model.

- **Q:** When is a rider at 42.5 m if h(t) = 30 − 25cos(πt/5)?
  **A:** cos(πt/5) = −0.5 → πt/5 = 2π/3 or 4π/3 → t = 10/3 ≈ 3.33 min and t = 20/3 ≈ 6.67 min (then every 10 min).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 4 test on trigonometric functions.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 4 test. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Why is arcsin(2) undefined?
  **A:** Sine values lie in [−1, 1], so no angle has sine 2.

**Assessment:** Unit 4 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 4 test sample key: 1) 210° = 7π/6. 2) cos(5π/3) = 1/2. 3) Arc length r = 6, θ = 2π/3: 4π. 4) y = −3sin(2(x − π/4)) + 1: A = 3, P = π, shift right π/4, midline 1. 5) Asymptotes of tan(2x): x = π/4 + kπ/2. 6) arccos(−√2/2) = 3π/4. 7) sin(arccos(3/5)) = 4/5. 8) 2cos²x − cos x − 1 = 0: 0, 2π/3, 4π/3. 9) Ferris wheel: 42.5 m at t ≈ 3.33 and 6.67 min. Scoring: 90%+ mastery, 75–89% proficient, 60–74% developing.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: The carbon cycle

**Prepare:** Bromothymol blue (aquarium/pool kits) or red cabbage indicator, straws, Elodea (aquarium store), clear tubes or jars, lamp, foil. Reservoir sizes (approx. GtC): atmosphere ~880; vegetation ~450–650; soils ~1,500–2,400; surface ocean ~900; deep ocean ~37,000; fossil fuels ~1,000–2,000+; sedimentary rocks ~tens of millions. Fluxes: photosynthesis ~120 GtC/yr; ocean–air exchange ~80–90 each way; human emissions ~10 GtC/yr. 1 ppm CO₂ ≈ 2.13 GtC.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking plants get their mass from soil. Most plant mass comes from CO₂ taken from the air.
- Believing the ocean absorbing CO₂ is harmless. It lowers ocean pH (acidification), harming shell-building organisms.
- Assuming all emitted CO₂ stays in the air. About half is absorbed by oceans and land.

### Day 1 (Monday): Reservoirs and fluxes
*Student:* Build a box-and-arrow carbon cycle model with reservoir sizes (GtC) and annual fluxes from NASA/NOAA data.

*How to teach:* Build a box-and-arrow model with reservoirs and annual fluxes.

- **Q:** Which reservoir holds the most carbon?
  **A:** Sedimentary rocks (limestone, etc.); of the active reservoirs, the deep ocean (~37,000 GtC).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): CO₂ and water
*Student:* Blow through a straw into indicator solution to watch it acidify; relate to ocean acidification.

*How to teach:* Blow through a straw into bromothymol blue (blue → green → yellow as CO₂ forms carbonic acid).

- **Q:** Write the reaction that acidifies the water.
  **A:** CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (carbonic acid) → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻; more H⁺ lowers pH.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Plants take up CO₂
*Student:* Place Elodea in acidified indicator under light and in the dark; record color changes to show photosynthesis removing CO₂.

*How to teach:* Place Elodea in acidified (yellow) indicator: one tube in light, one wrapped in foil, plus a control tube with no plant. Observe for 24 hours.

- **Q:** Predict the color change in the lit tube vs. the dark tube.
  **A:** Lit: turns blue/green as photosynthesis removes CO₂. Dark: stays yellow (or becomes more yellow) because the plant only respires, adding CO₂.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Human flux
*Student:* Calculate the net yearly increase in atmospheric carbon and compare human emissions to natural fluxes using the Keeling Curve.

*How to teach:* Use the Keeling Curve: CO₂ rises ~2.4 ppm/yr. Convert to GtC and compare to human emissions.

- **Q:** If CO₂ rises 2.4 ppm/yr and 1 ppm = 2.13 GtC, how much carbon accumulates in the air each year? What fraction of ~10 GtC human emissions is that?
  **A:** 2.4 × 2.13 ≈ 5.1 GtC/yr — about half of human emissions; the rest is absorbed by the ocean and land.
- **Q:** Why does the Keeling Curve zigzag each year?
  **A:** Northern Hemisphere plants take up CO₂ in spring/summer and release it in fall/winter.

### Day 5 (Friday): Model check
*Student:* Revise your quantitative model to include human emissions and explain what happens to excess carbon.

*How to teach:* Student revises the model to include human emissions and the fates of excess carbon.

- **Q:** Where does the excess carbon go?
  **A:** Roughly half stays in the atmosphere, about a quarter goes into the ocean (causing acidification), and about a quarter into land vegetation and soils.

**Assessment:** Quantitative carbon cycle model.

**Answer key:** Quantitative carbon cycle model rubric. 4 – Reservoirs with sizes (GtC) and fluxes (GtC/yr) from cited data, balanced natural fluxes, human fossil-fuel and land-use emissions (~10–11 GtC/yr), partition (≈half atmosphere, ≈quarter ocean, ≈quarter land), ocean acidification explained with chemistry, uses lab evidence. 3 (proficient) – Reservoirs and fluxes with numbers, human input, and fate of excess. 2 – Qualitative only. 1 – Incomplete.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Federalism and the California Constitution

**Prepare:** Open the California Constitution (leginfo.legislature.ca.gov) and an LAO budget overview. Pick a current California bill to track.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking federal law always wins. Federal law is supreme only within federal powers; states keep reserved powers.
- Assuming the California Constitution is like the U.S. one. It is far longer and amended often.
- Confusing the Assembly and Senate. The Assembly has 80 members with 2-year terms; the Senate has 40 with 4-year terms — like the U.S. House and Senate.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dividing power
*Student:* Create a Venn diagram of federal, state, and concurrent powers and discuss the 9th and 10th Amendments.

*How to teach:* Make a Venn diagram of powers; discuss the 9th and 10th Amendments. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Name a concurrent power.
  **A:** Taxing, borrowing, making courts, enforcing laws.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Federal-state conflict
*Student:* Analyze a federal-state conflict (cannabis law, vehicle emissions waivers, immigration) and how courts resolved it.

*How to teach:* Analyze a federal-state conflict. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What did Gonzales v. Raich (2005) decide?
  **A:** Congress can prohibit homegrown medical marijuana under the Commerce Clause, even where state law allows it.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): California's government
*Student:* Compare the California Constitution with the U.S. Constitution (length, amendment process, Declaration of Rights).

*How to teach:* Compare the two constitutions. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How can the California Constitution be amended?
  **A:** By legislative proposal (two-thirds of each house) or initiative (signatures equal to 8% of the last gubernatorial vote), then majority voter approval.
- **Q:** Name a right explicit in California but not the U.S. Constitution.
  **A:** Privacy (Article I, Section 1).

### Day 4 (Thursday): State lawmaking
*Student:* Track a bill in the California Legislature on leginfo.legislature.ca.gov and compare the process with Congress.

*How to teach:* Track a bill on leginfo. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How many members in the Assembly and Senate?
  **A:** 80 and 40.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz on federalism and California government.

*How to teach:* Give the federalism quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does the 10th Amendment reserve?
  **A:** Powers not delegated to the U.S. nor prohibited to states are reserved to the states or the people.

**Assessment:** Federalism quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Federal: coin money, declare war, regulate interstate commerce; state: run elections, set up local governments, regulate intrastate commerce; concurrent: tax, borrow, make courts. 2) 10th Amendment reserves undelegated powers to states or people. 3) Gonzales v. Raich (2005): federal ban on homegrown marijuana upheld under the Commerce Clause. 4) CA amendments: legislature (two-thirds each house) or initiative (8% signatures), then majority vote. 5) Privacy is explicit in CA Const. art. I §1. 6) Assembly 80 members (2-year terms), Senate 40 (4-year terms). Proficient: 5 of 6.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Artist research and influences

**Prepare:** Smithsonian Learning Lab and museum sites; sketchbook; materials for the first investigation piece. Discuss the difference between influence, homage, and appropriation.

**Watch for:**
- Copying an artist's image into your portfolio. AP prohibits plagiarism; work must move beyond references.
- Researching only famous Western artists. Look across cultures and contemporary artists.
- Treating research as separate from making. Research should change your choices.

### Day 1 (Monday): Research
*Student:* Research three artists whose work connects to your inquiry and build a visual reference page for each.

*How to teach:* Research three artists; build a reference page for each (images, process notes, connection to inquiry).

- **Q:** What is the difference between influence and appropriation?
  **A:** Influence: learning from an artist's ideas or methods in your own work. Appropriation: deliberately borrowing existing images or objects and recontextualizing them.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Process study
*Student:* Do a process study imitating one artist's technique.

*How to teach:* Process study imitating one artist's technique (not subject).

- **Q:** What is an homage?
  **A:** A work that openly honors another artist, often referencing their style or a specific work.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cultural connections
*Student:* Research a cultural or historical tradition connected to your inquiry (e.g., Chicano muralism, Japanese woodblock).

*How to teach:* Research a related cultural tradition (e.g., Chicano muralism, Japanese woodblock prints).

- **Q:** Name a feature of the Chicano mural movement.
  **A:** Large public murals (from the late 1960s–70s) celebrating Mexican-American identity, history, and political struggles, often made collaboratively with communities (e.g., Chicano Park, San Diego).

### Day 4 (Thursday): First piece
*Student:* Begin the first Sustained Investigation piece.

*How to teach:* Begin piece 1.

- **Q:** Why start piece 1 even if it isn't perfect?
  **A:** Making reveals problems and ideas that planning can't; revision is part of the investigation.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Write a process note on how research shaped your first piece.

*How to teach:* Process note: how research shaped piece 1.

- **Q:** What belongs in a process note?
  **A:** What you tried, why, what you learned, and how it changes the next step.

**Assessment:** Artist research pages.

**Answer key:** Artist research pages rubric. 4 – Three artists with images (cited), process analysis, explicit connections to inquiry, cultural tradition research, process study, and a process note showing influence on piece 1. 3 (proficient) – Three cited artists with connections and a process note. 2 – Images without analysis. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: 5K time trial

**Prepare:** Measured 5K course or local race registration. Have the mile time from week 1. Prepare the aerobic quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Judging the race only by time. Analyze pacing and effort.
- Expecting the 5K pace to equal mile pace. Longer distance = slower average pace.
- Skipping recovery after racing.

### Day 1 (Monday): 5K
*Student:* Run a timed 5K (local race or measured course) and record splits.

*How to teach:* Run the 5K; record mile or km splits.

- **Q:** How many miles is 5 km?
  **A:** About 3.1 miles.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Recovery
*Student:* Walk 30 minutes and stretch.

*How to teach:* Recovery walk 30 minutes and stretching.

- **Q:** What does active recovery do?
  **A:** Promotes blood flow and reduces stiffness without adding stress.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Analyze
*Student:* Compare 5K time to predicted time from your mile test; analyze pacing.

*How to teach:* Predict 5K time from the mile with Riegel's formula T2 = T1 × (D2/D1)^1.06 and compare to the actual result; analyze splits.

- **Q:** Using Riegel's formula, predict a 5K for an 8:00 mile.
  **A:** 8:00 × (3.107)^1.06 ≈ 8 × 3.33 ≈ 26:36.
- **Q:** Splits were 8:10, 8:40, 9:05. What happened?
  **A:** A positive split — the runner started too fast and slowed; a more even pace would likely give a faster time.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Strength
*Student:* Complete a full-body strength session.

*How to teach:* Full-body strength session.

- **Q:** Why keep strength training during running blocks?
  **A:** To maintain muscle, prevent injury, and improve running economy.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Take a quiz on aerobic training principles.

*How to teach:* Give the aerobic quiz.

- **Q:** What is lactate threshold?
  **A:** The exercise intensity above which lactate builds up in the blood faster than it can be cleared, leading to rapid fatigue.

**Assessment:** 5K result and pacing analysis.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Aerobic = with oxygen, long duration; anaerobic = short, high intensity. 2) VO2 max = max oxygen use. 3) Tempo raises lactate threshold. 4) 10% rule. 5) Taper = reduce volume before race. 6) Negative split = faster second half. 7) Karvonen formula. 8) Cross-training benefits. Pacing analysis rubric: proficient = actual time and splits, predicted time correctly computed, comparison, analysis of pacing, next goal.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Auto, renters, disability & life insurance

**Prepare:** Open the California Department of Insurance consumer pages (insurance.ca.gov). Know California's minimum auto liability limits (since January 1, 2025): $30,000 injury/death to one person, $60,000 per accident, $15,000 property damage. Find a renters insurance quote tool and an auto quote tool (no purchase). Prepare the Unit 5 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking full coverage pays everything. 'Full coverage' usually means liability plus collision and comprehensive, each with limits and deductibles.
- Thinking a landlord's insurance covers a tenant's belongings. It covers the building, not the renter's property or liability.
- Buying life insurance as a young adult with no dependents. Disability insurance (protecting income) usually matters more; life insurance matters when others depend on your income.

### Day 1 (Monday): Auto insurance
*Student:* Explain liability, collision, comprehensive, and uninsured motorist coverage and look up California's minimum liability limits.

*How to teach:* Explain liability, collision, comprehensive and uninsured motorist coverage; look up California's minimums.

- **Q:** What are California's minimum liability limits?
  **A:** $30,000 bodily injury per person, $60,000 per accident, $15,000 property damage (30/60/15).
- **Q:** Which coverage pays if your parked car is stolen?
  **A:** Comprehensive.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Auto quote lab
*Student:* Get two practice online quotes (no purchase) changing deductible and car type, and record how premiums change.

*How to teach:* Get two practice quotes, changing deductible and car type; record how premiums change.

- **Q:** What happens to the premium if you raise the deductible from $500 to $1,000?
  **A:** It goes down, because you take on more of the risk.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Renters insurance
*Student:* Inventory and value your belongings and price a renters policy; calculate cost per day of coverage.

*How to teach:* Inventory belongings and price a renters policy; compute cost per day.

- **Q:** A renters policy costs $180 a year. Cost per day?
  **A:** $180 ÷ 365 ≈ $0.49 a day.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Disability and life
*Student:* Compare term vs. whole life and explain when a young adult would need disability or life insurance.

*How to teach:* Compare term and whole life; explain when a young adult needs disability or life insurance.

- **Q:** When does a young adult need life insurance?
  **A:** When someone depends on their income (a spouse, child) or would be stuck with their debts.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Build a personal insurance plan for age 22 listing coverages, estimated premiums, and deductibles; unit test.

*How to teach:* Build the personal insurance plan for age 22 and give the Unit 5 test.

- **Q:** Why is disability insurance important for young workers?
  **A:** Your future earnings are your biggest asset; disability insurance replaces part of income if illness or injury stops you working.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test and personal insurance plan.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test answers: 1) Liability = pays for injuries/damage you cause to others. 2) Collision = damage to your car from a crash. 3) Comprehensive = non-collision damage (theft, fire, hail, vandalism). 4) Uninsured motorist = covers you if hit by a driver without insurance. 5) California minimum liability = 30/60/15. 6) Raising the deductible lowers the premium. 7) Renters insurance covers belongings and personal liability. 8) Term life = coverage for a set period, cheaper; whole life = permanent with cash value, more expensive. 9) Disability insurance replaces part of income if you can't work. 10) Premium, deductible, coinsurance definitions. Mastery 8+/10. Personal insurance plan rubric: 4 = lists health, auto (if driving), renters, disability (through employer or SDI) with estimated premiums and deductibles, explains why life insurance is or isn't needed at 22; 3 = most coverages; 2 = vague; 1 = incomplete.
