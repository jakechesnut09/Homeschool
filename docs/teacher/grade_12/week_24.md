# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 24

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Achebe's argument and postcolonial essay

**Prepare:** Obtain Achebe's 'An Image of Africa' (anthology/library) and the opening river-journey section of Heart of Darkness (Project Gutenberg). Parental preview: Conrad uses racist language and imagery, which is the subject of Achebe's critique — discuss it directly.

**Watch for:**
- Dismissing Achebe as merely offended, or dismissing Conrad as simply racist without engaging the argument. Evaluate premises and evidence.
- Confusing Marlow's views with Conrad's. Achebe argues Conrad provides no alternative frame; others argue the narrative frame creates distance — weigh both.
- Vague lens statements. Name the lens (postcolonial) and what it asks.

### Day 1 (Monday): Conrad excerpt
*Student:* Read the opening river-journey passage of Heart of Darkness and note how Africans are described.

*How to teach:* Read the river-journey passage aloud. The student notes how Africans are described (and not allowed to speak); check that they quote specific words.

- **Q:** How are Africans portrayed in the passage?
  **A:** Largely as a mass of bodies and noise — e.g., 'a whirl of black limbs' — rather than as individuals with speech and thought.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Achebe's critique
*Student:* Read 'An Image of Africa' and outline its premises, evidence, and conclusion about Conrad.

*How to teach:* Read 'An Image of Africa.' The student outlines premises, evidence, and conclusion. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is Achebe's central claim?
  **A:** That Heart of Darkness dehumanizes Africans, using Africa as a backdrop for a European's mind, and that Conrad was a racist (a 'thoroughgoing racist'); thus the novel shouldn't be celebrated uncritically.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Evaluate the reasoning
*Student:* Write a one-page evaluation of Achebe's argument: which premises are strongest, and what counterarguments exist?

*How to teach:* Model evaluating an argument: strongest premise, weakest premise, counterargument. The student writes a one-page evaluation.

- **Q:** Give a counterargument to Achebe.
  **A:** The novel critiques imperial brutality and greed, and Marlow's framed narration may distance Conrad from his narrator's views.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Essay draft
*Student:* Draft an 800-word essay: How does Things Fall Apart function as an answer to colonial literature?

*How to teach:* The student drafts 800 words on Things Fall Apart as an answer to colonial literature; check for specific contrasts. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Give one way Things Fall Apart answers Heart of Darkness.
  **A:** It shows Africans speaking, reasoning, and governing from the inside, and ends by exposing the Commissioner's reductive view.

### Day 5 (Friday): Revise & submit
*Student:* Revise for precise claims and integrated quotations; submit with a one-sentence statement of your lens.

*How to teach:* The student revises for precise claims and integrated quotations and submits with a lens statement. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Write a one-sentence lens statement.
  **A:** 'I read Things Fall Apart through a postcolonial lens, asking how it restores voice and complexity to a culture colonial writing reduced.'

**Assessment:** Postcolonial literary argument essay.

**Answer key:** Postcolonial essay rubric (4 levels): 4 = precise thesis, evidence from Achebe's novel and at least one colonial text (Kipling or Conrad), fair treatment of the critical debate, integrated quotations, MLA. 3 = clear argument with thinner evidence; 2 = summary; 1 = incomplete.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Two-way tables and categorical data

**Prepare:** Draft a two-question survey and a way to collect it (paper or online form). Have CODAP ready. Collect at least 30 responses during the week.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing joint, marginal, and conditional relative frequencies. Say which total is the denominator: grand total (joint/marginal) or a row/column total (conditional).
- Concluding there's an association from raw counts with unequal group sizes. Compare conditional percentages.
- Writing leading or double-barreled questions. Keep categories mutually exclusive and wording neutral.

### Day 1 (Monday): Survey design
*Student:* Write a two-question categorical survey (e.g., grade level vs. preferred study time) and collect 30+ responses from family/co-op/online group.

*How to teach:* Model writing two categorical questions with clear choices. The student drafts, gets feedback, and starts collecting responses.

- **Q:** Why is 'Don't you agree studying at night is better?' a poor question?
  **A:** It is leading — it pushes respondents toward 'yes.' Use 'When do you prefer to study? Morning / Afternoon / Evening.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Build tables
*Student:* Organize survey results in a two-way table and compute joint and marginal relative frequencies.

*How to teach:* Build a two-way table with totals. Model joint and marginal relative frequencies. Example: grades 9–10: morning 8, evening 12; grades 11–12: morning 4, evening 16.

- **Q:** Joint relative frequency of '9–10 and morning'? Marginal for morning?
  **A:** 8/40 = 0.20; 12/40 = 0.30.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Conditional frequencies
*Student:* Compute row and column conditional relative frequencies and create segmented bar graphs.

*How to teach:* Compute row conditional percentages and make segmented bar graphs. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** P(morning | 9–10) and P(morning | 11–12)?
  **A:** 8/20 = 0.40 and 4/20 = 0.20.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Association
*Student:* Decide whether there is an association between the variables and justify with conditional frequencies.

*How to teach:* If conditional distributions differ noticeably across groups, there's an association. The student writes a justified conclusion.

- **Q:** Is there an association in the example?
  **A:** Yes: 40% of 9th–10th graders prefer morning vs. 20% of 11th–12th graders. With only 40 responses the difference may be due to chance.

### Day 5 (Friday): Report
*Student:* Write a one-page survey report with table, graph, and conclusion.

*How to teach:* The student writes the one-page report: question, method, table, segmented bar graph, conclusion, limitations. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Name one limitation of a family/co-op sample.
  **A:** It is a convenience sample, not random, so results may not represent all students.

**Assessment:** Survey report.

**Answer key:** Survey report rubric: 4 = neutral questions, 30+ responses, correct two-way table with totals, correct joint/marginal/conditional frequencies, segmented bar graph, conclusion justified with conditional percentages, limitations (convenience sample, small n); 3 = minor computational or labeling errors; 2 = conclusion from raw counts or missing graph; 1 = incomplete.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Air quality

**Prepare:** Index cards, petroleum jelly, tape, magnifying glass, ruler; AirNow app/website. Place cards on Day 1 in four locations for 3 days.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking ozone is always good. Stratospheric ozone protects; ground-level ozone is a harmful pollutant.
- Believing indoor air is always cleaner. Cooking, cleaning products, and infiltrating smoke can make indoor air worse.
- Assuming only visible particles matter. PM2.5 is invisible individually and most harmful to lungs.

### Day 1 (Monday): Particle catchers
*Student:* Coat index cards with petroleum jelly and place them in 4 locations (near a road, indoors, backyard, garage) for 3 days.

*How to teach:* Coat cards with petroleum jelly; place near a road, indoors, backyard, garage (labeled with dates).

- **Q:** What is PM2.5?
  **A:** Particulate matter 2.5 micrometers or smaller in diameter — small enough to penetrate deep into lungs and the bloodstream.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Smog in California
*Student:* Explain how the Los Angeles basin's inversion layer traps pollutants; diagram ozone formation.

*How to teach:* Explain the LA basin inversion: a warm air layer above cooler marine air traps pollutants under it, surrounded by mountains. Diagram ozone formation: NOx + VOCs + sunlight → O₃.

- **Q:** What is a temperature inversion?
  **A:** A layer where temperature increases with height, so cooler air near the ground can't rise and pollutants are trapped.
- **Q:** Why is ozone smog worse on hot sunny afternoons?
  **A:** Ozone forms from NOx and VOCs in sunlight, and heat speeds the reactions.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): AQI data
*Student:* Graph a month of AQI data from AirNow for your area and identify causes of high-AQI days (wildfire smoke, heat).

*How to teach:* Graph a month of local AQI; identify causes of high days.

- **Q:** What AQI range is 'unhealthy for sensitive groups'?
  **A:** 101–150.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Count particles
*Student:* Collect cards and count particles under a magnifier in a 1 cm² square; compare locations.

*How to teach:* Count particles in a 1 cm² square on each card under a magnifier; compare.

- **Q:** Predict which location will have the most particles and why.
  **A:** Near the road — vehicle exhaust, brake/tire wear, and road dust.

### Day 5 (Friday): Report
*Student:* Write a lab report on particulate levels by location with recommendations.

*How to teach:* Lab report with recommendations.

- **Q:** Give one recommendation to reduce particulate exposure.
  **A:** Use HEPA filtration indoors, check AQI before outdoor exercise, avoid busy roads, keep windows closed during smoke events.

**Assessment:** Particulate lab report.

**Answer key:** Particulate lab report rubric. 4 – Clear question/hypothesis, controlled method (same card size, exposure time, counting square), data table with replicate counts and means, graph, explanation tied to sources (traffic, indoor activities), limitations (can't measure PM2.5 directly), recommendations. 3 (proficient) – Data, graph, explanation, recommendations. 2 – Missing analysis. 1 – Incomplete.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Entrepreneurship, financial markets, and location

**Prepare:** Pick a California startup story (company website, news profile). Bookmark Investor.gov and FRED (10-year Treasury yield, DGS10). Find a map of California agriculture and industry. Prepare the Unit 9 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking profit is guaranteed for entrepreneurs. Profit is the reward for bearing risk; most startups fail.
- Believing stocks and bonds are the same. Stocks are ownership; bonds are loans.
- Ignoring location factors like water, ports, and talent clusters.

### Day 1 (Monday): Entrepreneurs
*Student:* Research a California startup's origin and identify the risks and profit incentive behind it.

*How to teach:* Research a startup's origin. The student identifies the risks taken and the profit incentive. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What role do entrepreneurs play?
  **A:** They combine resources in new ways and bear risk in hope of profit, driving innovation.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Business plan
*Student:* Write a one-page lean business plan with costs, price, and break-even quantity.

*How to teach:* Model break-even: fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit). The student writes a one-page lean plan. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Fixed costs $600, price $5, variable cost $2 per unit. Break-even quantity?
  **A:** 600 ÷ (5 − 2) = 200 units.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Financial markets
*Student:* Explain how stocks, bonds, and banks channel savings to investment; track a stock and a Treasury yield for a week.

*How to teach:* Explain how banks, stocks, and bonds channel savings to investment. The student tracks a stock and the 10-year Treasury yield for a week.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a stock and a bond?
  **A:** A stock is partial ownership in a company; a bond is a loan to a company or government that pays interest.
- **Q:** If bond prices fall, what happens to yields?
  **A:** Yields rise — they move in opposite directions.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Location
*Student:* Analyze why the Central Valley grows almonds, why Silicon Valley clusters tech, and how ports shape logistics in the Inland Empire.

*How to teach:* Analyze location: Central Valley almonds, Silicon Valley tech, Inland Empire logistics. The student writes one paragraph each.

- **Q:** Why is the Inland Empire a logistics hub?
  **A:** Proximity to the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach, available land for warehouses, and highway/rail connections.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 9 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 9 test on markets.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 9 test. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is the profit incentive?
  **A:** The expectation of profit that motivates people to start and expand businesses.

**Assessment:** Business plan and Unit 9 test.

**Answer key:** Business plan rubric (4 levels): 4 = clear product and customer, cost table (fixed and variable), price, correct break-even calculation, risks. Unit 9 test: demand/supply shifts, elasticity, equilibrium, price controls, market structures, entrepreneurship, financial markets.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Golf course play and focus

**Prepare:** Locate a pitch-and-putt course (or mini golf if unavailable); scorecard. Learn basic etiquette.

**Watch for:**
- Talking or moving while others swing.
- Slow play. Be ready to hit when it's your turn; limit practice swings.
- Thinking a handicap is a penalty. It's a measure of skill allowing fair competition.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pre-shot routine
*Student:* Develop a pre-shot routine and practice at the range.

*How to teach:* Develop a pre-shot routine (pick a target, align, one practice swing, breathe, hit); practice at the range.

- **Q:** Why use a pre-shot routine?
  **A:** Consistency and focus — it reduces anxiety and helps repeat good mechanics.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Etiquette
*Student:* Learn course etiquette and rules; practice putting.

*How to teach:* Teach etiquette: stay still and quiet, stand out of the line of play, repair ball marks and divots, keep pace, yell 'Fore!' if a ball heads toward people.

- **Q:** What do you shout if your ball may hit someone?
  **A:** 'Fore!'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Course play
*Student:* Play 9 holes at a pitch-and-putt course and keep score.

*How to teach:* Play 9 holes; keep score.

- **Q:** On a par-3, you take 5 strokes. What is your score relative to par?
  **A:** Double bogey (+2).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Analyze
*Student:* Analyze your scorecard for strengths and weaknesses.

*How to teach:* Analyze scorecard: putts per hole, strengths, weaknesses.

- **Q:** Your 9-hole score is 36 on a par-27 course. How many over par?
  **A:** 9 over par.

### Day 5 (Friday): Fitness
*Student:* Do a rotational core and mobility workout for golf.

*How to teach:* Rotational core and mobility workout (Pallof press, cable/band rotations, thoracic mobility).

- **Q:** Why does golf need rotational core strength?
  **A:** The swing relies on trunk rotation and controlled deceleration; a strong core transfers force and protects the back.

**Assessment:** Scorecard analysis.

**Answer key:** Scorecard analysis rubric. 4 – Complete scorecard with strokes and putts per hole, score vs. par computed, strengths/weaknesses identified with numbers, practice plan, etiquette reflection. 3 (proficient) – Scorecard, relative-to-par score, one strength and one weakness, practice plan. 2 – Incomplete analysis. 1 – Missing.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Cost of attendance & the FAFSA

**Prepare:** Help the student create (or log into) a StudentAid.gov account. Gather the parents' prior-prior-year federal tax return and the student's (the FAFSA for a school year uses tax information from two years earlier). Parents who are contributors need their own StudentAid.gov accounts. Open the Federal Student Aid Estimator. Note California's priority date (the Cal Grant deadline).

**Watch for:**
- Not filing because 'we make too much.' Many colleges and California programs (including the Middle Class Scholarship and some institutional aid) require the FAFSA or CADAA.
- Thinking all aid is free money. Loans must be repaid; grants and scholarships don't.
- Missing contributor consent. Each required contributor (e.g. parents of a dependent student) must provide consent and information, or the FAFSA is incomplete.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cost of attendance
*Student:* Break down the cost of attendance for a UC, CSU, and community college into tuition, housing, food, books, and transportation.

*How to teach:* Break down COA for a UC, a CSU and a community college using each school's published budget.

- **Q:** What does cost of attendance include?
  **A:** Tuition and fees, housing, food, books and supplies, transportation and personal expenses.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Aid types
*Student:* Chart grants, scholarships, work-study, and loans: who pays, whether repaid, and sources.

*How to teach:* Chart grants, scholarships, work-study and loans: who pays, repaid or not, sources.

- **Q:** Which types of aid don't have to be repaid?
  **A:** Grants and scholarships (work-study is earned by working).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): FSA ID and estimator
*Student:* Create (or review) your StudentAid.gov account and run the Federal Student Aid Estimator.

*How to teach:* Create or review StudentAid.gov accounts and run the Federal Student Aid Estimator.

- **Q:** What does the Student Aid Index (SAI) do?
  **A:** It's a number from the FAFSA that colleges use to determine how much need-based aid a student is eligible for.

### Day 4 (Thursday): FAFSA walkthrough
*Student:* Complete or review each FAFSA section, inviting contributors and listing schools; mark the state's priority dates.

*How to teach:* Complete or review each FAFSA section, invite contributors and list schools; mark the state priority date.

- **Q:** Why list every college you applied to on the FAFSA?
  **A:** Each listed school receives your FAFSA information so it can build an aid offer.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on aid vocabulary and verify FAFSA submission status or a dated practice worksheet.

*How to teach:* Give the aid quiz and verify FAFSA status or the dated practice worksheet.

- **Q:** COA is $36,000, SAI is $10,000. What is the financial need?
  **A:** $26,000 (COA − SAI).

**Assessment:** FAFSA completion/checklist and aid quiz.

**Answer key:** FAFSA checklist (pass when complete): StudentAid.gov accounts for student and contributors; FAFSA submitted (or dated practice worksheet) listing every school the student applied to; contributor sections completed with consent; confirmation saved; FAFSA Submission Summary reviewed for errors; California deadline noted. Aid quiz: 1) Cost of attendance = tuition/fees + housing + food + books/supplies + transportation + personal expenses. 2) Grant = need-based, no repayment. 3) Scholarship = merit/other criteria, no repayment. 4) Work-study = part-time job earnings for students with need. 5) Loan = must be repaid with interest. 6) SAI = Student Aid Index, an eligibility number schools use to determine need. 7) Need = COA − SAI (minus other aid). 8) Most undergraduates under 24 are considered dependent students.
