# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 30

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Final paper and presentation prep

**Prepare:** Have the final draft ready. Install slide software. Find a model abstract (e.g., from a student research journal) to show.

**Watch for:**
- Writing the abstract as an introduction. An abstract summarizes the whole paper, including findings.
- Charts without sources or labels. Every visual needs a title, labeled axes, and a source caption.
- Planning to read the paper aloud as a presentation. Storyboard key ideas, not paragraphs.

### Day 1 (Monday): Abstract
*Student:* Write a 150-word abstract summarizing question, method, findings, and significance.

*How to teach:* Show a model abstract and label its four parts. The student writes a 150-word abstract. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What four elements does an abstract include?
  **A:** Question, method, findings, and significance.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Final proofread
*Student:* Do a final read-aloud proofread and submit the 8–10 page Senior Research Paper.

*How to teach:* The student reads the paper aloud to proofread, fixes errors, and submits the final 8–10 page paper. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Why proofread aloud?
  **A:** Hearing the text catches missing words, awkward sentences, and errors the eye skips.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Present data
*Student:* Turn one key finding into a clear chart or infographic with a source caption.

*How to teach:* The student turns one key finding into a chart or infographic. Check the title, labels, and source caption. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What must a chart caption include?
  **A:** What the chart shows and its source.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Storyboard
*Student:* Storyboard a 10-minute presentation: hook, question, findings, counterargument, conclusion, Q&A.

*How to teach:* The student storyboards the 10-minute presentation section by section, with a time estimate for each. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** List the storyboard sections.
  **A:** Hook, question, findings, counterargument, conclusion, Q&A.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflection
*Student:* Write a research reflection: what surprised you, what you would do differently, and what question remains.

*How to teach:* The student writes a research reflection. Discuss it briefly. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What should the reflection address?
  **A:** What surprised them, what they'd do differently, and what question remains.

**Assessment:** Final Senior Research Paper (major grade).

**Answer key:** Senior Research Paper rubric (each 1–4): Thesis (specific, arguable); Research (credible, varied sources including primary research); Synthesis and commentary; Counterargument and rebuttal; Organization and transitions; MLA citations and Works Cited; Conventions. Proficient: 21/28 with no score below 2.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Sampling, surveys, and experiments

**Prepare:** Collect 2–3 recent news articles that cite a study (health or education). Random number generator (random.org or calculator). Preview Intro Stats ch. 1.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a bigger sample fixes bias. Bias from a bad method doesn't shrink as n grows.
- Confusing random sampling (generalize to population) with random assignment (allows cause-and-effect).
- Calling any study with data an 'experiment.' Experiments impose a treatment.

### Day 1 (Monday): Populations and samples
*Student:* Distinguish parameters from statistics and practice simple random, stratified, and cluster sampling with a random number generator.

*How to teach:* Define population, sample, parameter, statistic. Practice SRS, stratified, and cluster sampling on a class list with a random number generator.

- **Q:** In 'A poll of 1,000 California voters found 54% support,' what is the parameter and what is the statistic?
  **A:** Parameter: the true proportion of all California voters who support it. Statistic: 54% from the sample.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Bias
*Student:* Identify voluntary response, undercoverage, nonresponse, and question-wording bias in 8 survey examples.

*How to teach:* Present the bias types; the student identifies the bias in each of 8 examples and suggests a fix. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** A radio station asks listeners to call in. What bias?
  **A:** Voluntary response bias.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Experiments
*Student:* Design a randomized experiment (e.g., does music affect memory?) with control, randomization, and replication.

*How to teach:* Model a randomized experiment design: control, randomization, replication. The student designs the music-and-memory experiment.

- **Q:** Why randomly assign participants?
  **A:** To balance unknown variables between groups so differences can be attributed to the treatment.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Evaluate reports
*Student:* Critique two news articles that cite statistical studies: what type of study, what conclusions are justified?

*How to teach:* Read two articles; the student identifies study type and whether causal claims are justified. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** An observational study finds coffee drinkers live longer. Can we conclude coffee causes longer life?
  **A:** No — observational studies can't establish cause; lurking variables could explain it.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz on study design and bias.

*How to teach:* Give the study-design quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is a placebo and why is it used?
  **A:** An inactive treatment used to control for the placebo effect.

**Assessment:** Study design quiz and article critiques.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Parameter vs statistic. 2) Bias: radio call-in = voluntary response. 3) Phone survey of landlines = undercoverage. 4) 'Don't you agree…' = question wording. 5) Experiment design. 6) Observational vs experiment. Article critique rubric: 4 = study type identified, sample/population discussed, causal language evaluated, a specific improvement proposed; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = study type wrong; 1 = summary only.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Defining a global challenge

**Prepare:** Engineering notebook. Data sources: WHO/UNICEF JMP (water access), EPA heat island pages, ReFED or FAO (food waste). Problem tree template.

**Watch for:**
- Jumping to a solution before defining the problem.
- Criteria that can't be measured ('works well'). Make them quantitative where possible.
- Ignoring who is affected (equity, culture, cost).

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose a challenge
*Student:* Choose a global challenge (clean water access, urban heat, food waste) and research its scale with data.

*How to teach:* Choose a challenge and research scale with data.

- **Q:** About how many people lack safely managed drinking water (WHO/UNICEF, 2022)?
  **A:** About 2.2 billion people.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Criteria and constraints
*Student:* Write quantitative and qualitative criteria and constraints that reflect societal needs.

*How to teach:* Write criteria and constraints.

- **Q:** Write a quantitative criterion for a household water filter.
  **A:** Example: 'Removes ≥99% of bacteria and produces at least 10 L per day for under $20.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Decompose
*Student:* Break the challenge into three smaller engineering problems with a problem tree diagram.

*How to teach:* Decompose into three sub-problems with a problem tree.

- **Q:** Why decompose a big problem?
  **A:** Smaller problems can be solved, tested, and combined; it clarifies causes and effects.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Brainstorm
*Student:* Brainstorm and sketch at least five possible solutions to one sub-problem.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm and sketch five solutions to one sub-problem.

- **Q:** What brainstorming rule helps creativity?
  **A:** Defer judgment — generate many ideas before evaluating.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check-in
*Student:* Present the problem definition and get feedback; refine criteria.

*How to teach:* Present the definition and refine criteria.

- **Q:** What is a trade-off?
  **A:** Giving up some of one quality to gain another (e.g., lower cost vs. higher performance).

**Assessment:** Problem definition and criteria sheet.

**Answer key:** Problem definition and criteria sheet rubric. 4 – Data-supported problem statement, measurable criteria prioritized, realistic constraints (cost, materials, safety, social), problem tree with three sub-problems, five sketches, revisions after feedback. 3 (proficient) – Clear problem, measurable criteria/constraints, sub-problems. 2 – Vague criteria. 1 – Incomplete.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Unemployment and inflation

**Prepare:** Bookmark the BLS Employment Situation summary and CPI Inflation Calculator, and FRED series CPIAUCSL. Help the student choose 10 basket items they buy regularly and find last year's prices (old receipts or the BLS average-price data).

**Watch for:**
- Counting discouraged workers as unemployed. Unemployed means without work AND actively looking in the past 4 weeks; discouraged workers are out of the labor force.
- Confusing the inflation rate with the price level. Falling inflation means prices rise more slowly, not that they fall.
- Thinking zero unemployment is achievable or desirable. Some frictional and structural unemployment always exists.

### Day 1 (Monday): Unemployment
*Student:* Calculate unemployment and labor-force participation rates from sample data; classify frictional, structural, and cyclical unemployment.

*How to teach:* Define labor force, unemployment rate, and LFPR, and the three types of unemployment. The student calculates rates from sample data and classifies examples.

- **Q:** Employed 155M, unemployed 6M, civilian population 16+ 250M. Unemployment rate and LFPR?
  **A:** Labor force 161M; unemployment 6/161 ≈ 3.7%; LFPR 161/250 = 64.4%.
- **Q:** Classify: a graduate searching for a first job; a worker replaced by automation; a worker laid off in a recession.
  **A:** Frictional; structural; cyclical.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Jobs report
*Student:* Read the latest BLS Employment Situation summary and record new jobs and the unemployment rate.

*How to teach:* Read the latest Employment Situation summary together. The student records payroll job change and the unemployment rate and compares with last month.

- **Q:** What two headline numbers lead the jobs report?
  **A:** The change in nonfarm payroll employment and the unemployment rate.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Build a CPI
*Student:* Build a personal 'market basket' of 10 items, price it now and from last year, and compute your own inflation rate.

*How to teach:* Price the basket now and a year ago. The student computes their personal inflation rate and compares it with official CPI.

- **Q:** Basket costs $200 a year ago and $208 now. Inflation rate?
  **A:** (208 − 200)/200 = 4%.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Inflation history
*Student:* Graph CPI inflation since 1970 from FRED and explain the 1970s, 2008, and 2021–2023 episodes.

*How to teach:* Graph CPI inflation (percent change from a year ago) since 1970. The student annotates the 1970s, 2008–09, and 2021–23. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What caused high inflation in the 1970s?
  **A:** Oil price shocks (1973, 1979) and loose monetary policy; it peaked around 13–14% in 1979–80 until the Volcker Fed raised rates.
- **Q:** What was the recent inflation peak?
  **A:** About 9.1% year-over-year in June 2022, driven by pandemic supply disruptions, strong demand, and energy prices.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz on unemployment and inflation.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz on unemployment and inflation. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Who is hurt most by unexpected inflation?
  **A:** People on fixed incomes and lenders/savers whose interest doesn't keep up; borrowers with fixed-rate loans benefit.

**Assessment:** Personal CPI project and quiz.

**Answer key:** Personal CPI rubric (4 levels): 4 = 10 items with sources, correct calculation, comparison to official CPI with explanation of differences. Quiz: rates 3.7% and 64.4%; three types of unemployment; CPI 4%; 1970s and 2022 inflation; who's hurt by inflation.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Selecting and sequencing

**Prepare:** Digital archive, printed thumbnails. Check requirements of target colleges (many use SlideRoom; typically 10–20 pieces). Look up National Portfolio Day dates.

**Watch for:**
- Including every piece. Curate: fewer strong works beat many uneven ones.
- Ignoring each school's specific requirements.
- Random sequencing. Open and close with strong pieces and create visual flow.

### Day 1 (Monday): Criteria
*Student:* Write selection criteria for a college art portfolio and for a general exhibition.

*How to teach:* Write selection criteria for a college portfolio and for an exhibition.

- **Q:** How might criteria differ for college admission vs. an exhibition?
  **A:** College: show range of skills, observational drawing, and creative thinking. Exhibition: a cohesive theme and visual impact for the public.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Select
*Student:* Select 15–20 pieces for a college portfolio and 5 for AP-style Selected Works.

*How to teach:* Select 15–20 for a college portfolio and 5 for AP-style Selected Works.

- **Q:** What does AP Selected Works demonstrate?
  **A:** Five works showing skillful synthesis of materials, processes, and ideas.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sequence
*Student:* Sequence the portfolio with printed thumbnails and justify the order.

*How to teach:* Sequence with printed thumbnails; justify order.

- **Q:** Where should your strongest piece go?
  **A:** First (to make a strong impression), with another strong piece last.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Feedback
*Student:* Get feedback from an art teacher, artist, or National Portfolio Day reviewer if available.

*How to teach:* Get feedback from a teacher, artist, or National Portfolio Day reviewer.

- **Q:** What is National Portfolio Day?
  **A:** Events where representatives from art colleges review prospective students' portfolios and give feedback.

### Day 5 (Friday): Revise
*Student:* Revise selection based on feedback.

*How to teach:* Revise selection.

- **Q:** What is curation?
  **A:** Selecting and arranging works to communicate ideas and quality.

**Assessment:** Curated portfolio selection.

**Answer key:** Curated portfolio selection rubric. 4 – Written criteria, 15–20 pieces showing range and quality, 5 Selected Works, thoughtful sequence with rationale, revisions documented from outside feedback. 3 (proficient) – Selection, sequence with justification, feedback applied. 2 – Uncurated. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Dance fitness choreography

**Prepare:** Music (mix of tempos, ~120–140 BPM for dance fitness), video recorder, a dance-fitness video to follow. Clear floor.

**Watch for:**
- Choreography that is all high-impact. Include low-impact options.
- Changing moves mid-phrase. Changes should happen at the start of an 8-count.
- Teaching too fast. Break moves down, then layer.

### Day 1 (Monday): Counts
*Student:* Learn to count music in 8s and follow a 30-minute dance fitness video.

*How to teach:* Count music in 8s; follow a 30-minute dance fitness video.

- **Q:** How do you find the '1' in music?
  **A:** Listen for the strongest beat where phrases start, often marked by a drum accent or a vocal entry.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Choreograph
*Student:* Choreograph a 3-minute dance fitness routine.

*How to teach:* Choreograph a 3-minute routine on paper by 8-counts, including warm-up tempo and low/high-impact options.

- **Q:** About how many 8-counts are in 3 minutes at 128 BPM?
  **A:** 128 × 3 = 384 beats ÷ 8 = 48 eight-counts.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rehearse
*Student:* Rehearse and refine the routine.

*How to teach:* Rehearse and refine transitions.

- **Q:** What is a transition in choreography?
  **A:** The connecting movement between two steps or sections.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Teach
*Student:* Teach the routine to family members.

*How to teach:* Teach the routine to family using add-on method.

- **Q:** What is the add-on (layering) teaching method?
  **A:** Teach move A, then B, then combine A+B, then add C, and so on.

### Day 5 (Friday): Record
*Student:* Record the routine and reflect on fitness benefits.

*How to teach:* Record the routine; reflect on fitness benefits (HR before/after).

- **Q:** Name two fitness benefits of dance fitness.
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance, coordination, muscular endurance, balance, mood/stress relief.

**Assessment:** Recorded routine.

**Answer key:** Recorded routine rubric. 4 – 3 minutes, on-beat changes at 8-count boundaries, variety of moves with low/high-impact options, clear cueing, HR data and reflection. 3 (proficient) – Complete routine on-beat, varied moves, reflection. 2 – Off-beat or short. 1 – Incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Transfer paths: ADT, TAG & ASSIST

**Prepare:** Open ASSIST.org, the UC Transfer Admission Guarantee (TAG) and UC Transfer Pathways pages, and CSU's Associate Degree for Transfer (ADT) pages. Note: Cal-GETC replaced IGETC and CSU GE-Breadth for students starting community college in fall 2025 or later.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking any 60 units will transfer. Units must be transferable and include major preparation listed on ASSIST.org.
- Assuming an ADT guarantees a specific CSU campus. It guarantees admission to the CSU system (not necessarily a particular campus or major) with priority consideration for a similar major.
- Missing the TAG filing window. UC TAGs are filed in September of the year before transfer.

### Day 1 (Monday): Transfer basics
*Student:* Explain Associate Degree for Transfer (CSU guarantee) and UC Transfer Admission Guarantee eligibility.

*How to teach:* Explain ADT (CSU guarantee) and UC TAG eligibility.

- **Q:** What does an Associate Degree for Transfer guarantee?
  **A:** Admission to the CSU system (with priority consideration for a similar major) and that only 60 more units are needed for the bachelor's degree in that major.
- **Q:** How many UC campuses offer the Transfer Admission Guarantee?
  **A:** Six: Davis, Irvine, Merced, Riverside, Santa Barbara and Santa Cruz.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): ASSIST lab
*Student:* Use ASSIST.org to find which courses at a local community college meet major prep for your target UC/CSU major.

*How to teach:* Use ASSIST.org to find courses at a local CCC that meet major prep for a target UC/CSU major.

- **Q:** What is articulation?
  **A:** An agreement showing which courses at one college are accepted as equivalent to courses at another.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): General education
*Student:* Map the Cal-GETC general education pattern into a two-year schedule.

*How to teach:* Map Cal-GETC into a two-year schedule.

- **Q:** What is Cal-GETC?
  **A:** The California General Education Transfer Curriculum — a single 34-unit general education pattern accepted by both UC and CSU, starting fall 2025.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Transfer plan
*Student:* Build a full 60-unit two-year transfer plan with TAG/ADT deadlines.

*How to teach:* Build a full 60-unit transfer plan with TAG/ADT deadlines.

- **Q:** When is the UC TAG application due?
  **A:** During September (Sept 1–30) of the year before you plan to transfer.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Present your transfer plan and compare four-year net cost with the direct freshman path.

*How to teach:* Present the plan and compare four-year net cost with the direct freshman path.

- **Q:** Two years at a CCC cost $3,000 total in fees and the university costs $15,000/year net. Compare 2+2 vs. four years at the university (fees/tuition only).
  **A:** 2+2: $3,000 + $30,000 = $33,000; four years: $60,000; savings about $27,000 (living costs aside).

**Assessment:** Two-year transfer plan and cost comparison.

**Answer key:** Two-year transfer plan rubric: 4 = names target university and major; 60 transferable units over four semesters including Cal-GETC (34 units) and major preparation from ASSIST.org; TAG or ADT path and deadlines (TAG in September; UC application in the fall; Cal State Apply in the fall); cost comparison of 2 years CCC + 2 years university vs. 4 years at the university with numbers; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = units not transferable or missing major prep; 1 = incomplete.
