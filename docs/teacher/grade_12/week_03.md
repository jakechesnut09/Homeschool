# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 3

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: College essay revision and publishing

**Prepare:** Have all PIQ drafts printed. Prepare a mechanics checklist (comma splices, run-ons, its/it's, possessives). Optional: Common App prompts for students applying beyond UC.

**Watch for:**
- Replacing clichés with fancier clichés. Replace abstractions with concrete actions or images.
- Comma splices (two independent clauses joined by only a comma). Fix with a period, semicolon, or comma + coordinating conjunction.
- Rehearsed, memorized interview answers. Aim for natural conversation with specific examples.

### Day 1 (Monday): Draft PIQs #3–4
*Student:* Draft the remaining two PIQ responses, choosing at least one that addresses academic interest or community contribution.

*How to teach:* The student drafts PIQs #3–4, including one on academic interest or community. Check they total four distinct responses.

- **Q:** Which PIQ prompts address academics or community?
  **A:** Prompt 6 (an academic subject that inspires you) and Prompt 7 (making your school or community a better place).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cliché hunt
*Student:* Mini-lesson on diction: replace every cliché and vague abstraction ('passionate,' 'made me who I am') with a concrete image or action in all four drafts.

*How to teach:* Model replacing 'I am passionate about science' with an action ('I spent three Saturdays recalibrating our pH sensors'). The student hunts clichés in all four drafts.

- **Q:** Revise the cliché 'This experience made me who I am today.'
  **A:** Replace with a specific change, e.g., 'Now I'm the one who volunteers to stay late and fix the inventory spreadsheet.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mechanics edit
*Student:* Edit all four PIQs for comma splices, run-ons, and apostrophe errors using a mechanics checklist; read each aloud backwards sentence by sentence to catch typos.

*How to teach:* Teach the checklist, then model reading backwards sentence by sentence. The student edits all four. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Fix: 'I love chemistry, it explains everything.'
  **A:** 'I love chemistry; it explains everything.' or 'I love chemistry because it explains everything.'
- **Q:** Its or it's: 'The team lost ___ best player.'
  **A:** its

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mock interview
*Student:* Do a 10-minute mock admissions conversation with a parent, answering 'Tell me about yourself' and one question drawn from each PIQ.

*How to teach:* Conduct a 10-minute mock interview: 'Tell me about yourself' plus one question per PIQ. Give feedback on specificity and eye contact.

- **Q:** What makes a strong answer to 'Tell me about yourself'?
  **A:** A brief, specific story connecting interests, an example, and future goals — not a résumé list.

### Day 5 (Friday): Publish & reflect
*Student:* Submit final versions of four PIQs (each ≤350 words) and write a one-paragraph reflection on your biggest revision change.

*How to teach:* The student submits four final PIQs (≤350 words each) and a reflection paragraph on their biggest revision. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What should the reflection name?
  **A:** One concrete change (e.g., cutting a summary paragraph and adding a scene) and why it improved the essay.

**Assessment:** Final four PIQs scored with a college-essay rubric (specificity, insight, voice, conventions).

**Answer key:** College-essay rubric, each criterion 1–4 (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): Specificity — concrete moments, names, details. Insight — reflection shows self-awareness and growth. Voice — sounds like the student. Conventions — no comma splices, run-ons, or apostrophe errors; each ≤350 words. Proficient: 12/16 with no criterion below 3 for conventions.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Combining and composing functions; average rate of change

**Prepare:** Preview OpenStax 1.4 (Composition of Functions) and 1.3 (Rates of Change). Have Desmos ready. Write out two function pairs for practice: f(x) = x² + 1, g(x) = 2x − 3.

**Watch for:**
- Treating f(g(x)) as multiplication f·g. Have the student literally replace every x in f with the whole expression g(x), in parentheses.
- Forgetting the domain restriction for f/g (where g(x) = 0) or for compositions. Check: the inner function's domain AND the outer function's restrictions both apply.
- In the difference quotient, writing f(x + h) as f(x) + h. Expand carefully: for f(x) = x², f(x + h) = x² + 2xh + h².

### Day 1 (Monday): Function operations
*Student:* Find (f+g), (f−g), (fg), (f/g) and their domains for 8 pairs of functions.

*How to teach:* Model (f + g)(x), (f − g)(x), (fg)(x), and (f/g)(x) for f(x) = x² + 1 and g(x) = 2x − 3, stating each domain. The student completes 8 pairs; check that every quotient lists excluded x-values.

- **Q:** For f(x) = x² + 1 and g(x) = 2x − 3, find (fg)(x) and the domain of (f/g)(x).
  **A:** (fg)(x) = 2x³ − 3x² + 2x − 3. Domain of f/g: all reals except x = 3/2.
- **Q:** Find (f − g)(2) for the same pair.
  **A:** f(2) = 5, g(2) = 1, so 5 − 1 = 4.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Composition
*Student:* Evaluate and simplify f(g(x)) and g(f(x)) and decompose 6 functions into compositions; model a sale price with stacked discounts.

*How to teach:* Show f(g(x)) as 'do g first, then f' with a function-machine sketch. Work f(g(x)) and g(f(x)) to show order matters. The student then decomposes the 6 functions and models a stacked discount; check by plugging a number into both forms.

- **Q:** With f(x) = x² + 1 and g(x) = 2x − 3, find f(g(x)), g(f(x)), and f(g(2)).
  **A:** f(g(x)) = (2x − 3)² + 1 = 4x² − 12x + 10. g(f(x)) = 2x² − 1. f(g(2)) = f(1) = 2.
- **Q:** A jacket is 20% off, then a $10 coupon is applied. Write the final price as a composition and compare to applying the coupon first.
  **A:** D(p) = 0.8p, C(x) = x − 10. C(D(p)) = 0.8p − 10. Coupon first: D(C(p)) = 0.8(p − 10) = 0.8p − 8, which costs $2 more.
- **Q:** Decompose h(x) = √(3x + 1) as f(g(x)).
  **A:** g(x) = 3x + 1, f(x) = √x.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Average rate of change
*Student:* Compute average rates of change over intervals from tables, graphs, and equations; interpret units in context (miles per hour, dollars per year).

*How to teach:* Define average rate of change as [f(b) − f(a)]/(b − a), the slope of the secant line, and always attach units. The student computes rates from tables, graphs, and equations; check that every answer has units like 'miles per hour.'

- **Q:** Find the average rate of change of f(x) = x² + 1 on [1, 3].
  **A:** (f(3) − f(1))/(3 − 1) = (10 − 2)/2 = 4.
- **Q:** A car is at mile 40 at 1 pm and mile 190 at 4 pm. Find and interpret the average rate of change.
  **A:** (190 − 40)/(4 − 1) = 50 miles per hour on average.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Difference quotient
*Student:* Simplify the difference quotient [f(x+h)−f(x)]/h for linear, quadratic, and reciprocal functions as a calculus preview.

*How to teach:* Model the difference quotient for f(x) = 3x + 5, then x², step by step: find f(x + h), subtract f(x), divide by h. The student does the linear, quadratic, and reciprocal cases; check that h cancels before stopping.

- **Q:** Simplify [f(x + h) − f(x)]/h for f(x) = x².
  **A:** (x² + 2xh + h² − x²)/h = 2x + h.
- **Q:** Simplify the difference quotient for f(x) = 1/x.
  **A:** [1/(x + h) − 1/x]/h = [(x − (x + h))/(x(x + h))]/h = −1/(x(x + h)).

### Day 5 (Friday): Compare representations
*Student:* Compare two functions given in different forms (a table and an equation) for rate of change and maximum value; take a quiz.

*How to teach:* Give a table for one function and an equation for another; have the student compare rate of change on the same interval and maximum values. Then give the quiz below.

- **Q:** f is given by the table x: 0, 2, 4; f(x): 3, 7, 11. g(x) = −x² + 4x + 5. Which has the greater average rate of change on [0, 2]? Which has the greater maximum on [0, 4]?
  **A:** f: (7 − 3)/2 = 2. g: (9 − 5)/2 = 2 — equal. On [0, 4], f's max is 11; g's max is g(2) = 9, so f is greater.

**Assessment:** Quiz on composition and rate of change.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) f(g(x)) = 4x² − 12x + 10 (f = x² + 1, g = 2x − 3). 2) g(f(x)) = 2x² − 1. 3) f(g(2)) = 2. 4) Domain of f/g: x ≠ 3/2. 5) ARC of f on [1, 3] = 4. 6) Difference quotient of x² = 2x + h. 7) Coupon-after-discount 0.8p − 10 vs. before 0.8p − 8; discount first is cheaper. 8) h(x) = √(3x + 1): inner 3x + 1, outer √x. Proficient: 6+ of 8 with correct work.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Stellar life cycles and nucleosynthesis

**Prepare:** Prepare or download a 30-star data table (temperature in K, luminosity in L☉) including the Sun (5,800 K, 1 L☉), Sirius A, Sirius B, Betelgeuse, Rigel, Proxima Centauri, Aldebaran. Colored pencils, periodic table (print a blank one). Preview a 'nucleosynthesis periodic table' image (e.g., from NASA or Jennifer Johnson/OSU).

**Watch for:**
- Plotting the H-R diagram with temperature increasing to the right. By convention, temperature decreases to the right (hot blue stars on the left).
- Thinking stars 'move along' the main sequence as they age. Stars stay at nearly one main-sequence position for most of their lives, then leave it.
- Believing all elements came from supernovae. H and He came mostly from the Big Bang; many elements come from ordinary stars and neutron-star mergers.

### Day 1 (Monday): H-R diagram
*Student:* Plot 30 stars by temperature and luminosity on a Hertzsprung-Russell diagram and identify main sequence, giants, and white dwarfs.

*How to teach:* Plot 30 stars on a log-luminosity vs. temperature graph (temperature reversed). Identify main sequence (diagonal), giants/supergiants (upper right), white dwarfs (lower left).

- **Q:** Where would a hot but very dim star appear on the H-R diagram, and what is it?
  **A:** Lower left — a white dwarf (e.g., Sirius B).
- **Q:** Why is the temperature axis reversed?
  **A:** Historical convention from spectral classes (O B A F G K M, hottest to coolest) — hot stars are on the left.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Life cycles
*Student:* Create a branching flowchart of low-mass vs. high-mass star life cycles.

*How to teach:* Create a branching flowchart: low-mass (< ~8 M☉): nebula → protostar → main sequence → red giant → planetary nebula → white dwarf. High-mass: nebula → protostar → main sequence → red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole.

- **Q:** What determines a star's life path?
  **A:** Its initial mass.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Element factories
*Student:* Color-code a periodic table by origin (Big Bang, fusion in low-mass stars, massive stars, supernovae/neutron-star mergers).

*How to teach:* Color-code a periodic table by origin: H, He (Big Bang; some Li); C, N and s-process elements (low-mass stars); O through Fe-group (massive stars and supernovae); heavy r-process elements like gold and platinum (neutron-star mergers and some supernovae).

- **Q:** Why can't normal fusion in stars make elements heavier than iron for energy?
  **A:** Iron-56 is among the most tightly bound nuclei; fusing heavier elements consumes energy rather than releasing it, so they form by neutron capture (s- and r-processes) instead.

### Day 4 (Thursday): We are stardust
*Student:* Trace the origin of 5 elements in your body (H, C, O, Ca, Fe) and write a short explainer for a younger audience.

*How to teach:* Trace H (Big Bang), C (mainly low-mass/intermediate stars), O (massive stars), Ca (massive stars/supernovae), Fe (supernovae, especially Type Ia). Write a short explainer for a younger reader.

- **Q:** Where did the hydrogen in your body's water come from?
  **A:** The Big Bang — it formed in the first minutes and has never been fused.

### Day 5 (Friday): Communicate
*Student:* Present a 3-minute infographic or podcast on how stars make elements and take a quiz.

*How to teach:* Student presents a 3-minute infographic/podcast; give a quiz.

- **Q:** What is nucleosynthesis?
  **A:** The creation of new atomic nuclei by nuclear reactions — in the Big Bang, in stellar cores, and in explosive events.

**Assessment:** Element-origin infographic/podcast and quiz.

**Answer key:** Infographic/podcast rubric. 4 – Accurately explains Big Bang, stellar, supernova, and neutron-star-merger nucleosynthesis with examples, includes star life-cycle path, visually clear, cites sources. 3 (proficient) – Accurate origins for at least four element groups and life cycles. 2 – Some errors (e.g., all elements from supernovae). 1 – Inaccurate. Quiz key: 1) Main sequence = core hydrogen fusion. 2) Sun → red giant → white dwarf. 3) Massive star → supernova → neutron star/black hole. 4) Fe is the fusion endpoint. 5) Gold mainly from neutron-star mergers (r-process). 6) H-R axes: luminosity vs. temperature (reversed).

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): The Bill of Rights

**Prepare:** Print the Bill of Rights. Set up iCivics 'Do I Have a Right?' Prepare 10 First Amendment scenarios and a short Kelo v. New London summary (Oyez).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the First Amendment protects all speech everywhere. It limits government, not private employers, and allows some limits (true threats, incitement, time/place/manner).
- Believing the Bill of Rights originally applied to states. It limited only the federal government until incorporation through the 14th Amendment.
- Confusing eminent domain with confiscation. The Takings Clause requires public use and just compensation.

### Day 1 (Monday): The First Amendment
*Student:* Read the First Amendment and classify 10 scenarios (school speech, protest permits, religious displays) as protected or not.

*How to teach:* Read the First Amendment's five freedoms. The student classifies 10 scenarios; check reasoning for each. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Can a public school punish a student for a silent armband protest?
  **A:** Not unless it substantially disrupts school (Tinker v. Des Moines, 1969).
- **Q:** Can a city require a permit for a large march?
  **A:** Yes — content-neutral time, place, and manner rules are allowed.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Amendments 2–8
*Student:* Create a chart of Amendments 2–8 with a modern example for each, focusing on rights of the accused.

*How to teach:* The student builds the Amendments 2–8 chart with modern examples. Focus on 4th, 5th, 6th, 8th. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does the 4th Amendment protect?
  **A:** Against unreasonable searches and seizures; generally requires warrants with probable cause.
- **Q:** Name three 5th Amendment protections.
  **A:** Grand jury indictment, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination, due process, just compensation.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Incorporation
*Student:* Read about the 14th Amendment's due process clause and incorporation; explain how the Bill of Rights came to limit states.

*How to teach:* Explain the 14th Amendment's due process clause and selective incorporation. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is incorporation?
  **A:** The process by which the Supreme Court applied most Bill of Rights protections to the states through the 14th Amendment's due process clause, beginning with cases like Gitlow v. New York (1925).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Economic rights
*Student:* Discuss economic rights (property, contracts, choosing an occupation, the Takings Clause) and analyze Kelo v. New London's eminent domain ruling.

*How to teach:* Discuss economic rights and Kelo. The student analyzes the ruling and the backlash. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What did Kelo v. New London (2005) decide?
  **A:** 5–4: taking private homes for economic development counts as 'public use' under the Takings Clause; many states then limited eminent domain.

### Day 5 (Friday): Rights game & check
*Student:* Play iCivics 'Do I Have a Right?' and take a Bill of Rights quiz.

*How to teach:* Play iCivics; give the quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What does the 9th Amendment say?
  **A:** Listing certain rights doesn't deny others retained by the people.

**Assessment:** Bill of Rights quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Five freedoms: religion, speech, press, assembly, petition. 2) 2nd: bear arms. 3) 3rd: quartering. 4) 4th: search/seizure. 5) 5th: self-incrimination, double jeopardy, due process, takings. 6) 6th: speedy public trial, counsel. 7) 7th: civil jury. 8) 8th: no cruel/unusual punishment, excessive bail. 9) 9th: unenumerated rights. 10) 10th: reserved powers. 11) Incorporation via 14th. 12) Kelo 2005.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Looking at contemporary art

**Prepare:** Choose an Art21 episode (art21.org, free; preview content). Sketchbook. Have a list of elements (line, shape, form, value, color, texture, space) and principles (balance, contrast, emphasis, movement, pattern, rhythm, unity/variety, proportion).

**Watch for:**
- Formal analysis that jumps straight to opinions. Describe and analyze before interpreting or judging.
- Copying an artist's style instead of learning from their process.
- Thinking contemporary art must be 'pretty' to be good. It's judged by ideas and execution together.

### Day 1 (Monday): Artist video
*Student:* Watch an Art21 episode on a contemporary artist and take notes on their process.

*How to teach:* Watch the episode; note materials, process, ideas, and how the artist works in series.

- **Q:** Define 'contemporary art.'
  **A:** Art made by living artists in the present era (roughly the late 20th century to today), often exploring current ideas and diverse media.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Formal analysis
*Student:* Write a formal analysis of one artwork (elements, principles, meaning) in 300 words.

*How to teach:* Student writes a 300-word formal analysis: description, elements and principles, meaning.

- **Q:** What is the difference between an element and a principle of art?
  **A:** Elements are the basic visual components (line, shape, color, value, texture, space, form); principles are how they're arranged (balance, contrast, emphasis, rhythm, unity, etc.).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Series thinking
*Student:* Study how an artist develops a series and sketch three variations on one of your own ideas.

*How to teach:* Study how the artist develops a series; student sketches three variations on one idea.

- **Q:** What makes a group of works a 'series'?
  **A:** They share an idea, approach, or visual language while each explores a variation.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mini-work
*Student:* Complete a small finished drawing inspired by the artist's process, not their style.

*How to teach:* Complete a small finished drawing inspired by the artist's process (not copying their look).

- **Q:** How can you be influenced by an artist without copying?
  **A:** Borrow a process or way of thinking (e.g., working from found materials, repetition) and apply it to your own subject and style.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflection
*Student:* Reflect on how your response to the artist changed after research.

*How to teach:* Reflect on how the student's response changed after research.

- **Q:** Why can research change how we see an artwork?
  **A:** Context — the artist's intent, history, and process — adds meaning that isn't visible at first glance.

**Assessment:** Formal analysis and mini-work.

**Answer key:** Formal analysis rubric. 4 – Accurate description, analysis using at least four elements/principles with specific visual evidence, supported interpretation, and context from the video; 300 words. 3 (proficient) – Description, analysis, and interpretation with evidence. 2 – Mostly opinion or description only. 1 – Incomplete. Mini-work: proficient = completed drawing applying the artist's process to the student's own idea with a short note explaining the connection.

## Lifetime Fitness II: SMART goals and motivation

**Prepare:** Fitness log, calendar, and a Darebee workout (darebee.com, free) chosen in advance. Research a new activity for Day 4 (climbing gym day pass, pool, spin video). Draft a simple fitness contract template.

**Watch for:**
- Goals based only on appearance. Use performance and behavior goals (minutes per week, mile time).
- Relying on willpower alone. Habits are built with cues, routines, tracking, and rewards.
- Setting too many goals. Three focused goals are manageable.

### Day 1 (Monday): SMART goals
*Student:* Write three SMART goals based on baseline data; do a 30-minute activity of choice.

*How to teach:* Student writes three SMART goals from baseline data (one cardio, one strength, one flexibility or habit); then 30-minute activity.

- **Q:** Is 'Run more this year' a SMART goal? Fix it.
  **A:** No. Example: 'Run 3 days/week for 30 minutes and lower my mile from 9:40 to 9:00 by December 15.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Barriers
*Student:* List personal barriers (time, cost, motivation) and two strategies each; complete a 25-minute jog.

*How to teach:* List barriers with two strategies each; 25-minute jog.

- **Q:** Define intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation.
  **A:** Intrinsic: doing it for internal satisfaction or enjoyment. Extrinsic: doing it for external rewards (grades, prizes, approval).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Habit tracking
*Student:* Set up a weekly habit tracker; do a Darebee 30-minute full-body workout.

*How to teach:* Set up a weekly habit tracker (checkboxes per day); complete the Darebee workout.

- **Q:** Name the three parts of a habit loop.
  **A:** Cue (trigger), routine (behavior), reward.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Activity sampler
*Student:* Try a new activity (rock climbing gym, swim, spin class video) for 30 minutes.

*How to teach:* Try a new activity for 30 minutes; journal enjoyment and intensity.

- **Q:** Why try new activities?
  **A:** Variety helps find enjoyable activities, which improves long-term adherence, and trains different skills and muscles.

### Day 5 (Friday): Goal check
*Student:* Share goals with a parent and sign a fitness contract.

*How to teach:* Student shares goals with a parent and both sign a fitness contract (goals, schedule, rewards, check-in dates).

- **Q:** Why sign a contract or share goals?
  **A:** Public commitment and accountability increase follow-through.

**Assessment:** SMART goals and fitness contract.

**Answer key:** SMART goals and fitness contract: proficient = three goals each Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound and tied to baseline data; contract lists schedule, barriers with strategies, tracking method, check-in dates, and signatures. 4 adds intrinsic motivators and contingency plans; 2 = goals missing measures/dates; 1 = incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Building a spending plan

**Prepare:** Make or print 30 expense cards (rent, groceries, phone plan, streaming, car, gym, concert tickets, etc.). Open Google Sheets or LibreOffice. Find the Khan Academy budgeting video and NGPF 'Budget Challenge' or SPENT (playspent.org).

**Watch for:**
- Budgeting only what's left after spending. 'Pay yourself first': savings is a line item paid at the start of the month.
- Forgetting irregular expenses (car registration, gifts, annual subscriptions). Divide annual costs by 12 and budget monthly.
- Using the 50/30/20 rule as a law. It's a starting guideline; high-rent areas may need adjustments.

### Day 1 (Monday): Needs vs. wants
*Student:* Sort 30 expense cards into needs and wants, then defend five borderline items (phone plan, car, streaming) in writing.

*How to teach:* Sort the 30 cards into needs and wants; the student defends five borderline items in writing.

- **Q:** Is a smartphone plan a need or a want?
  **A:** Basic phone service is arguably a need (for work and safety); an expensive unlimited plan or new phone is a want. A good answer explains the distinction.
- **Q:** What's the difference between a fixed and a variable expense?
  **A:** Fixed: same each month (rent, insurance). Variable: changes month to month (groceries, gas, entertainment).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Track your spending
*Student:* Begin a 7-day expense tracker logging every purchase, and watch the Khan Academy budgeting video, taking notes on the 50/30/20 rule.

*How to teach:* Start a 7-day expense tracker (every purchase) and watch the budgeting video, noting the 50/30/20 rule.

- **Q:** Apply 50/30/20 to $2,400 net income.
  **A:** Needs $1,200, wants $720, savings/debt repayment $480.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Budget spreadsheet
*Student:* Build a monthly budget spreadsheet with formulas for income, fixed and variable expenses, savings, and the balance for a $2,400 net income.

*How to teach:* Build the budget spreadsheet with SUM formulas and a balance cell (=income − total expenses − savings).

- **Q:** What formula totals cells B2 through B10?
  **A:** =SUM(B2:B10).
- **Q:** What does 'pay yourself first' mean?
  **A:** Set aside savings first, as soon as you're paid, before spending on anything else.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Budget challenge
*Student:* Play the NGPF budget simulation (e.g., 'Budget Challenge' or SPENT) and record three decisions you would change.

*How to teach:* Play the budget simulation and record three decisions to change.

- **Q:** Your car insurance is $1,200 a year. How much should you budget monthly?
  **A:** $100 per month.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Revise your budget after a surprise $400 car repair and write a paragraph on how you balanced it.

*How to teach:* The student revises the budget after a $400 car repair and writes a paragraph.

- **Q:** How could you cover a $400 surprise expense without debt?
  **A:** Use the emergency fund, or cut wants (e.g. dining out, entertainment) for a month or two to cover it, then rebuild savings.

**Assessment:** Graded budget spreadsheet with a balanced revision.

**Answer key:** Budget spreadsheet rubric (4 levels): 4 = $2,400 net income; formulas (=SUM) for income, fixed, variable and savings totals; balance formula = income − expenses − savings equals $0 (zero-based) or a positive surplus; categories reasonable for California; savings at least 10–20%; after the $400 car repair, the revision balances by cutting wants or temporarily using the emergency fund, with a paragraph explaining tradeoffs; 3 = balanced, minor formula issues; 2 = unbalanced or hard-coded totals; 1 = incomplete. 50/30/20 of $2,400: needs $1,200, wants $720, savings/debt $480.
