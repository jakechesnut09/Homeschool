# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 6

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: The Canterbury Tales: General Prologue

**Prepare:** Get a modern verse translation of The Canterbury Tales. Find an audio recording of the first 18 lines in Middle English. Prepare a vocabulary list (estate, pilgrimage, satire, irony, etc.).

**Watch for:**
- Taking the narrator's praise at face value. Chaucer's narrator often praises flaws naively — verbal irony.
- Assuming medieval satire attacks the Church as a whole. Chaucer satirizes corrupt individuals while presenting ideal figures like the Parson.
- Thinking Middle English is Old English. Old English (Beowulf) needs full translation; Middle English is readable with a glossary.

### Day 1 (Monday): Chaucer's England
*Student:* Read about the three estates (clergy, nobility, commoners) and the Black Death. Listen to a recording of the opening 18 lines in Middle English and compare to a modern translation.

*How to teach:* Explain the three estates and the Black Death. Listen to the Middle English opening and compare to translation. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Where are the pilgrims going and why?
  **A:** To Canterbury to the shrine of St. Thomas Becket.
- **Q:** What contest does the Host propose?
  **A:** Each pilgrim tells tales on the journey; the best storyteller wins a free supper at the Tabard Inn.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): The pilgrims I
*Student:* Read the General Prologue portraits of the Knight, Prioress, Monk, and Friar. Chart each character's ideal role vs. actual behavior.

*How to teach:* Read the Knight, Prioress, Monk, Friar. The student charts ideal role vs. behavior. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How does the Monk fall short of his ideal role?
  **A:** He loves hunting, fine horses, and fur-trimmed clothes, ignoring the rules of monastic life.
- **Q:** How is the Knight presented?
  **A:** As an ideal: brave, humble, courteous, coming straight from campaigns in his stained tunic.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): The pilgrims II
*Student:* Read portraits of the Wife of Bath, Parson, Miller, Summoner, and Pardoner. Mark lines of verbal irony where Chaucer's narrator praises a flaw.

*How to teach:* Read the Wife of Bath, Parson, Miller, Summoner, Pardoner. The student marks verbal irony. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is the Pardoner selling?
  **A:** Fake relics (e.g., pig's bones) and pardons.
- **Q:** How is the Parson different?
  **A:** Poor and genuinely good; he practices what he preaches.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Language change
*Student:* Study how English changed from Old to Middle to Modern (vocabulary from French, loss of inflections). Translate four Middle English lines with a glossary.

*How to teach:* Teach Old→Middle→Modern English changes. The student translates four lines. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Translate 'Whan that Aprill with his shoures soote.'
  **A:** When April with its sweet showers.

### Day 5 (Friday): Create a pilgrim
*Student:* Write a 20-line modern 'General Prologue' portrait of a contemporary type (influencer, politician, coach) using Chaucer's ironic praise technique; take a vocabulary quiz.

*How to teach:* The student writes a modern satirical portrait; give vocabulary quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is verbal irony in Chaucer's portraits?
  **A:** The narrator praises a character for traits that actually reveal a flaw.

**Assessment:** Modern satirical portrait and vocabulary quiz.

**Answer key:** Satirical portrait rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): 4 = 20 lines, contemporary type, narrator's ironic praise exposes gap between ideal and reality, specific details. Vocabulary quiz answers: estate = social class; satire = ridicule to expose faults; irony = contrast between expectation and reality.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Polynomial division and complex zeros

**Prepare:** Preview OpenStax 5.4 (Dividing Polynomials) and 5.5 (Zeros of Polynomial Functions). Prepare a list of 10 polynomials for synthetic division practice.

**Watch for:**
- Leaving out placeholder zeros for missing terms in long or synthetic division. Write x³ − 8 as x³ + 0x² + 0x − 8.
- Using the wrong sign in synthetic division: to divide by (x + 2), use −2.
- Listing p/q backwards in the Rational Root Theorem. p = factors of the constant term, q = factors of the leading coefficient.

### Day 1 (Monday): Long division
*Student:* Divide polynomials using long division for 8 problems including ones with missing terms.

*How to teach:* Model (x³ − 2x² − 5x + 6) ÷ (x − 1) with long division, then x³ − 8 ÷ (x − 2) with placeholders. The student does 8 problems; check by multiplying quotient × divisor + remainder.

- **Q:** Divide x³ − 2x² − 5x + 6 by x − 1.
  **A:** x² − x − 6, remainder 0. So x³ − 2x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 1)(x − 3)(x + 2).
- **Q:** Divide x³ − 8 by x − 2.
  **A:** x² + 2x + 4, remainder 0.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Synthetic division & Remainder Theorem
*Student:* Use synthetic division and the Remainder Theorem to evaluate and test factors of 10 polynomials.

*How to teach:* Teach synthetic division and state the Remainder Theorem: the remainder when P(x) is divided by (x − c) equals P(c). The student evaluates and tests factors for 10 polynomials; check one with direct substitution.

- **Q:** Use the Remainder Theorem to find the remainder of P(x) = x³ + 2x² − x + 4 divided by x + 2.
  **A:** P(−2) = −8 + 8 + 2 + 4 = 6.
- **Q:** Is (x − 3) a factor of x³ − 4x² + x + 6?
  **A:** P(3) = 27 − 36 + 3 + 6 = 0, so yes.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rational Root Theorem
*Student:* List possible rational zeros and fully factor 6 cubics and quartics.

*How to teach:* List candidates ±p/q, test with synthetic division, and factor the remaining quadratic. The student fully factors 6 cubics/quartics.

- **Q:** List the possible rational zeros of 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 and find all zeros.
  **A:** ±1, ±2, ±3, ±6, ±1/2, ±3/2. P(3) = 0; quotient 2x² + 3x − 2 = (2x − 1)(x + 2). Zeros: 3, 1/2, −2.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Complex zeros
*Student:* Find all zeros including complex conjugate pairs; compute moduli and quotients of complex numbers using conjugates.

*How to teach:* Factor x³ − x² + 4x − 4 by grouping to get (x − 1)(x² + 4) and find ±2i. Show that complex zeros of real polynomials come in conjugate pairs; compute modulus and a quotient using the conjugate.

- **Q:** Find all zeros of x³ − x² + 4x − 4.
  **A:** (x − 1)(x² + 4) = 0 → x = 1, 2i, −2i.
- **Q:** Find |3 + 4i| and (2 + 3i)/(1 − i).
  **A:** |3 + 4i| = √(9 + 16) = 5. (2 + 3i)(1 + i)/((1 − i)(1 + i)) = (2 + 5i + 3i²)/2 = (−1 + 5i)/2.
- **Q:** A polynomial with real coefficients has zeros 2 and 1 + i. What other zero must it have?
  **A:** 1 − i (the conjugate).

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Take a quiz on division, factor theorem, and finding all zeros.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz below (no calculator). Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** State the Factor Theorem.
  **A:** (x − c) is a factor of P(x) if and only if P(c) = 0.

**Assessment:** Zeros and division quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) (x³ − 2x² − 5x + 6) ÷ (x − 1) = x² − x − 6. 2) Remainder of x³ + 2x² − x + 4 ÷ (x + 2) = 6. 3) (x − 3) is a factor of x³ − 4x² + x + 6 (P(3) = 0); full factorization (x − 3)(x − 2)(x + 1). 4) Zeros of 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6: 3, 1/2, −2. 5) Zeros of x³ − x² + 4x − 4: 1, ±2i. 6) (2 + 3i)/(1 − i) = −1/2 + (5/2)i. Proficient: 5 of 6 with work shown.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Seafloor spreading and the ages of rocks

**Prepare:** Paper strips (two colors), markers, a box with a slit (or two desks). Print continent outlines, fossil distribution map (Mesosaurus, Glossopteris, Lystrosaurus, Cynognathus), and the NOAA seafloor age map.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Wegener's idea was rejected for lack of evidence of drift. He had strong evidence; it was rejected mainly because he lacked a convincing mechanism.
- Believing magnetic stripes are caused by the ocean. They record reversals of Earth's magnetic field frozen into cooling basalt.
- Thinking oceanic crust is as old as continents. Oceanic crust is recycled by subduction, so none is older than ~180–200 million years.

### Day 1 (Monday): Continental drift
*Student:* Fit cut-out continents together using fossil and rock-type evidence as Wegener did.

*How to teach:* Fit continents (especially South America and Africa) together; mark matching fossils and rock belts.

- **Q:** Name two lines of evidence Wegener used.
  **A:** Fit of continents, matching fossils (Mesosaurus, Glossopteris) across oceans, matching rock types and mountain belts, and paleoclimate clues (glacial deposits in tropics, coal in Antarctica).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Magnetic striping
*Student:* Model seafloor spreading by pulling two paper strips through a slit and marking alternating magnetic polarity bands.

*How to teach:* Pull two strips up through a slit in opposite directions, coloring alternating bands (normal/reversed polarity) as they emerge, producing symmetric stripes.

- **Q:** Why are magnetic stripes symmetric about the ridge?
  **A:** New crust forms at the ridge and moves away on both sides, so each polarity band is recorded on both sides equally.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Seafloor age map
*Student:* Analyze the NOAA seafloor age map: measure distance vs. age from the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and calculate spreading rate in cm/yr.

*How to teach:* Use the age map: measure distance from the Mid-Atlantic Ridge to crust of known age (e.g., 1,000 km to 50-million-year-old crust) and calculate rate.

- **Q:** Crust 50 million years old is 1,000 km from the ridge. What is the spreading (half) rate in cm/yr?
  **A:** 1,000 km = 10⁸ cm; 10⁸ ÷ (5 × 10⁷ yr) = 2 cm/yr.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Oldest rocks
*Student:* Compare the ages of the oldest oceanic crust (~180 million years) and continental rocks (~4 billion years) and explain the difference using subduction.

*How to teach:* Compare oldest oceanic crust (~180 Ma, western Pacific) with continental rocks (~4.0 Ga, Acasta Gneiss; zircons 4.4 Ga).

- **Q:** Why is oceanic crust so much younger than continental crust?
  **A:** Oceanic crust is dense and sinks back into the mantle at subduction zones; buoyant continental crust is not subducted and survives for billions of years.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluate evidence
*Student:* Write an evaluation of three lines of evidence for plate tectonics and take a quiz.

*How to teach:* Student evaluates three lines of evidence; give a quiz.

- **Q:** What is paleomagnetism?
  **A:** The record of Earth's past magnetic field preserved in the alignment of magnetic minerals in rocks.

**Assessment:** Evidence evaluation and quiz.

**Answer key:** Evidence evaluation rubric. 4 – Evaluates at least three evidence lines (continental fit/fossils/rocks, seafloor magnetic stripes, seafloor ages increasing from ridges, earthquake/volcano distribution) for strength and limitations, includes the spreading-rate calculation, explains why Wegener lacked a mechanism. 3 (proficient) – Three accurate evidence lines with reasoning. 2 – Description without evaluation. 1 – Inaccurate. Quiz key: 1) Mid-ocean ridge = divergent boundary where crust forms. 2) Stripes = magnetic reversals. 3) Oldest seafloor ~180–200 Ma. 4) Subduction recycles crust. 5) Rate = distance ÷ age.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): How a bill becomes law; amending the Constitution

**Prepare:** Print Article V. Bookmark congress.gov bill tracker. Prepare a bill template.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking most bills become law. The large majority die in committee.
- Assuming the president can veto parts of a bill. There is no line-item veto for presidents (Clinton v. City of New York, 1998).
- Confusing proposal and ratification of amendments.

### Day 1 (Monday): Lawmaking
*Student:* Flowchart how a bill becomes law, including committees, floor votes, conference, and veto override.

*How to teach:* Flowchart the process: introduction, committee, floor, other chamber, conference, president, override. The student explains each step.

- **Q:** How can Congress override a veto?
  **A:** Two-thirds vote in both houses.
- **Q:** What is a pocket veto?
  **A:** If Congress adjourns within 10 days (excluding Sundays) of sending a bill and the president doesn't sign it, it dies.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Track a bill
*Student:* Use congress.gov to track one current bill and record its sponsor, committee, and status.

*How to teach:* The student tracks one bill and records sponsor, committee, status. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Where do most bills stall?
  **A:** In committee.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mock legislature
*Student:* Write a short bill, 'mark it up' with family as a committee, and simulate a floor vote with amendments.

*How to teach:* Simulate a markup and floor vote. The student writes and defends a short bill. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What happens in markup?
  **A:** The committee debates and amends the bill before voting to report it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Amendments
*Student:* Read Article V; compare successful (19th, 26th) and failed (ERA) amendments and explain why some fail.

*How to teach:* Read Article V; compare 19th, 26th, and the ERA. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** How are amendments proposed and ratified?
  **A:** Proposed by two-thirds of both houses (or a convention called by two-thirds of states); ratified by three-fourths of states.
- **Q:** What did the 19th and 26th Amendments do?
  **A:** 19th (1920) women's suffrage; 26th (1971) voting age 18.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a 400-word reflection on why the founders made lawmaking slow; take a short quiz.

*How to teach:* The student writes the 400-word reflection; give the quiz. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Why did the Framers make lawmaking slow?
  **A:** To encourage deliberation and compromise and guard against hasty, factional majorities.

**Assessment:** Mock bill and lawmaking quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: veto override 2/3; pocket veto; most bills die in committee; amendment process 2/3 + 3/4; ERA failed to reach 38 states. Mock bill rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning): 4 = clear purpose, sections, amendment record, vote tally, reflection.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: The figure

**Prepare:** A willing family model (clothed) or a timed online pose site (e.g., Line of Action — check settings to exclude nudity), charcoal, newsprint pad, NGA open-access figure drawings by Michelangelo or Käthe Kollwitz.

**Watch for:**
- Starting with outlines and details in gesture drawing. Gesture captures movement and the line of action in seconds.
- Drawing heads too large. An adult figure is about 7.5–8 heads tall.
- Avoiding hands. Simplify into mitten shapes and boxes before fingers.

### Day 1 (Monday): Gesture
*Student:* Do 30 one-minute gesture drawings from a family model or timed online poses.

*How to teach:* 30 one-minute gestures: find the line of action first, then rhythm of limbs.

- **Q:** What is the 'line of action'?
  **A:** An imaginary curved line through the figure that captures its main movement or direction.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Proportion
*Student:* Study figure proportions (head units) and draw three 10-minute poses.

*How to teach:* Study head-unit proportions (≈7.5–8 heads tall; hips near the midpoint; elbows near the waist). Draw three 10-minute poses.

- **Q:** About how many head-lengths tall is an adult figure?
  **A:** About 7.5 to 8 heads.
- **Q:** Where does the halfway point of a standing adult fall?
  **A:** Around the hip/pubic bone.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hands and feet
*Student:* Fill a page with studies of your own hands and feet from multiple angles.

*How to teach:* Fill a page with your hands and feet from multiple angles.

- **Q:** What is foreshortening?
  **A:** The visual shortening of a form that points toward or away from the viewer.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Master study
*Student:* Copy a figure drawing by a master (e.g., Michelangelo, Käthe Kollwitz) from NGA open access.

*How to teach:* Copy a master figure drawing (e.g., Michelangelo's red chalk studies or Kollwitz's charcoal prints) to learn their mark-making.

- **Q:** What can a master copy teach?
  **A:** How the artist simplified forms, used line and value, and built anatomy — by reconstructing their decisions.

### Day 5 (Friday): Long pose
*Student:* Complete a 45-minute figure drawing applying gesture and proportion.

*How to teach:* 45-minute figure drawing using gesture first, then proportion and form.

- **Q:** Why start a long pose with a gesture?
  **A:** It establishes movement and overall proportion before details, keeping the figure alive and accurate.

**Assessment:** Figure drawing set.

**Answer key:** Figure drawing set rubric. 4 – 30 lively gestures, accurate proportions in 10-minute poses, varied hand/foot studies with foreshortening, faithful master copy, 45-minute drawing with gesture, proportion, and form. 3 (proficient) – All components with generally accurate proportions. 2 – Stiff gestures or missing parts. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Designing a personal strength program

**Prepare:** Program template (days, exercises, sets, reps, load, rest, progression). Weights or bands. Review the student's 10RMs.

**Watch for:**
- Programs with no progression plan. Write how load/reps increase each week.
- Training the same muscles hard on consecutive days. Alternate workouts and allow 48 hours.
- Ignoring recovery days. Active recovery keeps blood flowing without fatigue.

### Day 1 (Monday): Program design
*Student:* Write a 4-week full-body program (3 days/week) with exercises, sets, reps, and progression.

*How to teach:* Student writes a 4-week, 3-day full-body program alternating Workout A and B, with sets, reps, rest, and weekly progression.

- **Q:** What is the FITT principle applied to strength training?
  **A:** Frequency (e.g., 3 days/week), Intensity (load/RPE), Time (sets × reps, rest, session length), Type (exercises/equipment).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Workout A
*Student:* Complete Workout A of your program and log it.

*How to teach:* Complete Workout A; log.

- **Q:** Why alternate Workout A and B?
  **A:** To vary exercises and patterns, balance muscle groups, and manage fatigue.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Active recovery
*Student:* Do a 30-minute walk and mobility routine.

*How to teach:* Active recovery: 30-minute walk plus mobility.

- **Q:** What is periodization?
  **A:** Planned variation in training (volume, intensity) over time to maximize progress and prevent plateaus/overtraining.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Workout B
*Student:* Complete Workout B and log it.

*How to teach:* Complete Workout B; log.

- **Q:** What is a split routine?
  **A:** Training different muscle groups on different days (e.g., upper/lower).

### Day 5 (Friday): Workout A
*Student:* Complete Workout A with progression and note RPE.

*How to teach:* Workout A with progression; note RPE.

- **Q:** If RPE on Workout A was 6 last time, what progression could you apply?
  **A:** Increase load slightly or add 1–2 reps per set (aim for RPE 7–8).

**Assessment:** Written 4-week program.

**Answer key:** Written 4-week program rubric. 4 – 3 days/week full-body, A/B alternating; covers squat, hinge, push, pull, lunge, carry, core; sets/reps/rest/load; clear weekly progression; warm-up/cool-down; recovery days; safety notes. 3 (proficient) – All patterns, sets/reps/rest, progression. 2 – Missing patterns or progression. 1 – Incomplete.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Savings, interest & digital payments

**Prepare:** Open the Investor.gov compound interest calculator and a spreadsheet. Look up current high-yield savings APYs. Read the fraud-protection terms for Zelle, Venmo and Cash App.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing simple and compound interest. Compound interest earns interest on previous interest.
- Thinking P2P apps protect like credit cards. Payments to scammers through Zelle/Venmo/Cash App are often not recoverable; balances held in the app may not be FDIC insured unless the app passes deposits through to a bank.
- Keeping the emergency fund invested in stocks. It should be safe and accessible (high-yield savings).

### Day 1 (Monday): Emergency fund
*Student:* Calculate a 3-month emergency fund from your Unit 1 budget and plan how many months to reach it.

*How to teach:* Calculate a 3-month emergency fund from the Unit 1 budget's essential expenses and how many months to reach it.

- **Q:** Essential expenses are $1,600/month. 3-month fund? Saving $200/month, how long?
  **A:** $4,800; 24 months.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Simple vs. compound
*Student:* Compute simple and compound interest by hand for $1,000 at 4% over 5 years, then verify in a spreadsheet with =FV.

*How to teach:* Compute simple and compound interest for $1,000 at 4% for 5 years by hand, then verify with =FV(0.04,5,0,-1000).

- **Q:** Simple interest on $1,000 at 4% for 5 years?
  **A:** $1,000 × 0.04 × 5 = $200 → $1,200 total.
- **Q:** Compound annually?
  **A:** $1,000 × 1.04^5 = $1,216.65.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rule of 72
*Student:* Use the Rule of 72 to estimate doubling times at 2%, 6%, and 10% and compare to spreadsheet results.

*How to teach:* Use the Rule of 72 for 2%, 6% and 10% and compare to exact doubling times.

- **Q:** Rule of 72 estimates at 2%, 6%, 10%?
  **A:** 36, 12 and 7.2 years (exact: about 35.0, 11.9 and 7.3 years).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Digital payments
*Student:* Compare Zelle, Venmo, Cash App, and debit cards for fees, fraud protection, and FDIC coverage; write five safe-use rules.

*How to teach:* Compare Zelle, Venmo, Cash App and debit cards; write five safe-use rules.

- **Q:** Give two safe-use rules for P2P apps.
  **A:** Only send money to people you know and trust; double-check the recipient; never pay 'refunds' or prizes to strangers; turn on multifactor authentication; move balances to an insured bank account.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a savings-goal plan with interest projection and a 10-question quiz.

*How to teach:* Complete the savings-goal plan and the 10-question quiz in the key.

- **Q:** Why does compounding matter more the longer you save?
  **A:** Each year you earn interest on a larger balance that already includes past interest, so growth accelerates over time.

**Assessment:** Savings plan with compound-interest projection and quiz.

**Answer key:** Savings plan rubric: 4 = 3-month emergency fund computed from the Unit 1 budget; monthly deposit and months to reach goal; compound projection using =FV with correct inputs; 3 = minor calculation errors; 2 = no projection; 1 = incomplete. Quiz: 1) Simple interest $1,000 at 4% for 5 years = $200. 2) Compound annually = $1,216.65. 3) Rule of 72 at 6% → 12 years. 4) At 9% → 8 years. 5) =FV(4%,5,0,-1000) = 1,216.65. 6) Emergency fund = 3–6 months of essential expenses. 7) APY includes compounding. 8) Zelle payments to a scammer are usually hard to recover. 9) High-yield savings is FDIC insured if at an insured bank. 10) Debit cards have more protection than P2P apps under federal law for unauthorized transactions, if reported quickly.
