# Teacher's Guide — 12th Grade, Week 26

## English 12 – World & British Literature + Senior Research: Reading, note-taking, and annotated bibliography

**Prepare:** Bookmark Purdue OWL MLA (9th ed.). Gather sources. Prepare note-card or digital-note templates.

**Watch for:**
- Patchwriting — swapping synonyms while keeping the source's structure. Read, cover the source, write from memory, then compare.
- Notes without page numbers. Require source tag and page on every note so citations are easy later.
- Annotations that only summarize. Each annotation must also evaluate credibility and state how the source will be used.

### Day 1 (Monday): Paraphrase practice
*Student:* Practice paraphrasing three passages without patchwriting; compare to originals and fix any borrowed phrasing.

*How to teach:* Model paraphrasing a passage by reading, covering, writing, and comparing. The student paraphrases three passages and fixes borrowed phrasing.

- **Q:** What is patchwriting?
  **A:** Copying a source's sentence structure and changing a few words — a form of plagiarism.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Note cards
*Student:* Read two sources and create 15 tagged notes (quotation, paraphrase, or summary) with page numbers.

*How to teach:* Model tagging notes (Q/P/S) with source and page. The student reads two sources and makes 15 notes. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** When should you quote rather than paraphrase?
  **A:** When the exact wording is memorable, authoritative, or the subject of analysis.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): More notes
*Student:* Read two more sources and add 15 tagged notes; flag where sources agree or disagree.

*How to teach:* The student reads two more sources and adds 15 notes, flagging agreement and disagreement. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Why flag disagreements?
  **A:** They reveal the debate the paper can enter.

### Day 4 (Thursday): MLA formatting
*Student:* Learn MLA 9 container format; write Works Cited entries for a book, journal article, website, and interview.

*How to teach:* Teach MLA 9 containers. The student writes four entries. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Order of MLA core elements?
  **A:** Author. Title. Container, Other contributors, Version, Number, Publisher, Date, Location.

### Day 5 (Friday): Annotated bibliography
*Student:* Submit an annotated bibliography of 6–8 sources, each with a 100-word summary and evaluation.

*How to teach:* The student submits the annotated bibliography. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What goes in a 100-word annotation?
  **A:** Summary, evaluation of credibility, and how it will be used.

**Assessment:** Annotated bibliography scored for accuracy and evaluation quality.

**Answer key:** Annotated bibliography rubric (4 levels): 4 = 6–8 correctly formatted MLA entries, alphabetized with hanging indents, each annotation summarizes, evaluates, and states use. 3 = minor format errors; 2 = summary only; 1 = incomplete.

## Precalculus with Statistics: Probability rules

**Prepare:** Two dice, a standard 52-card deck, coins. Preview OpenStax Introductory Statistics ch. 3. Draw a 6 × 6 grid of two-dice sums for Day 2.

**Watch for:**
- Adding probabilities of overlapping events without subtracting the overlap. Draw the Venn diagram and point to the double-counted region.
- Expecting 100 rolls to match theory exactly. Results vary in the short run; the law of large numbers describes the long run.
- Treating 'at least one' as a single case. Use the complement: P(at least one) = 1 − P(none).

### Day 1 (Monday): Sample spaces
*Student:* List sample spaces for coin, die, and card experiments; describe events with unions, intersections, and complements using Venn diagrams.

*How to teach:* Say: 'A sample space lists every possible outcome; an event is a subset of it.' List the sample spaces for 3 coin flips and one die, then draw a Venn diagram for A = hearts and B = face cards and shade A ∪ B, A ∩ B, and Aᶜ. The student does the rest of the set; check that unions and intersections are shaded correctly.

- **Q:** How many outcomes are in the sample space for flipping 3 coins? List them.
  **A:** 8: HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT.
- **Q:** In a standard deck, how many cards are hearts AND face cards?
  **A:** 3 (jack, queen, king of hearts).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Experimental vs. theoretical
*Student:* Roll two dice 100 times, record sums, and compare to theoretical probabilities.

*How to teach:* Have the student predict the most common sum, then roll two dice 100 times and tally sums. Fill in the 6 × 6 grid to get theoretical probabilities and compare with a bar graph of experimental relative frequencies; discuss why they differ.

- **Q:** Theoretical P(sum = 7) and P(sum = 2)?
  **A:** 6/36 = 1/6 and 1/36.
- **Q:** Why is 7 the most likely sum?
  **A:** It can be made 6 ways (1+6, 2+5, 3+4, 4+3, 5+2, 6+1), more than any other sum.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Addition rule
*Student:* Apply P(A or B) = P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B) to card and survey problems.

*How to teach:* State the Addition Rule and model it with 'heart or king,' pointing out that the king of hearts would be counted twice. The student solves card and survey problems; check that each answer subtracts the overlap unless the events are mutually exclusive.

- **Q:** P(heart or king)?
  **A:** 13/52 + 4/52 − 1/52 = 16/52 = 4/13.
- **Q:** P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4, P(A and B) = 0.2. Find P(A or B).
  **A:** 0.5 + 0.4 − 0.2 = 0.7.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Complement
*Student:* Use the complement rule to solve 'at least one' problems such as the birthday problem; simulate it.

*How to teach:* Model 'at least one six in 4 rolls' as 1 − (5/6)⁴. Then set up the birthday problem as 1 − (365/365)(364/365)…, compute it for 23 people, and simulate with a random-number generator.

- **Q:** P(at least one six in 4 rolls)?
  **A:** 1 − (5/6)⁴ = 1 − 625/1296 ≈ 0.518.
- **Q:** P(at least two of 23 people share a birthday)?
  **A:** ≈ 0.507 — just over half.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Take a probability rules quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz below (calculator allowed), then review any errors. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** If P(A) = 0.3, what is P(not A)?
  **A:** 0.7

**Assessment:** Probability rules quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) 3 coins: 8 outcomes. 2) P(sum 7) = 1/6. 3) P(heart or king) = 4/13. 4) P(at least one six in 4 rolls) ≈ 0.518. 5) P(A or B) with P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4, P(A and B) = 0.2: 0.7. 6) Birthday problem, 23 people: ≈ 0.507. Proficient: 5 of 6.

## Earth & Space / Environmental Science: Evidence of climate change

**Prepare:** NASA GISS global temperature data (GISTEMP, anomalies vs. 1951–1980), NOAA sea-level data, NSIDC Arctic sea-ice extent, ice-core CO₂ (800,000 years; EPICA Dome C), spreadsheet. Local 30-year climate normals from NOAA.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing weather and climate. Weather is day-to-day; climate is the long-term (30-year) average and variability.
- Using one cold day or year to dismiss warming.
- Thinking anomaly means unusual event. It's the difference from a baseline average.

### Day 1 (Monday): Weather vs. climate
*Student:* Compare one week of local weather to 30-year normals and explain the difference between weather and climate.

*How to teach:* Compare a week's local weather to 30-year normals.

- **Q:** Define climate.
  **A:** The long-term average and variability of weather in a region, typically over 30 years.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Temperature record
*Student:* Graph NASA global temperature anomalies since 1880 and calculate the warming rate per decade for recent decades.

*How to teach:* Graph global temperature anomalies since 1880; calculate warming per decade since ~1980.

- **Q:** If the anomaly rose from 0.27 °C in 1980 to 1.17 °C in 2023 (GISTEMP vs. 1951–1980), what is the rate per decade?
  **A:** 0.90 °C over 4.3 decades ≈ 0.21 °C per decade.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ice and sea level
*Student:* Graph Arctic sea-ice extent and global sea-level data; calculate rates of change.

*How to teach:* Graph Arctic September sea-ice extent and sea-level data; calculate rates.

- **Q:** Satellite sea level rose about 100 mm from 1993 to 2023. Average rate?
  **A:** About 3.3 mm per year (and the rate has been accelerating).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Proxy data
*Student:* Compare current CO₂ levels to 800,000 years of ice-core data and describe how unusual the current rate is.

*How to teach:* Compare current CO₂ (above 420 ppm) to the 800,000-year range (~180–300 ppm).

- **Q:** How does today's CO₂ compare to the ice-core record?
  **A:** It is far higher than any value in 800,000 years (~180–300 ppm), and the rise of 100+ ppm in under two centuries is much faster than natural glacial–interglacial changes, which took thousands of years.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a data-interpretation quiz.

*How to teach:* Data-interpretation quiz.

- **Q:** What is proxy data?
  **A:** Indirect evidence of past climate, such as ice cores, tree rings, corals, and sediment layers.

**Assessment:** Data-interpretation quiz.

**Answer key:** Data-interpretation quiz key (sample): 1) Weather vs. climate. 2) Anomaly = difference from baseline. 3) Warming rate ≈ 0.2 °C/decade since 1980. 4) Global temperature ~1.2–1.3 °C above late-1800s levels in recent years. 5) Sea-level rise ~3–4 mm/yr in satellite era, accelerating. 6) Arctic summer sea ice declining ~12–13% per decade. 7) CO₂ >420 ppm vs. 180–300 ppm natural range. 8) Proxy data examples. Scoring: 90%+ advanced, 75–89% proficient.

## American Government & Civics (S1) / Economics (S2): Taxes, spending, and the budget

**Prepare:** Open CBO budget infographics (cbo.gov) and the California budget summary (ebudget.ca.gov). Bookmark FRED series GFDEGDQ188S (federal debt as % of GDP). Find a federal budget simulator (e.g., CRFB 'Debt Fixer').

**Watch for:**
- Confusing deficit and debt. Deficit is one year's shortfall; debt is the accumulated total.
- Thinking foreign aid is a large share of federal spending. It's about 1%.
- Calling sales taxes progressive. They take a larger share of income from lower earners (regressive).

### Day 1 (Monday): Federal budget
*Student:* Analyze CBO charts of federal revenue and spending; identify the largest categories.

*How to teach:* Analyze CBO charts. The student lists the largest revenue and spending categories. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What are the largest federal spending categories?
  **A:** Social Security, Medicare and other health programs, defense, and net interest.
- **Q:** Largest federal revenue source?
  **A:** Individual income taxes, followed by payroll taxes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): California budget
*Student:* Compare California's budget sources (income tax, sales tax) and spending (K-12, health, corrections).

*How to teach:* Compare California's budget sources and spending. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** What is California's largest general-fund revenue source?
  **A:** The personal income tax.
- **Q:** Largest spending area?
  **A:** K–12 education (guaranteed by Prop 98), followed by health and human services.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tax fairness
*Student:* Calculate tax burdens under progressive, proportional, and regressive systems for three sample incomes.

*How to teach:* Compute taxes for incomes $20,000, $50,000, $200,000 under three systems. Before moving on, have the student answer the check questions below without notes, then compare with the answers and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Under a 10% flat tax, what does each pay?
  **A:** $2,000, $5,000, $20,000 — proportional.
- **Q:** Under 0% up to $20,000, 10% to $50,000, 20% above, what does $200,000 pay?
  **A:** $0 + $3,000 + $30,000 = $33,000 (16.5% average rate).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Debt and deficits
*Student:* Graph debt-to-GDP since 1940 using FRED and explain causes of major increases.

*How to teach:* Graph debt-to-GDP since 1940. Explain major increases. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** When did debt-to-GDP peak and why?
  **A:** Right after World War II (about 106–119% depending on measure); it rose again after 2008 and 2020.

### Day 5 (Friday): Budget simulation
*Student:* Complete a federal budget simulation, balancing priorities, and take the Unit 10 test.

*How to teach:* Complete the simulation; give the Unit 10 test. Before moving on, have the student answer the check question below without notes, then compare with the answer and reteach anything missed.

- **Q:** Give two ways to reduce a deficit.
  **A:** Raise revenue (taxes) or cut spending.

**Assessment:** Budget simulation and Unit 10 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 10 test key: 1) Largest federal spending: Social Security, health (Medicare/Medicaid), defense, net interest. 2) Largest revenue: individual income tax, then payroll taxes. 3) CA: personal income tax; K–12 largest spending (Prop 98). 4) Flat 10%: $2,000/$5,000/$20,000. 5) Progressive example: $200,000 pays $33,000. 6) Deficit = annual shortfall; debt = accumulated. 7) Debt-to-GDP peaked after WWII. Budget simulation rubric (4 levels): 4 = meets the target, explains trade-offs for each choice and who is affected.

## Advanced Art & Portfolio: Relief printmaking

**Prepare:** Soft-cut linoleum or foam sheets, carving tools (V and U gouges) and a bench hook, water-based block ink, brayer, inking plate, spoon or baren, printing paper, pencil. SAFETY: always carve away from your body and keep the non-carving hand behind the blade. Images of Hokusai's The Great Wave off Kanagawa (c. 1831) and José Guadalupe Posada's calaveras.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting the image prints in reverse, especially text.
- Carving toward the hand. Use a bench hook and carve away.
- Inconsistent inking, which makes an uneven edition. Roll ink thinly and evenly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Printmaking history
*Student:* Study Japanese ukiyo-e and José Guadalupe Posada's prints; note how printmaking spreads ideas.

*How to teach:* Study ukiyo-e (Edo-period Japanese woodblock prints, e.g., Hokusai and Hiroshige, multiple blocks for color) and Posada (Mexican printmaker whose calavera prints like La Calavera Catrina satirized society in cheap broadsides).

- **Q:** How did printmaking spread ideas?
  **A:** Multiple copies could be made cheaply and distributed widely — Posada's broadsides reached ordinary people with news and satire.
- **Q:** What is ukiyo-e?
  **A:** 'Pictures of the floating world' — Japanese woodblock prints (17th–19th centuries) of landscapes, actors, and daily life.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Design
*Student:* Design a block related to your investigation, reversing the image.

*How to teach:* Design the block; reverse the image (trace and flip).

- **Q:** How do you prepare a design with text for a relief print?
  **A:** Draw or transfer it mirror-reversed onto the block so it prints correctly.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Carve
*Student:* Carve the block safely, testing proofs as you go.

*How to teach:* Carve safely; pull test proofs.

- **Q:** In relief printing, which areas print?
  **A:** The raised (uncarved) surfaces; carved-away areas stay the paper color.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Print
*Student:* Print an edition of five with consistent registration.

*How to teach:* Print an edition of five with consistent registration.

- **Q:** What is registration?
  **A:** Aligning the block and paper in the same position for each print (and for multiple colors).

### Day 5 (Friday): Sign
*Student:* Sign and number prints properly and evaluate the edition.

*How to teach:* Sign and number prints in pencil: edition number (e.g., 3/5) lower left, title center, signature lower right.

- **Q:** What does '3/5' on a print mean?
  **A:** It is the third print of an edition of five.

**Assessment:** Print edition.

**Answer key:** Print edition rubric. 4 – Five consistent prints with even inking and clean registration, design connects to inquiry, correct reversal, proper signing/numbering, safe carving. 3 (proficient) – Five prints mostly consistent, signed and numbered correctly. 2 – Inconsistent or unsigned. 1 – Fewer than three prints.

## Lifetime Fitness II: Map, compass, and orienteering

**Prepare:** Baseplate compass, USGS topographic map of a local area (free download), cones or markers. California declination is about 11–13° east; check the map's declination diagram.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing contour lines with trails or roads.
- Holding the compass near metal or phones.
- Forgetting declination when going between map and compass.

### Day 1 (Monday): Topo maps
*Student:* Read contour lines on a USGS topo map and identify hills, valleys, and saddles.

*How to teach:* Read contour lines: closely spaced = steep; circles = hill; V's pointing uphill = valley/stream; saddle = low point between two hills.

- **Q:** Which way do contour V's point when crossing a stream?
  **A:** Upstream (uphill).
- **Q:** What is a saddle?
  **A:** A low area between two high points on a ridge.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Compass
*Student:* Learn to take and follow a bearing; adjust for declination.

*How to teach:* Teach taking and following a bearing, then adjusting for declination: with east declination (California, about 11–13° E), subtract the declination from a map (true) bearing to get the magnetic bearing to follow, and add it when going from compass to map. Check against the map's declination diagram.

- **Q:** Map bearing is 90° and declination is 12° east. What magnetic bearing do you follow?
  **A:** 78° (for east declination, subtract when converting true/map to magnetic).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Backyard course
*Student:* Set up and complete a 5-point backyard or park orienteering course.

*How to teach:* Set up and complete a 5-point backyard/park course.

- **Q:** What is the back bearing of 300°?
  **A:** 120°.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Park course
*Student:* Complete a permanent orienteering course or geocaching route in a local park.

*How to teach:* Complete a permanent orienteering course or geocaching route.

- **Q:** What is geocaching?
  **A:** An outdoor activity using GPS coordinates to find hidden containers (caches).

### Day 5 (Friday): Skill check
*Student:* Pass a navigation skills test.

*How to teach:* Navigation skills test.

- **Q:** Contour interval is 40 ft; you cross 5 contour lines going uphill. How much did you climb?
  **A:** 200 ft.

**Assessment:** Navigation skills test.

**Answer key:** Navigation test key (sample): 1) Close contours = steep. 2) V's point upstream. 3) Back bearing of 45° = 225°. 4) Map 90°, 12° E declination → 78° magnetic. 5) 5 lines × 40 ft = 200 ft. 6) Scale 1:24,000 → 1 inch = 2,000 ft. 7) Practical: complete 5-point course finding all controls. Proficient = 80%+ written and all practical points found.

## Personal Finance (S1) & College/Career Readiness (S2): Cal Grant & Middle Class Scholarship

**Prepare:** Read csac.ca.gov pages for Cal Grant A, B and C, the Middle Class Scholarship, and the California College Promise Grant; check the current award amounts and income/asset ceilings (they change yearly). Have a calculator.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Cal Grant B is less valuable. Cal Grant B provides a living-expense 'access award' and, after the first year (in most cases), tuition — often valuable for low-income students.
- Forgetting income and asset ceilings. Cal Grants have family income and asset limits along with GPA minimums.
- Assuming community college is free automatically. The California College Promise Grant waives the $46-per-unit enrollment fee for eligible students; many colleges also have local promise programs.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cal Grant types
*Student:* Chart Cal Grant A, B, and C: what each pays, at which schools, and eligibility requirements.

*How to teach:* Chart Cal Grant A, B and C: what each pays, where, and eligibility.

- **Q:** What does Cal Grant A pay for?
  **A:** Tuition and fees at UC and CSU (and part of tuition at eligible private colleges).
- **Q:** What minimum GPA does each require?
  **A:** Cal Grant A: 3.0; Cal Grant B: 2.0; Cal Grant C: for career/technical programs (GPA submitted but no set minimum in most cases — check current rules).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Eligibility checks
*Student:* Determine sample students' Cal Grant eligibility from income, assets, GPA, and deadline data.

*How to teach:* Determine sample students' eligibility from income, assets, GPA and deadline data.

- **Q:** A student has a 2.6 GPA, low income and filed on time. Which Cal Grant might they get?
  **A:** Cal Grant B (GPA ≥ 2.0 but below the 3.0 needed for A).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Middle Class Scholarship
*Student:* Read the MCS page and estimate what a sample UC or CSU student might receive.

*How to teach:* Read the MCS page and estimate an award for a sample UC/CSU student.

- **Q:** Who is the Middle Class Scholarship for?
  **A:** UC and CSU undergraduates (and some community college bachelor's programs) whose family income and assets are below the program's ceiling — including many middle-income families that don't qualify for Cal Grants.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Community college aid
*Student:* Research the California College Promise Grant and local free-tuition programs at a nearby community college.

*How to teach:* Research the California College Promise Grant and local free-tuition programs.

- **Q:** What does the California College Promise Grant cover?
  **A:** It waives community college enrollment fees ($46 per unit).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Build a total state-aid estimate for your top three schools.

*How to teach:* Build a total state-aid estimate for the top three schools.

- **Q:** Why use current-year figures from csac.ca.gov?
  **A:** Award amounts and income ceilings change each year.

**Assessment:** State-aid estimate for three schools.

**Answer key:** State-aid estimate rubric: 4 = for three schools lists likely Cal Grant type and amount (A: tuition/fees at UC/CSU; B: access award of roughly $1,650 for living costs plus tuition after the first year; C: for career/technical programs), MCS estimate where eligible, CCPG for community college, sources cited and current-year figures used; 3 = minor errors; 2 = missing a program; 1 = incomplete. Eligibility check key: Cal Grant A minimum GPA 3.0; Cal Grant B minimum GPA 2.0; Cal Grant C for occupational/technical programs; all require meeting income/asset ceilings, California residency (or AB 540), and filing by the deadline.
