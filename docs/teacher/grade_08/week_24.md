# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 24

## English Language Arts 8: The Diary of Anne Frank, Act 1, Scenes 3–5

**Prepare:** Read Act 1, Scenes 3–5 of the play. Borrow 'Anne Frank: The Diary of a Young Girl' and choose one entry to compare (e.g., an entry about her mother or about Hanukkah 1942). Note: the names Van Daan and Dussel are pseudonyms; the real people were the van Pels family and Fritz Pfeffer.

**Watch for:**
- Seeing Anne as simply 'rude.' Help the child see a teenager confined for years, testing independence under fear.
- Treating tension as only physical danger. Tension also comes from conflicts among the people hiding.

### Day 1 (Monday): Act 1, Scene 3
*Student:* Read Scene 3 and analyze the conflicts between Anne and Mrs. Van Daan and Anne and her mother.

*How to teach:* Read Scene 3. Child lists Anne's conflicts with Mrs. Van Daan and with her mother, with a line for each.

- **Q:** Why does Mrs. Van Daan get angry with Anne?
  **A:** Anne is loud and teasing, pesters Peter, and spills milk on Mrs. Van Daan's prized fur coat.
- **Q:** Who arrives at the end of the scene?
  **A:** Mr. Dussel, a dentist, who brings news of deportations and must share Anne's room.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Scenes 4–5
*Student:* Read Scenes 4–5 and analyze how the Hanukkah celebration and the break-in noise build tension.

*How to teach:* Read Scenes 4–5 aloud. Ask the child to mark when the mood shifts from joy to fear in the Hanukkah scene.

- **Q:** How does the Hanukkah scene build tension?
  **A:** The joyful celebration and Anne's gifts are interrupted by a noise downstairs — a thief; Peter knocks over a lamp, and everyone freezes, afraid the thief will report them.
- **Q:** What happens after Anne's nightmare (Scene 4)?
  **A:** She calls for her father and rejects her mother's comfort; Mrs. Frank is deeply hurt.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Diary vs play
*Student:* Compare one diary entry with its dramatized scene and note how the playwrights changed it.

*How to teach:* Read one diary entry and its dramatized scene; list what the playwrights changed and why (stage time, visual action, compressing events). Before moving on, ask: "Why might playwrights combine or invent moments?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Why might playwrights combine or invent moments?
  **A:** Plays need visible action and conflict in limited time, so events are compressed or dramatized.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Character analysis
*Student:* Write a character analysis of Anne supported by four quotations.

*How to teach:* Child writes the character analysis of Anne with four quotations. Check understanding with: "Give one trait of Anne with evidence." If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Give one trait of Anne with evidence.
  **A:** Optimistic and energetic — she treats hiding as an adventure early on and makes gifts for everyone at Hanukkah.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on Act 1.

*How to teach:* Give the Act 1 quiz (key). To check, ask: "Why must they stay silent during the day?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Why must they stay silent during the day?
  **A:** Workers in the business below could hear them and report them.

**Assessment:** Character analysis and quiz.

**Answer key:** Character analysis rubric (4 levels): 4 = clear claim about Anne's character (e.g., lively, outspoken and hopeful but struggling with her mother and growing up), four accurate quotations from Act 1 with commentary, organized paragraphs; 3 = three quotations; 2 = traits listed with little evidence; 1 = summary. Act 1 quiz: Mr. Dussel, a dentist, joins them and shares Anne's room; Anne clashes with Mrs. Van Daan and with her mother; after her nightmare Anne wants her father, not her mother, hurting Mrs. Frank; during Hanukkah a thief downstairs hears Peter knock over a lamp; Anne makes handmade gifts for everyone.

## Mathematics 8: Two-way tables and categorical association

**Prepare:** Survey sheet with two yes/no questions; calculator. Prepare the Unit 6 review and test.

**Watch for:**
- Comparing raw counts when group sizes differ. Use relative frequencies (percents) within each row or column.
- Computing the percent of the wrong total. Ask: 'percent of what?' — the row total or column total.

### Day 1 (Monday): Two-way tables
*Student:* Survey 20 people (or use given data) on two yes/no questions such as 'plays an instrument' and 'likes math'; organize in a two-way table.

*How to teach:* Survey 20 people (or use data) and fill in a two-way table with totals. To check, ask: "Table: instrument & likes math 8; instrument & not 2; no instrument & likes math 4; no instrument & not 6. How many surveyed? How many like math?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Table: instrument & likes math 8; instrument & not 2; no instrument & likes math 4; no instrument & not 6. How many surveyed? How many like math?
  **A:** 20 surveyed; 12 like math.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Relative frequency
*Student:* Compute row and column relative frequencies as percents and decide whether an association exists.

*How to teach:* Compute row percents and compare. Finish by asking: "Using that table, is there an association?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Using that table, is there an association?
  **A:** Yes: 8/10 = 80% of instrument players like math vs 4/10 = 40% of non-players — a big difference.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Analyzing associations
*Student:* Analyze 3 data sets (e.g., curfew vs chores) and write a claim with evidence from relative frequencies.

*How to teach:* Child analyzes 3 data sets and writes claims with relative-frequency evidence. Before moving on, ask: "If 60% of teens with a curfew do chores and 58% without a curfew do chores, is there an association?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** If 60% of teens with a curfew do chores and 58% without a curfew do chores, is there an association?
  **A:** Little or none — the percents are nearly equal.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit 6 review
*Student:* Review scatter plots, lines of fit and two-way tables with a mixed practice set.

*How to teach:* Mixed review: scatter plots, lines of fit, two-way tables. Check understanding with: "Line through (2, 10) and (6, 22). Equation?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Line through (2, 10) and (6, 22). Equation?
  **A:** m = 12/4 = 3; y = 3x + 4.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 6 test
*Student:* Unit test on bivariate data.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test. To check, ask: "Does a strong association prove one variable causes the other?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Does a strong association prove one variable causes the other?
  **A:** No — a third factor or coincidence may explain it.

**Assessment:** Unit 6 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 6 test suggested items: 1) Describe a scatter plot (direction, form, strength, outliers). 2) Line through (2, 10) and (6, 22) → y = 3x + 4. 3) Predict y at x = 8 → 28. 4) Residual if actual is 30 → +2. 5) Two-way table: instrument yes: 8 like math, 2 don't; no: 4 like, 6 don't → 80% vs 40%, so there is an association. 6) Explain why association ≠ causation. 1–3 points each.

## Science 8 (Integrated): Embryological evidence

**Prepare:** Print embryo image cards (fish, chicken, pig, human at early and late stages; reputable sources such as university or museum sites). Prepare the Unit 5 practice test and test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking human embryos 'become fish' first. Early vertebrate embryos share structures (pharyngeal arches, tails) from common ancestry; they develop differently, not through adult fish stages.
- Treating one line of evidence as proof. Scientists combine fossils, anatomy, embryology and DNA.

### Day 1 (Monday): Embryo comparison
*Student:* Compare images of fish, chicken, pig and human embryos at early and late stages; note similarities.

*How to teach:* Compare early and late embryo images. To check, ask: "What do early vertebrate embryos share?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What do early vertebrate embryos share?
  **A:** Pharyngeal arches (pouches) and a tail.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Explaining patterns
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining what embryological similarities suggest about common ancestry.

*How to teach:* Write a paragraph on what the similarities suggest. Finish by asking: "What do embryo similarities suggest?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** What do embryo similarities suggest?
  **A:** The organisms share a common ancestor.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Evidence synthesis
*Student:* Make a graphic organizer with three lines of evidence for evolution.

*How to teach:* Make a graphic organizer of three lines of evidence. Before moving on, ask: "Name three lines of evidence for evolution." and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Name three lines of evidence for evolution.
  **A:** Fossil record, anatomical similarities, embryological similarities (also DNA).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit review
*Student:* Review rock strata, geologic time and fossil evidence with a practice test.

*How to teach:* Practice test with corrections. Check understanding with: "After 2 half-lives, what fraction remains?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** After 2 half-lives, what fraction remains?
  **A:** 1/4.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 test
*Student:* Unit test on Earth's history and evidence for common ancestry.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test. To check, ask: "What era did dinosaurs live in?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What era did dinosaurs live in?
  **A:** Mesozoic.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test sample answers: superposition and cross-cutting relationships; index fossils; geologic time scale with eras (Paleozoic, Mesozoic, Cenozoic); five mass extinctions; half-life (after 2 half-lives, 1/4 remains); whale transitional fossils; homologous vs analogous vs vestigial; embryos of vertebrates share pharyngeal arches and tails early, suggesting common ancestry.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: The war begins: North vs South

**Prepare:** Lincoln's First Inaugural Address (March 4, 1861) closing paragraphs; 1860 resource statistics (approx.: population North ~22 million vs South ~9 million, including ~3.5 million enslaved; railroad miles ~22,000 vs ~9,000; the North had the large majority of factories and industrial workers); American Battlefield Trust maps.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the North's victory was guaranteed. The South had skilled officers, interior lines and only needed to defend, not conquer.
- Calling Antietam a decisive Union battlefield victory. It was tactically a draw but strategically a Union success: Lee retreated, and Lincoln issued the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation.

### Day 1 (Monday): First Inaugural
*Student:* Read the closing paragraphs of Lincoln's First Inaugural ('better angels of our nature') and explain his message to the South.

*How to teach:* Read the First Inaugural's closing; explain his message.

- **Q:** What did Lincoln mean by 'the better angels of our nature'?
  **A:** He appealed to Southerners' shared memories and goodness, hoping they would choose union and peace: 'We are not enemies, but friends.'
- **Q:** Did Lincoln promise to end slavery in the South in 1861?
  **A:** No — he said he had no intention to interfere with slavery where it existed, but secession was illegal and he would hold federal property.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Resources compared
*Student:* Graph 1860 population, railroad miles and factory data for North and South and predict which side had the advantage.

*How to teach:* Graph 1860 resources; predict the advantage.

- **Q:** Which side had the advantage in resources, and why?
  **A:** The North — more than twice the population, far more railroads and most of the factories to produce weapons and supplies.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Strategies
*Student:* Compare the Union's Anaconda Plan with the Confederacy's defensive strategy on a map.

*How to teach:* Compare the Anaconda Plan with Confederate strategy on a map.

- **Q:** What were the two main parts of the Anaconda Plan?
  **A:** Blockade Southern ports and take control of the Mississippi River.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Early battles
*Student:* Map Fort Sumter, First Bull Run and Antietam using American Battlefield Trust maps and summarize outcomes.

*How to teach:* Map Fort Sumter, First Bull Run, Antietam; summarize outcomes.

- **Q:** Why was Antietam important?
  **A:** Lee's invasion of Maryland was stopped, giving Lincoln the opportunity to issue the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation and discouraging European recognition of the Confederacy.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on the war's beginning plus a resource-graph interpretation question.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and graph question.

- **Q:** What were the border states, and why did they matter?
  **A:** Slave states that stayed in the Union (DE, MD, KY, MO); they had key resources and locations (Maryland surrounds Washington, D.C.).

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: Fort Sumter (April 12, 1861) — Confederates fired on the fort in Charleston Harbor, starting the war; four more states seceded (Virginia, Arkansas, North Carolina, Tennessee); border states (Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri; later West Virginia) stayed in the Union; Anaconda Plan — blockade Southern ports and seize the Mississippi to split the Confederacy; Confederate strategy — defend, wear down Northern will, hope for British/French recognition ('King Cotton diplomacy'); First Bull Run (July 1861) — Confederate victory, showed the war would be long; Antietam (September 17, 1862) — bloodiest single day (about 23,000 casualties), Lee's invasion stopped. Graph interpretation: North's advantages in population, railroads and factories.

## Spanish 1B: Ayudamos en la comunidad

**Watch for:**

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Playwriting: ideas and structure

**Prepare:** Notebook, script-format template. Plot diagram (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution).

**Watch for:**
- Too many characters or locations for a short play. Correct: 2–3 characters and one setting for 5–8 minutes.
- No clear conflict. Correct: a character must want something and face an obstacle.

### Day 1 (Monday): Story sources
*Student:* Brainstorm 10 premises from news stories, history (e.g., a Gold Rush camp) and personal experience.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm 10 premises.

- **Q:** What is a premise?
  **A:** A short statement of the play's basic situation: who, where and what conflict.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Choosing a premise
*Student:* Choose one and write a one-paragraph premise naming characters, setting and central conflict.

*How to teach:* Choose one; write a premise paragraph.

- **Q:** What three elements must the premise include?
  **A:** Characters, setting and central conflict.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Plot structure
*Student:* Outline exposition, rising action, climax and resolution for a 5–8 minute play.

*How to teach:* Outline exposition, rising action, climax, resolution.

- **Q:** What is the climax?
  **A:** The turning point of highest tension where the conflict comes to a head.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Character sketches
*Student:* Write character sketches with objectives and obstacles for 2–3 characters.

*How to teach:* Character sketches with objectives and obstacles.

- **Q:** Why write character sketches?
  **A:** So each character has clear wants, traits and a distinct voice.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Pitch the play to family and revise based on questions.

*How to teach:* Pitch the play; revise.

- **Q:** What should a pitch include?
  **A:** Title, premise, main characters, conflict and why it's interesting, in under a minute.

**Assessment:** Play outline and character sketches.

**Answer key:** Play outline and sketches: 4 = clear premise, complete plot structure with a strong climax, 2–3 character sketches each with objective, obstacle and traits, revised after pitch; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = weak conflict; 1 = incomplete.

## Physical Education 8: Square and folk dance

**Prepare:** Music player; free square-dance music with calls (search 'beginner square dance calls'). Clear space for a square of 4 couples (or 2). Instructions for the Virginia reel or zapateado basic.

**Watch for:**
- Rushing ahead of the music. Correct: move on the beat; listen for calls.
- Ignoring partner etiquette. Correct: bow/curtsy, offer hands gently, thank partners.

### Day 1 (Monday): Basic calls
*Student:* Learn honor your partner, do-si-do, swing and promenade.

*How to teach:* Teach honor your partner, do-si-do (pass right shoulders, back-to-back, return), swing, promenade (counterclockwise).

- **Q:** Describe a do-si-do.
  **A:** Partners face, walk forward passing right shoulders, step side to side back-to-back, and walk backward to place passing left shoulders.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Squares
*Student:* Practice a simple square dance with family or a group of four to eight.

*How to teach:* Dance a simple square.

- **Q:** In which direction do couples promenade?
  **A:** Counterclockwise around the square.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Calling
*Student:* Take a turn as caller, giving clear calls on the beat.

*How to teach:* Take a turn as caller on the beat.

- **Q:** Why must calls come before the beat?
  **A:** So dancers have time to hear and start the move on the beat.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Folk dance
*Student:* Learn a Mexican folk dance step (e.g., zapateado basic) or the Virginia reel.

*How to teach:* Learn zapateado basic or Virginia reel.

- **Q:** What is zapateado?
  **A:** A Mexican folk dance footwork style with rhythmic heel and toe stamping.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Perform a full square dance; self-assess timing and etiquette.

*How to teach:* Perform; self-assess timing and etiquette.

- **Q:** Is square dancing MVPA?
  **A:** Yes — continuous moving dances can raise heart rate into the moderate range.

**Assessment:** Performance and self-assessment.

**Answer key:** Performance and self-assessment: 4 = executes all calls on the beat, correct formations, consistent etiquette, can call one sequence; 3 = minor timing errors; 2 = frequent errors; 1 = unable to complete.

## Health Education 8: Helping a friend and unit assessment

**Prepare:** Prepare scenario cards and a jeopardy grid (categories: Alcohol, Opioids, Other Drugs, Marketing, Addiction). Note SAMHSA National Helpline 1-800-662-4357. Write the unit test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking telling an adult is 'snitching.' Correct: getting help for a friend's health and safety is caring, not betrayal.
- Confronting a friend while they are high. Correct: talk when they are sober, in private, using I-messages.

### Day 1 (Monday): Signs
*Student:* List signs a friend may be using substances.

*How to teach:* List signs a friend may be using: changes in friends, grades, mood, sleep, secrecy, smell, missing money, paraphernalia.

- **Q:** Name three signs.
  **A:** Dropping grades, new friend group, mood swings, secrecy, red eyes or smell of smoke/vape, losing interest in activities, missing money.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Conversation
*Student:* Role-play expressing concern to a friend using I-messages.

*How to teach:* Teach I-messages ('I feel worried when I see you vaping before school because I care about you'). Role-play; switch roles.

- **Q:** Write an I-message for a friend who's drinking at parties.
  **A:** 'I feel worried when you drink at parties because I care about you and don't want you to get hurt. Can we talk?'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Getting help
*Student:* Identify adults and hotlines (e.g., SAMHSA helpline) to involve.

*How to teach:* List adults (parent, counselor, coach, doctor) and hotlines: SAMHSA 1-800-662-4357, 911 in emergencies.

- **Q:** What is the SAMHSA National Helpline?
  **A:** 1-800-662-HELP (4357), free, confidential, 24/7 treatment referral and information.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Review
*Student:* Review unit concepts with a jeopardy-style game.

*How to teach:* Play jeopardy reviewing weeks 19–23.

- **Q:** What drug can reverse an opioid overdose?
  **A:** Naloxone (Narcan).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit test
*Student:* Take the unit test on alcohol, opioids, other drugs, marketing and addiction.

*How to teach:* Give the unit test; review.

- **Q:** What does the Good Samaritan law do?
  **A:** Protects people who call 911 for an overdose from arrest for small-amount drug possession.

**Assessment:** Role-play and unit test.

**Answer key:** Unit test key: 1) only time lowers BAC 2) under-21 driving limit 0.01% 3) fentanyl in counterfeit pills 4) overdose signs: pinpoint pupils, slow breathing, unresponsive 5) naloxone steps 6) Good Samaritan law 7) inhalants can cause sudden death 8) teen caffeine ≤100 mg 9) higher THC potency risks 10) marketing tactics: flavors, influencers 11) CA flavored tobacco ban 12) addiction = chronic treatable brain disease 13) tolerance definition 14) I-messages and telling an adult. Role-play: proficient = I-message, caring tone, offers help, involves adult.
