# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 2

## English Language Arts 8: 'The Monkey's Paw': foreshadowing and dialogue

**Prepare:** Read 'The Monkey's Paw' (three parts). Print a plot diagram. Parental preview: a son dies in a machinery accident; the ending is eerie.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking foreshadowing is the same as prediction. Foreshadowing is the author's hint; a prediction is the reader's guess based on it.
- Dangling modifiers ('Walking home, the paw felt heavy'). The noun right after the participial phrase must be the one doing the action.

### Day 1 (Monday): Read Part I
*Student:* Read Part I and list every warning given about the paw; predict what the first wish will bring.

*How to teach:* Read Part I together; child lists every warning about the paw and predicts the first wish's result. Before moving on, ask: "What warnings does Sergeant-Major Morris give?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** What warnings does Sergeant-Major Morris give?
  **A:** He says a fakir put a spell on it to show that fate rules people's lives and those who interfere with it do so to their sorrow; the first owner's third wish was for death; he throws it on the fire and says 'Better let it burn'; and he warns, 'If you must wish, wish for something sensible.'
- **Q:** What does Mr. White wish for?
  **A:** Two hundred pounds.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Read Parts II–III
*Student:* Finish the story and complete a plot diagram marking where suspense peaks.

*How to teach:* Finish the story; child completes a plot diagram and marks the peak of suspense (the knocking at the door). Check understanding with: "How does the first wish come true?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** How does the first wish come true?
  **A:** Herbert is killed in the machinery at Maw and Meggins, and the company pays £200 as compensation.
- **Q:** What happens with the last two wishes?
  **A:** Mrs. White makes her husband wish Herbert alive; a knocking comes; Mr. White uses the third wish (implied to wish it away) and the road is empty when she opens the door.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dialogue that propels
*Student:* Choose 3 lines of dialogue (e.g., Sergeant-Major Morris's 'Better let it burn') and write how each propels action or reveals character.

*How to teach:* Model how one line of dialogue reveals character and moves the plot; the child does 3. To check, ask: "What does 'Better let it burn' reveal?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What does 'Better let it burn' reveal?
  **A:** Morris knows the paw's danger from experience and wants it destroyed, which raises the reader's dread and pushes Mr. White to rescue it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: participles
*Student:* Identify present and past participles used as adjectives; combine 10 pairs of sentences using participial phrases and fix 3 dangling modifiers.

*How to teach:* Teach present (-ing) and past (-ed/-en) participles as adjectives. Child combines 10 sentence pairs and fixes 3 dangling modifiers.

- **Q:** Fix: 'Clutching the paw, the wish was made.'
  **A:** 'Clutching the paw, Mr. White made the wish.'
- **Q:** Combine: 'The old man shook. He dropped the paw.'
  **A:** 'Shaking, the old man dropped the paw.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a timed constructed response: 'How does Jacobs use foreshadowing to build dread?' and take a roots quiz on cred-, fid-, -ulous.

*How to teach:* Timed response (25 minutes) on foreshadowing, then the roots quiz. Before moving on, ask: "What does 'credulity' mean and how does it fit the story?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** What does 'credulity' mean and how does it fit the story?
  **A:** A readiness to believe; the Whites move from mocking the paw to believing in it.

**Assessment:** Timed constructed response and vocabulary quiz.

**Answer key:** Constructed response rubric (4 levels): 4 = clear answer that Jacobs plants warnings to make readers dread the wishes, with 2–3 specific examples (Morris throwing the paw in the fire and saying 'Better let it burn'; the previous owner's third wish was for death; Herbert's joke that he never expects to see the money; the face Herbert sees in the fire), each explained; 3 = two examples, thin explanation; 2 = one example or plot summary; 1 = off-topic. Roots quiz: cred- = believe (credible, incredulous); fid- = faith/trust (fidelity, confide); -ulous = inclined to (credulous = too ready to believe).

## Mathematics 8: Properties of rigid motions and coordinates

**Prepare:** Gather graph paper, tracing paper, ruler, protractor; open GeoGebra (free at geogebra.org) and practice drawing a line and using the Rotate tool yourself first.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up the rotation rules. Have the child test with one point, e.g., (2, 0) rotated 90° counterclockwise must land on the positive y-axis at (0, 2), so the rule is (x, y) → (−y, x).
- Writing a translation rule with the wrong sign (moving left as +). Remind: right/up add, left/down subtract.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rigid motions preserve measures
*Student:* Measure side lengths and angles of a polygon and its image after three different moves; write a conclusion about what stays the same.

*How to teach:* Have the child measure all sides and angles of a quadrilateral, then perform a translation, a rotation and a reflection and measure the images. Ask them to write a conclusion; guide them to 'side lengths and angle measures stay the same.'

- **Q:** A triangle has sides 3, 4 and 5 cm and a 90° angle. After a reflection then a rotation, what are the image's measures?
  **A:** The same: sides 3, 4, 5 cm and a 90° angle, because rigid motions preserve lengths and angles.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Parallel lines stay parallel
*Student:* Rotate a pair of parallel lines 180 degrees about a point using GeoGebra and explain why the images are parallel to the originals.

*How to teach:* In GeoGebra draw two parallel lines and a point not on them; rotate both lines 180° about the point. Ask the child to explain why the images are parallel to the originals and to each other.

- **Q:** Why is a line's image under a 180° rotation parallel to the original line?
  **A:** A 180° rotation sends every direction to its exact opposite, so the image line runs in the same direction (same slope) as the original; and because rigid motions keep lines that never meet from meeting, parallel lines stay parallel.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Coordinate translations
*Student:* Translate figures on the coordinate plane and write the rule (x, y) -> (x + a, y + b) for 6 examples.

*How to teach:* Model one translation on the coordinate plane and build a before/after table of coordinates; ask what was added to x and to y. The child writes the rule (x, y) → (x + a, y + b) for 6 examples.

- **Q:** Apply (x, y) → (x − 3, y + 2) to the point (5, 1).
  **A:** (2, 3).
- **Q:** A point moves from (−1, 4) to (3, 0). Write the rule.
  **A:** (x, y) → (x + 4, y − 4).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Coordinate reflections & rotations
*Student:* Discover the rules for reflecting over the x- and y-axes and rotating 90 and 180 degrees about the origin by plotting and comparing coordinates.

*How to teach:* Have the child plot a triangle, reflect/rotate it with tracing paper, then list original and image coordinates side by side to discover each rule. Summarize on an index card: x-axis (x, −y); y-axis (−x, y); 90° CCW (−y, x); 90° CW (y, −x); 180° (−x, −y).

- **Q:** Rotate (2, −5) 90° counterclockwise about the origin.
  **A:** (5, 2), using (x, y) → (−y, x).
- **Q:** Reflect (−3, 7) over the y-axis.
  **A:** (3, 7).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a 10-problem coordinate-transformation worksheet, then write rules from before-and-after coordinate lists.

*How to teach:* Child completes the 10-problem worksheet, then is given before-and-after coordinate lists and names the rule. Review & check day: have the child redo any missed problem in the notebook and explain the error in one sentence.

- **Q:** Before: (1, 2), (3, 4). After: (−1, 2), (−3, 4). Which transformation?
  **A:** A reflection over the y-axis: (x, y) → (−x, y).

**Assessment:** Exit ticket: write the coordinate rule for 4 transformations and justify which properties are preserved.

**Answer key:** Four rules (1 point each): 1) translation 5 left, 1 up: (x, y) → (x − 5, y + 1); 2) reflection over the x-axis: (x, y) → (x, −y); 3) rotation 180° about origin: (x, y) → (−x, −y); 4) rotation 90° counterclockwise about origin: (x, y) → (−y, x). Justification (2 points): rigid motions preserve side lengths, angle measures and parallelism, so the image is congruent to the original; full credit names at least lengths and angles.

## Science 8 (Integrated): Forces, net force and friction

**Prepare:** Rubber bands (or a small spring scale), a shoe, string, a ruler, three floor surfaces (carpet, tile, wood). Bookmark the PhET tug-of-war screen.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking an object needs a net force to keep moving. Net force changes motion; with zero net force a moving object keeps moving at constant velocity.
- Adding forces in opposite directions instead of subtracting. Opposite forces subtract; the net force points toward the larger one.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pushes and pulls
*Student:* Draw force diagrams (arrows) for 6 everyday situations such as a book on a table and a tug-of-war.

*How to teach:* Draw arrows from the center of an object; arrow length shows strength. The child draws force diagrams for 6 situations, labeling each force.

- **Q:** Draw the forces on a book resting on a table.
  **A:** Gravity (weight) pointing down and the normal force from the table pointing up, equal in size, so net force is zero.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Net force
*Student:* Use the PhET Forces and Motion tug-of-war screen to predict and test motion for 8 combinations of forces; record net force calculations.

*How to teach:* In PhET tug-of-war, the child predicts motion before pressing Go for 8 combinations and calculates net force each time. Check understanding with: "100 N pulls left and 150 N pulls right. Net force and motion?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** 100 N pulls left and 150 N pulls right. Net force and motion?
  **A:** 50 N to the right; the cart accelerates right.
- **Q:** If the net force is zero, can the cart be moving?
  **A:** Yes — it keeps moving at constant velocity (or stays at rest).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Friction investigation
*Student:* Pull a shoe across carpet, tile and wood with a rubber-band force meter; measure stretch and rank surfaces by friction.

*How to teach:* Tie the shoe to a rubber band; pull it slowly at steady speed across each surface and measure stretch. Keep the same shoe, same pull speed and same rubber band (controlled variables).

- **Q:** Why use the same shoe on all surfaces?
  **A:** To control variables so only the surface changes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Analyzing friction data
*Student:* Graph friction results, identify variables held constant, and write a claim-evidence-reasoning (CER) paragraph.

*How to teach:* The child graphs stretch for each surface (bar graph), lists controlled variables, and writes a CER paragraph. Finish by asking: "Why a bar graph instead of a line graph?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Why a bar graph instead of a line graph?
  **A:** Surface type is categorical, not continuous.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on net force diagrams and the friction CER.

*How to teach:* Give the force-diagram quiz and collect the CER. Before moving on, ask: "What force always opposes sliding motion?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** What force always opposes sliding motion?
  **A:** Friction.

**Assessment:** Friction CER and force diagram quiz.

**Answer key:** Force-diagram quiz: 1) Book on a table: gravity down, table's normal force up, equal → net 0. 2) 30 N right and 20 N left → 10 N right. 3) 50 N and 50 N opposite → 0 N, balanced. 4) Two people pull right with 40 N and 25 N, one pulls left with 60 N → 5 N right. CER rubric (4 levels): 4 = claim answers the question; evidence cites specific measured data (numbers with units) from the lab; reasoning links evidence to the science idea; 3 = all parts, reasoning thin; 2 = claim with vague evidence; 1 = claim only. Sample claim: 'Carpet produces the most friction because the rubber band stretched the most (e.g., 6 cm vs 3 cm on tile).'

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: The Great Awakening and the road to revolution

**Prepare:** Map of the 13 colonies; a short Whitefield or Edwards sermon excerpt; Paul Revere's engraving 'The Bloody Massacre' (1770); Captain Thomas Preston's account; depositions from Lexington (e.g., from Avalon Project or teachinghistory.org).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking colonists objected to taxes because they were high. Their main complaint was taxation without representation in Parliament.
- Accepting Revere's engraving as an accurate photo-like record. It was propaganda: soldiers in a neat line firing on command, an officer ordering fire, and a 'Butcher's Hall' sign.

### Day 1 (Monday): Great Awakening
*Student:* Read a short excerpt from a George Whitefield or Jonathan Edwards sermon and discuss how revival ideas encouraged questioning authority.

*How to teach:* Read a Great Awakening sermon excerpt; discuss how revivals encouraged people to think for themselves.

- **Q:** How did the Great Awakening encourage questioning authority?
  **A:** It taught that individuals could have a direct relationship with God without relying on established ministers, created new churches and splits, and encouraged people to challenge traditional leaders.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Taxes and protest
*Student:* Create a cause-and-effect chain from the French and Indian War debt to the Stamp Act, Townshend Acts and boycotts.

*How to teach:* Build a cause-effect chain from French and Indian War debt to taxes and boycotts.

- **Q:** Why did Britain tax the colonies after 1763?
  **A:** To pay the huge debt from the French and Indian War and the cost of defending the colonies.
- **Q:** How did colonists resist the Stamp Act?
  **A:** Boycotts of British goods (nonimportation), the Stamp Act Congress, protests by the Sons of Liberty; it was repealed in 1766.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Boston Massacre sources
*Student:* Compare Paul Revere's engraving with Captain Preston's account; identify point of view and propaganda.

*How to teach:* Compare Revere's engraving with Preston's account.

- **Q:** How does Revere's engraving show point of view?
  **A:** It portrays soldiers as cruel attackers firing on calm, defenseless colonists — patriot propaganda.
- **Q:** Who defended Preston in court, and what was the result?
  **A:** John Adams; Preston was acquitted (two soldiers were convicted of manslaughter).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Lexington and Concord
*Student:* Read eyewitness depositions from April 19, 1775 and decide who fired first using evidence.

*How to teach:* Read the depositions; decide who fired first using evidence.

- **Q:** Can historians prove who fired first at Lexington?
  **A:** No — eyewitness accounts conflict, each side blamed the other; the best answer is 'unknown,' supported by contradictory testimony.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Timeline quiz on 1754–1775 and a short sourcing response.

*How to teach:* Give the timeline quiz and short sourcing response.

- **Q:** What were the Intolerable Acts?
  **A:** 1774 laws punishing Massachusetts for the Tea Party — closed Boston Harbor, limited town meetings, allowed quartering of troops.

**Assessment:** Sourcing response on the Boston Massacre and timeline quiz.

**Answer key:** Sourcing response rubric (1–4): identifies author, date and purpose of each source (Revere — Boston patriot silversmith, 1770, to rouse anger against Britain; Preston — the British captain, writing to defend himself), notes differences (Revere shows orderly firing on peaceful citizens; Preston describes a threatening mob with clubs, snowballs and a confused order), judges reliability considering bias. Timeline quiz key: French and Indian War 1754–1763; Proclamation of 1763; Stamp Act 1765; Townshend Acts 1767; Boston Massacre March 5, 1770; Boston Tea Party December 16, 1773; Intolerable (Coercive) Acts 1774; First Continental Congress 1774; Lexington and Concord April 19, 1775.

## Spanish 1B: The verb venir and describing a meal

**Prepare:** Conjuguemos account; plan a real family meal for day 3. A short restaurant scene video in Spanish (textbook video or YouTube).

**Watch for:**
- Conjugating venir as regular: 'yo veno.' The yo form is vengo; it is also e→ie: vienes, viene, vienen (venimos, venís unchanged).
- Saying 'La comida es rica' when you mean it tastes good right now. 'Está rica' = it tastes delicious (this dish); 'es rica' can describe a type of food or mean rich (wealthy) for people.

### Day 1 (Monday): Venir
*Student:* Learn the forms of venir (vengo, vienes, viene, venimos, venís, vienen) and complete a Conjuguemos drill to 90%.

*How to teach:* Teach venir forms; Conjuguemos to 90%.

- **Q:** Conjugate venir for yo and ellos.
  **A:** vengo; vienen.
- **Q:** Complete: Nosotros ___ a las ocho.
  **A:** venimos.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): ¿Quién viene?
*Student:* Write invitations for a family dinner and sentences stating who comes, when, and from where.

*How to teach:* Write dinner invitations and who comes from where.

- **Q:** Write: 'My aunt comes from San Diego on Saturday.'
  **A:** Mi tía viene de San Diego el sábado.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Taste words
*Student:* Learn delicioso, picante, salado, dulce, rico and describe five foods during a real meal.

*How to teach:* Describe five foods during a real meal.

- **Q:** Say: 'The salsa is very spicy.'
  **A:** La salsa está muy picante.
- **Q:** Say: 'The cake is sweet and delicious.'
  **A:** El pastel está dulce y delicioso.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Listening
*Student:* Watch a restaurant scene video in Spanish and list what each customer orders and how they describe it.

*How to teach:* Watch a restaurant scene; list what each customer orders and how they describe it.

- **Q:** If a customer says 'La sopa está un poco salada,' what's the complaint?
  **A:** The soup is a little salty.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a venir and food description quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the venir and description quiz.

- **Q:** Ask: 'Are you coming to dinner?'
  **A:** ¿Vienes a la cena?

**Assessment:** Venir quiz (80%+).

**Answer key:** Venir quiz: vengo, vienes, viene, venimos, venís, vienen. Samples: Mis abuelos (venir) de Texas → vienen; ¿(venir) tú a la fiesta? → vienes; Nosotros (venir) a las seis → venimos; Yo (venir) en coche → vengo. Food words: delicioso, picante (spicy), salado (salty), dulce (sweet), rico (tasty). 80% passing.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Value and shading

**Prepare:** Pencils (HB–4B), blending stump or tissue, a desk lamp in a dark room, white egg or ball, mug and small box.

**Watch for:**
- Outlining shapes in dark lines then filling in. Correct: form is shown by value changes, not outlines.
- Using only middle grays. Correct: include white highlights and the darkest darks for full contrast.

### Day 1 (Monday): Value scale
*Student:* Make a 9-step value scale and a smooth gradient bar.

*How to teach:* Make a 9-step value scale from white to darkest black and a smooth gradient bar.

- **Q:** What is value?
  **A:** The lightness or darkness of a color or tone.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Shading techniques
*Student:* Practice hatching, cross-hatching, stippling and blending in 4 squares.

*How to teach:* Fill four squares with hatching, cross-hatching, stippling and blending.

- **Q:** Which technique uses dots?
  **A:** Stippling.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Shading spheres
*Student:* Shade a sphere lit by a lamp showing highlight, core shadow and cast shadow.

*How to teach:* Shade a lamp-lit sphere showing highlight, light, core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow.

- **Q:** Where is the core shadow on a sphere?
  **A:** The darkest band on the form, just past where light stops hitting it, between the lit side and the reflected light.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Still life
*Student:* Draw a lit still life of an egg, mug and box with a full value range.

*How to teach:* Draw the lit still life with a full value range.

- **Q:** What makes a cast shadow look believable?
  **A:** It's darkest and sharpest near the object and lighter and softer farther away, and it falls opposite the light source.

### Day 5 (Friday): Critique
*Student:* Self-critique using a value checklist and revise the darkest darks.

*How to teach:* Self-critique with a checklist and darken the darkest areas.

- **Q:** Why does increasing contrast make a drawing look more 3-D?
  **A:** Strong lights next to deep darks create the illusion of form and depth.

**Assessment:** Still-life drawing graded with a value rubric.

**Answer key:** Value rubric (4 levels): Full value range (highlight to darkest dark); Form (core shadow, reflected light, cast shadow on each object); Technique (smooth, controlled shading); Observation (proportions and placement match the setup). Proficient = 3+ in each.

## Physical Education 8: Skill-related fitness components

**Prepare:** Set up cones for the Illinois agility run (10 m × 5 m with 4 center cones 3.3 m apart) and a 40-yard straight. Tape measure for long jump, chalk and wall for vertical jump, a 12-inch ruler, stopwatch.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing health-related and skill-related fitness. Correct: skill-related (agility, balance, coordination, power, reaction time, speed) supports sport performance; health-related supports overall health.
- Measuring the long jump to the toes. Correct: measure from the takeoff line to the back of the heel closest to the line.

### Day 1 (Monday): Six components
*Student:* Learn the six skill-related components and match each to a sport skill (e.g., power = volleyball spike); do a 20-minute circuit of one drill per component.

*How to teach:* Teach the six components and match each to a sport skill (agility – soccer dribbling cuts; balance – gymnastics beam; coordination – juggling/hitting; power – volleyball spike; reaction time – sprint start or goalkeeping; speed – stealing a base). Run a 20-minute circuit with one drill per component.

- **Q:** Match: a basketball rebound jump, a goalie save, a tennis player's footwork.
  **A:** Power; reaction time; agility.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Agility and speed
*Student:* Run the Illinois agility course set up with cones and a 40-yard dash; record best of two trials.

*How to teach:* Run the Illinois agility test (start lying face down) and a 40-yard dash; best of two each, with full rest.

- **Q:** What does the Illinois test measure and why start lying down?
  **A:** Agility — changing direction quickly; starting prone standardizes the start and adds getting up quickly.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Power
*Student:* Measure standing long jump and vertical jump (chalk-on-wall method); record best of three.

*How to teach:* Standing long jump: two-foot takeoff with arm swing, land on both feet. Vertical jump: chalk fingertips, mark standing reach, jump and touch; difference is the score. Best of three.

- **Q:** What technique adds distance to a standing long jump?
  **A:** Swing arms back then forcefully forward, bend hips/knees deeply, and drive out at about 45° while extending hips, knees and ankles.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Balance and reaction
*Student:* Time a one-leg stork stand with eyes closed and do the ruler-drop reaction test five times; convert drop distance to reaction time.

*How to teach:* Stork stand: hands on hips, one foot on the inside of the other knee, eyes closed, time until balance lost. Ruler drop: partner holds the ruler at 0 between the child's thumb and finger and drops without warning; record cm; reaction time t = √(2d ÷ 980) seconds.

- **Q:** If the ruler falls 15 cm, what is the reaction time?
  **A:** t = √(2 × 15 ÷ 980) = √0.0306 ≈ 0.175 seconds.

### Day 5 (Friday): Profile
*Student:* Create a skill-related fitness profile chart and identify your strongest and weakest component.

*How to teach:* The child charts all six components and identifies the strongest and weakest.

- **Q:** Which component is the weakest, and which drill would improve it?
  **A:** Answers vary; e.g., low agility → ladder and cone-cutting drills 2–3 times per week.

**Assessment:** Skill-related fitness profile chart.

**Answer key:** Profile chart: proficient (4) = all six components with a score, test name and unit; strongest and weakest identified with a sport connection and one improvement drill; 3 = one component missing; 2 = scores without analysis; 1 = incomplete.

## Health Education 8: Technology, social media and mental health

**Prepare:** Find where the device shows screen time (iPhone: Settings > Screen Time; Android: Digital Wellbeing). Preview a Common Sense Education lesson on digital wellbeing. Have last year's PE sleep log or start a two-day sleep log now.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all screen time is equally harmful. Correct: what you do (creating, connecting vs. endless scrolling), when (before bed) and how you feel afterward matter most.
- Believing 'the app is free.' Correct: the user's attention and data are the product; features are designed to keep people engaged longer.

### Day 1 (Monday): Screen audit
*Student:* Record your screen time by app for two days using device settings or a log.

*How to teach:* Show the child how to open the screen-time report by app. They log two days of minutes per app (or keep a paper log if no device) and note how they felt after each.

- **Q:** Which category of app takes most of your time, and is that how you want to spend it?
  **A:** Answers vary; a good answer names the app category with minutes and makes an honest judgment about whether it matches their priorities.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Algorithms
*Student:* Learn how feeds are designed to keep users scrolling; analyze one platform's features.

*How to teach:* Explain that feeds are ranked by algorithms that predict what keeps you watching. Pick one platform and list design features: infinite scroll, autoplay, notifications, streaks, likes, variable rewards.

- **Q:** Name two design features that keep users scrolling and explain one.
  **A:** Examples: infinite scroll removes natural stopping points; autoplay starts the next video automatically; streaks pressure daily use; notifications pull you back; likes give unpredictable rewards like a slot machine.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Comparison
*Student:* Discuss how edited images and highlight reels affect self-image; find two examples of filtered vs. real photos.

*How to teach:* Look at filtered vs. unfiltered photos (many creators post side-by-sides). Discuss highlight reels: people post their best moments. The child finds two examples and writes how they might affect someone's self-image.

- **Q:** Why can comparing yourself to social media posts lower self-esteem?
  **A:** Posts are edited, filtered and selected highlights, so you compare your everyday life to others' best moments, which is unrealistic.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sleep link
*Student:* Compare screen time before bed to your sleep log from PE and identify a pattern.

*How to teach:* Line up screen-time minutes in the hour before bed with bedtime and hours of sleep. The child writes one pattern (e.g., more late screen use → later sleep).

- **Q:** How do screens before bed affect sleep?
  **A:** Blue light can delay melatonin, and engaging content keeps the brain alert, so it takes longer to fall asleep and total sleep drops; teens need about 8–10 hours.

### Day 5 (Friday): Goal
*Student:* Write a SMART tech-balance goal and get a parent signature.

*How to teach:* Review SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound). The child writes a tech-balance goal; you check each letter and sign it.

- **Q:** Is 'I'll use my phone less' a SMART goal? Fix it.
  **A:** No — it is not specific, measurable or time-bound. Example fix: 'For the next 4 weeks, I will put my phone outside my bedroom by 9:30 p.m. on school nights and track it on a chart.'

**Assessment:** Screen-time analysis and SMART goal.

**Answer key:** Screen-time analysis (4 pts): two days logged by app, totals calculated, one pattern identified, one feeling noted. SMART goal (4 pts): 1 pt each for specific action, measurable tracking, realistic amount, time frame; parent signature required. Proficient: goal connects to a pattern found in the analysis (e.g., late-night use and sleep).
