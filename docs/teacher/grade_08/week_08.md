# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 8

## English Language Arts 8: The Outsiders, chapters 7–9: conflict and consequences

**Prepare:** Read chapters 7–9 ahead. Parental preview: the rumble (a gang fight) and Johnny's death in chapter 9 are emotional; be ready to talk about grief. Note: Johnny's line 'Useless...fighting's no good' actually comes in chapter 9, right after the rumble; in chapter 8 he says 'Sixteen years ain't long enough.'

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Greasers' rumble victory resolves the conflict. Johnny's dying words call fighting useless; nothing really changes.
- Believing passive voice always weakens writing. It can hide the doer on purpose (e.g., to show confusion in the chaos of a fight).

### Day 1 (Monday): Chapter 7
*Student:* Read chapter 7 and analyze Randy's conversation with Ponyboy as evidence that Socs face problems too.

*How to teach:* Read chapter 7. Ask the child to list everything Randy says to Ponyboy and decide what it proves about the Socs.

- **Q:** What does Randy tell Ponyboy?
  **A:** He's sick of the fighting, Bob's death has shaken him, and he won't go to the rumble — evidence that Socs suffer and question the violence too.
- **Q:** How do the newspapers describe Johnny and Ponyboy?
  **A:** As heroes for saving the children from the burning church.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Chapter 8
*Student:* Read chapter 8 and write about how Johnny's condition and words ('Useless...fighting's no good') affect Ponyboy.

*How to teach:* Read chapter 8. Discuss Johnny's condition and how it affects Ponyboy (fear, denial, guilt).

- **Q:** What does Johnny say about his life in chapter 8?
  **A:** 'Sixteen years ain't long enough' — he realizes he's done so little and wants to live.
- **Q:** Why won't Cherry visit Johnny?
  **A:** He killed Bob, whom she cared about.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Chapter 9
*Student:* Read chapter 9 and analyze the rumble and Johnny's last words.

*How to teach:* Read chapter 9. Child analyzes the rumble and Johnny's last words.

- **Q:** What does Johnny say to Dally and then to Ponyboy?
  **A:** 'Useless...fighting's no good' and then 'Stay gold, Ponyboy. Stay gold.'
- **Q:** Why is the victory hollow?
  **A:** Johnny dies right after; the fighting changed nothing important.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar for effect
*Student:* Rewrite a paragraph about the rumble in both active and passive voice and explain which creates more impact.

*How to teach:* Child rewrites a short rumble paragraph in active and passive voice and explains which is stronger. Check understanding with: "Rewrite in active voice: 'Ponyboy was kicked in the head by a Soc.'" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Rewrite in active voice: 'Ponyboy was kicked in the head by a Soc.'
  **A:** 'A Soc kicked Ponyboy in the head.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on chapters 7–9.

*How to teach:* Give the chapters 7–9 quiz (key). To check, ask: "Who wins the rumble?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Who wins the rumble?
  **A:** The Greasers.

**Assessment:** Quiz and voice rewrite.

**Answer key:** Chapters 7–9 quiz: 1) Newspapers call Ponyboy and Johnny heroes; Johnny's back is broken. 2) Randy tells Ponyboy he's sick of fighting and won't go to the rumble; Bob's death shook him. 3) Cherry won't visit Johnny because he killed Bob. 4) In chapter 8 Johnny says 'Sixteen years ain't long enough.' 5) The Greasers win the rumble. 6) After Dally says 'we won,' Johnny says 'Useless...fighting's no good' and then 'Stay gold, Ponyboy. Stay gold' before dying. Voice rewrite rubric: both versions grammatically correct (2), explanation of which has more impact and why (2) — e.g., active voice ('Darry slammed the Soc') is more vivid; passive can emphasize the victim.

## Mathematics 8: Proportional relationships and graphs

**Prepare:** Graph paper, a stopwatch, a measuring tape (10/20/30 m marked outside), Desmos. Find two real cell-plan prices for Thursday or use the ones in the questions.

**Watch for:**
- Putting the wrong quantity on top of the unit rate (minutes per mile vs miles per minute). Ask 'per one what?' and label units.
- Graphing a proportional relationship that does not start at (0, 0). Proportional graphs always pass through the origin.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rates review
*Student:* Find unit rates in tables, graphs and stories (e.g., 3 miles in 45 minutes) and write y = kx equations.

*How to teach:* Review: unit rate = output per 1 input. Model finding k from a table, graph and story, then writing y = kx; the child completes the set.

- **Q:** 3 miles in 45 minutes: what is the speed in miles per hour, and the equation?
  **A:** 45 min = 0.75 h, so 3 ÷ 0.75 = 4 mph; y = 4x (x in hours).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Graphing proportional relationships
*Student:* Graph 4 relationships, choosing scales for the axes, and identify the unit rate as the slope on each graph.

*How to teach:* Child graphs 4 relationships, choosing axis scales that fit the data, and marks the unit rate as the rise for a run of 1. Finish by asking: "The graph of a proportional relationship passes through (2, 5). What is the slope and equation?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** The graph of a proportional relationship passes through (2, 5). What is the slope and equation?
  **A:** Slope 2.5; y = 2.5x.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Walking-rate lab
*Student:* Time yourself and a family member walking 10, 20 and 30 meters; graph both on the same axes and compare speeds using slopes.

*How to teach:* Outside, time the child and a family member walking 10, 20 and 30 m. Graph distance vs time for both on one set of axes and compare slopes.

- **Q:** Someone walks 30 m in 20 s. What is their speed and which feature of the graph shows it?
  **A:** 1.5 m/s; the slope of the distance-time line.
- **Q:** Which walker is faster on the graph?
  **A:** The one with the steeper line (greater slope).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing representations
*Student:* Compare a table for one cell plan and an equation for another to decide which costs more per gigabyte; justify in writing.

*How to teach:* Present Plan A as a table (4 GB for $30, 10 GB for $75) and Plan B as y = 8x. The child finds each unit rate and writes a justified comparison.

- **Q:** Which plan costs more per gigabyte?
  **A:** Plan A is $30/4 = $7.50 per GB; Plan B is $8 per GB, so Plan B costs more.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: 5 problems comparing proportional relationships across tables, graphs and equations.

*How to teach:* Give the 5-problem quiz and collect the lab graph. Review & check day: have the child redo any missed problem in the notebook and explain the error in one sentence.

- **Q:** Is y = 3x + 2 proportional? Why?
  **A:** No — its graph does not pass through the origin (at x = 0, y = 2).

**Assessment:** Quiz plus walking-rate lab graph.

**Answer key:** Quiz (5 problems): 1) Table (2, 7), (4, 14), (6, 21) → k = 3.5, y = 3.5x. 2) Graph through (0,0) and (5, 20) → unit rate 4. 3) Which is faster: Ana y = 4.5x (miles in x hours) or Ben's table 9 miles in 2.5 hours? Ana 4.5 mph vs Ben 3.6 mph → Ana. 4) 3 miles in 45 min → 4 mph, y = 4x. 5) Plan A $30 for 4 GB vs Plan B y = 8x → A is $7.50/GB, B costs more. Lab graph: both lines start at the origin, labeled axes with units, steeper line identified as faster walker.

## Science 8 (Integrated): Energy transfer and transformation

**Prepare:** Foam pipe insulation cut lengthwise (or paper track), marbles, tape, string, washers. Bookmark PhET 'Energy Skate Park.'

**Watch for:**
- Thinking energy is 'used up' or lost when things slow down. Energy is conserved; it transforms into thermal energy and sound.
- Thinking a ball can rise higher than its starting point without extra energy. Without added energy, it can't exceed its starting height.

### Day 1 (Monday): Energy Skate Park
*Student:* Use the PhET Energy Skate Park sim to observe energy bar graphs as the skater moves; record 3 patterns.

*How to teach:* Run Energy Skate Park with bar graphs on. The child records 3 patterns (KE high at bottom, PE high at top, total constant without friction).

- **Q:** What happens to the total energy bar when friction is off?
  **A:** It stays constant; KE and PE trade off.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pendulum lab
*Student:* Build a string-and-washer pendulum and observe energy changes; explain why it eventually stops (thermal energy).

*How to teach:* Build a washer pendulum; observe swings and why it stops. Finish by asking: "Why does a pendulum eventually stop?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Why does a pendulum eventually stop?
  **A:** Air resistance and friction at the pivot transform its mechanical energy into thermal energy (and a little sound).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Marble roller coaster
*Student:* Build a foam-tube coaster with a loop; find the minimum starting height for the marble to complete the loop.

*How to teach:* Build a foam coaster with a loop; find the minimum start height. Before moving on, ask: "Why must the marble start higher than the top of the loop?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Why must the marble start higher than the top of the loop?
  **A:** It needs extra energy as KE at the top to stay on the track, plus energy lost to friction.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Argument writing
*Student:* Write a CER argument: 'When the marble slows down, where did its energy go?' using evidence from labs.

*How to teach:* The child writes a CER on where the marble's energy went. Check understanding with: "Where does energy go as a marble slows on a flat track?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Where does energy go as a marble slows on a flat track?
  **A:** Into thermal energy (heating the track and air) and sound through friction.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Peer/parent review of CER using a rubric; quiz on energy transfer.

*How to teach:* Review the CER with the rubric; give a short energy transfer quiz. To check, ask: "Name the energy transformation in a falling ball." and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Name the energy transformation in a falling ball.
  **A:** Gravitational PE to KE.

**Assessment:** Roller coaster design and energy CER.

**Answer key:** Coaster design: marble completes the loop; minimum start height recorded (theory for a frictionless slider is 2.5 times the loop radius above the bottom; real coasters need more because of friction and rolling). CER rubric (4 levels): 4 = claim answers the question; evidence cites specific measured data (numbers with units) from the lab; reasoning links evidence to the science idea; 3 = all parts, reasoning thin; 2 = claim with vague evidence; 1 = claim only. Proficient claim: 'The marble's energy wasn't destroyed; it was transformed into thermal energy and sound by friction, so the total stayed the same.'

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: Washington's presidency and the first parties

**Prepare:** Washington's Farewell Address excerpts (1796); background on Hamilton's financial plan and Jefferson's objections; Whiskey Rebellion summary.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking political parties were planned in the Constitution. Parties formed in the 1790s around Hamilton's and Jefferson's opposing visions.
- Assuming Washington wanted the U.S. isolated from all foreign contact. He warned against permanent alliances, while supporting commerce.

### Day 1 (Monday): Washington's precedents
*Student:* List precedents Washington set (cabinet, two terms, 'Mr. President') and their lasting effects.

*How to teach:* List Washington's precedents and their effects.

- **Q:** Name two precedents Washington set.
  **A:** Creating a cabinet; serving only two terms; being called 'Mr. President'; giving an inaugural address.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Hamilton vs Jefferson
*Student:* Debate the National Bank and the assumption of state debts; create a T-chart of the two visions.

*How to teach:* Debate the National Bank and assumption; T-chart of the two visions.

- **Q:** Why did Jefferson oppose the National Bank?
  **A:** He argued the Constitution didn't give Congress power to create a bank (strict construction) and it favored Northern merchants and bankers over farmers.
- **Q:** What was assumption?
  **A:** Hamilton's plan for the federal government to take over (assume) the states' Revolutionary War debts.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Whiskey Rebellion
*Student:* Compare the federal response to the Whiskey Rebellion with Shays' Rebellion.

*How to teach:* Compare the federal response to the Whiskey Rebellion with Shays' Rebellion.

- **Q:** How did the response to the Whiskey Rebellion (1794) differ from Shays' Rebellion?
  **A:** Under the Constitution, Washington called up about 13,000 militia and the rebellion collapsed, proving federal authority; under the Articles, Congress couldn't respond to Shays and Massachusetts relied on a privately funded militia.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Farewell Address
*Student:* Read excerpts and identify Washington's warnings about parties and foreign alliances.

*How to teach:* Read Farewell Address excerpts; identify warnings.

- **Q:** What two dangers did Washington warn against?
  **A:** Political parties (factions) and permanent alliances with foreign nations (also sectionalism).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on the Washington years and party formation.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and Farewell Address analysis.

- **Q:** Name the two first political parties and a leader of each.
  **A:** Federalists (Hamilton, Adams); Democratic-Republicans (Jefferson, Madison).

**Assessment:** Quiz and Farewell Address analysis.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: precedents — cabinet of advisers, two-term limit (followed until FDR; then 22nd Amendment), title 'Mr. President', inaugural address; first cabinet — Jefferson (State), Hamilton (Treasury), Knox (War), Randolph (Attorney General); Hamilton — national bank, assumption of state debts, tariffs, loose construction, strong central government, manufacturing; Jefferson — strict construction, states' rights, agrarian economy, opposed the bank; Federalist Party (Hamilton, Adams) vs Democratic-Republican Party (Jefferson, Madison); Whiskey Rebellion 1794 — Washington led about 13,000 militia, rebellion collapsed, showing the new government could enforce its laws (unlike Shays' Rebellion under the Articles); Proclamation of Neutrality 1793. Farewell Address analysis: warns against the 'baneful effects of the spirit of party', sectionalism and 'permanent alliances' with foreign nations.

## Spanish 1B: Los quehaceres and affirmative tú commands

**Prepare:** Chore chart. Chores: hacer la cama, lavar los platos, sacar la basura, pasar la aspiradora, poner la mesa, quitar la mesa, limpiar el baño, cortar el césped, dar de comer al perro, lavar la ropa, sacudir los muebles, cocinar, arreglar el cuarto, planchar.

**Watch for:**
- Using the infinitive as a command: 'Lavar los platos.' The tú command is the él form: Lava los platos.
- Forgetting irregulars: 'hace la cama' (he makes) vs 'haz la cama' (make the bed!).

### Day 1 (Monday): Chores
*Student:* Learn 14 chores and make a family chore chart in Spanish.

*How to teach:* Learn 14 chores; make a family chore chart.

- **Q:** How do you say 'to take out the trash'?
  **A:** sacar la basura.
- **Q:** What does 'pasar la aspiradora' mean?
  **A:** To vacuum.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Regular tú commands
*Student:* Learn that the affirmative tú command equals the él form (limpia, lava, come) and write commands for each chore.

*How to teach:* Teach regular tú commands (= él form); write a command for each chore.

- **Q:** Command: wash the dishes.
  **A:** Lava los platos.
- **Q:** Command: clean the bathroom.
  **A:** Limpia el baño.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Irregular commands
*Student:* Learn di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven and complete a 20-item command practice.

*How to teach:* Teach di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven; 20-item practice.

- **Q:** Command: set the table.
  **A:** Pon la mesa.
- **Q:** Command: go to your room.
  **A:** Ve a tu cuarto.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Simón dice
*Student:* Play Simón dice with a family member using 20 commands, then actually do three chores as commanded.

*How to teach:* Play Simón dice with 20 commands; do three chores as commanded.

- **Q:** Command: come to the kitchen.
  **A:** Ven a la cocina.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a chores and commands quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the commands quiz.

- **Q:** Command: make the bed.
  **A:** Haz la cama.

**Assessment:** Commands quiz and chore role-play.

**Answer key:** Commands quiz key: regular — limpia, lava, saca, pasa, cocina, come, bebe, escribe, barre; irregular — decir → di, hacer → haz, ir → ve, poner → pon, salir → sal, ser → sé, tener → ten, venir → ven. Samples: Make your bed → Haz tu cama; Put the table (set it) → Pon la mesa; Take out the trash → Saca la basura; Come here → Ven aquí; Be good → Sé bueno. Chore role-play: gives and follows 5 commands correctly.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Portraits and identity

**Prepare:** Images via Smithsonian Learning Lab or Google Arts & Culture: Gilbert Stuart's 'Lansdowne' portrait of George Washington (1796) and Kehinde Wiley's portrait of Barack Obama (2018); Frida Kahlo's 'Self-Portrait with Thorn Necklace and Hummingbird' (1940).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a portrait must be realistic. Correct: portraits can use symbols, color and setting to convey identity.
- Using random symbols. Correct: each symbol should have a reason connected to the subject.

### Day 1 (Monday): Presidential portraits
*Student:* Compare Gilbert Stuart's 'Lansdowne' portrait of Washington with Kehinde Wiley's portrait of Barack Obama; list how each uses pose and background.

*How to teach:* Compare pose and background in both portraits.

- **Q:** How do the backgrounds differ?
  **A:** Stuart uses classical columns, drapery and a rainbow sky to show authority; Wiley seats Obama in a chair against green foliage with flowers symbolizing places in his life (chrysanthemums for Chicago, jasmine for Hawaii, African blue lilies for Kenya).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Frida Kahlo
*Student:* Analyze Frida Kahlo's 'Self-Portrait with Thorn Necklace and Hummingbird' and explain the meaning of three symbols.

*How to teach:* Analyze three Kahlo symbols.

- **Q:** What might the thorn necklace and dead hummingbird mean?
  **A:** Thorns suggest pain and suffering (like Christ's crown of thorns); the hummingbird, a Mexican folk charm for luck in love, is dead — often linked to her divorce from Diego Rivera. The black cat suggests bad luck/death; butterflies suggest rebirth.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Group identity
*Student:* Examine portraits from a culture or community you belong to and write how art reinforces group identity.

*How to teach:* Examine portraits from a culture/community and write how art reinforces identity.

- **Q:** How can portraits reinforce group identity?
  **A:** They show shared clothing, symbols, settings or values that a community recognizes and celebrates.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Plan your portrait
*Student:* List 5 symbols, colors and a setting that represent you and sketch 3 thumbnail compositions.

*How to teach:* List 5 symbols, colors and a setting; 3 thumbnails.

- **Q:** Why choose a color scheme for your portrait?
  **A:** Color sets the mood and can carry meaning about personality.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Choose one plan, get feedback from a family member, and revise the composition sketch on final paper.

*How to teach:* Get feedback; revise composition on final paper.

- **Q:** What feedback question helps most?
  **A:** 'What do you think this portrait says about me?' — to see whether the symbols read clearly.

**Assessment:** Identity portrait plan with symbol key.

**Answer key:** Identity portrait plan: 4 = 5 symbols with meanings in a key, chosen color scheme with reason, setting, three thumbnails and a revised final composition reflecting feedback; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = symbols without meanings; 1 = incomplete.

## Physical Education 8: Team handball: skills

**Prepare:** A small soft ball, cones, tape for a 6 m semicircle around a goal (two cones about 3 m apart or a wall target).

**Watch for:**
- Running with the ball. Correct: max 3 steps while holding and 3 seconds holding, or dribble.
- Stepping into the goal area. Correct: only the goalkeeper may stand in the 6 m area; shooters can jump in and release before landing.

### Day 1 (Monday): Intro
*Student:* Learn team handball's origins and rules (3 seconds, 3 steps, goal area); play a passing keep-away game.

*How to teach:* Teach origins (popular in Europe, Olympic sport since 1972 indoors) and rules: 3 seconds, 3 steps, goal area. Play keep-away.

- **Q:** What are the 3-second and 3-step rules?
  **A:** A player may hold the ball no more than 3 seconds and take no more than 3 steps while holding it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Passing
*Student:* Practice shoulder, bounce and wrist passes on the move in triangle passing drills.

*How to teach:* Practice shoulder, bounce and wrist passes on the move in a triangle.

- **Q:** When is a bounce pass useful?
  **A:** To get past a defender's raised arms or to a teammate on the move close by.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Shooting
*Student:* Practice the step shot and jump shot from outside a taped 6-meter arc at a cone goal.

*How to teach:* Step shot and jump shot from outside the arc.

- **Q:** Why use a jump shot?
  **A:** It lets the shooter release the ball over defenders and lean into the area while in the air.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Goalkeeping
*Student:* Take turns as goalkeeper practicing ready stance and blocking low and high shots.

*How to teach:* Goalkeeper ready stance (feet shoulder-width, knees bent, hands up) and blocks.

- **Q:** What is the goalkeeper's ready position?
  **A:** Feet shoulder-width, knees slightly bent, weight on balls of feet, hands up and out.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Perform a skills check: 10 passes on the move and 5 shots on goal; record results.

*How to teach:* Skills check: 10 passes on the move, 5 shots; record.

- **Q:** How many players per team on the court in official handball?
  **A:** Seven (six court players and a goalkeeper).

**Assessment:** Handball skills checklist.

**Answer key:** Skills checklist: passes — step toward target, accurate to chest, on the move (8/10 = proficient); shots — outside 6 m line, step or jump shot form, on target (3/5 = proficient); follows 3-step/3-second rules.

## Health Education 8: Body image, media and eating disorders

**Prepare:** Collect three magazine ads or social posts with idealized bodies. Read NEDA or KidsHealth eating-disorder fact sheets. Parental preview: discuss eating disorders without numbers (weights, calories) that could be triggering; watch for signs of disordered eating in your own child.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking eating disorders are a choice or only affect thin girls. Correct: they are serious mental illnesses that affect all genders, body sizes and backgrounds.
- Believing photos in ads show real bodies. Correct: most are professionally lit, posed and digitally edited.

### Day 1 (Monday): Media lens
*Student:* Analyze three ads or posts for unrealistic body messages and photo editing.

*How to teach:* Analyze each ad: Who made it? What is the body message? What might be edited? What is it selling?

- **Q:** What clues suggest an image was edited?
  **A:** Unrealistically smooth skin, warped backgrounds near the body, impossible proportions, no pores or stretch marks, inconsistent shadows.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Body image
*Student:* Discuss influences on body image (family, peers, culture, sports) and write positive self-talk statements.

*How to teach:* Discuss influences (family comments, peers, culture, sports with weight classes or appearance focus). The child writes five positive self-talk statements about what their body can do.

- **Q:** Give a positive self-talk statement focused on function, not appearance.
  **A:** Example: 'My legs are strong enough to run a mile,' 'My body lets me play music and hug my family.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Eating disorders
*Student:* Learn warning signs and health risks of anorexia, bulimia and binge eating disorder.

*How to teach:* Read the fact sheet together and make a chart of anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder: key behaviors and health risks.

- **Q:** Describe the three eating disorders.
  **A:** Anorexia: severe restriction and intense fear of weight gain; risks include heart problems, bone loss. Bulimia: binge eating followed by purging (vomiting, laxatives) or excessive exercise; risks include electrolyte imbalance, tooth and throat damage. Binge eating disorder: repeated episodes of eating large amounts with loss of control, without purging; risks include distress, high blood pressure, diabetes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Getting help
*Student:* Practice telling a trusted adult about concern for a friend and list where to find help.

*How to teach:* Role-play telling a parent or counselor 'I'm worried about my friend; she's skipping meals and seems upset.' List help sources: trusted adult, doctor, school counselor, NEDA resources.

- **Q:** Why tell an adult rather than confront the friend alone?
  **A:** Eating disorders are serious medical and mental illnesses that need professional treatment; a friend cannot fix it, but an adult can get help.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a media-literacy response to one ad, explaining its message and a healthier counter-message.

*How to teach:* The child writes a response to one ad: message, techniques, who benefits and a healthier counter-message.

- **Q:** What is a counter-message?
  **A:** A message that challenges the ad's idea, e.g., 'Healthy bodies come in many shapes and sizes.'

**Assessment:** Media-literacy response.

**Answer key:** Media-literacy response: 4 = identifies the ad's explicit and implied message, at least two techniques (editing, idealized model, emotional appeal), who profits, and a clear healthy counter-message; 3 = message and one technique plus counter-message; 2 = describes the ad without analysis; 1 = incomplete.
