# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 33

## English Language Arts 8: Research: notes, paraphrase and citation

**Prepare:** Note cards (or a digital notes document), the child's 4 sources, Purdue OWL MLA guide (owl.purdue.edu). Pick 5 short passages from the child's sources for paraphrase practice.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking paraphrases and summaries don't need citations. Any borrowed idea or fact that isn't common knowledge must be cited.
- Patchwriting — swapping a few synonyms while keeping the original sentence structure. That is still plagiarism.

### Day 1 (Monday): Note-taking
*Student:* Take notes on cards organized by sub-question, recording source and page for every fact.

*How to teach:* Model one note card: heading (sub-question), fact in own words or quoted, source code and page. Child takes notes from at least two sources, organized by sub-question.

- **Q:** What must every note include?
  **A:** The source and page (or paragraph/URL) so it can be cited later.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Paraphrase practice
*Student:* Practice paraphrasing 5 passages and compare them with the originals to avoid plagiarism.

*How to teach:* Model the 'read, cover, write, check' paraphrase method. Child paraphrases 5 passages and compares each to the original for borrowed phrases.

- **Q:** Original: 'Gutenberg's press could print thousands of pages a day.' Give an acceptable paraphrase.
  **A:** Example: 'With Gutenberg's invention, printers could produce pages by the thousands every day' — plus a citation.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Quotations and ellipses
*Student:* Practice integrating quotations with signal phrases and using ellipses for omissions.

*How to teach:* Teach signal phrases ('According to ...,' 'Historian X argues ...') and ellipses for omissions. Child integrates 5 quotations.

- **Q:** Fix this dropped quotation: 'The printing press mattered. "It spread ideas quickly."'
  **A:** 'According to historian Elizabeth Eisenstein, the press "spread ideas quickly" (45).' — introduce with a signal phrase and cite. [example]

### Day 4 (Thursday): MLA citation
*Student:* Format a works-cited list for 4 sources in MLA style using Purdue OWL.

*How to teach:* Using Purdue OWL, child formats 4 works-cited entries and checks each against the model. To check, ask: "Give the MLA pattern for a book." and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Give the MLA pattern for a book.
  **A:** Last, First. Title in Italics. Publisher, Year.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Submit organized notes and the works-cited page; take a quick quiz on plagiarism.

*How to teach:* Collect notes and works cited; give the short plagiarism quiz (key). Finish by asking: "Is it plagiarism to reuse your own old essay without permission?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Is it plagiarism to reuse your own old essay without permission?
  **A:** Yes, in most classes — 'self-plagiarism' — unless the teacher allows it.

**Assessment:** Notes and works-cited page.

**Answer key:** Notes: every card has a sub-question heading, one fact or idea, source and page (1 point per 5 complete cards). Works cited (MLA 9): alphabetical by author's last name, hanging indent, double-spaced, each entry with author, title, container, publisher/date, URL or pages as available. Plagiarism quiz answers: 1) Must you cite a paraphrase? Yes. 2) Must you cite 'George Washington was the first U.S. president'? No — common knowledge. 3) Is changing a few words of a sentence acceptable without quotation marks? No — patchwriting. 4) Where does an in-text citation go? At the end of the sentence, before the period: (Smith 42).

## Mathematics 8: Approximating irrational numbers

**Prepare:** Number line strips, calculator (for checking only Monday). Prepare the Unit 8 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 22/7 equals π. It is a rational approximation (3.142857...).
- Ordering numbers by looking at the radicand only. Convert each to a decimal approximation first.

### Day 1 (Monday): Zooming in on sqrt 2
*Student:* Use successive number lines to trap sqrt 2 between tenths, then hundredths, without a calculator.

*How to teach:* Trap √2 with successive number lines. Finish by asking: "Between which tenths and hundredths is √2?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Between which tenths and hundredths is √2?
  **A:** 1.4² = 1.96 and 1.5² = 2.25, so between 1.4 and 1.5; 1.41² = 1.9881 and 1.42² = 2.0164, so between 1.41 and 1.42.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ordering real numbers
*Student:* Order sets like {pi, sqrt 10, 3.1, 22/7, 3.15} on a number line with justification.

*How to teach:* Convert each number to a decimal and order on a number line. Before moving on, ask: "Order π, √10, 3.1, 22/7, 3.15." and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Order π, √10, 3.1, 22/7, 3.15.
  **A:** 3.1 < π (3.1416) < 22/7 (3.1429) < 3.15 < √10 (3.1623).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Estimating expressions
*Student:* Estimate values like pi^2 and 2 sqrt 5 + 1 and compare estimates to calculator values.

*How to teach:* Estimate then check with calculator. Check understanding with: "Estimate π² and 2√5 + 1." If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Estimate π² and 2√5 + 1.
  **A:** π² ≈ 9.87; √5 ≈ 2.24, so 2√5 + 1 ≈ 5.47.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit 8 review
*Student:* Mixed review: roots, Pythagorean theorem, distance and real numbers.

*How to teach:* Mixed review of the unit. To check, ask: "Distance from (−1, −1) to (5, 7)?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Distance from (−1, −1) to (5, 7)?
  **A:** Legs 6 and 8: 10.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 8 test
*Student:* Unit test on the Pythagorean theorem and irrational numbers.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test. Finish by asking: "Is √50 rational?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Is √50 rational?
  **A:** No — 50 is not a perfect square, so √50 is irrational (≈ 7.07).

**Assessment:** Unit 8 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 8 test suggested items: 1) √2 is between 1.41 and 1.42. 2) Order π, √10, 3.1, 22/7, 3.15 → 3.1 < π < 22/7 < 3.15 < √10. 3) Legs 9, 12 → 15. 4) Distance (−1, −1) to (5, 7) → 10. 5) Is 8-15-17 right? Yes. 6) ∛216 = 6. 7) 0.4545... = 5/11. 8) Estimate 2√5 + 1 ≈ 5.47. 9) Classify √49 (rational) and √50 (irrational). 1–2 points each.

## Science 8 (Integrated): Adaptation and unit review

**Prepare:** Notebook; reading on a California species such as the island fox (Channel Islands National Park / NPS site). Prepare a data-based practice test and the Unit 7 test.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing acclimation with adaptation. Acclimation is a change within one individual's lifetime (tanning, thicker blood cells at altitude); adaptations are inherited traits shaped over generations.
- Saying organisms evolve 'because they need to.' Natural selection acts on variation already present.

### Day 1 (Monday): Adaptation vs acclimation
*Student:* Sort 10 examples (tanning vs dark skin, polar bear fur) into inherited adaptations or individual changes.

*How to teach:* Explain the difference with two examples (a tan vs naturally dark skin in high-UV regions). The child sorts 10 examples and justifies each.

- **Q:** Is a person's tan an adaptation?
  **A:** No — it's acclimation, a change within one lifetime that isn't inherited.
- **Q:** Is a polar bear's thick white fur an adaptation?
  **A:** Yes — an inherited trait shaped by natural selection.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Local adaptations
*Student:* Research a California species (e.g., island fox) and its adaptations; make an infographic.

*How to teach:* The child researches a California species (e.g., the island fox, smaller than its mainland gray-fox ancestor) and makes an infographic of 3 adaptations with how each helps survival. Before moving on, ask: "Why might island foxes be small?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Why might island foxes be small?
  **A:** Limited food on small islands favored smaller bodies that need less energy (island dwarfism).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Evolution review
*Student:* Build a concept map linking fossils, anatomy, embryos, genetics and selection.

*How to teach:* The child builds a concept map linking fossils, anatomy, embryos, genetics and natural selection, writing a linking phrase on every arrow. Check understanding with: "How are mutations and natural selection linked?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** How are mutations and natural selection linked?
  **A:** Mutations create new variation; natural selection determines which variations become more common.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Practice assessment
*Student:* Complete a data-based practice test.

*How to teach:* The child completes the practice test independently, then corrects with the key. To check, ask: "Define common descent." and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Define common descent.
  **A:** The idea that different species share common ancestors.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 test
*Student:* Unit test on natural selection.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test (key). Finish by asking: "Can an individual organism evolve?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Can an individual organism evolve?
  **A:** No — populations evolve over generations.

**Assessment:** Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 7 test sample answers: 1) Steps of natural selection: variation, inheritance, overproduction/competition, differential survival and reproduction, change in trait frequency over generations. 2) Graph: dark mice rise from 5% to 80% on lava → selection by owl predation. 3) Adaptation vs acclimation: polar bear fur = adaptation; a person's tan = acclimation. 4) Finches: deeper beaks after 1977 drought. 5) Antibiotic resistance: resistant bacteria survive and multiply. 6) Evidence for common descent: fossils, anatomy, embryos, DNA.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: Immigration, cities and nativism

**Prepare:** Immigration data by region 1870–1910; 'The New Colossus' (Emma Lazarus, 1883); translated Angel Island poems; Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) summary; Jacob Riis photographs from 'How the Other Half Lives' (1890); John Muir Yosemite excerpts.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Ellis Island and Angel Island were similar. Ellis Island (opened 1892) processed most European immigrants in hours; Angel Island (1910–1940) detained many Chinese immigrants for weeks or months under exclusion laws.
- Thinking 'new immigrants' came from the same places as earlier ones. After about 1880 most came from Southern and Eastern Europe (Italy, Russia, Austria-Hungary, Poland), not Northern and Western Europe.

### Day 1 (Monday): New immigrants
*Student:* Graph immigration by region of origin 1870–1910 and read 'The New Colossus.'

*How to teach:* Graph immigration by region 1870–1910; read 'The New Colossus.'

- **Q:** What shift does the graph show?
  **A:** Immigration shifted from Northern/Western Europe to Southern/Eastern Europe after about 1880.
- **Q:** What image of America does 'The New Colossus' present?
  **A:** A welcoming 'Mother of Exiles' offering refuge to the poor and oppressed.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Angel Island and exclusion
*Student:* Read translated Angel Island wall poems and the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882); compare with Ellis Island experiences.

*How to teach:* Read Angel Island poems and the Chinese Exclusion Act; compare with Ellis Island.

- **Q:** What did the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) do?
  **A:** Banned Chinese laborers from immigrating and barred Chinese immigrants from becoming citizens.
- **Q:** What feelings do Angel Island poems express?
  **A:** Frustration, loneliness, humiliation and anger at being detained and treated unjustly.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tenement life
*Student:* Analyze Jacob Riis photographs from 'How the Other Half Lives' and describe urban living conditions.

*How to teach:* Analyze Riis photographs; describe urban conditions.

- **Q:** Describe tenement conditions in Riis's photos.
  **A:** Overcrowded, dark, poorly ventilated rooms; families and workers in tiny spaces; little sanitation; children working or sleeping on streets.
- **Q:** What was Riis's purpose?
  **A:** To shock middle-class readers into supporting housing reform.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Conservation
*Student:* Read John Muir excerpts about Yosemite and explain the start of the conservation movement.

*How to teach:* Read Muir; explain the start of conservation.

- **Q:** What did John Muir advocate?
  **A:** Preserving wilderness like Yosemite for its own sake and public enjoyment; he co-founded the Sierra Club.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on immigration and urbanization.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz.

- **Q:** Why did many immigrants settle in cities?
  **A:** Factory jobs, cheap (tenement) housing and existing ethnic communities.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 'old' immigrants (before 1880) mostly from Northern/Western Europe (Ireland, Germany, Britain, Scandinavia); 'new' immigrants mostly from Southern/Eastern Europe, often Catholic, Orthodox or Jewish; Ellis Island (New York Harbor, 1892); Angel Island (San Francisco Bay, 1910); Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) — banned Chinese laborers for 10 years, extended and made permanent, repealed 1943 — first major law barring a group by nationality; 'The New Colossus' — 'Give me your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free' (on the Statue of Liberty's pedestal); tenements — crowded, poorly lit and ventilated apartment buildings; Riis exposed conditions with flash photography; assimilation — adopting the new culture; nativism — opposition to immigrants; John Muir — naturalist who helped make Yosemite a national park (1890) and founded the Sierra Club (1892).

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Choreographic devices

**Prepare:** Open space, music, recording device.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing canon and unison. Correct: unison = everyone together; canon = same movement starting at staggered times.
- Random pathways. Correct: plan pathways and levels with intent.

### Day 1 (Monday): Devices
*Student:* Learn repetition, canon, unison and retrograde by applying each to your phrase.

*How to teach:* Apply repetition, canon, unison and retrograde to the phrase.

- **Q:** What is retrograde?
  **A:** Performing a movement phrase in reverse order, like rewinding.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Structure
*Student:* Organize phrases into ABA form.

*How to teach:* Organize into ABA.

- **Q:** What makes the B section contrast?
  **A:** Different tempo, energy, level or movement vocabulary.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Spatial design
*Student:* Use levels and pathways intentionally.

*How to teach:* Use levels and pathways intentionally.

- **Q:** Name three levels.
  **A:** High, middle, low.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Collaboration
*Student:* Teach part of your dance to a family member and create a duet.

*How to teach:* Teach part to a family member; create a duet.

- **Q:** Give a duet device.
  **A:** Mirroring, canon, call-and-response, counterbalance.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Record the draft dance.

*How to teach:* Record the draft.

- **Q:** Why record drafts?
  **A:** To check spacing, timing and whether devices read clearly.

**Assessment:** Recorded draft.

**Answer key:** Recorded draft: 4 = ABA structure, at least three devices (repetition, canon, unison, retrograde) clearly visible, intentional levels and pathways, duet section; 3 = two devices; 2 = one device; 1 = incomplete.

**Prepare:** PACER audio, stopwatch, mat, tape measure, ruler, cones for agility. Week 1 and week 19 scores.

*How to teach:* PACER or mile; record.

*How to teach:* Curl-ups and push-ups to cadence.

- **Q:** What is cadence and why use it?

*How to teach:* Sit-and-reach and trunk lift.

*How to teach:* Retest agility, power, reaction time.

## Health Education 8: Public health and health careers

**Prepare:** Bookmark CDC, CDPH and your county health department websites. Prepare a simple map and case list for an outbreak activity (e.g., John Snow's 1854 cholera map). Find career profiles (BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking public health is only about hospitals. Correct: public health prevents disease in populations (vaccines, clean water, food inspections, tracking outbreaks).
- Believing all health careers require medical school. Correct: many need a certificate, associate's or bachelor's degree.

### Day 1 (Monday): Agencies
*Student:* Learn the roles of the CDC, CDPH and your county health department.

*How to teach:* Compare roles: CDC (national), CDPH (state), county (local inspections, clinics, vaccines).

- **Q:** Which agency inspects local restaurants?
  **A:** The county (or city) environmental health department.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Epidemics
*Student:* Study how public health tracks outbreaks using a simple case-map activity.

*How to teach:* Plot cases on the map and find the cluster/source, like John Snow's Broad Street pump.

- **Q:** What did John Snow discover in 1854?
  **A:** Mapping cholera deaths in London showed a cluster around the Broad Street water pump, showing cholera spread through contaminated water.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Careers
*Student:* Research two health careers including education required.

*How to teach:* Research two careers: duties, education, salary (BLS).

- **Q:** What education does a registered nurse need?
  **A:** An associate's degree or bachelor's in nursing (ADN or BSN) and passing the NCLEX-RN licensing exam.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Interview
*Student:* Interview someone who works in a health-related job, or watch a career video and take notes.

*How to teach:* Interview someone in a health job or watch a career video and take notes.

- **Q:** What is one good interview question?
  **A:** Examples: 'What does a typical day look like?' 'What training did you need?' 'What's the hardest part?'

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Create a health career profile card.

*How to teach:* The child makes a career profile card.

- **Q:** What is an epidemiologist?
  **A:** A public health scientist who studies patterns and causes of disease in populations.

**Assessment:** Career profile card.

**Answer key:** Career profile card: 4 = job title, duties, education/training, workplace, salary range (BLS), one interesting fact and personal connection; 3 = one element missing; 2 = vague; 1 = incomplete.
