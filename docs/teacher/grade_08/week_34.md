# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 34

## English Language Arts 8: Drafting the research paper

**Prepare:** Notes, an outline template, a word processor. Plan four writing sessions; the paper is 4–6 pages.

**Watch for:**
- Writing one paragraph per source ('Source 1 says...'). Synthesis organizes paragraphs by idea and combines several sources in each.
- Informal style (contractions, 'you,' slang). Use formal, third-person academic voice.

### Day 1 (Monday): Outline
*Student:* Build an outline with a thesis and 3–4 sections supported by notes from multiple sources.

*How to teach:* Child builds an outline: thesis, 3–4 sections, each with notes from at least two sources. Check that every section supports the thesis.

- **Q:** Turn the question 'How did the printing press change Europe?' into a thesis.
  **A:** Example: 'The printing press transformed Europe by spreading literacy, fueling the Reformation, and accelerating scientific exchange.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Draft part 1
*Student:* Draft the introduction and first two body sections with in-text citations.

*How to teach:* Child drafts the introduction and first two sections with in-text citations; remind them to keep moving and fix later. Check understanding with: "Where does an MLA in-text citation go?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Where does an MLA in-text citation go?
  **A:** At the end of the sentence before the period, e.g., (Eisenstein 45).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft part 2
*Student:* Draft remaining sections and the conclusion.

*How to teach:* Child drafts the remaining sections and the conclusion. To check, ask: "What should a research conclusion do?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What should a research conclusion do?
  **A:** Restate the thesis in new words, pull the main points together and explain why the topic matters today.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revise
*Student:* Revise for synthesis (combining sources in one paragraph), formal style and precise academic vocabulary.

*How to teach:* Revise together for synthesis, formal style and precise vocabulary: highlight each source in a different color to see whether paragraphs combine sources. Finish by asking: "Give a formal replacement for 'a lot of people thought.'" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Give a formal replacement for 'a lot of people thought.'
  **A:** 'Many contemporaries believed...'

### Day 5 (Friday): Edit & publish
*Student:* Edit for punctuation and citation format and publish the final 4–6 page paper.

*How to teach:* Child edits punctuation and citation format, formats the works-cited page, and publishes the final paper. Before moving on, ask: "Does the works-cited page count toward the 4–6 pages?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Does the works-cited page count toward the 4–6 pages?
  **A:** Usually not — it's a separate final page.

**Assessment:** Research paper (rubric).

**Answer key:** Research paper rubric (4 levels per trait): Thesis (clear, answers the research question); Organization (logical sections, topic sentences, transitions); Evidence & synthesis (multiple sources per section, quotations and paraphrases integrated); Citation (every borrowed idea cited; in-text citations match the works cited); Style (formal, precise vocabulary); Conventions; Length 4–6 pages.

## Mathematics 8: Modeling: Fermi problems and estimation

**Prepare:** Measuring tape, calculator, a ping-pong ball or marble; poster paper and markers.

**Watch for:**
- Demanding an exact answer. A Fermi estimate aims for the right order of magnitude with stated assumptions.
- Forgetting unit consistency (meters vs centimeters). Convert everything before multiplying.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is a Fermi problem?
*Student:* Estimate how many ping-pong balls fit in your house's living room using volume of spheres and a room measurement; list assumptions.

*How to teach:* Measure the living room; estimate how many 4-cm ping-pong balls fit. Before moving on, ask: "Room 5 m × 4 m × 2.5 m; ball volume ≈ 33.5 cm³. Estimate." and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Room 5 m × 4 m × 2.5 m; ball volume ≈ 33.5 cm³. Estimate.
  **A:** Room = 50 m³ = 5 × 10⁷ cm³. 5 × 10⁷ ÷ 33.5 ≈ 1.5 × 10⁶; with packing gaps (≈ 64% filled) about 1 × 10⁶ balls.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Fermi problem 2
*Student:* Estimate how many heartbeats you will have in your lifetime and express in scientific notation.

*How to teach:* Estimate lifetime heartbeats. Check understanding with: "At 70 beats/min for 80 years, about how many heartbeats?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** At 70 beats/min for 80 years, about how many heartbeats?
  **A:** 70 × 60 × 24 × 365 × 80 ≈ 2.9 × 10⁹, about 3 billion.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Choose your own
*Student:* Pick a question (e.g., how many pizzas are eaten in California per year), research a few facts and build an estimate.

*How to teach:* Child picks a question, researches a few facts, builds an estimate. To check, ask: "California has about 39 million people. If each eats about 1/2 pizza per week, how many pizzas per year?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** California has about 39 million people. If each eats about 1/2 pizza per week, how many pizzas per year?
  **A:** 3.9 × 10⁷ × 0.5 × 52 ≈ 1 × 10⁹ pizzas.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Presentation
*Student:* Present your Fermi estimate on a poster with the steps, assumptions and an order-of-magnitude reasonableness check.

*How to teach:* Child presents the poster; you ask about assumptions. Finish by asking: "How do you check reasonableness?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** How do you check reasonableness?
  **A:** Compare with a known quantity or re-estimate with different assumptions; the result should be in the same order of magnitude.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Self- and parent-assess the poster with a rubric; complete 6 cumulative review problems on exponents and volume.

*How to teach:* Score with the rubric together; child completes 6 cumulative review problems. Before moving on, ask: "(6 × 10⁴)(5 × 10³)?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** (6 × 10⁴)(5 × 10³)?
  **A:** 3 × 10⁸.

**Assessment:** Fermi-problem poster rubric.

**Answer key:** Poster rubric (4 levels): 4 = clear question, listed assumptions with sources, step-by-step calculation with units, answer in scientific notation, order-of-magnitude reasonableness check; 3 = all steps, one weak assumption or unit slip; 2 = answer with few steps or no assumptions; 1 = guess only. Cumulative review answers: give 6 items, e.g., 2³ × 2⁴ = 128; (6 × 10⁴)(5 × 10³) = 3 × 10⁸; cylinder r = 2, h = 5 → 20π ≈ 62.8; cone r = 3, h = 4 → 12π ≈ 37.7; sphere r = 1 → (4/3)π ≈ 4.19; 10⁻³ = 0.001.

## Science 8 (Integrated): Capstone: define and design

**Prepare:** Recyclables, tape, cardboard, rubber bands, a kitchen scale. Have the child choose a capstone challenge: helmet padding test, phone-drop protector, soundproof box, or solar oven.

**Watch for:**
- Writing vague criteria ('it works well'). Criteria must be measurable (e.g., 'reduces sound by at least 20 dB').
- Researching only products, not the science. Connect each design idea to forces, energy or waves.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose a problem
*Student:* Choose a capstone challenge (bike helmet padding test, phone-drop protector, soundproof box or solar oven) and write a design brief.

*How to teach:* Talk through the four options; the child picks one and writes a design brief with the problem, user, criteria and constraints. Before moving on, ask: "Write a measurable criterion for a solar oven." and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Write a measurable criterion for a solar oven.
  **A:** Example: 'Raise the inside temperature at least 20 °C above air temperature within 30 minutes.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Research
*Student:* Research existing solutions and the science behind them (forces, energy, waves).

*How to teach:* The child researches existing solutions and the science behind them. Check understanding with: "What science explains a phone-drop protector?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** What science explains a phone-drop protector?
  **A:** Increasing collision time reduces force (Newton's laws, impulse).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Brainstorm & sketch
*Student:* Sketch at least 3 solutions with labels.

*How to teach:* The child sketches at least 3 labeled solutions. To check, ask: "Why sketch several ideas?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Why sketch several ideas?
  **A:** To compare options before committing materials.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Decision matrix
*Student:* Score designs against criteria and choose one to build.

*How to teach:* The child scores designs in a decision matrix and chooses one. Finish by asking: "How do weights help in a matrix?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** How do weights help in a matrix?
  **A:** They make the most important criteria count more.

### Day 5 (Friday): Plan check
*Student:* Write a materials list and test plan with measurable outcomes.

*How to teach:* The child writes a materials list and a test plan with measurable outcomes and number of trials. Before moving on, ask: "How many trials per test?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** How many trials per test?
  **A:** At least 3, to average out errors.

**Assessment:** Design brief and decision matrix.

**Answer key:** Design brief rubric: problem statement, user, measurable criteria (2–4), constraints (materials, size, budget, time), science background. Decision matrix: 3+ designs scored on weighted criteria with totals; choice justified by scores.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: Labor and farmer movements

**Prepare:** Lewis Hine child labor photographs (Library of Congress, National Child Labor Committee collection); readings on Gompers and the AFL, the Haymarket Affair (1886) and the Pullman Strike (1894); Omaha Platform (1892) excerpts; Bryan's 'Cross of Gold' (1896) excerpts.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking strikes usually succeeded. Haymarket and Pullman ended in defeat for workers; government often sided with employers using courts and troops.
- Thinking the silver debate was about jewelry. 'Free silver' meant coining silver to expand the money supply, raising prices and helping indebted farmers.

### Day 1 (Monday): Child labor
*Student:* Analyze 3 Lewis Hine photographs using a see-think-wonder chart and write captions.

*How to teach:* Analyze 3 Hine photographs with see-think-wonder; write captions.

- **Q:** What purpose did Lewis Hine's photographs serve?
  **A:** To document child labor and persuade the public and lawmakers to pass child labor laws.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Labor unions
*Student:* Read about Samuel Gompers and the AFL and compare the Haymarket and Pullman strikes.

*How to teach:* Read about Gompers/AFL; compare Haymarket and Pullman.

- **Q:** What were the AFL's goals?
  **A:** Higher wages, shorter hours and better conditions for skilled workers through collective bargaining.
- **Q:** How did the Pullman Strike end?
  **A:** The federal government obtained a court injunction and sent troops; the strike collapsed and Debs was jailed.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Farmers organize
*Student:* Explain the Grange's goals and read excerpts of the Populist Omaha Platform (1892).

*How to teach:* Explain the Grange; read the Omaha Platform.

- **Q:** Why were farmers angry in the late 1800s?
  **A:** Falling crop prices, high railroad shipping rates, debt and tight money (deflation).
- **Q:** Name two Omaha Platform demands that later became law.
  **A:** Graduated income tax (16th Amendment) and direct election of senators (17th Amendment); also the secret ballot and 8-hour day.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cross of Gold
*Student:* Read excerpts of William Jennings Bryan's 'Cross of Gold' speech and explain the debate over silver.

*How to teach:* Read Bryan's 'Cross of Gold'; explain the silver debate.

- **Q:** What did 'free silver' supporters want?
  **A:** Unlimited coinage of silver (at 16 to 1 with gold) to increase the money supply, raise prices and ease farmers' debts.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on labor and farmer movements.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz.

- **Q:** What is collective bargaining?
  **A:** Workers negotiating as a group with employers over wages and conditions.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: child labor — children worked long hours in mills, mines and factories for low pay; Hine's photos built support for child-labor laws; labor unions use collective bargaining and strikes; AFL (1886, Samuel Gompers) organized skilled workers by craft for 'bread-and-butter' goals — higher wages, shorter hours (8-hour day), safer conditions; Haymarket (Chicago, May 1886) — bomb at a labor rally killed police; anarchists convicted; public turned against unions (Knights of Labor declined); Pullman Strike (1894) — Pullman cut wages without cutting company-town rents; Eugene Debs's American Railway Union boycotted Pullman cars; federal injunction and troops broke the strike; laissez-faire — government stays out of business; Grange (1867) — farmers' cooperatives, pushed for railroad regulation (Granger laws); Populist Omaha Platform — free coinage of silver, graduated income tax, government ownership of railroads and telegraphs, direct election of senators, secret ballot, 8-hour day; Bryan's 'Cross of Gold' (1896) — 'You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold'; Bryan lost to McKinley.

## Spanish 1B: Spanish I review: present tense system

**Prepare:** Flashcards, Conjuguemos, picture for description task.

**Watch for:**
- Applying stem changes to nosotros. Review the boot.
- Mixing DOP and IOP: 'Lo di a ella.' Use Se lo di only in Spanish II; for now: Le di el libro.

### Day 1 (Monday): Regular present
*Student:* Review regular -ar/-er/-ir verbs from 1A with a timed Conjuguemos drill.

*How to teach:* Timed Conjuguemos regular present drill.

- **Q:** Conjugate escribir for nosotros and hablar for ellos.
  **A:** escribimos; hablan.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Irregular & stem-changers
*Student:* Review ser, estar, ir, tener, venir and o→ue, e→ie, e→i stem-changers.

*How to teach:* Review irregulars and all stem-changers.

- **Q:** Conjugate tener, venir and poder for yo.
  **A:** tengo, vengo, puedo.
- **Q:** Conjugate servir for ella.
  **A:** sirve.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Commands & progressive
*Student:* Review tú commands and the present progressive with a picture-description task.

*How to teach:* Review tú commands and progressive with picture description.

- **Q:** Command: 'Tell the truth.'
  **A:** Di la verdad.
- **Q:** Say: 'They are sleeping.'
  **A:** Están durmiendo.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Pronouns
*Student:* Review direct and indirect object pronouns in a 20-item practice.

*How to teach:* 20-item DOP/IOP practice.

- **Q:** Replace the object: Veo la película.
  **A:** La veo.
- **Q:** Translate: 'I write him a letter.'
  **A:** Le escribo una carta.

### Day 5 (Friday): Practice test
*Student:* Take a self-scored present-tense practice test and list your weakest areas.

*How to teach:* Self-scored practice test; list weak areas.

- **Q:** Which stem-change type is 'preferir'?
  **A:** e→ie.

**Assessment:** Practice test.

**Answer key:** Present-tense practice test self-scored with keys from prior weeks: regular -ar/-er/-ir; ser, estar, ir, tener, venir; o→ue (puedo, duermo), e→ie (quiero, prefiero), e→i (pido, sirvo); tú commands (regular = él form; irregular di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven); progressive (estar + -ando/-iendo); DOPs (lo, la, los, las) and IOPs (me, te, le, nos, les). Weakest areas listed with a plan.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Dance performance

**Prepare:** Music, costume pieces, lamp(s) for simple lighting, recording device.

**Watch for:**
- Blank faces during performance. Correct: facial expression and focus are part of expression.
- Skipping a full dress rehearsal. Correct: run it with costume and lighting.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rehearsal
*Student:* Rehearse focusing on expression.

*How to teach:* Rehearse for expression.

- **Q:** What is performance 'focus'?
  **A:** Where the dancer directs their eyes and attention to communicate intention.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Production elements
*Student:* Add costume and lighting.

*How to teach:* Add costume and lighting.

- **Q:** How can a costume support a dance?
  **A:** Color and movement of fabric can reinforce mood and theme.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dress rehearsal
*Student:* Run-through.

*How to teach:* Dress rehearsal run-through.

- **Q:** What is a dress rehearsal?
  **A:** A full run with all costumes and production elements as in performance.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Performance
*Student:* Perform and record.

*How to teach:* Perform and record.

- **Q:** What should you do if you forget a step?
  **A:** Keep going with confident movement in the style until you find your place.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflection
*Student:* Write about choices.

*How to teach:* Write about choices.

- **Q:** What should the reflection explain?
  **A:** Why you chose specific movements, devices, music, costumes and lighting and how they express your idea.

**Assessment:** Performance and reflection.

**Answer key:** Performance and reflection: 4 = confident, expressive, accurate performance with production elements; reflection explains intent and choices with dance vocabulary; 3 = minor errors; 2 = hesitant/partial; 1 = incomplete.

## Physical Education 8: Two-week personal fitness plan: design

**Prepare:** Planning template (14 days × FITT), fitness journal.

**Watch for:**
- Training the same way every day. Correct: alternate cardio, strength, flexibility and rest to allow recovery.
- No backup plan. Correct: write alternatives for bad weather and busy days.

### Day 1 (Monday): Plan structure
*Student:* Outline 14 days with cardio, strength, flexibility and rest days using FITT for each.

*How to teach:* Outline 14 days with FITT.

- **Q:** Write FITT for a cardio day.
  **A:** Example: Frequency 3×/week; Intensity moderate–vigorous (HR 140–170 or talk test); Time 30 min; Type jogging/biking.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Activity choice
*Student:* Choose enjoyable activities from this year's units and justify them.

*How to teach:* Choose enjoyable activities and justify.

- **Q:** Why choose enjoyable activities?
  **A:** People stick with activities they enjoy, which makes lifelong fitness more likely.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Barriers
*Student:* Add backup plans for bad weather, busy days or no equipment.

*How to teach:* Add backup plans.

- **Q:** Give a no-equipment backup workout.
  **A:** Body-weight circuit: squats, push-ups, lunges, planks, jumping jacks, 3 rounds.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Start
*Student:* Begin day 1 of your plan and log it.

*How to teach:* Start day 1 and log.

- **Q:** What should a log entry include?
  **A:** Date, activity, time, intensity (HR/RPE), and notes on how it felt.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Have a parent review the plan for safety and balance; revise if needed.

*How to teach:* Parent reviews for safety and balance; revise.

- **Q:** How many rest/recovery days are appropriate in 14 days?
  **A:** About 2–4 rest or active-recovery days.

**Assessment:** Two-week fitness plan.

**Answer key:** Two-week plan: 4 = 14 days with FITT for each session, balanced cardio/strength/flexibility, 2–4 rest days, warm-ups/cool-downs, backup plans, justified choices; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = unbalanced or missing FITT; 1 = incomplete.

## Health Education 8: Health advocacy campaign: plan

**Prepare:** Prepare a campaign planning template (issue, data, audience, message, call to action, materials, timeline). Help locate valid sources.

**Watch for:**
- Choosing a topic too broad ('health'). Correct: narrow to one behavior and audience ('getting 9 hours of sleep for middle schoolers').
- Using opinions as evidence. Correct: cite data from valid sources.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose
*Student:* Choose a health issue from this year (vaping, sleep, mental health, safety).

*How to teach:* The child chooses a topic from the year and explains why it matters.

- **Q:** Why is youth vaping a good advocacy topic?
  **A:** Example: many teens use or are exposed; nicotine is addictive and harms the developing brain; teens can influence peers.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Research
*Student:* Gather three valid sources and data.

*How to teach:* Gather three valid sources and two statistics.

- **Q:** Give one valid source type.
  **A:** Government (.gov: CDC, CDPH), medical organizations (AAP, AHA), or university/hospital sites.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Audience
*Student:* Define the target audience and call to action.

*How to teach:* Define audience and a specific call to action.

- **Q:** Example call to action for a sleep campaign?
  **A:** 'Charge your phone outside your bedroom tonight.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Plan
*Student:* Plan campaign materials (poster, video, letter to a local official).

*How to teach:* Plan materials and timeline.

- **Q:** Why match materials to audience?
  **A:** Different audiences use different media (video for teens, letter for officials).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Get parent feedback on your campaign plan.

*How to teach:* Share plan with a parent and revise.

- **Q:** What makes advocacy different from just informing?
  **A:** Advocacy persuades people to take a specific healthy action or supports a change.

**Assessment:** Campaign plan.

**Answer key:** Campaign plan: 4 = focused issue, 3 valid sources with data, defined audience, specific call to action, planned materials and timeline; 3 = one weak element; 2 = vague topic or no sources; 1 = incomplete.
