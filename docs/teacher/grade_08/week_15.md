# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 15

## English Language Arts 8: Argument essay workshop

**Prepare:** Source notes, argument essay template. Print an excerpt of Frederick Douglass's 1852 speech 'What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?' (often titled 'The Hypocrisy of American Slavery').

**Watch for:**
- Ending with a summary only. A strong conclusion adds a call to action or the stakes.
- Citing evidence without explaining it. Every piece of evidence needs 1–2 sentences of reasoning.

### Day 1 (Monday): Draft introduction & body
*Student:* Draft an introduction with a hook and claim and two body paragraphs, each with cited evidence and reasoning.

*How to teach:* Model a hook and a clear claim; child drafts intro and two body paragraphs with cited evidence. Check understanding with: "Give a hook type." If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Give a hook type.
  **A:** A surprising statistic, a question, or a brief anecdote.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Counterclaim & conclusion
*Student:* Draft a counterclaim-and-rebuttal paragraph and a conclusion with a call to action.

*How to teach:* Draft counterclaim-and-rebuttal and conclusion with a call to action. To check, ask: "Sentence frame for a counterclaim?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Sentence frame for a counterclaim?
  **A:** 'Some argue that ... However, ...'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Revise
*Student:* Revise with a parent or peer using a rubric, focusing on whether every piece of evidence clearly supports the claim.

*How to teach:* Revise with you using the rubric; check each piece of evidence clearly supports the claim. Finish by asking: "What should you do with evidence that doesn't support the claim?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** What should you do with evidence that doesn't support the claim?
  **A:** Cut it or replace it with relevant evidence.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Edit & publish
*Student:* Edit for mood and voice shifts and comma use; type and publish with a works-cited list.

*How to teach:* Edit for shifts and commas; publish with MLA works cited. Before moving on, ask: "Where does the works-cited page go?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Where does the works-cited page go?
  **A:** On its own page at the end, alphabetized by author.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 test
*Student:* Cold-read test analyzing an unfamiliar speech (Frederick Douglass, 'The Hypocrisy of American Slavery' excerpt) for claims and rhetoric.

*How to teach:* Cold-read test on the Douglass excerpt. Check understanding with: "What does Douglass say the Fourth of July is to the slave?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** What does Douglass say the Fourth of July is to the slave?
  **A:** 'a day that reveals to him, more than all other days in the year, the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim.'

**Assessment:** Argument essay (rubric) and Unit 3 test.

**Answer key:** Argument essay rubric (4 levels per trait): Claim; Evidence (cited, credible, relevant); Reasoning; Counterclaim & rebuttal; Organization; Conventions (no mood/voice shifts, commas, works cited). Unit 3 cold-read answers: 1) Claim: the Fourth of July reveals the 'gross injustice and cruelty' of slavery; America's celebration of liberty is hypocrisy while it enslaves millions. 2) 'This Fourth of July is yours, not mine' — contrast/antithesis, pathos. 3) 'What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July?' — rhetorical question. 4) 'it is not light that is needed, but fire; it is not the gentle shower, but thunder' — metaphor/parallelism arguing that scorn, not calm argument, is needed. 5) Audience: Northern white listeners in Rochester, NY (1852).

## Mathematics 8: Systems of linear equations by graphing

**Prepare:** Graph paper, Desmos.

**Watch for:**
- Reading the intersection inaccurately from a hand graph. Always verify the point in both equations.
- Thinking a system solution is one number. It is an ordered pair (x, y) that works in both equations.

### Day 1 (Monday): On or off the line?
*Student:* Use 'two runners' or 'two water tanks' scenarios to find when two linear quantities are equal using tables and graphs.

*How to teach:* Use two runners: A starts at 0 m running 5 m/s; B starts 30 m ahead at 3 m/s. Make tables and graphs to find when they're even.

- **Q:** When and where does runner A catch runner B?
  **A:** 5t = 30 + 3t → t = 15 s, at 75 m.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Graphing systems
*Student:* Graph 6 systems by hand and identify the intersection point; verify it in both equations.

*How to teach:* Child graphs 6 systems by hand and checks each intersection in both equations. To check, ask: "Solve y = x + 1 and y = −x + 5 by graphing." and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Solve y = x + 1 and y = −x + 5 by graphing.
  **A:** (2, 3); check: 3 = 2 + 1 and 3 = −2 + 5.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Special systems
*Student:* Graph systems with parallel lines and identical lines and connect them to no solution and infinitely many solutions.

*How to teach:* Graph systems with parallel and identical lines; connect to no solution and infinitely many. Finish by asking: "How many solutions: y = 3x + 1 and y = 3x − 2?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** How many solutions: y = 3x + 1 and y = 3x − 2?
  **A:** None — parallel lines (same slope, different intercepts).
- **Q:** How many solutions: y = 2x + 4 and 2y = 4x + 8?
  **A:** Infinitely many — the second simplifies to y = 2x + 4, the same line.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Writing systems from stories
*Student:* Write systems for 4 contexts (e.g., ticket sales of adults and students) and estimate solutions from Desmos graphs.

*How to teach:* Model writing two equations for the ticket problem; child writes systems for 4 contexts and estimates from Desmos. Before moving on, ask: "100 tickets sold; adult $8, student $5; total $650. Write and solve the system." and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** 100 tickets sold; adult $8, student $5; total $650. Write and solve the system.
  **A:** a + s = 100 and 8a + 5s = 650 → a = 50 adults, s = 50 students.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: solve 4 systems by graphing and interpret each solution in context.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Review & check day: have the child redo any missed problem in the notebook and explain the error in one sentence.

- **Q:** What does the intersection point of two lines represent?
  **A:** The one (x, y) pair that makes both equations true at the same time.

**Assessment:** Quiz on graphing systems.

**Answer key:** Quiz (solve by graphing, verify, interpret): 1) y = 2x − 1 and y = −x + 5 → (2, 3). 2) y = x + 4 and y = −2x + 1 → (−1, 3). 3) y = 0.5x + 1 and y = −x + 4 → (2, 2). 4) y = 3x and y = x + 4 → (2, 6). Context interpretation example: 'at 2 hours both tanks hold 6 gallons.' 2 points each (graph + interpretation).

## Science 8 (Integrated): Wave properties

**Prepare:** A Slinky or long rope, a ruler, a pan of water, pebbles. Bookmark PhET 'Wave on a String.'

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the material travels with the wave. Waves carry energy; the medium only vibrates in place.
- Thinking higher frequency means faster waves. In the same medium speed is constant; higher frequency means shorter wavelength.

### Day 1 (Monday): Making waves
*Student:* Use a Slinky or rope to make transverse and longitudinal waves; sketch and label each.

*How to teach:* Make transverse and longitudinal waves with the Slinky; sketch and label. Check understanding with: "Which kind of wave is sound?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Which kind of wave is sound?
  **A:** Longitudinal.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Wave on a String
*Student:* Use PhET to change amplitude and frequency; measure wavelength and record how frequency affects wavelength.

*How to teach:* In PhET, change amplitude and frequency; measure wavelength. To check, ask: "What happens to wavelength as frequency increases?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What happens to wavelength as frequency increases?
  **A:** It decreases.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Amplitude and energy
*Student:* Drop a pebble from different heights into a pan of water and observe wave height; relate amplitude to energy.

*How to teach:* Drop a pebble from different heights; observe wave height. Finish by asking: "What does greater amplitude mean?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** What does greater amplitude mean?
  **A:** More energy.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Wave math
*Student:* Use v = frequency x wavelength to solve 8 problems and graph amplitude vs energy data.

*How to teach:* Teach v = fλ; the child solves 8 problems. Before moving on, ask: "v = 340 m/s, f = 170 Hz. Wavelength?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** v = 340 m/s, f = 170 Hz. Wavelength?
  **A:** 2 m.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on labeling waves and wave math.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Check understanding with: "Define wavelength." If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Define wavelength.
  **A:** Distance from one crest to the next.

**Assessment:** Wave diagram labels and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: label crest, trough, wavelength (crest to crest), amplitude (rest to crest). v = fλ: 1) f = 5 Hz, λ = 2 m → 10 m/s. 2) v = 340 m/s, f = 170 Hz → λ = 2 m. 3) v = 12 m/s, λ = 3 m → f = 4 Hz. 4) Transverse = vibrations perpendicular to wave direction (rope); longitudinal = parallel (sound, Slinky push).

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: Life under slavery and resistance

**Prepare:** Parent preview: these sources describe violence and family separation; read them first. WPA Slave Narratives (Library of Congress 'Born in Slavery'), Harriet Jacobs's 'Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl' excerpt, The Confessions of Nat Turner excerpt, readings on Denmark Vesey, recordings of 'Wade in the Water' and 'Follow the Drinking Gourd.' Write the unit 5 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking enslaved people passively accepted slavery. They resisted daily (work slowdowns, sabotage, learning to read, keeping family and culture), escaped and occasionally rebelled.
- Treating 'secret codes' in every spiritual as proven fact. Some songs may have carried hidden meanings; historians debate specific claims (the 'Drinking Gourd' lyrics as we know them were published in 1928).

### Day 1 (Monday): Narratives of slavery
*Student:* Read two WPA Slave Narrative interviews and a chapter excerpt from Harriet Jacobs's 'Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl'; note family, labor and punishment details.

*How to teach:* Read two WPA narratives and the Jacobs excerpt; note family, labor and punishment details.

- **Q:** What should you consider when sourcing a WPA narrative?
  **A:** Interviews were in the 1930s with elderly people who were children under slavery; interviewers were often white Southerners, which may have influenced what people said.
- **Q:** How did Harriet Jacobs escape slavery?
  **A:** She hid for nearly seven years in a cramped attic space in her grandmother's house before escaping north by boat (1842).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Resistance and rebellion
*Student:* Read excerpts from 'The Confessions of Nat Turner' (1831) and accounts of Denmark Vesey's plan; list Southern legal responses.

*How to teach:* Read Nat Turner and Vesey accounts; list Southern legal responses.

- **Q:** What happened in Nat Turner's Rebellion?
  **A:** In 1831 in Southampton County, Virginia, Turner led enslaved people who killed about 55–60 white people; the militia crushed it, and Turner and many others (including innocent Black people) were executed or killed.
- **Q:** How did Southern states respond?
  **A:** Tightened slave codes — banning teaching enslaved people to read and write, limiting gatherings and Black preachers, increasing patrols.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Culture and spirituals
*Student:* Listen to 'Wade in the Water' and 'Follow the Drinking Gourd' and analyze possible double meanings in the lyrics.

*How to teach:* Listen to the spirituals; analyze possible double meanings.

- **Q:** What might 'Wade in the Water' have meant to freedom seekers?
  **A:** A possible hint to travel through water to throw tracking dogs off the scent; it also has biblical meaning (deliverance). Historians debate the code interpretation.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Free Black people, North and South
*Student:* Compare primary sources on free Black communities in Philadelphia and New Orleans in a T-chart of rights and restrictions.

*How to teach:* T-chart free Black life in Philadelphia and New Orleans.

- **Q:** Name one right and one restriction for free Black people in the North.
  **A:** Rights: own property, found churches and schools; restrictions: segregation, exclusion from many jobs, and voting bans in most states (Pennsylvania removed Black voting in 1838).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 test
*Student:* Unit test on the South and slavery, including a document-based short answer.

*How to teach:* Give the unit 5 test.

- **Q:** Name two forms of everyday resistance.
  **A:** Slowing work, breaking tools, pretending illness, secretly learning to read, running away, preserving culture and family.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test (suggested): cotton gin; Cotton Kingdom; Southern social structure; WPA narratives — interviews in the 1930s with formerly enslaved people (consider age and white interviewers when sourcing); Harriet Jacobs hid nearly seven years in her grandmother's tiny attic crawlspace before escaping north; Nat Turner's Rebellion (August 1831, Southampton County, Virginia) killed about 55–60 white people; Southern responses — laws banning teaching enslaved people to read, restricting gatherings and Black preachers, harsher patrols; Denmark Vesey (Charleston, 1822) planned a revolt, was betrayed and executed with about 34 others; responses — the AME church was destroyed and Negro Seamen Act restricted Black sailors; free Black communities (Philadelphia — churches, schools, some property, but no vote after 1838; New Orleans — larger free population of color with property and trades but legal restrictions). Document-based short answer graded on claim, evidence, sourcing.

## Spanish 1B: ¿Qué compraste? Preterite of regular -ar verbs

**Prepare:** Conjuguemos preterite -ar practice; three store receipts.

**Watch for:**
- Dropping accents: 'compro' (I buy) vs 'compró' (he bought). The accent changes meaning and tense.
- Confusing nosotros forms. -ar nosotros is the same in present and preterite (hablamos); context (ayer) tells the tense.

### Day 1 (Monday): Preterite -ar
*Student:* Learn the preterite -ar endings (-é, -aste, -ó, -amos, -asteis, -aron) and conjugate comprar, hablar, and mirar.

*How to teach:* Teach preterite -ar endings; conjugate comprar, hablar, mirar.

- **Q:** Conjugate hablar for yo and ella.
  **A:** hablé; habló.
- **Q:** Conjugate mirar for ellos.
  **A:** miraron.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Time expressions
*Student:* Learn ayer, anoche, la semana pasada, hace dos días and write 10 sentences about last weekend.

*How to teach:* Teach time expressions; write 10 sentences about last weekend.

- **Q:** Write: 'Last night I studied Spanish.'
  **A:** Anoche estudié español.
- **Q:** Write: 'Two days ago we bought shoes.'
  **A:** Hace dos días compramos zapatos.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Present vs. preterite
*Student:* Contrast nosotros forms (hablamos) and identify present or past from context in 15 sentences.

*How to teach:* Identify present vs past from context in 15 sentences.

- **Q:** Is 'Hablamos con el profesor ayer' present or past?
  **A:** Past (preterite) — 'ayer' signals yesterday.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Receipts
*Student:* Look at three store receipts and write what each person bought and how much they paid.

*How to teach:* Write what people bought and paid from receipts.

- **Q:** Write: 'My mom paid 50 dollars.'
  **A:** Mi mamá pagó cincuenta dólares.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a preterite -ar quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the preterite -ar quiz.

- **Q:** Translate: 'Did you (tú) buy the jacket?'
  **A:** ¿Compraste la chaqueta?

**Assessment:** Preterite -ar quiz.

**Answer key:** Preterite -ar quiz: comprar — compré, compraste, compró, compramos, comprasteis, compraron. Samples: Ayer yo (hablar) con mi abuela → hablé; Ellos (mirar) la tele anoche → miraron; ¿Tú (comprar) la camisa? → compraste; La semana pasada nosotros (caminar) al parque → caminamos; Ella (trabajar) el sábado → trabajó. 80% proficient.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Spirituals, work songs and the blues

**Prepare:** Recordings via Library of Congress or Smithsonian Folkways: Fisk Jubilee Singers, field/work songs, Bessie Smith, B.B. King. Keyboard. Connects to the HSS unit on slavery.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking spirituals were just religious songs. Correct: they expressed faith and hope and could carry coded messages about freedom.
- Writing blues lyrics without AAB. Correct: line 1 is stated, repeated, then answered by a rhyming line 3.

### Day 1 (Monday): Spirituals
*Student:* Listen to the Fisk Jubilee Singers and analyze 'Swing Low, Sweet Chariot' (connects to HSS slavery unit).

*How to teach:* Listen to the Fisk Jubilee Singers; analyze 'Swing Low, Sweet Chariot.'

- **Q:** Who were the Fisk Jubilee Singers?
  **A:** A choir of Black students from Fisk University (Nashville) who began touring in 1871, bringing spirituals to worldwide audiences and raising money for the school.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Work songs
*Student:* Listen to work songs and identify call-and-response patterns.

*How to teach:* Listen to work songs; identify call-and-response.

- **Q:** What is call-and-response?
  **A:** A leader sings a phrase and the group answers.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): The blues
*Student:* Listen to Bessie Smith and B.B. King; identify 12-bar form and AAB lyric pattern.

*How to teach:* Listen to Bessie Smith and B.B. King; identify 12-bar form and AAB lyrics.

- **Q:** What does AAB lyric form mean?
  **A:** The first line is sung twice (A, A), then a third line (B) answers or resolves it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Writing blues lyrics
*Student:* Write a 3-verse blues lyric in AAB form about a frustration in your life.

*How to teach:* Write three verses in AAB form.

- **Q:** Write a sample AAB blues verse.
  **A:** 'My phone just died right in the middle of my game, / My phone just died right in the middle of my game, / Now I'm sittin' here and nothing feels the same.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform your blues lyrics over the 12-bar progression.

*How to teach:* Perform the lyrics over the 12-bar progression.

- **Q:** How does each AAB verse line up with the 12 bars?
  **A:** Each line takes 4 bars: A over bars 1–4 (I), A over 5–8 (IV-I), B over 9–12 (V-IV-I).

**Assessment:** Blues lyric and performance.

**Answer key:** Blues lyric and performance: 4 = three AAB verses with rhyming B lines, personal topic, sung/spoken in time over correct 12-bar progression; 3 = minor timing issues; 2 = form errors; 1 = incomplete.

## Physical Education 8: Body systems and exercise

**Prepare:** Heart-rate monitor or a watch for 15-second pulse counts (×4). Fitness journal. Diagram templates.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the heart gets bigger only in disease. Correct: training enlarges and strengthens the heart muscle so stroke volume increases and resting heart rate drops.
- Believing muscles grow during exercise. Correct: exercise creates the stimulus; muscles repair and grow during recovery.

### Day 1 (Monday): Short-term responses
*Student:* Record heart rate, breathing rate and skin temperature at rest, after 5 minutes of jogging, and after 5 minutes of rest.

*How to teach:* Record HR, breaths/min and skin temperature at rest, after 5 min jogging and after 5 min rest.

- **Q:** Why does heart rate rise during exercise?
  **A:** Working muscles need more oxygen and nutrients, and carbon dioxide must be removed faster.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cardiorespiratory
*Student:* Learn how stroke volume and cardiac output increase with training; do a 25-minute interval run.

*How to teach:* Teach stroke volume (blood per beat) and cardiac output = HR × SV. Do a 25-minute interval run.

- **Q:** If HR = 70 bpm and SV = 70 mL, what is cardiac output?
  **A:** 70 × 70 = 4,900 mL/min ≈ 4.9 L/min.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Muscles and bones
*Student:* Explain how resistance exercise builds muscle and bone density; do a plyometric circuit (jumps, skips).

*How to teach:* Explain resistance and impact exercise stimulate muscle protein synthesis and bone remodeling. Plyometric circuit.

- **Q:** Why do jumping activities strengthen bones?
  **A:** Impact loads stimulate bone-building cells to add mineral, increasing bone density — especially important during adolescence.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Nervous system
*Student:* Explain how practice builds neuromuscular coordination; do a 15-minute footwork ladder drill.

*How to teach:* Explain practice strengthens nerve pathways (motor learning). Ladder drill.

- **Q:** Why do skills feel easier with practice?
  **A:** Neural pathways become more efficient and coordinated, so movements become more automatic.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Create a diagram showing how four body systems respond and adapt to exercise.

*How to teach:* Diagram four systems (cardiovascular, respiratory, muscular, skeletal/nervous) with short-term responses and long-term adaptations.

- **Q:** Give a long-term adaptation of the respiratory system.
  **A:** Stronger breathing muscles and more efficient oxygen use, so breathing rate during submaximal exercise is lower.

**Assessment:** Body systems diagram.

**Answer key:** Diagram key — Cardiovascular: short-term HR and SV rise; long-term lower resting HR, larger stroke volume. Respiratory: breathing rate/depth rise; long-term stronger diaphragm, better efficiency. Muscular: more blood flow, heat, fatigue; long-term strength, endurance, more mitochondria. Skeletal: loading; long-term higher bone density. Nervous: motor unit recruitment; long-term better coordination. Proficient: 4 systems with one response and one adaptation each.

## Health Education 8: Gender identity, sexual orientation and respect

**Prepare:** Read the CHYA-aligned glossary (e.g., from your county or the CA Department of Education/KidsHealth 'Sexual Orientation' and 'Gender Identity'). Prepare scenario cards about identity-based teasing. Discuss family values beforehand; the lesson's focus is respect and safety for all.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing gender identity, gender expression and sexual orientation. Correct: identity is one's internal sense of gender; expression is how it is shown outwardly; orientation is who one is attracted to.
- Thinking jokes about identity are harmless. Correct: identity-based bullying is linked to depression, anxiety and higher suicide risk; it also violates school policy and California law.

### Day 1 (Monday): Vocabulary
*Student:* Define gender identity, gender expression and sexual orientation using a medically accurate glossary; discuss with a parent.

*How to teach:* Read each term from the glossary and the child explains it in their own words; talk about questions with you.

- **Q:** Define gender identity, gender expression and sexual orientation.
  **A:** Gender identity: a person's internal sense of their own gender. Gender expression: how a person shows gender outwardly (clothing, hair, behavior). Sexual orientation: who a person is emotionally, romantically or physically attracted to.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Stereotypes
*Student:* Identify gender stereotypes in ads, toys and media and how they limit people.

*How to teach:* Look at toy aisles, ads or shows and list stereotypes (e.g., 'boys don't cry,' 'girls aren't good at science'). Discuss how they limit choices.

- **Q:** How can gender stereotypes limit people?
  **A:** They pressure people into narrow roles and discourage interests, careers or emotions that don't fit the stereotype.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Respect
*Student:* Discuss how bullying based on identity harms mental health and practice upstander responses.

*How to teach:* Discuss the harm of identity-based bullying and model upstander responses: 'That's not cool,' check on the target, report to an adult.

- **Q:** Name two upstander responses.
  **A:** Speak up calmly, support the person being targeted afterward, don't laugh or share, report to a trusted adult or school.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Support
*Student:* Identify supportive resources and trusted adults for any teen with questions.

*How to teach:* List supportive resources: trusted adults, school counselor, doctor, The Trevor Project (crisis support for LGBTQ+ youth), 988.

- **Q:** Where can a teen with questions about identity find support?
  **A:** A parent or trusted adult, school counselor, doctor, or confidential lines such as The Trevor Project or 988.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on how to treat all people with dignity and respect.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph explaining how to treat all people with dignity and respect, with two concrete actions.

- **Q:** What does treating someone with dignity look like at school?
  **A:** Using kind words and people's names, including others, not spreading rumors or jokes, and standing up when someone is mistreated.

**Assessment:** Respect paragraph.

**Answer key:** Respect paragraph: 4 = clear claim that all people deserve dignity, accurate use of at least two terms, two concrete actions (upstander response, inclusive language), mention of harm caused by bullying; 3 = clear with one action; 2 = general statement only; 1 = incomplete or disrespectful.
