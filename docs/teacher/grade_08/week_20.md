# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 20

## English Language Arts 8: Poetry of American identity: Whitman and Hughes

**Prepare:** Print 'O Captain! My Captain!' and 'I Hear America Singing' (Walt Whitman) and 'I, Too' (Langston Hughes) from Poetry Foundation. Choose an unfamiliar Whitman stanza (e.g., from 'Song of Myself') for Friday.

**Watch for:**
- Reading 'O Captain!' literally as a sea story. It's an elegy using an extended metaphor for Lincoln's assassination at the end of the Civil War.
- Thinking free verse has no craft. Whitman builds rhythm with repetition, catalogs and parallel lines.

### Day 1 (Monday): 'O Captain! My Captain!'
*Student:* Read the elegy for Lincoln and map the extended metaphor (ship = nation, captain = Lincoln, voyage = war).

*How to teach:* Read the elegy aloud. Child maps the extended metaphor: ship = the nation, captain = Lincoln, fearful trip = the Civil War, prize = Union victory/preserved nation.

- **Q:** Why does the speaker contrast ringing bells with the fallen captain?
  **A:** The crowds celebrate victory while the speaker grieves — joy and loss collide as Lincoln is killed just as the war ends.
- **Q:** What kind of poem is it?
  **A:** An elegy — a poem mourning a death (Lincoln's, 1865).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): 'I Hear America Singing'
*Student:* Read the poem and analyze Whitman's catalog of workers and free-verse rhythm.

*How to teach:* Read 'I Hear America Singing.' Child lists every worker in the catalog and explains the effect of the long, rhythmic free-verse lines. Finish by asking: "Name four workers in Whitman's catalog." — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Name four workers in Whitman's catalog.
  **A:** Mechanics, carpenter, mason, boatman, deckhand, shoemaker, hatter, wood-cutter, ploughboy, mother, young wife, girl sewing or washing.
- **Q:** What does 'Each singing what belongs to him or her and to none else' suggest?
  **A:** Every person's work and identity is unique and valuable to the nation.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): 'I, Too'
*Student:* Read Hughes's response to Whitman and explain how 'the darker brother' reshapes the idea of who sings America.

*How to teach:* Read 'I, Too.' Ask who 'they' are and what the kitchen and the table symbolize. Before moving on, ask: "Who is 'the darker brother'?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Who is 'the darker brother'?
  **A:** A Black American, sent to eat in the kitchen when company comes — excluded from equal place.
- **Q:** What will happen 'Tomorrow'?
  **A:** He'll be at the table, and 'they'll see how beautiful I am / And be ashamed' — 'I, too, am America.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing themes
*Student:* Write a paragraph comparing the theme of 'I Hear America Singing' and 'I, Too' using evidence from both.

*How to teach:* Child writes the comparison paragraph with evidence from both poems. Check understanding with: "How does Hughes's first line answer Whitman?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** How does Hughes's first line answer Whitman?
  **A:** 'I, too, sing America' adds a voice Whitman's catalog left out.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on figurative language terms using an unfamiliar Whitman stanza.

*How to teach:* Give the figurative-language quiz on the unfamiliar stanza. To check, ask: "Define anaphora." and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Define anaphora.
  **A:** Repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive lines.

**Assessment:** Comparison paragraph and figurative language quiz.

**Answer key:** Comparison paragraph rubric (4 levels): 4 = states Whitman's theme (America is made of many workers, each singing 'what belongs to him or her') and Hughes's response (Black Americans are part of that song though excluded, and will claim their seat at the table), with a quotation from each and explanation; 3 = both poems, weak explanation; 2 = one poem; 1 = summary. Figurative language quiz: correctly identifies in the stanza a catalog, anaphora, free verse, the speaker, and any metaphor (1 point each), plus explains one effect.

## Mathematics 8: Volume of cylinders and cones

**Prepare:** Several cans of different sizes, a cereal box, paper and tape to build a cone, rice or dry beans, ruler, measuring cup (mL).

**Watch for:**
- Using diameter instead of radius in πr²h. Have the child write r = d ÷ 2 first.
- Forgetting the 1/3 in the cone formula. Recall the lab: 3 cones fill 1 cylinder.

### Day 1 (Monday): Volume of prisms review
*Student:* Review V = Bh with rectangular prisms; measure a cereal box and compute its volume.

*How to teach:* Review V = Bh. Child measures a cereal box and computes its volume.

- **Q:** A box is 19 cm by 7 cm by 28 cm. Find its volume.
  **A:** 19 × 7 × 28 = 3,724 cm³.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cylinder volume
*Student:* Measure 3 cans, compute V = pi r^2 h, and check by filling with water and measuring in a measuring cup (1 mL = 1 cubic cm).

*How to teach:* Child measures 3 cans, computes V = πr²h, then fills with water and pours into a measuring cup to compare (1 mL = 1 cm³). Finish by asking: "A can has radius 4 cm and height 10 cm. Find its volume." — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** A can has radius 4 cm and height 10 cm. Find its volume.
  **A:** π(16)(10) = 160π ≈ 502.7 cm³ (about 503 mL).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cone vs cylinder lab
*Student:* Build a paper cone with the same base and height as a can; count how many cones of rice fill the can to discover V = (1/3) pi r^2 h.

*How to teach:* Build a paper cone with the same base and height as a can; count cones of rice to fill the can. Before moving on, ask: "How many cones fill the matching cylinder, and what formula results?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** How many cones fill the matching cylinder, and what formula results?
  **A:** About 3, so V(cone) = (1/3)πr²h.
- **Q:** Find the volume of a cone with r = 3 cm and h = 8 cm.
  **A:** (1/3)π(9)(8) = 24π ≈ 75.4 cm³.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Missing dimensions
*Student:* Solve 6 problems finding the radius or height of cylinders and cones given volume.

*How to teach:* Model solving the volume formula for h or r. Child solves 6.

- **Q:** A cylinder has volume 100π and radius 5. Find h.
  **A:** 100π = 25πh → h = 4.
- **Q:** A cone has volume 12π and height 4. Find r.
  **A:** 12π = (1/3)πr²(4) → r² = 9 → r = 3.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: 6 cylinder and cone volume problems including a real-world one (a cone-shaped paper cup).

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Review & check day: have the child redo any missed problem in the notebook and explain the error in one sentence.

- **Q:** A cone-shaped cup has r = 4 cm, h = 9 cm. How much does it hold?
  **A:** (1/3)π(16)(9) = 48π ≈ 150.8 cm³ ≈ 151 mL.

**Assessment:** Cone-cylinder lab write-up and quiz.

**Answer key:** Lab write-up rubric: 4 = measured r and h, computed both volumes, found ~3 cones fill the cylinder, explained V = (1/3)πr²h; 3 = minor errors; 2 = data without conclusion; 1 = incomplete. Quiz: 1) Cylinder r = 4, h = 10 → 160π ≈ 502.7 cm³. 2) Cone r = 3, h = 8 → 24π ≈ 75.4 cm³. 3) Cylinder V = 100π, r = 5 → h = 4. 4) Cone V = 12π, h = 4 → r = 3. 5) Cylinder d = 6, h = 5 → 45π ≈ 141.4. 6) Paper cup cone r = 4 cm, h = 9 cm → 48π ≈ 150.8 cm³ (about 151 mL).

## Science 8 (Integrated): Rock layers and relative dating

**Prepare:** Clear jar with lid, sand, soil, gravel, water; colored play-dough; small objects as 'fossils'; clear straws.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the top layer is oldest. By superposition, in undisturbed layers the bottom is oldest.
- Thinking a fault or intrusion is older than the layers it cuts. Whatever cuts across layers is younger.

### Day 1 (Monday): Layering jar
*Student:* Pour sand, soil and gravel into a jar of water in stages; let it settle and observe layering.

*How to teach:* Pour sand, soil and gravel into water in stages; let settle. To check, ask: "Which settles first?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Which settles first?
  **A:** Gravel (largest, heaviest particles).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Superposition
*Student:* Build play-dough layers with 'fossils' and a 'fault'; core-sample with a straw and interpret the sequence.

*How to teach:* Build play-dough layers with fossils and a fault; core-sample with a straw. Finish by asking: "Which layer is oldest?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Which layer is oldest?
  **A:** The bottom one (if undisturbed).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cross-cutting relationships
*Student:* Order events in 4 rock-layer diagrams with intrusions and faults.

*How to teach:* Order events in 4 diagrams with intrusions and faults. Before moving on, ask: "An igneous intrusion cuts through layers A–C. Older or younger?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** An igneous intrusion cuts through layers A–C. Older or younger?
  **A:** Younger than A–C.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Index fossils
*Student:* Correlate layers from three 'sites' using index fossils.

*How to teach:* Correlate layers from three sites using index fossils. Check understanding with: "What makes a good index fossil?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** What makes a good index fossil?
  **A:** Widespread, easily recognized, and lived for a short time.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: sequence events in two geologic cross sections.

*How to teach:* Give the sequencing quiz. To check, ask: "State the law of superposition." and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** State the law of superposition.
  **A:** In undisturbed layers, the oldest is at the bottom.

**Assessment:** Cross-section sequencing quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: sequence two cross sections (accept sequences matching your diagrams); rules applied: superposition (oldest at bottom), cross-cutting relationships (intrusions/faults are younger than layers they cut), index fossils correlate layers of the same age.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: The abolition movement

**Prepare:** Pennsylvania Gradual Abolition Act (1780) excerpt; Northwest Ordinance Article 6; Garrison's 'To the Public' (The Liberator, January 1, 1831); Douglass's 'What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?' (July 5, 1852) excerpt; Underground Railroad map; readings on Harriet Tubman, William Still, Ohio's 1804 Black Laws and James Forten.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Underground Railroad was an actual railroad with tunnels. It was a secret network of people and safe houses; most freedom seekers relied on themselves and Black communities.
- Assuming all abolitionists agreed. Some sought gradual emancipation or colonization; Garrison demanded immediate abolition and moral persuasion; Douglass later supported political action.

### Day 1 (Monday): Early abolition
*Student:* Read Pennsylvania's 1780 Gradual Abolition Act and the Northwest Ordinance's Article 6; map Northern states' abolition dates.

*How to teach:* Read the 1780 Act and Article 6; map Northern abolition dates.

- **Q:** Did Pennsylvania's 1780 act free all enslaved people immediately?
  **A:** No — it was gradual: those already enslaved stayed enslaved; children born after it were free after serving until age 28.
- **Q:** Which was the first state (as a republic) to ban adult slavery outright?
  **A:** Vermont, in its 1777 constitution.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Garrison and Douglass
*Student:* Read Garrison's 'To the Public' (1831) and Douglass's 'What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?' (1852); compare tone and strategy.

*How to teach:* Read Garrison and Douglass; compare tone and strategy.

- **Q:** Quote or paraphrase Garrison's promise in 'To the Public.'
  **A:** 'I will be as harsh as truth… I will not equivocate — I will not excuse — I will not retreat a single inch — AND I WILL BE HEARD.'
- **Q:** What was Douglass's main point in 'What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?'
  **A:** Independence Day celebrations of liberty were hypocrisy to enslaved people, revealing the nation's injustice.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Underground Railroad
*Student:* Map major routes and read about Harriet Tubman's missions and William Still's records.

*How to teach:* Map routes; read about Tubman and Still.

- **Q:** Why was Harriet Tubman called 'Moses'?
  **A:** She led enslaved people to freedom, returning to Maryland about 13 times and guiding about 70 people.
- **Q:** Why are William Still's records valuable?
  **A:** He interviewed and recorded hundreds of freedom seekers, preserving their names and stories.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Free Black lives and laws
*Student:* Read about Northern Black laws (e.g., Ohio's 1804 law) and the achievements of leaders such as James Forten.

*How to teach:* Read about Northern Black laws and James Forten.

- **Q:** What did Ohio's Black Laws require?
  **A:** Black residents had to show certificates of freedom and register; an 1807 law required a $500 bond, discouraging Black settlement.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on abolitionists and their strategies.

*How to teach:* Give the abolition quiz.

- **Q:** Name two abolitionist strategies.
  **A:** Newspapers and speeches (moral persuasion), petitions, the Underground Railroad, political parties, slave narratives.

**Assessment:** Abolition quiz.

**Answer key:** Abolition quiz key: Pennsylvania's 1780 act was gradual — it freed no one immediately; children born to enslaved mothers after it were free but bound to serve until age 28; Northwest Ordinance Article 6 banned slavery in the territory; Northern abolition dates — Vermont 1777, Pennsylvania 1780 (gradual), Massachusetts 1783 (court rulings), Connecticut and Rhode Island 1784 (gradual), New York 1799 (gradual; complete 1827), New Jersey 1804 (gradual); Garrison — The Liberator, immediate abolition ('I will be heard'); Douglass — escaped slavery 1838, orator, North Star newspaper; Tubman — escaped 1849, about 13 trips rescuing about 70 people; William Still — Philadelphia Vigilance Committee, recorded hundreds of freedom seekers' stories (published 1872); Ohio Black Laws (1804, 1807) required proof of freedom and later a $500 bond; James Forten — wealthy Black Philadelphia sailmaker who funded abolitionism.

## Spanish 1B: Preterite of regular -er and -ir verbs

**Prepare:** Conjuguemos preterite -er/-ir practice.

**Watch for:**
- Using -ar endings for -er/-ir verbs: 'comé.' Use comí, comió.
- Forgetting the y in leer/oír/creer third person: leyó, leyeron (not leió).

### Day 1 (Monday): Endings
*Student:* Learn the shared -er/-ir preterite endings (-í, -iste, -ió, -imos, -isteis, -ieron) and conjugate comer and salir.

*How to teach:* Teach shared -er/-ir endings; conjugate comer and salir.

- **Q:** Conjugate salir for ella and ellos.
  **A:** salió; salieron.
- **Q:** Conjugate comer for yo.
  **A:** comí.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Drill
*Student:* Complete a Conjuguemos preterite -er/-ir drill to 90%.

*How to teach:* Conjuguemos drill to 90%.

- **Q:** Conjugate vivir for nosotros (preterite).
  **A:** vivimos (same as present — context decides).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Leer and oír
*Student:* Learn the y-forms (leyó, leyeron, oyó) and write five sentences with them.

*How to teach:* Teach leyó/leyeron, oyó/oyeron; five sentences.

- **Q:** Write: 'My sister read the brochure.'
  **A:** Mi hermana leyó el folleto.
- **Q:** Write: 'They heard the music.'
  **A:** Ellos oyeron la música.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Trip interview
*Student:* Interview a parent about their best vacation using -ar, -er, and -ir preterite questions.

*How to teach:* Interview a parent about their best vacation.

- **Q:** Ask: 'What did you eat on the trip?'
  **A:** ¿Qué comiste en el viaje?

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a mixed regular preterite quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the mixed regular preterite quiz.

- **Q:** Translate: 'We left at seven and returned at ten.'
  **A:** Salimos a las siete y volvimos a las diez.

**Assessment:** Regular preterite quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: comer — comí, comiste, comió, comimos, comisteis, comieron; salir — salí, saliste, salió, salimos, salisteis, salieron; leer — leí, leíste, leyó, leímos, leísteis, leyeron; oír — oí, oíste, oyó, oímos, oísteis, oyeron. Samples: Ayer nosotros (beber) limonada → bebimos; Ellos (escribir) postales → escribieron; Mi padre (volver) tarde → volvió; ¿Tú (aprender) a bucear? → aprendiste. 80% proficient.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Objectives, obstacles and tactics

**Prepare:** Scenario cards with two characters who want different things (e.g., teen wants to go to a party, parent wants homework done). A familiar film scene to analyze.

**Watch for:**
- Playing emotions instead of objectives. Correct: actors play what the character wants (action verbs); emotion follows.
- Using only one tactic. Correct: when a tactic fails, the character changes tactics (charm, plead, threaten).

### Day 1 (Monday): Objectives
*Student:* Learn objective-obstacle-tactic and analyze a scene from a familiar film.

*How to teach:* Teach objective (what the character wants), obstacle (what's in the way), tactic (how they try to get it); analyze a film scene.

- **Q:** Define objective, obstacle and tactic.
  **A:** Objective: what the character wants. Obstacle: what blocks it. Tactic: the action used to try to get it (persuade, flatter, threaten).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Improvisation
*Student:* Improvise 3 two-person scenes where each character wants something different.

*How to teach:* Improvise three scenes with conflicting objectives.

- **Q:** What makes an improv scene work?
  **A:** Clear wants, listening, 'yes, and' acceptance, and real conflict.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Subtext
*Student:* Say the line 'I'm fine' 5 ways with different subtext.

*How to teach:* Say 'I'm fine' five ways with different subtext.

- **Q:** What is subtext?
  **A:** The unspoken meaning or feeling beneath the words.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Script analysis
*Student:* Break a short scene into beats and mark objectives.

*How to teach:* Break a short scene into beats; mark objectives.

- **Q:** What is a beat?
  **A:** A unit of a scene in which one objective or tactic is pursued; a new beat starts when it changes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform an improvised scene and identify your tactics.

*How to teach:* Perform an improvised scene and identify tactics.

- **Q:** Name three tactics as action verbs.
  **A:** Charm, beg, bribe, threaten, guilt, reason, tease.

**Assessment:** Improv performance and beat analysis.

**Answer key:** Improv and beat analysis: 4 = clear objective pursued with at least two tactic shifts, responds to partner; beat analysis marks each beat with objective/tactic verbs; 3 = one tactic shift; 2 = unclear objective; 1 = incomplete.

## Physical Education 8: Golf and disc golf

**Prepare:** Plastic or whiffle golf balls, a putter or broom handle, cups/targets, hula hoops, a frisbee or disc, tape measure. Set up a 6-hole course with par values.

**Watch for:**
- Swinging hard on putts. Correct: putting is a smooth pendulum from the shoulders with the wrists quiet.
- Releasing the disc high and flat. Correct: keep the disc level or slightly nose-down and release on a straight line.

### Day 1 (Monday): Putting
*Student:* Practice the putting grip and pendulum stroke to a cup or target from 3, 6 and 9 feet.

*How to teach:* Putting grip; pendulum stroke from shoulders; putts from 3, 6, 9 feet.

- **Q:** What makes a putting stroke consistent?
  **A:** Rocking the shoulders like a pendulum with still wrists and head, and a stroke length matched to distance.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Chipping
*Student:* Practice chipping whiffle balls into a hula hoop from 10 and 20 feet.

*How to teach:* Chip into a hoop from 10 and 20 feet.

- **Q:** How is a chip different from a full swing?
  **A:** Short, controlled swing with little wrist; the ball flies low then rolls.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Disc drive
*Student:* Practice the backhand drive with reach-back and follow-through; measure distances.

*How to teach:* Backhand drive: reach back, pull across the chest, snap wrist, follow-through; measure distances.

- **Q:** Why does reach-back add distance?
  **A:** It lengthens the pull, so the disc accelerates over a longer distance before release.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Course
*Student:* Set up a 6-hole disc golf or backyard golf course and play a round, keeping score relative to par.

*How to teach:* Play a 6-hole round, scoring relative to par.

- **Q:** If par is 3 and you take 4 strokes, what's your score on the hole?
  **A:** +1 (a bogey).

### Day 5 (Friday): Analyze
*Student:* Explain how angle of release and follow-through affected your distance and accuracy.

*How to teach:* Explain how angle of release and follow-through affected results.

- **Q:** What happens if the disc is released nose-up?
  **A:** It climbs, stalls and drops short.

**Assessment:** Scorecard and written analysis.

**Answer key:** Scorecard: all 6 holes scored with total relative to par (correct arithmetic). Written analysis: 4 = explains release angle, follow-through and force with specific examples from the round and one adjustment; 3 = one factor explained; 2 = describes results only; 1 = incomplete.

## Health Education 8: Opioids, fentanyl and naloxone

**Prepare:** Parental preview: overdose content. Watch a naloxone (Narcan) nasal-spray training video (SAMHSA, CDPH or manufacturer) first. Use a doll or pillow and an empty spray bottle as a model. Learn California's Good Samaritan law (Health & Safety Code 11376.5).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking pills from friends or social media are safe if they look real. Correct: counterfeit pills often contain fentanyl; one pill can kill.
- Believing naloxone is dangerous if the person didn't take opioids. Correct: naloxone has no effect on someone without opioids in their system and is safe to give if overdose is suspected.

### Day 1 (Monday): Opioids
*Student:* Learn how opioids affect breathing and why fentanyl in counterfeit pills is deadly.

*How to teach:* Explain opioids (oxycodone, heroin, fentanyl) bind receptors in the brainstem and slow breathing; fentanyl is 50 times stronger than heroin and 2 mg can be fatal.

- **Q:** Why do opioid overdoses kill?
  **A:** Opioids slow or stop breathing, so the brain and body don't get oxygen.
- **Q:** Why are counterfeit pills so dangerous?
  **A:** They can contain unknown, lethal amounts of fentanyl and look identical to real prescription pills.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Overdose signs
*Student:* Identify overdose signs (pinpoint pupils, slow breathing, unresponsive).

*How to teach:* List overdose signs together: pinpoint pupils, unresponsive/can't wake, slow or stopped breathing, gurgling/snoring sounds, blue or gray lips/fingertips, limp body.

- **Q:** Name three signs of opioid overdose.
  **A:** Pinpoint pupils, unresponsiveness, slow or no breathing, blue/gray lips or nails, choking or gurgling sounds, limp body.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Naloxone
*Student:* Watch a naloxone nasal-spray training video and practice the steps on a model.

*How to teach:* Watch the video, then practice: shout and rub sternum, call 911, tilt head back, insert nozzle into a nostril and press plunger, place in recovery position, give second dose in 2–3 minutes if no response, stay until help arrives.

- **Q:** After giving naloxone, what next?
  **A:** Stay with the person, give rescue breathing/CPR if trained and needed, put them in the recovery position, and give a second dose after 2–3 minutes if they don't respond; naloxone can wear off, so they still need medical care.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Good Samaritan
*Student:* Learn California's Good Samaritan law that protects people who call 911.

*How to teach:* Explain the Good Samaritan law: a person who seeks medical help for an overdose is protected from arrest/prosecution for small amounts of drugs for personal use.

- **Q:** Why does California have a Good Samaritan overdose law?
  **A:** To encourage people to call 911 without fear of being arrested for minor drug possession, saving lives.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Sequence the steps for responding to a suspected overdose.

*How to teach:* The child orders shuffled step cards for responding to an overdose and explains each.

- **Q:** What is the first thing to do if someone won't wake up?
  **A:** Try to wake them (shout, rub sternum) and call 911 immediately.

**Assessment:** Overdose response sequence.

**Answer key:** Correct sequence: 1) Check responsiveness (shout, sternum rub) 2) Call 911 3) Give naloxone into one nostril 4) Support breathing (rescue breaths/CPR if trained) 5) Recovery position on side 6) Second dose after 2–3 minutes if no response 7) Stay until EMS arrives and tell them what happened. Proficient: all steps in order with reasons.
