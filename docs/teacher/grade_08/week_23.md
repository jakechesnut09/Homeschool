# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 23

## English Language Arts 8: Historical context and The Diary of Anne Frank, Act 1, Scenes 1–2

**Prepare:** Get the play 'The Diary of Anne Frank' (Goodrich and Hackett). Read USHMM Holocaust Encyclopedia articles (ushmm.org). Parental preview: Holocaust content — persecution, fear, and the knowledge that most characters die in camps. Preview USHMM videos before showing.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the play is Anne's diary. It's a dramatization by two playwrights based on it.
- Ignoring stage directions. They carry setting, action and mood.

### Day 1 (Monday): Historical context
*Student:* Read USHMM articles on Nazi Germany and the occupation of the Netherlands and create a timeline of events from 1933–1945.

*How to teach:* Read two or three USHMM Holocaust Encyclopedia articles together (Nazi rise to power; the Netherlands; Anne Frank). The child builds a timeline of 8–10 key events from 1933 to 1945.

- **Q:** When did Germany invade the Netherlands?
  **A:** May 1940.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Elements of drama
*Student:* Learn stage directions, acts and scenes; read Act 1, Scene 1 and note how the play opens with Mr. Frank's return.

*How to teach:* Explain acts, scenes and stage directions (italic text describing setting and action). Read Act 1, Scene 1 aloud, including directions, and ask why the play begins after the war ends.

- **Q:** How does the play open?
  **A:** November 1945: Mr. Frank returns, broken, to the empty attic; Miep gives him Anne's diary and he begins to read — the action flashes back to 1942.
- **Q:** Why open with the ending?
  **A:** The audience knows the outcome, creating dramatic irony.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Act 1, Scene 2
*Student:* Read Scene 2 aloud with assigned parts and record what each character's first lines reveal about them.

*How to teach:* Read Act 1, Scene 2 aloud with assigned parts (you can take several). The child records what each character's first lines reveal about him or her.

- **Q:** What do Peter's first actions reveal?
  **A:** Shy, quiet; he arrives with his cat and later tears off his yellow star.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mediums compared
*Student:* Compare a photo archive, a USHMM video and a written article about the Secret Annex; evaluate each medium's strengths.

*How to teach:* Look at photographs (Anne Frank House website), watch a short USHMM video and read an article about the Secret Annex. The child charts what each medium shows best and what it leaves out.

- **Q:** Strength of photographs?
  **A:** They show the real place.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on background and Scenes 1–2.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz on historical background and Scenes 1–2; collect the timeline. Check understanding with: "Who gives Anne the diary in Scene 2?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Who gives Anne the diary in Scene 2?
  **A:** Her father.

**Assessment:** Background timeline and quiz.

**Answer key:** Timeline (key dates to include): 1933 Hitler becomes chancellor (Jan.) and the Franks begin moving to Amsterdam; 1935 Nuremberg Laws; Nov. 1938 Kristallnacht; Sept. 1939 Germany invades Poland; May 1940 Germany invades the Netherlands; 1942 Jews in the Netherlands forced to wear yellow stars; July 1942 Franks go into hiding; Aug. 4, 1944 arrest; early 1945 Anne dies at Bergen-Belsen; Jan. 27, 1945 Auschwitz liberated; May 1945 war ends in Europe. Quiz: Scene 1 is set in November 1945 with Mr. Frank returning alone and Miep giving him the diary; Scene 2 flashes back to July 1942 as the Franks and Van Daans arrive.

## Mathematics 8: Lines of fit and linear models

**Prepare:** Spaghetti strand or clear ruler, graph paper, last week's arm-span data, Desmos.

**Watch for:**
- Drawing a line that connects the first and last points. A good line of fit has about as many points above as below and follows the trend.
- Trusting predictions far outside the data. Extrapolation can give impossible results (e.g., a negative car price).

### Day 1 (Monday): Fitting a line
*Student:* Lay a strand of spaghetti over the arm span scatter plot to fit a line; compare lines and discuss closeness of fit.

*How to teach:* Lay spaghetti on the scatter plot to fit a line. Compare with a second placement and ask which is closer to more points.

- **Q:** What makes a good line of fit?
  **A:** It follows the trend with points roughly balanced above and below and small distances from the points.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Equations of fitted lines
*Student:* Write the equation of the fitted line using two points on it; interpret the slope (cm of height per cm of arm span).

*How to teach:* Pick two points ON the line (not necessarily data points), compute slope and intercept, and interpret. To check, ask: "A fitted line passes through (150, 150) and (180, 180) (arm span, height in cm). Write it and interpret the slope." and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** A fitted line passes through (150, 150) and (180, 180) (arm span, height in cm). Write it and interpret the slope.
  **A:** Slope 30/30 = 1, so y = x: about 1 cm more height per 1 cm more arm span.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Predictions
*Student:* Use models to predict values (e.g., price of a used car at 80,000 miles) and judge whether the prediction is reasonable.

*How to teach:* Use a model to predict and judge reasonableness. Finish by asking: "Price model p = −0.1m + 20,000 (m = miles). Predict at 80,000 miles. Is 250,000 miles reasonable?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Price model p = −0.1m + 20,000 (m = miles). Predict at 80,000 miles. Is 250,000 miles reasonable?
  **A:** At 80,000: −8,000 + 20,000 = $12,000. At 250,000 the model gives −$5,000, which is impossible — too far beyond the data.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Residuals
*Student:* Calculate how far 5 data points are from the line and decide which of two lines fits better; check with Desmos regression.

*How to teach:* Teach residual = actual − predicted. Child computes 5 residuals for two lines and compares; check with Desmos regression (y1 ~ mx1 + b).

- **Q:** Actual height 165 cm, predicted 162 cm. Residual?
  **A:** +3 cm (the point is 3 cm above the line).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: fit a line, write its equation and interpret slope and intercept in context.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Review & check day: have the child redo any missed problem in the notebook and explain the error in one sentence.

- **Q:** A model of test score vs study hours is y = 5x + 60. Interpret 5 and 60.
  **A:** Each extra hour of study is associated with about 5 more points; 60 is the predicted score with 0 hours.

**Assessment:** Quiz on lines of fit and interpretation.

**Answer key:** Quiz rubric: line drawn through the middle of the cloud (2), equation from two points on the line correct (2), slope interpreted with units, e.g., 'about 1 cm of height per cm of arm span' (2), intercept interpreted or noted as not meaningful (1), one prediction with reasonableness comment (1). Example: line through (150, 150) and (180, 180) → y = x.

## Science 8 (Integrated): Anatomical similarities

**Prepare:** Forelimb diagrams (human, cat, whale, bat), colored pencils, a cooked chicken wing (optional), paper towels.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing homologous and analogous structures. Homologous share ancestry (same bones, different functions); analogous share function but not ancestry (bird wing vs insect wing).
- Thinking vestigial structures have no function. They are reduced compared with ancestors; some still have minor functions.

### Day 1 (Monday): Forelimbs compared
*Student:* Color-code bones in human, cat, whale and bat forelimbs and describe similarities.

*How to teach:* Color-code bones in four forelimbs. Check understanding with: "What do the four forelimbs share?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** What do the four forelimbs share?
  **A:** The same bone pattern: one upper bone, two lower bones, wrist and finger bones.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Chicken wing dissection
*Student:* Examine a cooked chicken wing to identify bones homologous to the human arm.

*How to teach:* Examine the chicken wing (wash hands after). To check, ask: "Which human bones match the wing?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Which human bones match the wing?
  **A:** Humerus, radius and ulna.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Vestigial structures
*Student:* Research 4 vestigial structures (whale pelvis, human tailbone) and explain their significance.

*How to teach:* Research 4 vestigial structures. Finish by asking: "What does a whale's pelvis suggest?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** What does a whale's pelvis suggest?
  **A:** Whales descended from four-legged land ancestors.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cladograms
*Student:* Build a simple cladogram from a table of shared traits for 6 animals.

*How to teach:* Build a cladogram from a shared-traits table. Before moving on, ask: "What does a branch point represent?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** What does a branch point represent?
  **A:** A common ancestor with a new trait.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on homologous and analogous structures and cladograms.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Check understanding with: "Homologous or analogous: bat wing and bird wing?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Homologous or analogous: bat wing and bird wing?
  **A:** As wings, analogous; as forelimbs, homologous.

**Assessment:** Cladogram and quiz.

**Answer key:** Cladogram rubric: shared traits placed at branch points; organisms with more shared derived traits branch closer together. Quiz: human arm, cat leg, whale flipper and bat wing are homologous; bird wing and butterfly wing are analogous; whale pelvis and human tailbone are vestigial.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: Nullification and secession

**Prepare:** Webster's Second Reply to Hayne (1830) excerpt; Calhoun's nullification arguments (South Carolina Exposition and Protest, 1828); John Brown's last courtroom speech (November 2, 1859) and newspaper reactions; 1860 electoral map and popular vote; South Carolina's Declaration of the Causes of Secession (December 24, 1860). Write the unit 7 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking secession declarations focused only on 'states' rights' in general. South Carolina's declaration repeatedly names the protection of slavery and Northern hostility to it as the cause.
- Thinking Lincoln won a majority of the popular vote in 1860. He won about 40% in a four-way race, but a clear Electoral College majority (180 of 303) entirely from free states.

### Day 1 (Monday): Webster vs Calhoun
*Student:* Compare excerpts of Webster's 'Liberty and Union' reply and Calhoun's nullification arguments from the 1832 crisis.

*How to teach:* Compare Webster and Calhoun.

- **Q:** What is nullification?
  **A:** The theory that a state can declare a federal law void within its borders.
- **Q:** What was Webster's famous line?
  **A:** 'Liberty and Union, now and forever, one and inseparable!'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): John Brown
*Student:* Read John Brown's final courtroom speech and contrasting Northern and Southern newspaper reactions to Harpers Ferry.

*How to teach:* Read John Brown's speech and contrasting newspaper reactions.

- **Q:** What did John Brown attempt at Harpers Ferry (1859)?
  **A:** To seize the federal arsenal and spark an armed uprising of enslaved people; he was captured and hanged.
- **Q:** How did reactions differ North and South?
  **A:** Many Northerners (e.g., Emerson, Thoreau) saw him as a martyr; Southerners saw proof that Northerners wanted to incite slave rebellion.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Election of 1860
*Student:* Analyze the 1860 electoral map and popular-vote data to explain Lincoln's victory without Southern votes.

*How to teach:* Analyze the 1860 electoral map and popular vote.

- **Q:** How did Lincoln win without Southern votes?
  **A:** He carried nearly all the populous free states, giving him an Electoral College majority (180 votes) with about 40% of the popular vote.
- **Q:** Name Lincoln's three opponents.
  **A:** Stephen Douglas, John C. Breckinridge and John Bell.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Secession
*Student:* Read South Carolina's Declaration of the Causes of Secession and identify the main reason it gives.

*How to teach:* Read South Carolina's declaration; identify its main reason.

- **Q:** What main reason does South Carolina give for seceding?
  **A:** Northern states' hostility to slavery — refusing to enforce the Fugitive Slave Act and electing a president whose 'opinions and purposes are hostile to slavery.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 test
*Student:* Unit test on abolition and the road to war.

*How to teach:* Give the unit 7 test.

- **Q:** When did South Carolina secede?
  **A:** December 20, 1860.

**Assessment:** Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 7 test (suggested): abolition leaders and strategies; Missouri Compromise; Wilmot Proviso; Compromise of 1850; Fugitive Slave Act; Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852); Kansas-Nebraska Act; Dred Scott; Lincoln–Douglas; nullification — Calhoun argued a state could void a federal law (Tariff of 1828/1832); Webster: 'Liberty and Union, now and forever, one and inseparable!'; crisis resolved 1833 with Force Bill and compromise tariff; John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry (October 1859), hanged December 1859 — martyr in the North, proof of Northern aggression in the South; election of 1860 — Lincoln (Republican), Douglas (Northern Democrat), Breckinridge (Southern Democrat), Bell (Constitutional Union); Lincoln not on the ballot in most Southern states; South Carolina seceded December 20, 1860, citing Northern hostility to slavery, nonenforcement of the Fugitive Slave Act and election of a president opposed to slavery.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Watching and responding to theatre

**Prepare:** A recorded production (PBS Great Performances or a free filmed school production — preview for content). Note sheet: set, costume, lighting, sound, acting.

**Watch for:**
- Reviews that only summarize the plot. Correct: evaluate choices with evidence.
- Saying 'it was good/bad' without reasons. Correct: explain why with specific moments.

### Day 1 (Monday): Watch part 1
*Student:* Watch the first half of a recorded production and note design choices.

*How to teach:* Watch part 1; note design choices.

- **Q:** What does the set tell the audience?
  **A:** Time, place, mood and sometimes character's status.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Watch part 2
*Student:* Finish the production and note acting choices.

*How to teach:* Finish; note acting choices.

- **Q:** Give an example of an acting choice to note.
  **A:** A change in voice, a pause, a gesture or movement that revealed character.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Analyzing choices
*Student:* Analyze how set, costume and lighting support the story.

*How to teach:* Analyze how set, costume and lighting support the story.

- **Q:** How can lighting change mood?
  **A:** Warm, bright light can feel happy; cool, dim or harsh light can feel tense or sad; spotlights focus attention.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Review writing
*Student:* Write a 300-word theatre review with evidence.

*How to teach:* Write a 300-word review with evidence.

- **Q:** What does a strong review include?
  **A:** Brief context, analysis of acting and design with specific examples, an overall evaluation with reasons.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Share the review and discuss.

*How to teach:* Share and discuss.

- **Q:** How is a review different from a summary?
  **A:** A review evaluates and gives opinions supported by evidence; a summary retells events.

**Assessment:** Theatre review.

**Answer key:** Theatre review: 4 = ~300 words, brief context, analyzes acting and at least two design elements with specific moments, clear supported evaluation, organized paragraphs; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = mostly summary; 1 = incomplete.

## Physical Education 8: Student-designed game

**Prepare:** Any available equipment; game-design template (name, objective, players, equipment, area diagram, rules, scoring, safety, modifications).

**Watch for:**
- Too many rules. Correct: start simple; add rules only if play-testing shows a need.
- Forgetting safety. Correct: include boundaries, equipment checks and contact rules.

### Day 1 (Monday): Design
*Student:* Design a game combining elements of two game categories (e.g., target + invasion); write rules and scoring.

*How to teach:* Combine two categories; write rules and scoring.

- **Q:** What game categories exist?
  **A:** Invasion, net/wall, striking/fielding, target.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Safety
*Student:* Add safety rules and equipment checks; draw the playing area.

*How to teach:* Add safety rules; draw the area.

- **Q:** Give two safety rules for a game you design.
  **A:** No contact above the waist; boundaries 3 m from walls; soft balls only.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Test
*Student:* Play-test with family or friends and note problems.

*How to teach:* Play-test and note problems.

- **Q:** Why play-test?
  **A:** To find unclear rules, unfair scoring or unsafe parts before finalizing.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Refine
*Student:* Revise rules based on the play-test and add a modification for younger players.

*How to teach:* Revise rules; add modification.

- **Q:** Give an example modification for younger players.
  **A:** Larger target, closer distances, softer ball, more players per side.

### Day 5 (Friday): Present
*Student:* Teach and play the final version; submit the written rules.

*How to teach:* Teach and play the final version; submit rules.

- **Q:** What makes a game fun and fair?
  **A:** Everyone is active, rules are clear, scoring is balanced and teams are even.

**Assessment:** Written game rules and teaching session.

**Answer key:** Game rules and teaching rubric: 4 = clear objective, rules, scoring, diagram, safety rules, modification, evidence of revision after play-test, taught clearly with demo; 3 = one element weak; 2 = missing safety or unclear rules; 1 = incomplete.

## Health Education 8: Addiction, treatment and recovery

**Prepare:** Read NIDA for Teens 'Drugs and the Brain' and a recovery story (NIDA, SAMHSA or a reputable news profile). Health journal ready.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking addiction is a lack of willpower. Correct: it is a chronic, treatable brain disease involving changes in reward, motivation and self-control circuits.
- Believing relapse means treatment failed. Correct: relapse rates are similar to other chronic diseases like asthma; it signals a need to adjust treatment.

### Day 1 (Monday): Brain changes
*Student:* Review how dopamine and tolerance lead to addiction.

*How to teach:* Explain dopamine and the reward system, tolerance (needing more for the same effect) and dependence/withdrawal.

- **Q:** What is tolerance?
  **A:** When the body adapts so a person needs more of a drug to get the same effect.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Disease model
*Student:* Learn why addiction is considered a chronic, treatable disease.

*How to teach:* Explain why addiction is called a chronic disease: lasting brain changes, compulsive use despite harm, can be managed but relapse is possible.

- **Q:** Why is addiction called a disease rather than a choice?
  **A:** Repeated use changes brain circuits for reward, stress and self-control, making it very hard to stop even when the person wants to; the first use is a choice, but addiction is not.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Treatment
*Student:* Research types of treatment (counseling, medication, support groups).

*How to teach:* The child researches treatments: counseling/behavioral therapy, medications (e.g., buprenorphine, methadone, naltrexone for opioids), support groups, inpatient/outpatient.

- **Q:** Name two types of addiction treatment.
  **A:** Behavioral counseling (e.g., CBT), medication for opioid use disorder, support groups (12-step, SMART Recovery), residential or outpatient programs.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Recovery stories
*Student:* Read or watch a recovery story and identify supports that helped.

*How to teach:* Read or watch a recovery story and list supports that helped (family, treatment, new friends, purpose).

- **Q:** What supports help people stay in recovery?
  **A:** Ongoing treatment, supportive family and friends, sober activities, support groups, coping skills and purpose.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining addiction to a younger student.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph explaining addiction to a younger student in simple words.

- **Q:** What's a simple way to explain addiction?
  **A:** 'Drugs can change how your brain works so it feels like you need them, even when they hurt you. It's a sickness that doctors can help treat.'

**Assessment:** Explanatory paragraph.

**Answer key:** Explanatory paragraph: 4 = explains dopamine/reward and tolerance in simple terms, calls addiction a treatable disease, mentions treatment and where to get help, age-appropriate; 3 = accurate with one gap; 2 = mostly 'just say no' without explanation; 1 = inaccurate.
