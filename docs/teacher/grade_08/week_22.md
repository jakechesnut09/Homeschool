# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 22

## English Language Arts 8: Narrative poetry, recitation and original poems

**Prepare:** Print Longfellow's 'Paul Revere's Ride' and Poetry Out Loud recitation tips (poetryoutloud.org). Find a brief historical account of April 18–19, 1775.

**Watch for:**
- Believing the poem is accurate history. Longfellow wrote it in 1860 to inspire unity; Revere did not ride alone, did not wait for the lantern signal himself, and was captured before reaching Concord.
- Reciting too fast. Mark pauses at punctuation, not at line breaks (enjambment).

### Day 1 (Monday): 'Paul Revere's Ride'
*Student:* Read the poem and compare Longfellow's version with the historical record of April 18, 1775.

*How to teach:* Read 'Paul Revere's Ride' aloud for its galloping rhythm. Then read a short historical account of April 18–19, 1775, and the child lists three differences between poem and history.

- **Q:** What does 'One if by land, and two if by sea' mean?
  **A:** Lanterns hung in the Old North Church tower would signal whether the British were coming by land or by water (across the Charles River).
- **Q:** Name two differences from history.
  **A:** Revere arranged the signal for others and was rowed across; William Dawes and Samuel Prescott also rode; Revere was captured and never reached Concord (Prescott did).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Choosing a recitation
*Student:* Choose and memorize a poem from the unit; mark pauses and emphasis.

*How to teach:* The child chooses a poem from the unit (or Poetry Out Loud's anthology) and marks a copy with slashes for pauses and underlines for emphasis. Memorize a stanza at a time by reading aloud, covering, and reciting.

- **Q:** What is enjambment?
  **A:** A sentence continuing past the end of a line without punctuation — don't pause there.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Writing poems
*Student:* Write two original poems: one free verse catalog in Whitman's style and one with a rhyme scheme and refrain.

*How to teach:* Show a few lines of Whitman's catalog and a rhymed stanza with a refrain as models. The child drafts one free-verse catalog poem and one poem with a rhyme scheme and a refrain.

- **Q:** What makes Whitman's style a 'catalog'?
  **A:** A long list of specific people, things or images, often with repeated openings.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Recitation
*Student:* Recite the poem for family, focusing on volume, pacing and eye contact.

*How to teach:* The child recites the memorized poem for family. Listeners give one specific compliment and one suggestion on volume, pacing or eye contact.

- **Q:** Name three recitation qualities.
  **A:** Volume, pacing, eye contact (also articulation and accuracy).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 test
*Student:* Unit test with an unfamiliar poem and a portfolio of original poems.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test on an unfamiliar poem; collect the portfolio. Before moving on, ask: "Define narrative poem." and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Define narrative poem.
  **A:** A poem that tells a story with characters and plot.

**Assessment:** Recitation rubric, original poems and Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Recitation rubric (Poetry Out Loud style, 4 levels): physical presence, voice and articulation, dramatic appropriateness, accuracy (few or no missed words). Original poems: free-verse catalog uses repetition and a list of specific images; rhymed poem has a consistent scheme and a refrain. Unit 5 test: answers depend on the chosen poem; key items — speaker, form, rhyme scheme, two figurative devices with effects, and theme statement.

## Mathematics 8: Scatter plots and patterns

**Prepare:** Tape measure; recruit 8 people for arm-span/height (or use a provided dataset); graph paper. Find or make a car age vs value dataset.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking an association proves cause. Arm span doesn't cause height; both relate to body size.
- Choosing axis scales that start at 0 and squash the data. Start the axes near the smallest values and mark a break.

### Day 1 (Monday): Collecting bivariate data
*Student:* Measure arm span and height of at least 8 people (or use a provided data set) and organize in a table.

*How to teach:* Measure arm span and height (fingertip to fingertip, arms straight) of 8+ people and record in a table with units. Before moving on, ask: "Which variable should go on the x-axis and why?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Which variable should go on the x-axis and why?
  **A:** Arm span, if we're using it to predict height — the explanatory variable goes on x.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Making scatter plots
*Student:* Plot arm span vs height by hand with appropriate scales; describe the pattern.

*How to teach:* Child chooses scales and plots by hand; describe the pattern. Check understanding with: "What pattern do you expect for arm span vs height?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** What pattern do you expect for arm span vs height?
  **A:** A positive, roughly linear association — arm span is close to height for most people.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Describing association
*Student:* Classify 8 scatter plots as positive, negative, or no association and linear or nonlinear; identify outliers and clusters.

*How to teach:* Child classifies 8 scatter plots by direction (positive/negative/none) and form (linear/nonlinear), marking outliers and clusters. To check, ask: "What association do you expect between a car's age and its value?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What association do you expect between a car's age and its value?
  **A:** Negative — older cars are generally worth less.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Interpreting outliers
*Student:* Explain possible reasons for outliers in a car age vs value data set.

*How to teach:* Discuss the car data's outliers and possible reasons. Finish by asking: "An old car worth much more than others its age is an outlier. Give a reason." — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** An old car worth much more than others its age is an outlier. Give a reason.
  **A:** It may be a classic or collectible car, or exceptionally well kept, so its value rose instead of falling.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: build and describe a scatter plot from a 12-row data table.

*How to teach:* Quiz: child builds and describes a scatter plot from a 12-row table. Review & check day: have the child redo any missed problem in the notebook and explain the error in one sentence.

- **Q:** What does 'no association' look like on a scatter plot?
  **A:** Points scattered with no upward or downward trend.

**Assessment:** Scatter plot project and quiz.

**Answer key:** Project rubric: 4 = labeled axes with units and consistent scales, all points plotted accurately, correct description (direction, form, strength, outliers/clusters); 3 = minor plotting or labeling errors; 2 = plot present but description missing or wrong; 1 = incomplete. Quiz on the 12-row table: accept the description that matches the data, e.g., 'strong positive linear association; one outlier at (x, y).'

## Science 8 (Integrated): Patterns in the fossil record

**Prepare:** Clay, a shell, plaster or glue; graph paper; marine family diversity data; whale fossil images (Pakicetus, Ambulocetus, Rodhocetus).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking every organism becomes a fossil. Fossilization is rare; it requires rapid burial and usually hard parts.
- Thinking 'transitional fossil' means a direct ancestor. It shows a mix of traits linking groups.

### Day 1 (Monday): How fossils form
*Student:* Make a shell impression in clay and a 'cast' with plaster or glue; explain fossilization conditions.

*How to teach:* Make a shell impression in clay and a cast. Before moving on, ask: "What conditions help fossilization?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** What conditions help fossilization?
  **A:** Rapid burial in sediment, hard parts, low oxygen.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Diversity data
*Student:* Graph number of marine animal families over time and identify mass extinctions.

*How to teach:* Graph marine families over time. Check understanding with: "What does a sharp drop indicate?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** What does a sharp drop indicate?
  **A:** A mass extinction.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Whale ancestors
*Student:* Analyze fossils of Pakicetus, Ambulocetus and Rodhocetus in order and describe changes in limbs and habitat.

*How to teach:* Analyze the three whale ancestors in order. To check, ask: "Describe the change from Pakicetus to Rodhocetus." and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Describe the change from Pakicetus to Rodhocetus.
  **A:** From a four-legged land animal to an amphibious one with shorter limbs, more adapted to water.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fossil gallery
*Student:* Explore a natural history museum virtual collection and record 5 fossils with age and significance.

*How to teach:* Explore a museum virtual collection; record 5 fossils. Finish by asking: "What is the fossil record?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** What is the fossil record?
  **A:** All known fossils and their positions in rock layers.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz interpreting fossil data tables.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Before moving on, ask: "Why are soft-bodied fossils rare?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Why are soft-bodied fossils rare?
  **A:** Soft tissue decays quickly.

**Assessment:** Fossil data analysis quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: interpret a fossil data table — e.g., sharp drops in marine families mark mass extinctions; diversity recovers and generally increases afterward; whale sequence shows hind limbs shrinking and adaptation to water over about 50–40 million years ago.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: Bleeding Kansas, Dred Scott and the rise of Lincoln

**Prepare:** Map of the Kansas-Nebraska Act; Dred Scott decision excerpts (Chief Justice Taney, 1857); Lincoln's 'House Divided' speech (June 1858); Lincoln–Douglas debate excerpts.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Lincoln was an abolitionist in 1858. He opposed slavery's expansion into the territories, not immediate abolition where it existed.
- Thinking Dred Scott only affected one man. It declared the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional and said Black Americans could not be citizens.

### Day 1 (Monday): Kansas-Nebraska Act
*Student:* Map the act and explain how popular sovereignty undid the Missouri Compromise and led to violence in Kansas.

*How to teach:* Map the Kansas-Nebraska Act; explain how popular sovereignty undid the Missouri Compromise.

- **Q:** Why did the Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) cause violence?
  **A:** It let settlers vote on slavery in territories north of 36°30', so proslavery and antislavery settlers rushed into Kansas and fought ('Bleeding Kansas').
- **Q:** Who sponsored the act?
  **A:** Senator Stephen Douglas of Illinois.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dred Scott
*Student:* Read excerpts of Chief Justice Taney's opinion and list the decision's three main holdings and their effects.

*How to teach:* Read Taney's opinion; list three holdings and effects.

- **Q:** What were the Dred Scott decision's three main holdings?
  **A:** 1) Black Americans, free or enslaved, were not citizens and could not sue in federal court; 2) Congress could not ban slavery in the territories, so the Missouri Compromise was unconstitutional; 3) living in free territory did not make Scott free — enslaved people were property protected by the Fifth Amendment.
- **Q:** How did Northerners react?
  **A:** With outrage; it strengthened the new Republican Party.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): A House Divided
*Student:* Close-read Lincoln's 'House Divided' speech and explain its central metaphor in your own words.

*How to teach:* Close-read 'A House Divided'; explain its metaphor.

- **Q:** Explain 'A house divided against itself cannot stand.'
  **A:** The nation could not last half slave and half free; it would eventually become all one or all the other.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Lincoln-Douglas debates
*Student:* Compare Lincoln's and Douglas's positions on slavery's expansion using debate excerpts in a T-chart.

*How to teach:* T-chart Lincoln's and Douglas's positions.

- **Q:** What was Douglas's Freeport Doctrine?
  **A:** Territories could keep slavery out by refusing to pass local laws protecting it, despite Dred Scott.
- **Q:** Who won the 1858 Senate race?
  **A:** Douglas (chosen by the Illinois legislature), but Lincoln gained national fame.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a DBQ paragraph: 'Was the Civil War inevitable by 1858?'

*How to teach:* Write the DBQ paragraph.

- **Q:** What was the Republican Party's main goal in the 1850s?
  **A:** To stop the spread of slavery into the western territories.

**Assessment:** DBQ paragraph.

**Answer key:** DBQ paragraph rubric (1–4): claim (e.g., 'By 1858 compromise had largely failed, making war very likely but not yet certain'); evidence from at least 3 documents — Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) repealed the Missouri line and led to Bleeding Kansas (sack of Lawrence, Pottawatomie killings, 1856); Dred Scott (1857); 'A House Divided' (1858); Lincoln–Douglas debates; analysis of how each pushed the sections apart; counterclaim (Douglas's popular sovereignty and the Freeport Doctrine still offered compromise; secession was not inevitable until 1860).

## Spanish 1B: Destinos del mundo hispano

**Prepare:** World map, library books/websites. A Spanish article about Machu Picchu (e.g., from a tourism or encyclopedia site for students).

**Watch for:**
- Placing Machu Picchu in Mexico or Chichén Itzá in Peru. Machu Picchu (Inca) is in Peru; Chichén Itzá (Maya) is in Mexico's Yucatán.
- Copying research sentences in Spanish verbatim. Take notes in key words and rewrite.

### Day 1 (Monday): Geography
*Student:* Locate eight famous sites (Machu Picchu, Chichén Itzá, Salar de Uyuni, El Yunque, la Alhambra) and identify their landforms.

*How to teach:* Locate eight sites and identify their landforms.

- **Q:** Where is the Salar de Uyuni and what is it?
  **A:** In Bolivia; the world's largest salt flat.
- **Q:** Who built Machu Picchu?
  **A:** The Inca, in the 1400s, in the Andes of Peru.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Reading
*Student:* Read an article in Spanish about Machu Picchu and answer comprehension questions.

*How to teach:* Read the Machu Picchu article; answer questions.

- **Q:** What does 'las ruinas' mean?
  **A:** The ruins.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Research
*Student:* Research one destination's history, landforms, food, and festivals and take notes in Spanish.

*How to teach:* Research one destination; take notes in Spanish.

- **Q:** Write: 'Chichén Itzá is in Mexico; it has a famous pyramid.'
  **A:** Chichén Itzá está en México; tiene una pirámide famosa.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparación
*Student:* Compare your destination with a California landmark (Yosemite, Death Valley) in six sentences.

*How to teach:* Compare with a California landmark in six sentences.

- **Q:** Write: 'Death Valley is hotter than Machu Picchu.'
  **A:** El Valle de la Muerte es más caluroso que Machu Picchu (or hace más calor en el Valle de la Muerte que en Machu Picchu).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Share research findings orally in a 1-minute summary.

*How to teach:* Give a 1-minute oral summary.

- **Q:** What is 'el patrimonio' (as in Patrimonio de la Humanidad)?
  **A:** Heritage — UNESCO World Heritage Site.

**Assessment:** Research notes check and oral summary.

**Answer key:** Site key: Machu Picchu — Inca citadel (15th century) high in the Andes of Peru near Cusco, mountains; Chichén Itzá — Maya city with the pyramid El Castillo (Temple of Kukulcán), Yucatán Peninsula, Mexico, flat limestone plain with cenotes; Salar de Uyuni — world's largest salt flat, Bolivia, high plateau (Altiplano); El Yunque — tropical rainforest, Puerto Rico; La Alhambra — Moorish palace-fortress in Granada, Spain, on a hill below the Sierra Nevada. Oral summary rubric: 1 minute, 5+ facts in Spanish (location, landform, history, food, festival), pronunciation.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Scene study

**Prepare:** A two-person scene script (public domain or written for the course); a family scene partner; simple props and costume pieces; recording device. Stage-direction diagram (upstage/downstage, stage left/right from the actor's view).

**Watch for:**
- Confusing stage left/right. Correct: they are from the actor's perspective facing the audience.
- Waiting for your line instead of listening. Correct: react truthfully to the partner.

### Day 1 (Monday): Read-through
*Student:* Read a two-person scene and discuss characters.

*How to teach:* Read through; discuss characters.

- **Q:** What questions should you ask about your character?
  **A:** Who am I? What do I want? What's in my way? What's my relationship to the other character?

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Blocking
*Student:* Block the scene using stage directions.

*How to teach:* Block with stage directions.

- **Q:** Where is downstage?
  **A:** Toward the audience.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rehearsal
*Student:* Rehearse focusing on listening and reacting.

*How to teach:* Rehearse listening and reacting.

- **Q:** Why does listening matter in acting?
  **A:** Real reactions make the scene believable and keep it alive.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Polish
*Student:* Polish with props and costume pieces.

*How to teach:* Add props and costume pieces.

- **Q:** Why rehearse with props early?
  **A:** So handling them becomes natural and doesn't interrupt the scene.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform the scene and record it.

*How to teach:* Perform and record.

- **Q:** What does 'cheat out' mean?
  **A:** Angle the body toward the audience so faces and voices can be seen and heard.

**Assessment:** Scene performance video.

**Answer key:** Scene video rubric: Memorization, Blocking (uses stage space, opens to audience), Listening/reacting, Voice (projection, diction), Character choices. 4 levels each; proficient = 3+.

## Physical Education 8: Cricket and kickball strategy

**Prepare:** Flat bat or paddle, tennis ball, cones/bucket as a wicket, kickball. Print simple cricket rules.

**Watch for:**
- Bending the elbow when bowling in cricket. Correct: the arm must stay straight (that's a 'throw' if bent).
- Thinking cricket batters must run on every hit. Correct: they run only when it's safe.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cricket intro
*Student:* Learn cricket's basic rules and bowl a straight-arm underhand-to-overhand delivery.

*How to teach:* Teach basics: two teams; bowler delivers to batter defending the wicket; runs scored by running between wickets; out if bowled, caught, run out. Practice straight-arm bowling.

- **Q:** Name two ways a cricket batter gets out.
  **A:** Bowled (ball hits wicket), caught (fielder catches before bounce), run out, LBW (leg before wicket), stumped.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cricket batting
*Student:* Practice defensive and drive strokes to protect the wicket.

*How to teach:* Defensive and drive strokes.

- **Q:** When would a batter play a defensive stroke?
  **A:** When the ball is aimed at the wicket and hard to hit safely — block it to protect the wicket.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fielding
*Student:* Practice fielding positions and backing up in kickball and cricket.

*How to teach:* Fielding positions and backing up.

- **Q:** What is backing up?
  **A:** A fielder positions behind a teammate receiving a throw to stop the ball if it's missed.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Game
*Student:* Play a modified cricket or kickball game, rotating batting order.

*How to teach:* Play a modified game, rotating batting order.

- **Q:** How is a run scored in cricket?
  **A:** Both batters cross and reach the opposite crease safely (4 runs if the ball reaches the boundary on the ground, 6 if over it in the air).

### Day 5 (Friday): Compare
*Student:* Compare strategy in cricket, softball and kickball (striking/fielding games).

*How to teach:* Compare striking/fielding strategy.

- **Q:** One strategy shared by cricket, softball and kickball?
  **A:** Hit to open space where fielders aren't; fielders position to cover likely hits and back up throws.

**Assessment:** Striking/fielding comparison chart.

**Answer key:** Comparison chart: 4 = compares cricket, softball and kickball on scoring, outs, field positions and strategy (placing hits, base running/running between wickets, fielding positioning) with accurate details; 3 = minor errors; 2 = partial; 1 = incomplete.

## Health Education 8: Youth-targeted marketing

**Prepare:** Find 2–3 online examples of vape, alcohol or cannabis ads (or Truth Initiative examples). Print a media-literacy checklist (who made it, target audience, techniques, what's left out). Read about California's flavored tobacco ban (SB 793, upheld by voters with Prop 31 in 2022).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking ads only target adults. Correct: flavors, bright packaging, cartoons and influencers appeal to youth, who are 'replacement customers.'
- Believing vapes are just water vapor. Correct: aerosol contains nicotine, heavy metals and ultrafine particles.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tactics
*Student:* Identify marketing tactics (flavors, influencers, packaging, sponsorships) aimed at youth.

*How to teach:* List tactics: flavors (candy, fruit), influencer posts, sleek packaging, sponsorships of events/music, small discreet devices, 'free' samples.

- **Q:** Why do companies use sweet flavors?
  **A:** Flavors hide the harsh taste and appeal especially to young people, making it easier to start.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Analyze
*Student:* Analyze two ads using a media-literacy checklist.

*How to teach:* Analyze two ads with the checklist and write findings.

- **Q:** What does an alcohol ad usually leave out?
  **A:** Risks: hangovers, impaired driving, addiction, health problems, violence, and the legal age.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Industry
*Student:* Learn about California's flavored tobacco ban and why it passed.

*How to teach:* Explain the flavored tobacco ban: California prohibits retail sale of most flavored tobacco products, including menthol and flavored vapes, approved by voters (Prop 31) in 2022.

- **Q:** Why did California ban flavored tobacco sales?
  **A:** Most youth who use tobacco start with flavored products; the ban aims to reduce youth vaping and smoking.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Counter-ad
*Student:* Design a counter-ad that exposes one tactic.

*How to teach:* The child sketches a counter-ad that exposes one tactic (e.g., 'They flavor it like candy because they want kids hooked').

- **Q:** What makes a counter-ad effective?
  **A:** It reveals the hidden tactic or truth, targets the audience clearly and uses a strong image and short message.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Present the counter-ad to a parent and explain the tactic.

*How to teach:* The child presents the counter-ad and explains the tactic and evidence.

- **Q:** Who are 'replacement smokers'?
  **A:** Young people the industry needs to recruit to replace customers who quit or die.

**Assessment:** Counter-ad.

**Answer key:** Counter-ad rubric: 4 = names a specific tactic, accurate fact, clear target audience, strong visual and slogan, explained confidently; 3 = tactic and message clear, weaker design; 2 = general anti-drug message without tactic; 1 = incomplete.
