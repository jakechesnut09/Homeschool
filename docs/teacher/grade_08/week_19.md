# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 19

## English Language Arts 8: Informative essay on Douglass

**Prepare:** Narrative, informative essay template. Prepare the Unit 4 test from the questions in the key.

**Watch for:**
- Arguing instead of informing. An informative essay explains a topic with evidence; it does not push the reader to act.
- Paraphrasing too closely (swapping a few words). Read, cover the source, write from memory, then check and cite.

### Day 1 (Monday): Socratic seminar
*Student:* Discuss 'Why was reading so dangerous to slaveholders?' citing at least three passages.

*How to teach:* Hold a seminar: 'Why was reading so dangerous to slaveholders?' Require the child to cite at least three passages by chapter or page, and push back with 'Where does it say that?' Check understanding with: "What is a strong answer?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** What is a strong answer?
  **A:** Reading let enslaved people learn about rights and abolition (The Columbian Orator; the word 'abolition'), think critically about slavery, and potentially forge passes; Mr. Auld said it would 'unfit' a slave — knowledge undermined control.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plan
*Student:* Outline an informative essay with a controlling idea, three subtopics and evidence for each.

*How to teach:* Model a controlling idea (not a claim). Child outlines three subtopics with evidence for each.

- **Q:** Give an example controlling idea.
  **A:** 'In the Narrative, Douglass shows that literacy was both his path to freedom and a source of painful awareness.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft
*Student:* Draft the essay, integrating quotations and paraphrases with parenthetical page citations.

*How to teach:* Child drafts, mixing direct quotations with paraphrases and adding page citations. Finish by asking: "Format a parenthetical citation for a quotation on page 37." — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Format a parenthetical citation for a quotation on page 37.
  **A:** ..."the pathway from slavery to freedom" (Douglass 37). [page varies by edition]

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revise & edit
*Student:* Revise for precise vocabulary and varied sentence openings; edit for punctuation of quotations.

*How to teach:* Revise for precise vocabulary and varied sentence openings; edit quotation punctuation. Before moving on, ask: "Fix: Douglass said 'I now understood'." and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Fix: Douglass said "I now understood".
  **A:** Douglass wrote, "I now understood...." — introduce with a comma and put end punctuation inside the quotation marks.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 test
*Student:* Unit test on the Narrative and submit the final essay.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 4 test and collect the final essay. Check understanding with: "Who first taught Douglass the alphabet?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Who first taught Douglass the alphabet?
  **A:** Sophia Auld, his mistress in Baltimore, until her husband stopped her.

**Assessment:** Informative essay (rubric) and Unit 4 test.

**Answer key:** Informative essay rubric (4 levels per trait): controlling idea; three subtopics each with quotation and paraphrase cited by page; precise vocabulary; varied sentence openings; correctly punctuated quotations. Unit 4 test answers: 1) Purpose: expose slavery's cruelty and support abolition; audience: Northern white readers. 2) Garrison wrote the preface to vouch for authenticity. 3) Mr. Auld said reading would 'forever unfit him to be a slave' — Douglass saw 'the pathway from slavery to freedom.' 4) He learned by trading bread for lessons, The Columbian Orator, ship-timber letters and copybooks. 5) The fight with Covey was the turning point. 6) He hides escape details to protect helpers. 7) Short analysis of tone in the grandmother passage (bitter irony).

## Mathematics 8: Linear functions, rates of change, and graphs of situations

**Prepare:** Graph paper, Desmos. Draw 6 story graphs (distance vs time) for Tuesday.

**Watch for:**
- Reading a distance-time graph as a picture of the path (e.g., thinking a hill shape means riding up a hill). The graph shows distance vs time, not the route.
- Forgetting that horizontal segments mean 'no change' (stopped), not 'moving at constant speed.'

### Day 1 (Monday): Linear function models
*Student:* Build functions for situations such as a phone battery draining 8% per hour from 100%; interpret rate and initial value.

*How to teach:* Build the battery model: start 100%, −8% per hour. Ask what the rate and initial value mean.

- **Q:** Write the function and find when the battery reaches 0%.
  **A:** B = 100 − 8h; 0 = 100 − 8h → h = 12.5 hours.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Qualitative graphs
*Student:* Match 6 stories (a bike ride with a stop, a filling bathtub) to graphs and describe where they increase, decrease or stay constant.

*How to teach:* Child matches 6 stories to graphs and labels intervals as increasing, decreasing or constant. To check, ask: "On a distance-from-home vs time graph, what does a horizontal segment mean?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** On a distance-from-home vs time graph, what does a horizontal segment mean?
  **A:** The rider is stopped — distance is not changing.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sketching graphs
*Student:* Sketch graphs from verbal descriptions and write a story for 3 given graphs.

*How to teach:* Child sketches graphs from descriptions and writes a story for 3 given graphs. Finish by asking: "Sketch water level in a bathtub that fills, is used for a bath, then drains." — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Sketch water level in a bathtub that fills, is used for a bath, then drains.
  **A:** Rises (increasing), then roughly flat (constant) with a small jump when the person gets in, then falls to 0 (decreasing).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Piecewise linear functions
*Student:* Analyze a graph of a water tank filled and drained in stages; find the rate of change for each piece.

*How to teach:* Analyze the water-tank graph piece by piece, computing each rate of change. Before moving on, ask: "A tank goes from 0 to 60 gallons in 4 minutes, stays full until minute 6, then empties by minute 10. Find each rate." and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** A tank goes from 0 to 60 gallons in 4 minutes, stays full until minute 6, then empties by minute 10. Find each rate.
  **A:** 15 gal/min, 0 gal/min, and −15 gal/min.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on modeling with linear functions and interpreting qualitative graphs.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Review & check day: have the child redo any missed problem in the notebook and explain the error in one sentence.

- **Q:** A function's graph is a line falling from (0, 30) to (6, 0). Find the rate of change.
  **A:** −30/6 = −5 per unit.

**Assessment:** Quiz on function models and graph interpretation.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Battery at 100% drains 8% per hour → B = 100 − 8h; empty at 12.5 h. 2) Match a bike ride with a 10-minute stop → graph with a flat segment. 3) Tank fills 0→60 gal in 4 min (15 gal/min), stays constant 4–6 min (0), drains 60→0 in 6–10 min (−15 gal/min). 4) Describe where a given graph increases/decreases/is constant. 5) Write a story for a given graph (rubric: matches every segment, mentions rates). 1–3 points each.

## Science 8 (Integrated): Analog vs digital signals

**Prepare:** Flashlight, paper, pencil, phone. Prepare the Unit 4 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking digital is always higher quality. Digital is more reliable to copy and transmit, but quality depends on sampling rate and compression.
- Thinking Wi-Fi and cell signals are sound waves. They are electromagnetic (radio) waves.

### Day 1 (Monday): Encoding messages
*Student:* Send a message to a family member using flashlight blinks in binary code; discuss errors.

*How to teach:* Agree on a binary code (e.g., short blink = 0, long blink = 1); send a 3-letter message with a flashlight. Check understanding with: "What number is 101 in binary?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** What number is 101 in binary?
  **A:** 5.
- **Q:** What caused errors?
  **A:** Noise — mistiming, missed blinks.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Analog vs digital
*Student:* Read about records, cassettes and digital audio; list advantages of each in a T-chart.

*How to teach:* Read about records, cassettes and digital audio; make a T-chart. To check, ask: "Advantage of digital?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Advantage of digital?
  **A:** Copies without degradation; easy to store, compress, transmit.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Telephone game
*Student:* Play 'telephone' with a spoken message vs a written numeric code to model noise in analog vs digital transmission.

*How to teach:* Play telephone with a spoken message vs a numeric code. Finish by asking: "Why did the code survive better?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Why did the code survive better?
  **A:** Discrete digits are easier to transmit accurately than continuous speech.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Research reading
*Student:* Read about how cell phones and Wi-Fi use waves to transmit digitized data; summarize in a paragraph.

*How to teach:* Read about cell phones and Wi-Fi; summarize. Before moving on, ask: "What carries cell phone signals?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** What carries cell phone signals?
  **A:** Radio waves (electromagnetic).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 test
*Student:* Unit test on waves and information technology.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 4 test. Check understanding with: "Analog vs digital?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Analog vs digital?
  **A:** Analog is continuous; digital uses discrete values.

**Assessment:** Unit 4 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 4 test sample answers: label wave parts; v = fλ; sound needs a medium; light reflection/refraction; transparent/translucent/opaque; analog = continuous signal, digital = discrete values (often 0s and 1s); digital signals resist noise because receivers only need to tell 0 from 1; binary 101 = 5; cell phones use radio waves to carry digitized data.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: The Mexican-American War and the Gold Rush

**Prepare:** Polk's war message (May 1846) excerpt; Lincoln's 'spot resolutions' (December 1847); Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo Articles 8–9; Calisphere Gold Rush photos; a forty-niner's letter; a short article on the war's legacy for Mexican Americans. Write the unit 6 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the border was clearly the Rio Grande. Mexico considered the Nueces River the boundary; the land between was disputed, which is why Lincoln demanded the 'spot.'
- Assuming treaty promises to Mexican residents were fully kept. Many Californios lost land through the 1851 Land Act's costly title challenges, squatters and debt.

### Day 1 (Monday): Causes of war
*Student:* Compare President Polk's 1846 war message with Lincoln's 'spot resolutions' and decide whose claim is better supported.

*How to teach:* Compare Polk's war message and Lincoln's spot resolutions.

- **Q:** What was Polk's claim?
  **A:** Mexico had 'invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil.'
- **Q:** What did Lincoln demand?
  **A:** That Polk identify the exact 'spot' where blood was shed, implying it was Mexican or disputed land, not U.S. soil.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo
*Student:* Read Articles 8 and 9 and evaluate whether promises of property and citizenship to Mexican residents were kept.

*How to teach:* Read Articles 8 and 9; evaluate whether promises were kept.

- **Q:** What did Article 8 promise?
  **A:** Mexicans living in the ceded lands could stay or leave, keep their property ('inviolably respected'), and choose U.S. citizenship.
- **Q:** Were these promises kept in California?
  **A:** Only partly — the Land Act of 1851 forced Californios to prove titles in long, expensive court cases; many lost land to lawyers' fees, squatters and debt.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): The Gold Rush
*Student:* Read a forty-niner's letter home and examine Calisphere photos; chart impacts on Native Californians, Californios and Chinese miners.

*How to teach:* Read a forty-niner's letter; examine photos; chart impacts on Native Californians, Californios and Chinese miners.

- **Q:** How did the Gold Rush affect Native Californians?
  **A:** Their population collapsed from disease, starvation, displacement and state-sponsored violence; many were forced into labor.
- **Q:** How did the Foreign Miners' Tax affect Chinese miners?
  **A:** It charged foreign miners monthly fees (1852 version aimed at Chinese), pushing them out of mining or into poorer claims.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Legacy today
*Student:* Read a short article on the war's effects on Mexican Americans today and write a reflection connecting past and present.

*How to teach:* Read about the war's legacy for Mexican Americans; reflect.

- **Q:** What does the saying 'We didn't cross the border; the border crossed us' mean?
  **A:** Many Mexican families became U.S. residents in 1848 because the border moved, not because they migrated.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 6 test
*Student:* Unit test on Jacksonian democracy and westward expansion.

*How to teach:* Give the unit 6 test.

- **Q:** What was the Gadsden Purchase?
  **A:** The 1853 U.S. purchase of land in southern Arizona and New Mexico from Mexico, for a southern railroad route.

**Assessment:** Unit 6 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 6 test (suggested): Jacksonian democracy and spoils; Bank War; Indian Removal Act (1830); Worcester v. Georgia; Trail of Tears; Manifest Destiny; trails; Mexican California; Texas independence and annexation (1845); Mexican-American War (1846–1848): Polk claimed Mexico 'shed American blood upon the American soil'; Lincoln's spot resolutions challenged that; Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) — Mexico ceded about half its territory (California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, parts of Colorado and Wyoming) for $15 million, Rio Grande border; Articles 8–9 promised Mexicans could stay, keep property, become U.S. citizens; Gadsden Purchase (1853); gold discovered at Sutter's Mill, Coloma (January 1848); Foreign Miners' Tax (1850, $20/month; 1852 version targeted Chinese miners); California's Native population fell from about 150,000 to about 30,000 by 1870 due to disease, starvation and violence; statehood 1850.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Theatre warm-ups and the actor's instrument

**Prepare:** Open space, mirror, a phone for recording. Tongue-twister list: 'red leather, yellow leather,' 'unique New York,' 'toy boat,' 'she sells seashells,' 'Peter Piper.' One-sentence character descriptions on cards.

**Watch for:**
- Speaking loudly by straining the throat. Correct: projection comes from breath support (diaphragm), not shouting.
- Acting only with the face. Correct: the whole body, posture and walk communicate character.

### Day 1 (Monday): Vocal warm-ups
*Student:* Practice breathing, projection and 5 tongue twisters ('red leather, yellow leather'); record before and after.

*How to teach:* Breathing (inhale 4, exhale on 'sss' for 8+), projection to the far wall, five tongue twisters; record before and after.

- **Q:** What supports vocal projection?
  **A:** Deep diaphragmatic breathing, open throat and good posture.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Physical warm-ups
*Student:* Do a mirror exercise and a walk exploring how different characters lead with head, chest or hips.

*How to teach:* Mirror exercise with a partner; character walks leading with head, chest or hips.

- **Q:** What might leading with the chest suggest about a character?
  **A:** Confidence, pride or authority.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tableau
*Student:* Create 3 frozen tableaux showing a scene from a fairy tale and photograph them.

*How to teach:* Three fairy-tale tableaux; photograph.

- **Q:** What is a tableau?
  **A:** A frozen stage picture showing a moment of a story through poses and levels.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Character walk
*Student:* Develop a character's walk, posture and voice from a one-sentence description.

*How to teach:* Develop a walk, posture and voice from one sentence.

- **Q:** Name three tools of the actor's instrument.
  **A:** Voice, body, imagination (also face/expression and concentration).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform a 30-second character introduction using voice and body.

*How to teach:* 30-second character introduction.

- **Q:** How can you show age with the body?
  **A:** Posture, walking speed, gestures, voice pitch and energy.

**Assessment:** Character introduction performance.

**Answer key:** Character introduction rubric: 4 = distinct voice (pitch, pace, volume), clear physicality and walk, stays in character for 30 seconds, projects clearly; 3 = mostly consistent; 2 = breaks character or unclear; 1 = incomplete.

## Physical Education 8: Semester fitness check and plan revision

**Prepare:** Fitness journal, stopwatch, mat, week 1 scores and the SMART goal sheet. Prepare the progress-report template.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a plateau means failure. Correct: plateaus are normal; change the training stimulus (FITT) or allow more recovery.
- Believing more training is always better. Correct: without rest, overtraining causes fatigue, injury and worse performance.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mid-year re-test
*Student:* Retest the two fitness items tied to your SMART goals.

*How to teach:* Retest the two items tied to the SMART goals using the same protocols.

- **Q:** Why use the same test protocol each time?
  **A:** So results are comparable and changes reflect fitness, not testing differences.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Analyze
*Student:* Compare results with week 1 and identify whether you progressed, plateaued or declined.

*How to teach:* Compare with week 1; classify as progressed, plateaued or declined.

- **Q:** Your push-ups went from 15 to 15. What might you change?
  **A:** Increase frequency or intensity (more sets, harder variation), check consistency, sleep and nutrition.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Overtraining
*Student:* Learn signs of overtraining and injury (persistent soreness, fatigue) and how rest prevents them.

*How to teach:* List overtraining signs: persistent soreness, fatigue, poor sleep, irritability, declining performance, elevated resting HR, frequent injuries/illness.

- **Q:** Name three signs of overtraining.
  **A:** Persistent fatigue and soreness, decreased performance, trouble sleeping, moodiness, elevated resting heart rate, frequent injuries or illness.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revise
*Student:* Revise your SMART goals and weekly activity plan for semester 2.

*How to teach:* Revise SMART goals and weekly plan.

- **Q:** How do you revise a goal you already met?
  **A:** Set a new, slightly more challenging target with a new date.

### Day 5 (Friday): Active day
*Student:* Complete a 40-minute activity of your choice combining cardio and strength.

*How to teach:* 40-minute activity combining cardio and strength.

- **Q:** Name the FITT parts.
  **A:** Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type.

**Assessment:** Revised goals and mid-year progress report.

**Answer key:** Progress report: 4 = both retests recorded with comparison to week 1, classified correctly, reasons explained, revised SMART goals with weekly plan; 3 = mostly complete; 2 = scores without analysis; 1 = incomplete.

## Health Education 8: Alcohol, BAC and impaired driving

**Prepare:** Print a BAC factor chart and the CA DMV handbook section on alcohol and driving. Set up a straight tape line on the floor for the impairment activity. Write a 5-question recap quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking coffee, a cold shower or food 'sobers you up.' Correct: only time lowers BAC; the liver removes alcohol at a fixed rate (about one standard drink per hour).
- Believing a person 'seems fine' so they can drive. Correct: judgment and reaction time are impaired before obvious signs appear.

### Day 1 (Monday): Grade 7 recap
*Student:* Review alcohol's effects on the body from grade 7 with a quick quiz.

*How to teach:* Give a quick 5-question recap (alcohol is a depressant; affects brain, liver, heart; slows reaction time; teen brain still developing).

- **Q:** Is alcohol a stimulant or depressant?
  **A:** A depressant — it slows the central nervous system.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): BAC
*Student:* Learn factors affecting BAC (size, sex, food, time) and why the teen brain is more vulnerable.

*How to teach:* Explain BAC is the percent of alcohol in the blood. Go over factors: body size, sex (body water and enzymes), food in stomach, number of drinks and time. Explain the developing prefrontal cortex and hippocampus are more vulnerable.

- **Q:** Why would the same number of drinks give a smaller person a higher BAC?
  **A:** Less body mass and water to dilute the alcohol, so the concentration in the blood is higher.
- **Q:** Which part of the brain, still developing in teens, controls judgment?
  **A:** The prefrontal cortex (develops into the mid-20s).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Impairment
*Student:* Try a coordination task while 'impaired' with drunk-buster-style challenges (walking a line while counting backward).

*How to teach:* Have the child walk the tape line while counting backward by 7s from 100, then try with a spinning or eye-cover challenge (with a spotter). Discuss how divided attention and impaired balance compare to drinking.

- **Q:** What did the activity show about impairment?
  **A:** Balance, coordination and attention worsen together, and you can't fully judge your own impairment.

### Day 4 (Thursday): The law
*Student:* Learn California's zero-tolerance law for drivers under 21 and social host liability.

*How to teach:* Read the DMV section: drivers under 21 may not drive with BAC 0.01% or higher (zero tolerance); the adult limit is 0.08%. Discuss social host liability for adults who give alcohol to minors.

- **Q:** What is California's BAC limit for drivers under 21?
  **A:** 0.01% (zero tolerance); drivers 21+ are illegal at 0.08% or higher. Refusing a chemical test or a BAC of 0.01%+ leads to license suspension.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a plan for what to do if a ride's driver has been drinking.

*How to teach:* The child writes a safe-ride plan: don't get in, call a parent (no-questions-asked agreement), code word, ride-share with parent's account, stay over.

- **Q:** Your ride has been drinking. What do you do?
  **A:** Do not get in; call or text a parent or trusted adult for a ride, use a code word if needed, and stay with sober friends until help arrives.

**Assessment:** Safe ride plan and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) depressant 2) only time lowers BAC 3) factors: weight, sex, food, number of drinks, time 4) prefrontal cortex develops into mid-20s 5) CA under-21 limit 0.01%, adults 0.08%. Safe-ride plan (4): refuses ride, names two alternatives, contact numbers, code word/agreement with parent.
