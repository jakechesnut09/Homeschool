# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 12

## English Language Arts 8: The Declaration of Independence and the Gettysburg Address

**Prepare:** Print the Declaration of Independence and Gettysburg Address (National Archives transcripts).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Declaration's main body is the 'all men are created equal' sentence. Most of the text is the list of grievances.
- Using 'was' in the subjunctive ('If I was king'). Use 'were.'

### Day 1 (Monday): Declaration structure
*Student:* Outline the Declaration's four parts and explain how the second paragraph's sentences build from self-evident truths to the right to revolution.

*How to teach:* Outline the four parts: preamble, statement of rights, grievances, resolution of independence. To check, ask: "What steps does the second paragraph build?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What steps does the second paragraph build?
  **A:** Self-evident truths → governments exist to secure rights with consent of the governed → when government destroys these ends, the people may alter or abolish it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Loaded words
*Student:* List 10 words with strong connotations ('tyranny', 'usurpations', 'merciless') and explain how they shape tone toward the King.

*How to teach:* List 10 loaded words and their effect on tone. Finish by asking: "What does 'usurpations' suggest?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** What does 'usurpations' suggest?
  **A:** Seizing power illegally — the King looks like a criminal tyrant.
- **Q:** Note on 'merciless Indian Savages'?
  **A:** A dehumanizing slur reflecting colonists' prejudice; discuss how loaded language can be propaganda.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Gettysburg Address
*Student:* Close-read the Gettysburg Address, marking parallelism ('we can not dedicate—we can not consecrate') and the past-present-future structure.

*How to teach:* Close-read the Gettysburg Address. Before moving on, ask: "Give the past-present-future structure." and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Give the past-present-future structure.
  **A:** Past: 'Four score and seven years ago' the nation was founded; present: 'Now we are engaged in a great civil war'; future: 'a new birth of freedom.'
- **Q:** Example of parallelism?
  **A:** 'government of the people, by the people, for the people.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: conditional and subjunctive
*Student:* Write 8 sentences using conditional ('would', 'could') and subjunctive ('If I were', 'I insist that he be') moods for persuasive effect.

*How to teach:* Teach conditional and subjunctive; child writes 8 sentences. Check understanding with: "Correct: 'If I was in Congress, I would vote yes.'" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Correct: 'If I was in Congress, I would vote yes.'
  **A:** 'If I were in Congress, I would vote yes.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a short response comparing how each text uses structure to persuade.

*How to teach:* Write the comparison response. To check, ask: "Year of the Gettysburg Address?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Year of the Gettysburg Address?
  **A:** 1863 (November 19).

**Assessment:** Structure comparison response.

**Answer key:** Response rubric (4 levels): 4 = explains that the Declaration persuades by moving logically from principles (rights, consent) to evidence (grievances) to conclusion (independence), and the Gettysburg Address by moving past–present–future and parallelism to rededicate listeners; cites words from each; 3 = both texts, thin evidence; 2 = only one text; 1 = summary only.

## Mathematics 8: Balanced moves and solving equations

**Prepare:** Hanger-diagram worksheet (Illustrative Math style), algebra tiles or paper cutouts; optional balance scale.

**Watch for:**
- Distributing only to the first term: 3(x + 4) = 3x + 4. Draw arrows from the 3 to each term: 3x + 12.
- Sign errors when subtracting a negative term or distributing a negative. Rewrite subtraction as adding the opposite before moving terms.

### Day 1 (Monday): Hanger diagrams
*Student:* Use hanger diagrams to reason about equations like 3x + 1 = x + 7 and record each balanced move.

*How to teach:* Show a hanger with 3 triangles and 1 square on one side, 1 triangle and 7 squares on the other. Remove the same thing from both sides and record each move as an equation.

- **Q:** Solve 3x + 1 = x + 7 using balanced moves.
  **A:** Subtract x: 2x + 1 = 7; subtract 1: 2x = 6; divide by 2: x = 3.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Balanced moves
*Student:* Decide which moves keep equations equivalent; solve 8 two-step and multi-step equations with integers.

*How to teach:* Discuss which moves keep balance (same operation on both sides). Child solves 8 equations.

- **Q:** Solve 5x − 7 = 18.
  **A:** 5x = 25, x = 5.
- **Q:** Solve −3x + 4 = 19.
  **A:** −3x = 15, x = −5.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Distributive property equations
*Student:* Solve equations like 4(x - 3) = 2x + 6 and -2(5 - x) = 3x + 1, showing every step.

*How to teach:* Model distributing to every term, including negatives, then collecting like terms. Child shows every step.

- **Q:** Solve 4(x − 3) = 2x + 6.
  **A:** 4x − 12 = 2x + 6 → 2x = 18 → x = 9.
- **Q:** Solve −2(5 − x) = 3x + 1.
  **A:** −10 + 2x = 3x + 1 → x = −11. Check: −2(16) = −32 and 3(−11) + 1 = −32.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Error analysis
*Student:* Find and correct mistakes in 5 worked solutions; write a rule for each error type (sign errors, distributing only to the first term).

*How to teach:* Child finds and fixes errors in 5 worked solutions and writes a rule for each error type. Check understanding with: "A student wrote 3(x + 4) = 3x + 4. Fix it." If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** A student wrote 3(x + 4) = 3x + 4. Fix it.
  **A:** 3(x + 4) = 3x + 12 — multiply the 3 by both terms.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: solve 8 multi-step equations and check each solution by substitution.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz; require substitution checks. Review & check day: have the child redo any missed problem in the notebook and explain the error in one sentence.

- **Q:** Solve 2(3x − 1) = 4(x + 2).
  **A:** 6x − 2 = 4x + 8 → 2x = 10 → x = 5.

**Assessment:** Quiz on multi-step equations.

**Answer key:** Quiz (check each by substitution): 1) 2x + 5 = 17 → x = 6. 2) −4x + 3 = −13 → x = 4. 3) 3(x + 2) = 24 → x = 6. 4) 5x − 2 = 2x + 10 → x = 4. 5) −2(x − 4) = 14 → x = −3. 6) 6x − (2x + 3) = 13 → x = 4. 7) 7 − 3x = 2x − 8 → x = 3. 8) 2(3x − 1) = 4(x + 2) → x = 5. 80% = 7/8 with checks shown.

## Science 8 (Integrated): Seasons

**Prepare:** Flashlight, graph paper, a globe or ball with an axis, a lamp, sunrise/sunset data for your city and a Southern Hemisphere city (timeanddate.com).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking seasons are caused by Earth's distance from the Sun. Earth is closest in early January — during Northern Hemisphere winter.
- Thinking the tilt changes direction. The axis stays pointed toward the North Star as Earth orbits.

### Day 1 (Monday): Misconceptions
*Student:* Write what causes seasons; examine Earth-Sun distance data showing Earth is closest to the Sun in January.

*How to teach:* The child writes what causes seasons, then examines Earth-Sun distance data. To check, ask: "When is Earth closest to the Sun?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** When is Earth closest to the Sun?
  **A:** Early January (about 147 million km).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Light angle lab
*Student:* Shine a flashlight straight down and at an angle on graph paper; count squares lit to compare energy concentration.

*How to teach:* Shine the flashlight straight down and at an angle onto graph paper; count lit squares. Finish by asking: "Why is angled light weaker?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Why is angled light weaker?
  **A:** The same energy spreads over a larger area.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Day-length data
*Student:* Graph monthly day length for your city and a city in the Southern Hemisphere; describe patterns.

*How to teach:* Graph day length for both cities. Before moving on, ask: "How do the graphs compare?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** How do the graphs compare?
  **A:** They are opposites: when one has long days, the other has short.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Modeling the orbit
*Student:* Walk a tilted globe around a lamp and explain solstices and equinoxes; finish moon log.

*How to teach:* Walk the tilted globe around the lamp. Check understanding with: "At the June solstice, which hemisphere tilts toward the Sun?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** At the June solstice, which hemisphere tilts toward the Sun?
  **A:** Northern.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a scientific explanation of seasons and complete a quiz.

*How to teach:* Write the seasons explanation; give the quiz. To check, ask: "What causes seasons?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What causes seasons?
  **A:** Earth's tilted axis.

**Assessment:** Seasons explanation and quiz.

**Answer key:** Seasons explanation rubric: Earth's 23.5° tilt means each hemisphere receives more direct sunlight and longer days in its summer and less direct sunlight and shorter days in winter; references flashlight lab (same light spread over more area at an angle) and day-length data; addresses the distance misconception. Quiz: solstices ~June 21 and ~December 21; equinoxes ~March 20 and ~September 22 (about 12 hours of day everywhere); Southern Hemisphere has opposite seasons.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: Industrial Revolution in the Northeast

**Prepare:** Excerpts from Harriet Hanson Robinson's 'Loom and Spindle' or Lowell Offering letters; Erie Canal and National Road map; immigration data 1820–1860 and a nativist cartoon.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Whitney's cotton gin only affected the South. It also fueled Northern textile mills, shipping and banking.
- Thinking all immigrants were welcomed. Irish Catholics and Germans faced nativist hostility (e.g., the Know-Nothing Party).

### Day 1 (Monday): Factories
*Student:* Read letters from Lowell mill workers (Harriet Robinson) and describe working conditions.

*How to teach:* Read Lowell letters; describe conditions.

- **Q:** Who worked in the Lowell mills and what were conditions like?
  **A:** Mostly young unmarried New England farm women; long (12–14 hour) days, noisy machines, strict rules, boardinghouses, but cash wages.
- **Q:** What did Harriet Robinson describe about the 1836 strike?
  **A:** As an 11-year-old she led her room out in a 'turn-out' protesting higher board charges/wage cuts.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Transportation revolution
*Student:* Map the Erie Canal and National Road; explain Clay's American System.

*How to teach:* Map the Erie Canal and National Road; explain the American System.

- **Q:** Why was the Erie Canal important?
  **A:** It connected the Great Lakes to the Atlantic via the Hudson, lowering shipping costs and making New York City the nation's leading port.
- **Q:** What were the three parts of Clay's American System?
  **A:** A protective tariff, a national bank and federally funded internal improvements (roads, canals).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Inventions
*Student:* Make a chart of inventions (cotton gin, steamboat, telegraph, steel plow) and effects.

*How to teach:* Chart inventions and effects.

- **Q:** How did the steel plow change farming?
  **A:** It cut through tough prairie soil, opening the Midwest to large-scale farming.
- **Q:** What did the telegraph do?
  **A:** Sent messages almost instantly over wires using Morse code (first public line 1844).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Immigration
*Student:* Analyze Irish and German immigration data and a nativist cartoon.

*How to teach:* Analyze immigration data and a nativist cartoon.

- **Q:** What caused the surge in Irish immigration in the late 1840s?
  **A:** The potato famine (blight), which caused mass starvation in Ireland.
- **Q:** What is nativism?
  **A:** Favoring native-born people and opposing immigrants.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on industrial growth in the Northeast.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz.

- **Q:** What are interchangeable parts?
  **A:** Identical, standardized parts that can replace each other, allowing mass production and easy repair.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: Lowell mills — young New England women worked about 12–14-hour days, lived in boardinghouses, organized 'turn-outs' (strikes) in 1834 and 1836; Erie Canal (completed 1825) linked the Hudson River at Albany to Lake Erie at Buffalo, cutting freight costs dramatically and making New York City a leading port; National Road began at Cumberland, Maryland; Clay's American System — protective tariff, national bank, internal improvements; inventions — cotton gin (Whitney, 1793), steamboat (Fulton's Clermont, 1807), steel plow (John Deere, 1837), telegraph (Morse, 1844), interchangeable parts (Whitney and others); Irish immigration surged after the potato famine (1845–1852); German immigrants often settled in the Midwest; nativism = hostility to immigrants.

## Spanish 1B: Demonstrative adjectives and numbers 100–1,000,000

**Prepare:** Price tags on household items, a currency converter website (check current rates). Demonstratives chart.

**Watch for:**
- Using 'doscientos' with feminine nouns. Hundreds agree: doscientas camisas.
- Saying 'un mil.' 1,000 is mil; 1,000,000 is un millón (de): un millón de dólares.

### Day 1 (Monday): Demonstratives
*Student:* Learn este, ese, aquel and their forms and point out clothing at three distances in 12 sentences.

*How to teach:* Teach demonstratives at three distances; 12 sentences.

- **Q:** Say: 'This shirt and those shoes (over there).'
  **A:** Esta camisa y aquellos zapatos.
- **Q:** Say: 'those pants (near you).'
  **A:** esos pantalones.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Big numbers
*Student:* Learn 100–1,000,000 including doscientos/quinientos/setecientos and read 20 prices aloud.

*How to teach:* Teach 100–1,000,000; read 20 prices.

- **Q:** Write 750 in words.
  **A:** setecientos cincuenta.
- **Q:** Write 2.500 in words.
  **A:** dos mil quinientos.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Currencies
*Student:* Learn the currencies of eight Spanish-speaking countries and convert three clothing prices to dollars.

*How to teach:* Learn currencies of eight countries; convert three prices with a current rate.

- **Q:** What is the currency of Guatemala? Of Peru?
  **A:** The quetzal; the sol.

### Day 4 (Thursday): ¿Cuánto cuesta?
*Student:* Practice ¿Cuánto cuesta(n)? and answer with prices and demonstratives using price-tagged items at home.

*How to teach:* Practice ¿Cuánto cuesta(n)? with tagged items.

- **Q:** Ask how much these boots cost and answer 'They cost 80 dollars.'
  **A:** ¿Cuánto cuestan estas botas? —Cuestan ochenta dólares.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a demonstratives and numbers dictation quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the demonstratives and number dictation quiz.

- **Q:** Write 500 and 900.
  **A:** quinientos; novecientos.

**Assessment:** Demonstratives and large-number quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: este/esta/estos/estas (this/these, near speaker), ese/esa/esos/esas (that/those, near listener), aquel/aquella/aquellos/aquellas (that/those over there). Numbers: 100 cien, 101 ciento uno, 200 doscientos, 300 trescientos, 400 cuatrocientos, 500 quinientos, 600 seiscientos, 700 setecientos, 800 ochocientos, 900 novecientos, 1.000 mil, 2.000 dos mil, 1.000.000 un millón. Dictation samples: 550 quinientos cincuenta; 1.975 mil novecientos setenta y cinco; 25.000 veinticinco mil. Currencies: México — peso; España — euro; Guatemala — quetzal; Perú — sol; Costa Rica — colón; Argentina — peso; Colombia — peso; Ecuador and El Salvador — U.S. dollar; Venezuela — bolívar; Paraguay — guaraní; Honduras — lempira. 80% proficient.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Keyboard basics and scales

**Prepare:** Keyboard or online keyboard app; metronome; sheet music for 'Ode to Joy' (right hand: E E F G G F E D C C D E E D D).

**Watch for:**
- Running out of fingers in a scale. Correct: C major RH 1-2-3, thumb under, 1-2-3-4-5.
- Forgetting the formula's half steps. Correct: half steps fall between notes 3–4 and 7–8.

### Day 1 (Monday): Keyboard geography
*Student:* Locate all Cs and play C-D-E-F-G five-finger patterns in each hand.

*How to teach:* Locate all Cs (just left of the two-black-key groups); five-finger C-G each hand.

- **Q:** How do you find C on a keyboard?
  **A:** It's the white key just left of each group of two black keys.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): C major scale
*Student:* Practice the C major scale hands separately with thumb-under fingering at 60 bpm.

*How to teach:* C major scale hands separately with thumb-under at 60 bpm.

- **Q:** What is the RH fingering for C major?
  **A:** 1-2-3-1-2-3-4-5 ascending (thumb under after E).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Building scales
*Student:* Use the W-W-H-W-W-W-H formula to build G and F major scales and play them.

*How to teach:* Build G and F major with W-W-H-W-W-W-H.

- **Q:** What sharp or flat is in G major? F major?
  **A:** G major has F♯; F major has B♭.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Simple melody
*Student:* Learn 'Ode to Joy' right hand from notation.

*How to teach:* Learn 'Ode to Joy' RH.

- **Q:** Who composed 'Ode to Joy'?
  **A:** Ludwig van Beethoven (Symphony No. 9).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Play the C major scale and 'Ode to Joy' for a family member and self-evaluate with a checklist.

*How to teach:* Play scale and melody for family; self-evaluate.

- **Q:** What does the checklist look for?
  **A:** Correct notes, correct fingering, steady beat, smooth tone.

**Assessment:** Scale and melody playing check.

**Answer key:** Playing check: C major scale (correct notes, thumb-under fingering, steady 60 bpm) and 'Ode to Joy' (correct notes and rhythm, steady tempo). 4 = no errors, 3 = 1–2 errors, 2 = several errors/stops, 1 = incomplete.

## Physical Education 8: Tennis and table tennis: rallies and scoring

**Prepare:** Tennis gear, table tennis paddles and balls (or improvised table with a net of books). Scoring sheets.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking tennis points go 1-2-3-4. Correct: love (0), 15, 30, 40, game; at 40-40 (deuce) a player must win by two.
- Serving table tennis anywhere. Correct: toss the ball at least 6 inches from an open palm and strike it so it bounces on your side first.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tennis rallies
*Student:* Rally cooperatively with a partner aiming for 10 consecutive hits.

*How to teach:* Cooperative rallies aiming for 10.

- **Q:** Why practice cooperative rallies?
  **A:** To build consistency and control before competitive play.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tennis scoring
*Student:* Learn love-15-30-40-deuce scoring and play a tiebreaker game.

*How to teach:* Teach scoring and play a tiebreaker (first to 7, win by 2).

- **Q:** Server has 40, receiver has 30. Server wins the point. What's the score?
  **A:** Game to the server.
- **Q:** What happens after deuce if the server wins a point?
  **A:** 'Advantage server'; winning the next point wins the game, losing it returns to deuce.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Table tennis
*Student:* Learn the shakehand grip, push and forehand drive on a table or improvised surface.

*How to teach:* Shakehand grip; push and forehand drive.

- **Q:** What is the shakehand grip?
  **A:** Hold the paddle as if shaking hands, index finger along the back of the blade, thumb on the front.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Spin
*Student:* Experiment with topspin and backspin in table tennis and describe how the ball reacts.

*How to teach:* Experiment with topspin and backspin.

- **Q:** How does topspin make the ball behave?
  **A:** It dips down faster and kicks forward after bouncing; backspin floats and slows or checks back.

### Day 5 (Friday): Mini-match
*Student:* Play a best-of-three table tennis match to 11 and a short tennis set.

*How to teach:* Best-of-three table tennis to 11 and a short tennis set.

- **Q:** How does a table tennis game end?
  **A:** First to 11 points, winning by 2; serve alternates every 2 points (every 1 point at 10-10).

**Assessment:** Scoring quiz and match play observation.

**Answer key:** Scoring quiz key: 1) 0-15-30-40-game 2) deuce at 40-40 3) win by two after deuce 4) a set is first to 6 games, win by 2 (tiebreak at 6-6) 5) tiebreak first to 7 by 2 6) table tennis to 11 by 2 7) serve changes every 2 points 8) let = serve touches the net and lands in, replay. Match observation: keeps score correctly, legal serves, good sportsmanship.

## Health Education 8: Reproduction, pregnancy and fetal development

**Prepare:** Parent preview diagrams of fertilization and fetal development (e.g., KidsHealth 'Pregnancy' or a medical textbook). Prepare 8 timeline cards (key below) for the quiz. Opt-out alternative: growth and development across the lifespan timeline.

**Watch for:**
- Believing pregnancy lasts 9 months from conception exactly. Correct: it is about 40 weeks counted from the first day of the last menstrual period (about 38 weeks from fertilization).
- Thinking a small amount of alcohol is safe in pregnancy. Correct: no amount of alcohol is known to be safe; it can cause fetal alcohol spectrum disorders.

### Day 1 (Monday): Fertilization
*Student:* Study a diagram of fertilization and implantation with parent guidance. (Sexual health lesson: parents may opt out under CA Ed. Code 51938; parent previews materials.)

*How to teach:* Using the diagram, explain: sperm meets egg in the fallopian tube (fertilization), the zygote divides as it travels, and implants in the uterine lining about 6–10 days later.

- **Q:** Where does fertilization usually occur?
  **A:** In the fallopian tube.
- **Q:** What is implantation?
  **A:** When the developing ball of cells (blastocyst) attaches to the lining of the uterus.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Trimesters
*Student:* Create a timeline of fetal development across three trimesters.

*How to teach:* The child builds a three-trimester timeline with key events from a reliable source.

- **Q:** Name one milestone in each trimester.
  **A:** 1st (weeks 1–13): heart begins beating around week 5–6, organs begin forming, called a fetus from week 9–10. 2nd (14–27): movement felt, sex can be seen on ultrasound, hearing develops. 3rd (28–40): rapid weight gain, lungs mature, baby turns head-down.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Prenatal care
*Student:* Explain how nutrition, alcohol, tobacco and prenatal checkups affect a developing baby.

*How to teach:* Discuss how prenatal care, folic acid, nutrition and avoiding alcohol, tobacco and drugs protect the baby.

- **Q:** Why is folic acid important?
  **A:** It helps prevent neural tube defects such as spina bifida; it matters very early, often before a person knows they are pregnant.
- **Q:** Name a risk of smoking during pregnancy.
  **A:** Low birth weight, premature birth, miscarriage, SIDS.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Responsibilities
*Student:* Interview a parent about the responsibilities and costs of raising a child.

*How to teach:* The child interviews you about responsibilities and costs (time, sleep, money, childcare, emotional demands).

- **Q:** Name three responsibilities of raising a child.
  **A:** Examples: providing food, shelter and health care; supervision and time; emotional support; costs of diapers, childcare and education.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Complete a fetal development timeline quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the timeline quiz: the child places cards in order and answers short questions.

- **Q:** About how long is a full-term pregnancy?
  **A:** About 40 weeks (counted from the last menstrual period), or roughly 9 months.

**Assessment:** Timeline quiz.

**Answer key:** Timeline quiz key (correct order): 1) fertilization in fallopian tube 2) implantation in uterus (about 1 week) 3) heartbeat detectable (~6 weeks) 4) embryo becomes fetus (~9–10 weeks) 5) end of 1st trimester (~13 weeks) 6) movement felt (~16–20 weeks) 7) third trimester begins (~28 weeks): lungs mature, weight gain 8) full term ~39–40 weeks. Short answers: no amount of alcohol is safe; prenatal checkups monitor health of parent and baby; folic acid prevents neural tube defects.
