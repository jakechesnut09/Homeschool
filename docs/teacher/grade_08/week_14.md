# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 14

## English Language Arts 8: Contemporary argument: conflicting sources

**Prepare:** Choose paired articles on one debate (CommonLit or ReadWorks, e.g., cell phones in schools); print a credibility checklist; find a short student debate or TED-Ed talk on the topic.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a source is credible because it looks professional. Check author expertise, date, purpose and evidence.
- Treating any disagreement as a disagreement about facts. Often both sides accept the same facts but interpret them differently.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose a debate
*Student:* Read two articles on opposite sides of a teen-relevant debate and annotate each article's claim and strongest evidence.

*How to teach:* Child reads both articles and annotates each claim and strongest evidence. Before moving on, ask: "What is the difference between a claim and evidence?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a claim and evidence?
  **A:** A claim is the arguable position; evidence is the facts, data or examples that support it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Fact vs interpretation
*Student:* Mark each place the articles disagree and label whether they disagree on facts or on interpretation of the same facts.

*How to teach:* Mark disagreements; label each fact vs interpretation. Check understanding with: "Two articles cite the same study showing test scores rose after a phone ban; one says bans work, the other says other changes explain it. Fact or interpretation?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Two articles cite the same study showing test scores rose after a phone ban; one says bans work, the other says other changes explain it. Fact or interpretation?
  **A:** Interpretation — they agree on the data but disagree about the cause.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Evaluating sources
*Student:* Apply a credibility checklist (author, date, purpose, evidence, bias) to 4 additional online sources and keep the best two.

*How to teach:* Apply the checklist to 4 more sources. To check, ask: "A 2009 blog by an anonymous phone seller — keep it?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** A 2009 blog by an anonymous phone seller — keep it?
  **A:** Reject: outdated, no author expertise, commercial bias.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Listening to arguments
*Student:* Watch a short student debate or TED-Ed talk on the topic and evaluate whether each speaker's evidence is relevant and sufficient.

*How to teach:* Watch the debate; evaluate relevance and sufficiency. Finish by asking: "What makes evidence 'sufficient'?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** What makes evidence 'sufficient'?
  **A:** Enough credible evidence to support the claim — more than one anecdote.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write your own claim with three reasons and note one counterclaim you must answer.

*How to teach:* Child writes claim, three reasons, one counterclaim. Before moving on, ask: "Why include a counterclaim?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Why include a counterclaim?
  **A:** Addressing it shows fairness and strengthens the argument.

**Assessment:** Source evaluation chart and claim statement.

**Answer key:** Source evaluation chart: each of 4 sources rated on author, date, purpose, evidence and bias with a keep/reject decision and reason (1 point per source); claim statement: arguable claim + three distinct reasons + one named counterclaim (4 points). Example: 'Middle schools should require phones to stay in lockers during class because they distract learning, enable cyberbullying, and reduce face-to-face interaction; opponents say phones are needed for emergencies.'

## Mathematics 8: How many solutions?

**Prepare:** Index cards (12 equations written out), Desmos.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 0 = 0 means x = 0. Explain: a true statement with no variable means every value of x works.
- Thinking 'no solution' means the answer is 0. x = 0 is a solution; no solution means a false statement like 5 = 1.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sorting equations
*Student:* Solve and sort 12 equations into one solution, no solution and infinitely many solutions; describe what the final line looks like for each.

*How to teach:* Child solves 12 card equations and sorts them by the final line: x = number, false statement, or true statement. Before moving on, ask: "Solve 2(x + 3) = 2x + 6." and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Solve 2(x + 3) = 2x + 6.
  **A:** 2x + 6 = 2x + 6 → 6 = 6, true for all x: infinitely many solutions.
- **Q:** Solve 2x + 5 = 2x + 1.
  **A:** 5 = 1 is false: no solution.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Graphical meaning
*Student:* Graph each side of an equation as a line in Desmos to see one intersection, parallel lines or the same line.

*How to teach:* Graph each side as y = left side and y = right side in Desmos. One intersection, parallel lines, or the same line.

- **Q:** Graph y = 2x + 5 and y = 2x + 1. What does the picture say about 2x + 5 = 2x + 1?
  **A:** The lines are parallel (same slope, different intercepts), never meet, so there is no solution.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Create your own
*Student:* Write two equations of each type and trade them with a family member to solve.

*How to teach:* Child writes two equations of each type, trades with you to solve, then checks. To check, ask: "Give an example of an equation with infinitely many solutions." and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Give an example of an equation with infinitely many solutions.
  **A:** Example: 3(x + 1) = 3x + 3 (both sides are equivalent expressions).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Context problems
*Student:* Solve problems such as 'When will two savings accounts have equal balances?' and interpret no-solution answers in context.

*How to teach:* Model a savings-account problem and interpret 'no solution' in context. Finish by asking: "Account A has $100 and gains $10/week; B has $50 and gains $10/week. When are they equal?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Account A has $100 and gains $10/week; B has $50 and gains $10/week. When are they equal?
  **A:** Never: 100 + 10w = 50 + 10w gives 100 = 50, false. Same rate, different starts.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: classify and solve 8 equations and justify one of each type in writing.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz with one written justification for each type. Review & check day: have the child redo any missed problem in the notebook and explain the error in one sentence.

- **Q:** Solve 9 − 2x = 3x − 6.
  **A:** 15 = 5x, x = 3.

**Assessment:** Quiz on the number of solutions.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) 4x − 3 = 4x + 5 → no solution. 2) 3(x − 2) = 3x − 6 → infinitely many. 3) 5x + 1 = 2x + 10 → x = 3. 4) 2(2x + 1) = 4x + 2 → infinitely many. 5) 6 − x = −(x − 6) → infinitely many. 6) 0.5(4x + 6) = 2x + 1 → no solution (3 = 1). 7) 3x + 4 = −x − 8 → x = −3. 8) 9 − 2x = 3x − 6 → x = 3. Written justification: one solution ends x = number; no solution ends with a false statement; infinitely many ends with a true statement like 6 = 6.

## Science 8 (Integrated): Scale of the solar system

**Prepare:** Toilet paper roll, marker, calculator, NASA planet fact sheet, hallway or outdoor space. Prepare the Unit 3 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking textbook diagrams show true scale. Planets are tiny and far apart; diagrams compress distances.
- Thinking a light-year is time. It's a distance (about 9.5 trillion km).

### Day 1 (Monday): Size data
*Student:* Compare planet diameters from NASA data; calculate how many Earths fit across Jupiter and the Sun.

*How to teach:* Compare diameters: Earth 12,756 km, Jupiter 142,984 km, Sun ~1,392,700 km. Before moving on, ask: "How many Earths fit across Jupiter? Across the Sun?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** How many Earths fit across Jupiter? Across the Sun?
  **A:** About 11 across Jupiter and about 109 across the Sun.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Toilet paper solar system
*Student:* Make a distance scale model on a toilet-paper roll (1 square = about 10 million km) in a hallway or outside.

*How to teach:* Unroll the toilet paper; mark planets at their squares. Check understanding with: "Where is Earth?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Where is Earth?
  **A:** About 15 squares from the Sun.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Size vs distance models
*Student:* Explain why one model cannot show both sizes and distances to the same scale; calculate a scale where Earth is a peppercorn.

*How to teach:* Explain why one model can't show both size and distance. To check, ask: "If Earth is a 2-mm peppercorn, how far from the Sun?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** If Earth is a 2-mm peppercorn, how far from the Sun?
  **A:** About 23.5 m, with a Sun about 22 cm wide.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit review
*Student:* Review gravity, moon phases, eclipses, seasons and scale with a self-made study guide.

*How to teach:* Review with a study guide. Finish by asking: "What is an AU?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** What is an AU?
  **A:** Earth-Sun average distance, about 150 million km.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 test
*Student:* Unit test on gravity and the solar system.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 test. Before moving on, ask: "What is a light-year?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** What is a light-year?
  **A:** The distance light travels in a year, about 9.5 trillion km.

**Assessment:** Scale model and Unit 3 test.

**Answer key:** Scale model rubric: planets placed at correct squares (1 square ≈ 10 million km): Mercury ~6, Venus ~11, Earth ~15, Mars ~23, Jupiter ~78, Saturn ~143, Uranus ~287, Neptune ~450 squares. Unit 3 test sample answers: gravity depends on mass and distance; phases from viewing angle of the sunlit half; solar eclipse at new moon, lunar at full; seasons from tilt; orbits from inertia + gravity; 1 AU ≈ 150 million km.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: Cotton and the Southern economy

**Prepare:** Blank map of the South; data on cotton bales and enslaved population 1790–1860 (U.S. Census; e.g., about 700,000 enslaved people in 1790 and about 3.95 million in 1860; cotton output grew from a few thousand bales around 1790 to roughly 4 million bales by 1860); graph paper.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking most white Southerners owned enslaved people. In 1860 roughly one-quarter of white Southern families did; most were yeoman farmers, but slavery shaped the whole society and economy.
- Believing the cotton gin reduced slavery by saving labor. It made short-staple cotton profitable, so demand for enslaved labor and land grew.

### Day 1 (Monday): Geography of the South
*Student:* Label cotton-producing states, major rivers and port cities on a blank map; explain how climate and soil favored cotton.

*How to teach:* Label cotton states, rivers and ports; explain climate and soil.

- **Q:** Why did cotton grow well in the Deep South?
  **A:** Long, warm growing season, ample rain and rich soil (like Alabama and Mississippi's Black Belt).
- **Q:** Why were rivers and ports important to the cotton economy?
  **A:** They moved bales cheaply to ports like New Orleans for shipment to Northern and British mills.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): The cotton gin
*Student:* Graph cotton production and the enslaved population from 1790 to 1860 and write a paragraph explaining the relationship to Whitney's 1793 gin.

*How to teach:* Graph cotton production and enslaved population 1790–1860; write the relationship.

- **Q:** What relationship does the graph show?
  **A:** Both rose sharply together after the 1793 cotton gin made cotton profitable, increasing demand for enslaved labor.
- **Q:** About how many enslaved people lived in the U.S. in 1860?
  **A:** Nearly 4 million.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Southern society
*Student:* Build a social pyramid of the South (planters, yeoman farmers, poor whites, free Black people, enslaved people) using census statistics.

*How to teach:* Build a social pyramid using census statistics.

- **Q:** Which group made up the majority of white Southerners?
  **A:** Yeoman farmers who owned small farms and few or no enslaved people.

### Day 4 (Thursday): A one-crop economy
*Student:* Trace a bale of cotton from a Mississippi plantation to a Lowell or Manchester mill and explain the South's dependence on Northern and British markets.

*How to teach:* Trace a bale of cotton from Mississippi to Lowell/Manchester.

- **Q:** How did the North profit from slavery?
  **A:** Northern mills processed cotton; Northern banks, insurers and shippers financed and carried the trade.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on the Southern economy plus a short response comparing Northern and Southern economies.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and comparison response.

- **Q:** Why was cotton called 'King'?
  **A:** It was the South's dominant crop and the nation's leading export, central to the economy.

**Assessment:** Quiz and North-South comparison response.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: cotton grew best in the Deep South's long growing season, warm climate and rich soil (Black Belt); major rivers (Mississippi, Alabama, Tennessee, Red) and ports (New Orleans, Mobile, Savannah, Charleston) moved cotton; Whitney's 1793 gin separated seeds from short-staple cotton quickly; by 1860 cotton was the largest U.S. export ('King Cotton'); society — a small planter elite (20+ enslaved people), yeoman farmers (majority of whites), poor whites, about 260,000 free Black people in the South, nearly 4 million enslaved people; the South depended on Northern shipping, banking and manufactured goods and British/Northern mills. North–South comparison rubric: North — industry, cities, immigrant wage labor, railroads; South — agriculture, cash crops, enslaved labor, fewer cities and factories; uses specific evidence.

## Spanish 1B: Unit 3 assessment: Catálogo de moda

**Prepare:** Paper or slide software for a catalog. An authentic online store page in Spanish (e.g., a Spanish clothing retailer's site).

**Watch for:**
- Copying store-page text. Write your own descriptions using structures learned.
- Forgetting euros use a comma for decimals in Spain (19,99 €).

### Day 1 (Monday): Design
*Student:* Design a catalog page with eight items, colors, sizes, and prices in euros or pesos.

*How to teach:* Design a catalog page with 8 items, colors, sizes, prices.

- **Q:** Write a price: 45 euros and 99 cents.
  **A:** cuarenta y cinco euros con noventa y nueve (céntimos).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Write
*Student:* Write a description for each item using demonstratives, comparisons, and e→ie verbs.

*How to teach:* Write descriptions with demonstratives, comparisons and e→ie verbs.

  **A:** Estas botas son mejores que esos zapatos.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Reading
*Student:* Read an authentic online store page in Spanish and answer comprehension questions.

*How to teach:* Read an authentic store page; answer questions.

- **Q:** What does 'añadir a la cesta' mean on a store site?
  **A:** Add to cart/basket.

*Student:* Present your catalog as a 90-second TV shopping segment.

*How to teach:* Present a 90-second TV shopping segment.

- **Q:** Say: 'Do you want a modern style? This sweater is perfect.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 test
*Student:* Take the unit 3 test on clothing, stem-changers, demonstratives, numbers, and shopping.

**Assessment:** Catalog project rubric and unit 3 test.

**Answer key:** Catalog project rubric (1–4): 8 items with colors, sizes and prices; descriptions use demonstratives, comparisons and e→ie verbs (e.g., 'Esta chaqueta es más cómoda que aquel abrigo. ¿Prefieres el color azul?'); accurate agreement; attractive layout; 90-second TV segment with enthusiasm and clear pronunciation. Unit 3 test: clothing, e→ie/o→ue, demonstratives, numbers to 1,000,000, shopping phrases.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Performance and listening analysis

**Prepare:** Keyboard; recordings of Beethoven's 'Für Elise' and a verse-chorus pop song (preview lyrics). Form chart template.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking every piece repeats the same way. Correct: identify sections by contrast and return.
- Recording once and stopping. Correct: listen back critically and re-record.

### Day 1 (Monday): Form listening
*Student:* Listen to 'Für Elise' and diagram its rondo form (A-B-A-C-A).

*How to teach:* Diagram 'Für Elise' as rondo A-B-A-C-A.

- **Q:** What is rondo form?
  **A:** A recurring main theme (A) alternating with contrasting sections: A-B-A-C-A.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Contrasting structures
*Student:* Compare the verse-chorus form of a pop song with 'Für Elise' using a chart of form, dynamics and tempo.

*How to teach:* Compare verse-chorus form to 'Für Elise.'

- **Q:** How does verse-chorus form work?
  **A:** Verses with changing lyrics alternate with a repeated chorus; often there's a bridge.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rehearsal
*Student:* Rehearse your chosen piece with dynamics and articulation markings, setting personal criteria for 'ready.'

*How to teach:* Rehearse with dynamics and articulation; set criteria.

- **Q:** What does 'p' and 'f' mean?
  **A:** Piano = soft; forte = loud.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Recording
*Student:* Record a performance and listen back for accuracy.

*How to teach:* Record and listen back.

- **Q:** What should you listen for?
  **A:** Correct notes and rhythm, steady tempo, dynamics, smooth phrasing.

### Day 5 (Friday): Recital
*Student:* Perform for family and complete a self-evaluation.

*How to teach:* Perform; self-evaluate.

- **Q:** What is articulation?
  **A:** How notes are played/connected, e.g., legato (smooth) or staccato (short).

**Assessment:** Recorded performance and listening comparison.

**Answer key:** Recorded performance: meets self-set criteria for accuracy, tempo and dynamics (4 levels). Listening comparison: 4 = correct form diagrams for both (rondo A-B-A-C-A; verse-chorus), compares dynamics and tempo with specific moments.

## Physical Education 8: Net games practice and feedback

**Prepare:** Rackets, balls, volleyball, practice log. Prepare a 10-question written test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking blocked practice (same skill repeatedly) is always better because it feels better. Correct: random practice feels harder but leads to better retention and game transfer.
- Peer feedback that's only criticism. Correct: use praise-fix-praise.

### Day 1 (Monday): Practice types
*Student:* Learn blocked vs. random practice; do 10 minutes of each with tennis forehands and note the difference.

*How to teach:* 10 minutes blocked (all forehands), 10 random (forehands/backhands mixed); note difference.

- **Q:** Which practice type leads to better long-term learning?
  **A:** Random practice — even though performance during practice is lower, retention and transfer are better.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mental rehearsal
*Student:* Visualize a serve for 2 minutes before 20 serves and compare accuracy to 20 serves without it.

*How to teach:* Visualize serves for 2 minutes, then 20 serves; compare to 20 without.

- **Q:** What is mental rehearsal?
  **A:** Imagining performing a skill in detail without moving, which strengthens the neural pattern.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Peer coaching
*Student:* Coach a partner on one net-game skill using a 'praise-fix-praise' feedback format.

*How to teach:* Coach a partner with praise-fix-praise.

- **Q:** Give an example of praise-fix-praise.
  **A:** 'Great toss height. Try stepping in with your front foot. Your follow-through is strong!'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Choice tournament
*Student:* Play a mini-tournament in the net game of your choice.

*How to teach:* Mini-tournament in chosen net game.

- **Q:** Name a net-game strategy.
  **A:** Hit to open space, move opponent side to side, return to ready position in the center.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a written test on net-game strategy, scoring and practice principles.

*How to teach:* Give the written test.

- **Q:** What does 'side-out' mean in volleyball?
  **A:** The receiving team wins the rally and gains the serve.

**Assessment:** Net games written test.

**Answer key:** Written test key: 1) hit to open space 2) return to base position after each shot 3) tennis scoring 0-15-30-40 4) deuce/advantage 5) table tennis to 11 win by 2 6) volleyball rally scoring to 25 win by 2 7) rotate clockwise on side-out 8) blocked vs random practice; random better for retention 9) mental rehearsal improves performance 10) praise-fix-praise feedback. 8/10 proficient.

## Health Education 8: HIV and STI prevention

**Prepare:** Parent preview CDC STI fact sheets and HIV.gov 'HIV Basics.' Make 10 fact/myth cards. Look up a local clinic or Planned Parenthood/county clinic. Opt-out alternative: communicable diseases (flu, COVID-19, TB) transmission and prevention.

**Watch for:**
- Believing you can tell if someone has an STI by looking. Correct: many STIs have no symptoms; only testing tells.
- Thinking HIV is spread by hugging, sharing drinks or mosquitoes. Correct: HIV is spread through certain body fluids (blood, semen, vaginal fluids, breast milk), mainly through sex or sharing needles.

### Day 1 (Monday): Transmission
*Student:* Learn how HIV and common STIs are and are not transmitted using a fact/myth sort. (Sexual health lesson: parents may opt out under CA Ed. Code 51938; parent previews materials.)

*How to teach:* Sort cards: HIV can be spread by sharing needles (fact); by toilet seats (myth); by kissing (myth, essentially no risk); from mother to baby during birth or breastfeeding (fact).

- **Q:** Name two ways HIV is transmitted and two ways it is not.
  **A:** Is: sexual contact, sharing needles, from mother to baby (pregnancy, birth, breastfeeding). Is not: hugging, shaking hands, sharing dishes, toilet seats, mosquitoes, saliva/tears/sweat.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Symptoms
*Student:* Learn that many STIs have no symptoms and why testing matters.

*How to teach:* Explain that chlamydia, gonorrhea, HPV and herpes often cause no symptoms, which is why testing matters.

- **Q:** Why can an STI spread even when no one has symptoms?
  **A:** Many infections are asymptomatic, so people don't know they have them and can pass them on; testing is the only way to know.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Prevention and treatment
*Student:* Describe prevention strategies and that HIV is treatable with medicine.

*How to teach:* Discuss prevention: abstinence, condoms, mutual monogamy with a tested partner, HPV vaccine, PrEP. Explain bacterial STIs (chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis) can be cured with antibiotics; viral (HIV, herpes, HPV) can be treated but not cured.

- **Q:** Is HIV curable?
  **A:** No, but it is treatable. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) keeps people healthy, and an undetectable viral load means HIV is not transmitted sexually (U=U).
- **Q:** What vaccine prevents some STIs?
  **A:** The HPV vaccine prevents infections that cause most cervical and several other cancers and genital warts; the hepatitis B vaccine also helps.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rights and resources
*Student:* Learn California minors' rights to confidential STI testing and find a local clinic.

*How to teach:* Explain California law lets minors 12 and older consent to their own confidential STI testing and treatment. Find a local clinic together.

- **Q:** At what age can a California minor consent to STI testing and treatment?
  **A:** 12 and older.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz on STI and HIV transmission, prevention and testing.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (key below).

- **Q:** Which STIs can be cured with antibiotics?
  **A:** Bacterial ones: chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis (also trichomoniasis, a parasite, with medicine).

**Assessment:** STI/HIV quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) many STIs have no symptoms – true 2) HIV spread by sharing needles – true 3) HIV spread by hugging – false 4) abstinence is the only 100% prevention 5) condoms reduce risk of HIV and many STIs 6) bacterial STIs cured by antibiotics: chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis 7) HIV, herpes, HPV are viral, treatable not curable 8) HPV vaccine prevents cancer-causing HPV 9) testing is the only way to know status 10) CA minors 12+ can consent to confidential STI services. 8/10 proficient.
