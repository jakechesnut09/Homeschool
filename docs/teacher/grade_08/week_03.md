# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 3

## English Language Arts 8: 'The Gift of the Magi': irony and theme

**Prepare:** Read 'The Gift of the Magi' (short). Make a three-column irony chart (situational, verbal, dramatic).

**Watch for:**
- Calling any coincidence 'irony.' Situational irony is when the outcome is the opposite of what is expected.
- Putting opinions in an 'objective summary.' Remove words like 'sad,' 'beautiful' or 'I think.'

### Day 1 (Monday): Read 'The Gift of the Magi'
*Student:* Read the story and track each character's sacrifice and what the reader knows that the characters do not.

*How to teach:* Read the story; child tracks each sacrifice and what the reader knows that characters don't. Check understanding with: "What does each character sacrifice and buy?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** What does each character sacrifice and buy?
  **A:** Della sells her hair (for $20) to buy a platinum fob chain for Jim's watch; Jim sells his watch to buy her a set of combs.
- **Q:** How much money does Della have at the start?
  **A:** One dollar and eighty-seven cents.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Types of irony
*Student:* Sort 10 examples from the three stories studied so far into situational, verbal and dramatic irony; explain the effect of each.

*How to teach:* Teach the three ironies; child sorts 10 examples from the three stories and explains effects. To check, ask: "What type of irony ends 'The Gift of the Magi'?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What type of irony ends 'The Gift of the Magi'?
  **A:** Situational — each gift is useless because of the other's sacrifice.
- **Q:** Where is dramatic irony in 'The Tell-Tale Heart'?
  **A:** We know the body is under the floor while the officers chat calmly, increasing tension.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Theme & summary
*Student:* Write an objective summary (no opinions) and a theme statement for the story supported by two quotations.

*How to teach:* Model an objective summary (who, what, outcome, no opinion). Child writes one plus a theme statement with two quotations.

- **Q:** Why does O. Henry call Jim and Della 'the magi'?
  **A:** Though their gifts are 'foolish,' they gave the greatest gift — sacrificing their treasures out of love — so 'of all who give gifts these two were the wisest.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: infinitives
*Student:* Identify infinitives and infinitive phrases used as nouns, adjectives and adverbs; write a paragraph using all three verbal types correctly.

*How to teach:* Teach infinitives (to + verb) as noun, adjective, adverb. Child writes a paragraph using gerund, participle and infinitive.

- **Q:** Label the infinitive: 'Jim came home to give Della her gift.'
  **A:** 'to give' — adverb (tells why he came).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on irony and verbals; discuss with a family member which story's ending is most effective and why.

*How to teach:* Give the verbals/irony quiz; discuss which ending is most effective. Check understanding with: "Which ending has situational irony in all three stories?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Which ending has situational irony in all three stories?
  **A:** All three: the narrator betrays himself; the wish for money brings death; the gifts cancel each other.

**Assessment:** Verbals quiz and theme statement.

**Answer key:** Verbals quiz example: identify gerund/participle/infinitive in 'Della decided to sell her hair' (infinitive, noun as object), 'Selling her hair took courage' (gerund, subject), 'The waiting husband came home' (participle, adjective), 'She had $1.87 to spend' (infinitive, adjective) — 1 point each. Theme statement: proficient = a complete sentence that is a universal idea (e.g., 'True love is shown through sacrifice, which matters more than the gifts themselves'), supported by two quotations such as the narrator calling the couple 'the wisest' and 'the magi.'

## Mathematics 8: Congruence through sequences of rigid motions

**Prepare:** Gather tracing paper, graph paper, cardstock, scissors. Cut 4–6 quadrilaterals from cardstock in advance, including two that are congruent but mirror images and one pair that is close but not congruent.

**Watch for:**
- Saying figures are congruent because they 'look the same.' Require a sequence of rigid motions that maps one exactly onto the other.
- Thinking a flipped (mirror-image) figure is not congruent. A reflection is a rigid motion, so mirror images are congruent.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is congruence?
*Student:* Sort 8 pairs of figures into congruent / not congruent and justify each answer with a rigid-motion argument rather than 'looks the same'.

*How to teach:* Show 8 pairs of figures; for each, the child decides congruent or not and must justify with a move ('a 90° rotation maps one onto the other') or a measurement that breaks congruence. Check understanding with: "A 2-by-6 rectangle and a 3-by-4 rectangle both have area 12. Are they congruent?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** A 2-by-6 rectangle and a 3-by-4 rectangle both have area 12. Are they congruent?
  **A:** No. Their side lengths differ, and rigid motions preserve lengths, so no sequence of moves can map one onto the other.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sequences of moves
*Student:* Find a sequence of two or three transformations mapping figure A to figure B for 6 grid problems; compare two different valid sequences.

*How to teach:* Model finding a sequence: first match one vertex (translate), then line up a side (rotate), then flip if needed (reflect). The child solves 6 grid problems and compares two different valid sequences for one problem.

- **Q:** Find a move taking triangle (1,1),(3,1),(1,4) to (−1,−1),(−3,−1),(−1,−4).
  **A:** A 180° rotation about the origin (or a reflection over the x-axis followed by a reflection over the y-axis).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Congruent polygons
*Student:* Cut out cardstock quadrilaterals and test congruence by moving one onto the other; write the sequence used.

*How to teach:* The child traces and moves cardstock quadrilaterals onto each other and records the sequence used. Ask: if you had to flip it over, does that still count?

- **Q:** One quadrilateral only fits on another after you flip it over. Are they congruent?
  **A:** Yes — flipping is a reflection, a rigid motion, so they are congruent.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Points, segments and circles
*Student:* Show that two segments of the same length and two circles of the same radius are congruent by describing the moves.

*How to teach:* Discuss: how can you prove any two segments of length 5 are congruent? Model: translate one endpoint onto the other, rotate until the segments lie on the same ray, and the far endpoints match because lengths are equal. Repeat the argument for circles of equal radius.

- **Q:** Explain why two circles with radius 4 cm are congruent.
  **A:** Translate one circle so its center lands on the other's center. Every point of each circle is 4 cm from that center, so the circles now coincide.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: describe sequences of moves for 4 pairs and explain why one pair cannot be congruent.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (4 pairs + 1 impossible pair); child writes full sentences for each sequence. Review & check day: have the child redo any missed problem in the notebook and explain the error in one sentence.

- **Q:** What single fact is enough to show two triangles are NOT congruent?
  **A:** A pair of corresponding sides (or angles) with different measures, since rigid motions preserve all lengths and angles.

**Assessment:** Quiz on congruence sequences with written justifications.

**Answer key:** Rubric per pair (4 pairs, 3 points each): correct sequence of moves (e.g., 'reflect across the y-axis, then translate 2 down'), moves stated precisely, and verification that vertices match. Sample answers: a pair related by rotation 90° CCW about origin then 3 right; a pair related by a single reflection across x = 1. The non-congruent pair: full credit explains a specific mismatch, e.g., 'one side is 3 units and the corresponding side is 4 units, and rigid motions preserve length, so no sequence works.'

## Science 8 (Integrated): Newton's second law: force, mass and acceleration

**Prepare:** Index card, coin, cup; toy car, washers or coins, tape, a board-and-books ramp, meter stick, stopwatch.

**Watch for:**
- Expecting heavier cars to roll down a ramp much faster. Gravity pulls harder on more mass, but more mass is harder to accelerate, so (ignoring friction) acceleration is about the same.
- Thinking inertia is a force. Inertia is an object's resistance to changes in motion, which depends on mass.

### Day 1 (Monday): Inertia
*Student:* Do the coin-on-card flick and tablecloth-style demonstrations; explain Newton's first law in your notebook.

*How to teach:* Place the card on a cup with a coin on top; flick the card out. The child explains why the coin drops into the cup (inertia) and writes Newton's first law.

- **Q:** Why does the coin fall into the cup instead of flying off with the card?
  **A:** The coin's inertia keeps it at rest; the quick flick applies little friction force to it, so it stays put and gravity pulls it into the cup.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Planning the investigation
*Student:* Write a plan to test how added mass changes the speed of a car released down a ramp: question, variables, procedure, data table.

*How to teach:* Model a good plan: question, hypothesis, independent/dependent/controlled variables, numbered procedure, blank data table. The child writes the plan.

- **Q:** Name the independent, dependent and two controlled variables.
  **A:** Independent: number of washers; dependent: time (and speed) down the ramp; controlled: ramp height, release point, distance, the car.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Carrying out the investigation
*Student:* Run 3 trials each with 0, 2, 4 and 6 washers taped to the car; calculate average times and speeds.

*How to teach:* Run 3 trials each for 0, 2, 4 and 6 washers; the child records times and calculates average speeds. Finish by asking: "If the 6-washer car's time is nearly the same as the empty car's, what does that suggest?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** If the 6-washer car's time is nearly the same as the empty car's, what does that suggest?
  **A:** Added mass doesn't much change speed down a ramp — gravity's pull increases with mass, but so does inertia.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Force & mass math
*Student:* Graph your results; solve 8 F = ma problems and relate the math to your data.

*How to teach:* The child graphs average speed vs washers, then solves 8 F = ma problems. Connect: on a ramp both force and mass increase together.

- **Q:** A 2-kg cart is pushed with 10 N. Acceleration?
  **A:** a = F/m = 5 m/s².
- **Q:** Same 10 N on a 4-kg cart?
  **A:** 2.5 m/s² — double the mass, half the acceleration.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a lab report conclusion and take a quiz on Newton's first and second laws.

*How to teach:* The child writes the conclusion; give the quiz (key). Check understanding with: "What force gives a 5-kg object an acceleration of 3 m/s²?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** What force gives a 5-kg object an acceleration of 3 m/s²?
  **A:** 15 N.

**Assessment:** Lab report on mass and motion; quiz.

**Answer key:** Lab report rubric (4 levels): question, hypothesis, variables (independent = washers, dependent = time/speed, controlled = ramp height, release point, distance), data table with 3 trials and averages, graph, conclusion that matches data (expected: little change in speed with added mass; small differences due to friction and timing error), sources of error. Quiz: 1) Newton's first law: an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity unless acted on by an unbalanced force. 2) F = ma: 10 N on 2 kg → 5 m/s². 3) 3 kg accelerating at 4 m/s² → 12 N. 4) 20 N gives 4 m/s² → 5 kg. 5) Same force on double mass → half the acceleration.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: The Declaration of Independence and the Revolution

**Prepare:** Declaration of Independence transcript (National Archives); Common Sense excerpts; a map of the Revolution; the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789). Write the unit 1 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking independence was declared on July 4. Congress voted for independence on July 2, 1776, and adopted the Declaration's text on July 4.
- Assuming all colonists were Patriots. Many were Loyalists or neutral.

### Day 1 (Monday): Common Sense
*Student:* Read excerpts from Thomas Paine's Common Sense and identify his three strongest arguments for independence.

*How to teach:* Read Common Sense excerpts; identify Paine's three strongest arguments.

- **Q:** Name two of Paine's arguments for independence.
  **A:** It is absurd for an island to rule a continent; hereditary monarchy is unnatural; independence would free trade and keep America out of Britain's wars; reconciliation was impossible after bloodshed.
- **Q:** Why was Common Sense so influential?
  **A:** It was written in plain language, sold widely (well over 100,000 copies in 1776), and was read aloud, persuading ordinary colonists.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): The Declaration
*Student:* Close-read the preamble of the Declaration; paraphrase each key phrase and list 5 grievances against the king.

*How to teach:* Close-read the preamble; paraphrase key phrases; list 5 grievances.

- **Q:** Paraphrase 'deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed.'
  **A:** Government only has legitimate power when the people agree to it.
- **Q:** List three grievances against the king.
  **A:** Imposing taxes without consent; keeping standing armies in peacetime without consent; quartering troops; cutting off trade; depriving colonists of trial by jury; dissolving colonial legislatures.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Winning the war
*Student:* Map key battles (Saratoga, Valley Forge, Yorktown) and explain the role of French alliance and of Black and women patriots such as James Armistead and Mercy Otis Warren.

*How to teach:* Map Saratoga, Valley Forge, Yorktown; explain the French alliance and contributions of James Armistead and Mercy Otis Warren.

- **Q:** Why was Saratoga (1777) a turning point?
  **A:** The American victory convinced France to ally with the United States (1778), bringing troops, money and a navy.
- **Q:** Who was James Armistead (Lafayette)?
  **A:** An enslaved man from Virginia who served as a double agent spy for Lafayette, providing intelligence before Yorktown; he later gained his freedom.
- **Q:** Who was Mercy Otis Warren?
  **A:** A Massachusetts writer whose plays and later history of the Revolution supported the Patriot cause.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Global impact
*Student:* Compare the Declaration with the French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789) in a Venn diagram.

*How to teach:* Venn diagram: Declaration of Independence vs Rights of Man (1789).

- **Q:** Name one similarity between the two documents.
  **A:** Both state that people have natural rights (liberty, property, etc.) and that government's purpose is to protect them; power comes from the people/nation.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 test
*Student:* Unit test and a short essay on how the Declaration defines the purpose of government.

*How to teach:* Give the unit 1 test and constructed response.

- **Q:** According to the Declaration, what is the purpose of government?
  **A:** To secure people's unalienable rights, including life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test with constructed response.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test (suggested): Magna Carta 1215 limited the king; Locke — natural rights/consent; Montesquieu — separation of powers; Stamp Act 1765; Boston Massacre 1770; Lexington and Concord 1775; Common Sense 1776 (Paine); Declaration July 4, 1776 (Jefferson main author); Saratoga 1777 turning point that brought the French alliance (1778); Valley Forge winter 1777–78; Yorktown 1781 (Cornwallis surrendered, with French army and navy help); Treaty of Paris 1783 recognized independence with borders to the Mississippi. Constructed response rubric: explains that government exists to secure unalienable rights (life, liberty, pursuit of happiness), gets its just powers from the consent of the governed, and the people may alter or abolish a government that destroys these ends; cites the text.

## Spanish 1B: Ser vs. estar

**Prepare:** Make 20 sentence cards mixing ser and estar uses. StudySpanish.com ser/estar lesson.

**Watch for:**
- Using estar for professions: 'Está doctora.' Occupation uses ser: Es doctora.
- Using ser for location of things/people: location uses estar (El restaurante está en la calle Main). Exception: events — La fiesta es en mi casa.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rules
*Student:* Learn the DOCTOR (ser) and PLACE (estar) memory aids and sort 20 sentence cards.

*How to teach:* Teach DOCTOR and PLACE; sort 20 cards.

- **Q:** Which verb for 'Ella ___ abogada'? Why?
  **A:** es — occupation (DOCTOR).
- **Q:** Which verb for 'Yo ___ nervioso hoy'? Why?
  **A:** estoy — emotion/condition (PLACE).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Meaning changes
*Student:* Study adjectives that change meaning (es aburrido vs. está aburrido; es rico vs. está rico) and write 6 contrasting pairs.

*How to teach:* Study meaning-change adjectives: es aburrido (is boring) vs está aburrido (is bored); es listo (is clever) vs está listo (is ready); es rico (is rich) vs está rico (tastes good); write 6 pairs.

- **Q:** Translate: 'The movie is boring' and 'I am bored.'
  **A:** La película es aburrida. Estoy aburrido/a.
- **Q:** What's the difference between 'es listo' and 'está listo'?
  **A:** Es listo = he is clever; está listo = he is ready.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Food & feelings
*Student:* Describe a meal: how the food is (ser) and how it tastes and is today (estar), plus how diners feel.

*How to teach:* Describe a meal: what food is (ser) and how it tastes today (estar), plus diners' feelings.

- **Q:** Write: 'Paella is a typical dish; today it is delicious.'
  **A:** La paella es un plato típico; hoy está deliciosa.

### Day 4 (Thursday): StudySpanish practice
*Student:* Read the StudySpanish.com ser/estar lesson and complete its practice exercises.

*How to teach:* Complete the StudySpanish lesson and exercises.

- **Q:** Choose: ¿Dónde ___ los baños? (son/están)
  **A:** están — location.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a 25-item ser vs. estar quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the 25-item quiz.

- **Q:** Choose: ___ las diez de la noche. (Son/Están)
  **A:** Son — time.

**Assessment:** Ser vs. estar quiz (80%+).

**Answer key:** 25-item quiz guide: ser for DOCTOR — Description (permanent traits), Occupation, Characteristic, Time/date, Origin, Relationship; estar for PLACE — Position, Location, Action (progressive), Condition, Emotion. Samples: Mi madre ___ profesora (es); La sopa ___ fría (está); ___ las dos (son); Nosotros ___ cansados (estamos); El museo ___ cerca (está); Él ___ mi primo (es); Ellos ___ de Chile (son); Yo ___ comiendo (estoy); Hoy ___ lunes (es); La fiesta ___ en el parque (es). 20/25 = 80%.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: One-point perspective

**Prepare:** Ruler, sharp pencils, drawing paper. Draw a sample: horizon line with one vanishing point.

**Watch for:**
- Drawing receding lines in different directions. Correct: all receding (depth) lines go to the single vanishing point; verticals stay vertical and horizontals stay horizontal.
- Forgetting the horizon line is eye level. Correct: boxes above it show their bottoms, below it their tops.

### Day 1 (Monday): Perspective basics
*Student:* Draw boxes above, on and below the horizon line using one vanishing point.

*How to teach:* Draw boxes above, on and below the horizon line.

- **Q:** If a box is below the horizon line, which side can you see?
  **A:** The top.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Block letters
*Student:* Draw your initials in one-point perspective block letters.

*How to teach:* Draw block initials in one-point perspective.

- **Q:** Which lines in block letters stay parallel to the page edges?
  **A:** The front face's horizontal and vertical lines; only depth lines go to the vanishing point.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hallway or road
*Student:* Draw a hallway, road or railroad track receding to a vanishing point.

*How to teach:* Draw a hallway, road or railroad track receding to the VP.

- **Q:** Why do railroad tracks seem to meet?
  **A:** Parallel lines moving away from us appear to converge at a vanishing point on the horizon.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Room interior
*Student:* Draw your bedroom in one-point perspective with furniture.

*How to teach:* Draw the bedroom interior with furniture.

- **Q:** Where is the vanishing point in a room drawing?
  **A:** On the back wall at the viewer's eye level (horizon line).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Add value to the room drawing and check lines with a ruler.

*How to teach:* Add value; check lines with a ruler.

- **Q:** What is the horizon line?
  **A:** A horizontal line representing the viewer's eye level.

**Assessment:** One-point perspective room drawing.

**Answer key:** One-point room drawing: 4 = accurate horizon line and single VP, all depth lines converge correctly, at least 4 furniture items in correct perspective, ruler-straight lines, value added; 3 = 1–2 minor errors; 2 = several lines miss the VP; 1 = incomplete.

## Physical Education 8: Goals, motivation and activity tracking

**Prepare:** Fitness journal, a goal sheet, and an optional step counter. Prepare a list of 12 reasons people exercise. Plan a 20-minute jog/walk interval route.

**Watch for:**
- Relying only on outside rewards. Correct: intrinsic motivation (enjoyment, feeling good) keeps people active longer.
- Setting a goal without a weekly action. Correct: each goal needs specific weekly steps.

### Day 1 (Monday): Motivation
*Student:* Sort a list of reasons people exercise into intrinsic and extrinsic, then list your own top three motivators; finish with a 20-minute jog/walk interval.

*How to teach:* Sort reasons (fun, weight, prize, friends, stress relief, parent's request, trophies, feeling strong) into intrinsic vs. extrinsic; child picks top three motivators. Run a 20-minute jog/walk interval (e.g., 2 min jog/1 min walk).

- **Q:** Is 'I run because I enjoy the feeling afterward' intrinsic or extrinsic?
  **A:** Intrinsic — the reward comes from the activity itself.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Barriers
*Student:* Identify three barriers to being active (time, weather, screens) and write a strategy for each; complete a 15-minute body-weight circuit.

*How to teach:* The child names three barriers and a strategy for each, then does a 15-minute circuit (squats, push-ups, jumping jacks, plank, lunges; 40 s on/20 s off).

- **Q:** Give a strategy for 'too much screen time.'
  **A:** Set an active break every 30 minutes, replace one show with a walk, or exercise during a show.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): SMART goals
*Student:* Write two SMART goals (one health-related, one skill-related) with a weekly action step for each.

*How to teach:* The child writes two SMART goals with weekly action steps.

- **Q:** Make this SMART: 'Get better at the PACER.'
  **A:** Example: 'Increase my PACER from 40 to 48 laps by week 19 by running intervals 3 days a week for 25 minutes.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Activity log
*Student:* Begin a 4-day-per-week MVPA log and do a 30-minute activity of your choice outside of class.

*How to teach:* Start a log of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) 4 days a week; do a 30-minute activity outside class.

- **Q:** How can you tell activity is moderate vs. vigorous?
  **A:** Talk test: moderate — can talk but not sing; vigorous — can only say a few words without pausing for breath.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Share goals with a parent, who signs the goal sheet; play an active game of choice.

*How to teach:* Review goals together; parent signs. Play an active game.

- **Q:** How many minutes of activity do teens need daily?
  **A:** At least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity.

**Assessment:** Signed SMART goal sheet and first week of activity log.

**Answer key:** Signed SMART goal sheet: 1 pt each for Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound per goal + weekly action steps + signature (proficient = both goals meet all criteria). Activity log: 4 entries with date, activity, minutes and intensity.

## Health Education 8: Mental illness and reducing stigma

**Prepare:** Print or bookmark NAMI or KidsHealth fact sheets on anxiety disorders, ADHD, depression and OCD. Draw a continuum line labeled Healthy – Reacting – Struggling – Unwell (or Thriving to Crisis).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking mental illness means someone is weak or 'crazy.' Correct: mental illnesses are common health conditions involving the brain, and they are treatable.
- Confusing normal sadness or nervousness with a disorder. Correct: a disorder involves symptoms that are intense, last a long time and interfere with daily life.

### Day 1 (Monday): Continuum
*Student:* Learn the mental health continuum and place everyday examples on it.

*How to teach:* Draw the continuum and explain that everyone moves along it. Give everyday examples (good week, stressed before a test, can't sleep for weeks) and have the child place each.

- **Q:** Where would 'feeling nervous before a big game but performing fine' go on the continuum?
  **A:** Toward the healthy/reacting end — it is a normal, temporary stress response that does not interfere with functioning.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Common conditions
*Student:* Research one condition (anxiety disorder, ADHD, depression, OCD) and list symptoms and treatments.

*How to teach:* The child picks one condition, reads a fact sheet and lists 3–4 symptoms and 2 treatments. Check facts against the source.

- **Q:** Name one symptom and one treatment for depression.
  **A:** Symptoms: persistent sadness or emptiness for two weeks or more, loss of interest, sleep or appetite changes, trouble concentrating, hopelessness. Treatments: talk therapy (e.g., CBT), medication, support, exercise and sleep routines.
- **Q:** How is an anxiety disorder different from ordinary worry?
  **A:** The worry or fear is excessive, hard to control, lasts months and interferes with school, sleep or relationships.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Stigma
*Student:* Analyze stigmatizing language in media and rewrite it with person-first language.

*How to teach:* Show examples of stigmatizing words ('psycho,' 'crazy,' 'she's so OCD'). Teach person-first language and have the child rewrite three phrases.

- **Q:** Rewrite 'He's a schizophrenic' using person-first language.
  **A:** 'He is a person who has schizophrenia' or 'He lives with schizophrenia.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Treatment
*Student:* Learn what counselors, psychologists and psychiatrists do and how to access them.

*How to teach:* Explain each professional: school counselor (support, referrals), psychologist (therapy and testing, not usually medication), psychiatrist (medical doctor who can prescribe medication), licensed therapist. Discuss how to access: tell a parent, school counselor, doctor.

- **Q:** Which professional can prescribe medication?
  **A:** A psychiatrist (a medical doctor); a primary-care doctor or psychiatric nurse practitioner can also prescribe. Psychologists generally do not.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Create a one-page anti-stigma infographic.

*How to teach:* The child makes a one-page infographic with at least two facts, one myth corrected, person-first language and where to get help.

- **Q:** What is stigma and why does it matter?
  **A:** Negative attitudes and stereotypes about people with mental illness; it makes people ashamed and less likely to seek help.

**Assessment:** Anti-stigma infographic.

**Answer key:** Infographic rubric (4 levels): 4 = at least two accurate facts with source, one myth corrected, person-first language throughout, help resources (988, counselor, doctor), clear design; 3 = accurate facts and resources, minor gaps; 2 = one fact, no myth or resources, some stigmatizing wording; 1 = inaccurate or incomplete.
