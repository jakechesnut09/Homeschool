# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 4

## English Language Arts 8: 'The Most Dangerous Game' and narrative techniques

**Prepare:** Read 'The Most Dangerous Game' (1924, public domain). Print a story map. Parental preview: a man hunts humans; violence including the deaths of a dog and a servant.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing internal and external conflict. Internal = inside the character's mind (fear, doubt); external = against another person, nature or society.
- Ending a twist story with 'it was all a dream.' Twists should be set up by earlier clues.

### Day 1 (Monday): Read Part 1
*Student:* Read to Rainsford's arrival at the chateau; annotate imagery that creates an ominous mood.

*How to teach:* Read to Rainsford's arrival at the chateau; mark imagery of ominous mood. To check, ask: "What ominous details appear near Ship-Trap Island?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What ominous details appear near Ship-Trap Island?
  **A:** Sailors' superstition about the island, the 'moonless Caribbean night' with darkness 'like moist black velvet,' a gunshot, and a scream.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Read Part 2
*Student:* Finish the story; chart Rainsford's internal and external conflicts and how Zaroff's dialogue reveals his values.

*How to teach:* Finish the story; chart Rainsford's conflicts and Zaroff's values. Finish by asking: "What does Zaroff hunt and how does he justify it?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** What does Zaroff hunt and how does he justify it?
  **A:** Humans — sailors he lures with false lights; he says 'Life is for the strong' and calls his victims 'scum.'
- **Q:** How does the story end?
  **A:** Rainsford hides in Zaroff's bedroom, defeats him, and 'had never slept in a better bed' — implying Zaroff died.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mentor techniques
*Student:* Collect 5 suspense techniques from the unit (short sentences, foreshadowing, cliffhangers, sensory details, unreliable narrator) with examples.

*How to teach:* Collect 5 suspense techniques with examples. Before moving on, ask: "Give a suspense technique from 'The Monkey's Paw.'" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Give a suspense technique from 'The Monkey's Paw.'
  **A:** Foreshadowing (Morris's warnings) or the cliffhanger knocking at the door.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Plan a narrative
*Student:* Plan an original 2–3 page suspense story with a narrator, conflict, rising action and a twist, using a story map.

*How to teach:* Plan a 2–3 page suspense story with a story map, including a planted twist clue. Check understanding with: "What makes a twist fair?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** What makes a twist fair?
  **A:** Earlier clues make it surprising but believable when re-read.

### Day 5 (Friday): Draft the opening
*Student:* Write the opening scene (at least 400 words) using a hook, sensory details and one deliberate foreshadowing clue.

*How to teach:* Draft the opening (≥400 words) with a hook, sensory details and a foreshadowing clue. To check, ask: "What is the 'hook' of 'The Tell-Tale Heart'?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What is the 'hook' of 'The Tell-Tale Heart'?
  **A:** The narrator's frantic denial of madness in the first line.

**Assessment:** Narrative plan and opening scene checked against a narrative rubric.

**Answer key:** Narrative rubric for plan + opening (4 levels): 4 = plan shows narrator, clear conflict, rising action and a twist with a planted clue; opening ≥400 words with a hook, sensory details and one foreshadowing clue; 3 = all parts, weaker clue or details; 2 = plan or opening incomplete; 1 = little planning.

## Mathematics 8: Angles: parallel lines, transversals and triangles

**Prepare:** Gather a protractor, ruler, scissors and a few paper triangles. Prepare the Unit 1 test (items in the assessment key) and review your own notes on angle vocabulary.

**Watch for:**
- Assuming alternate interior angles are equal when lines are NOT parallel. Point out the angle facts about transversals only hold when the lines are parallel.
- Adding angles to 360° in a triangle. Revisit the torn-corners activity: the three angles form a straight line, 180°.
- Solving for x and stopping. Always substitute x back to find the actual angle measures the question asks for.

### Day 1 (Monday): Vertical & supplementary angles
*Student:* Use a 180-degree rotation to explain why vertical angles are equal; solve 8 missing-angle problems.

*How to teach:* Draw two intersecting lines and label the 4 angles. Rotate a tracing 180° about the intersection point to show vertical angles swap places, so they are equal. The child solves 8 missing-angle problems.

- **Q:** Two lines intersect; one angle is 47°. Find the other three.
  **A:** 47° (vertical), and 133° and 133° (each supplementary to 47°).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Parallel lines and transversals
*Student:* Draw two parallel lines cut by a transversal, measure all 8 angles, and use translations to explain why corresponding angles match.

*How to teach:* Child draws two parallel lines (use both edges of a ruler) cut by a transversal, measures all 8 angles, and colors equal angles the same. Explain that translating one intersection along the transversal lands it on the other, so corresponding angles match.

- **Q:** Parallel lines are cut by a transversal and one angle is 65°. Give the corresponding, alternate interior and same-side interior angles.
  **A:** Corresponding 65°, alternate interior 65°, same-side (consecutive) interior 115°.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Triangle angle sum
*Student:* Tear the three corners off a paper triangle and line them up on a straight line; write an argument using parallel lines for the 180-degree sum.

*How to teach:* Child tears the three corners off a paper triangle and lines them up; they form a straight angle. Then model the proof: draw a line through one vertex parallel to the opposite side; alternate interior angles show the three angles fill 180°.

- **Q:** A triangle has angles of 48° and 67°. Find the third angle.
  **A:** 65°, because 180 − 48 − 67 = 65.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Exterior angles
*Student:* Explain the exterior angle theorem and solve 6 algebraic angle problems such as (2x + 10) + (3x - 5) = 120.

*How to teach:* Explain that an exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles (both equal 180° minus the adjacent interior angle). Do one algebra example together, then the child solves 6.

- **Q:** Solve (2x + 10) + (3x − 5) = 120 and find both angles.
  **A:** 5x + 5 = 120, so x = 23; the angles are 56° and 64°.
- **Q:** Remote interior angles are 40° and 75°. Find the exterior angle.
  **A:** 115°.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 test
*Student:* Unit test on rigid transformations, coordinate rules, congruence and angle relationships; reflect on two missed items.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 test (key below) with no notes. Afterward the child picks two missed items and writes what went wrong and the correct solution.

- **Q:** What three angle relationships come from parallel lines and a transversal?
  **A:** Corresponding angles are equal, alternate interior angles are equal, and same-side interior angles are supplementary.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test (transformations, congruence, angle relationships).

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test suggested items and answers: 1) Reflect (3, −2) over the y-axis → (−3, −2). 2) Rotate (2, 5) 90° clockwise about origin → (5, −2). 3) Translate (−4, 1) by (x + 6, y − 3) → (2, −2). 4) Write the rule for a 180° rotation → (x, y) → (−x, −y). 5) Two triangles: give a sequence showing congruence (accept any valid sequence). 6) Angle supplementary to 72° → 108°. 7) Vertical angle to 3x + 5 is 50° → x = 15. 8) Parallel lines cut by a transversal, one angle 65°: alternate interior = 65°, same-side interior = 115°. 9) Triangle with angles 35° and 90° → third angle 55°. 10) Exterior angle with remote interior angles 40° and 75° → 115°. 11) (2x + 10) + (3x − 5) = 120 → x = 23, angles 56° and 64°. Score: 1 point each, plus reflection on two missed items.

## Science 8 (Integrated): Newton's third law and collisions

**Prepare:** Balloons (3), string (5 m), straw, tape, marbles of two sizes, a ruler with a groove. Find a slow-motion crash test video (IIHS or NHTSA on YouTube).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking action and reaction forces cancel. They act on different objects, so they don't cancel each other.
- Thinking the bigger object exerts a bigger force in a collision. The forces are equal and opposite; the effects differ because masses differ.

### Day 1 (Monday): Balloon rocket
*Student:* Build a balloon rocket on a string line; measure distance traveled for small, medium and large balloon inflations and identify the force pair.

*How to teach:* Thread the string through the straw, tape a balloon to it, release and measure distance for 3 inflation sizes. Identify the force pair.

- **Q:** What is the force pair in the balloon rocket?
  **A:** The balloon pushes air out the back; the air pushes the balloon forward.
- **Q:** Why does a bigger inflation go farther?
  **A:** More air pushed out for longer gives a larger/longer forward push.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Force pairs
*Student:* Label action-reaction pairs in 8 scenarios (swimmer, rocket, rowing, hammer and nail) and explain why the pairs do not cancel.

*How to teach:* The child labels action-reaction pairs in 8 scenarios and explains why they don't cancel. Finish by asking: "Why don't action-reaction forces cancel?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Why don't action-reaction forces cancel?
  **A:** They act on different objects; forces cancel only when they act on the same object.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Marble collisions
*Student:* Roll marbles of the same and different sizes along a ruler groove into each other; record and explain what happens after collisions.

*How to teach:* Roll marbles into each other along the groove; record same-size and different-size collisions. Before moving on, ask: "A moving marble hits an identical still marble head-on. What happens?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** A moving marble hits an identical still marble head-on. What happens?
  **A:** The moving one nearly stops and the other rolls away at about the same speed.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Collision analysis
*Student:* Watch slow-motion crash test footage and explain the forces on the car and the dummy; connect to seatbelts and airbags.

*How to teach:* Watch the crash-test video in slow motion. Discuss how seatbelts and airbags increase the time over which the dummy stops, reducing force.

- **Q:** How does an airbag reduce injury?
  **A:** It increases the stopping time and spreads the force over a larger area, so the force on the person is smaller.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on Newton's third law; draw force-pair diagrams for two collisions.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and the two force-pair diagrams. To check, ask: "A truck hits a bug. Which feels the larger force?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** A truck hits a bug. Which feels the larger force?
  **A:** Neither — the forces are equal; the bug has far less mass, so it undergoes a huge acceleration.

**Assessment:** Collision lab notes and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) State Newton's third law: for every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force. 2) Swimmer: pushes water back; water pushes swimmer forward. 3) Rocket: pushes gas down/back; gas pushes rocket up/forward. 4) Hammer hits nail: hammer pushes nail in; nail pushes back on hammer. 5) Truck hits bug: forces equal; bug's tiny mass gets huge acceleration. Force-pair diagrams: arrows equal length, opposite direction, on different objects. Collision notes: equal marbles — the moving marble stops and the other moves on; big hits small — both move forward, small faster; small hits big — small bounces back, big moves slowly.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: The Articles of Confederation

**Prepare:** Excerpts of the Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776) and Massachusetts Constitution (1780); Articles of Confederation; graph paper for a township grid; Northwest Ordinance; Washington's and Jefferson's letters about Shays' Rebellion.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Articles accomplished nothing. They won the war, negotiated the Treaty of Paris and created the land ordinances.
- Thinking Shays' Rebellion was against Britain. It was Massachusetts farmers (1786–87) protesting debts, taxes and farm foreclosures.

### Day 1 (Monday): State constitutions
*Student:* Compare excerpts of the 1776 Virginia Declaration of Rights and 1780 Massachusetts Constitution; list common principles.

*How to teach:* Compare the Virginia Declaration of Rights and Massachusetts Constitution; list common principles.

- **Q:** Name two principles both documents share.
  **A:** All power comes from the people; natural rights; freedom of the press; trial by jury; limits on government (separation of powers in MA).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Articles of Confederation
*Student:* Read selected articles and sort features into strengths and weaknesses.

*How to teach:* Read selected articles; sort strengths and weaknesses.

- **Q:** Why couldn't the Confederation Congress pay its debts?
  **A:** It had no power to tax; it could only ask the states for money.
- **Q:** How many states had to approve an amendment?
  **A:** All 13 (unanimous).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Land ordinances
*Student:* Draw a township grid using the Land Ordinance of 1785 and read the Northwest Ordinance's provisions on education and slavery.

*How to teach:* Draw a township grid (6 × 6 miles, 36 one-square-mile sections of 640 acres; section 16 for schools). Read the Northwest Ordinance's provisions.

- **Q:** What did the Northwest Ordinance (1787) say about slavery and education?
  **A:** Slavery was banned in the Northwest Territory, and 'schools and the means of education shall forever be encouraged.'
- **Q:** How many people did a territory need to apply for statehood?
  **A:** 60,000 free inhabitants.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Shays' Rebellion
*Student:* Read letters reacting to Shays' Rebellion (Washington, Jefferson) and explain why leaders called for a new constitution.

*How to teach:* Read Washington's and Jefferson's reactions to Shays' Rebellion.

- **Q:** How did Washington and Jefferson react differently?
  **A:** Washington was alarmed and saw the need for a stronger government; Jefferson, in Paris, wrote that 'a little rebellion now and then is a good thing.'
- **Q:** Why did Shays' Rebellion lead to the Constitutional Convention?
  **A:** It showed the national government couldn't keep order or respond to crises, convincing leaders the Articles needed replacing.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz plus a paragraph: 'Were the Articles a failure?'

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and argument paragraph.

- **Q:** What was the Land Ordinance of 1785?
  **A:** A law that surveyed western land into townships and sections for sale, setting aside a section for schools.

**Assessment:** Articles quiz and argument paragraph.

**Answer key:** Articles quiz key: weaknesses — no power to tax, no power to regulate interstate/foreign trade, no executive to enforce laws, no national courts, each state one vote, 9 of 13 states to pass laws, unanimous consent to amend, no national army (relied on states), no common currency control; strengths — waged the war, Treaty of Paris (1783), Land Ordinance of 1785, Northwest Ordinance of 1787. Argument paragraph rubric (1–4): clear claim (failure, partial success), two pieces of evidence (e.g., couldn't raise money to pay soldiers/stop Shays' Rebellion vs. Northwest Ordinance's lasting success), reasoning, counterclaim acknowledged.

## Spanish 1B: Unit 1 assessment: En el restaurante

**Prepare:** Print an authentic menu from a Spanish or Mexican restaurant's website. Write cue cards template. Read about tapas, comida corrida and sobremesa.

**Watch for:**
- Assuming tipping customs match the U.S. In Spain tips are small or optional (service is included); in Mexico about 10–15% is common.
- Using tú with the waiter. Use usted: ¿Qué me recomienda?

### Day 1 (Monday): Culture: comer fuera
*Student:* Read about tapas in Spain, comida corrida in Mexico, and sobremesa, and compare meal times with yours.

*How to teach:* Read about tapas, comida corrida and sobremesa; compare meal times with yours.

- **Q:** What is 'la sobremesa'?
  **A:** Relaxing and talking at the table after a meal.
- **Q:** When do many Spaniards eat dinner?
  **A:** Late — around 9 to 10 p.m. (lunch, the main meal, around 2–3 p.m.).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Authentic menu
*Student:* Read a printed authentic Spanish-language menu and answer 10 questions about dishes and prices.

*How to teach:* Read the authentic menu; answer 10 questions about dishes and prices.

- **Q:** What are 'entradas' or 'entrantes' on a menu?
  **A:** Appetizers/starters.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Role-play prep
*Student:* Write cue cards for a restaurant role-play using usted, polite requests, and venir.

*How to teach:* Write cue cards for the role-play.

- **Q:** Ask the waiter what he recommends.
  **A:** ¿Qué me recomienda?

### Day 4 (Thursday): Role-play
*Student:* Perform the restaurant role-play with a parent from arrival to paying la cuenta.

*How to teach:* Perform the role-play from arrival to paying.

- **Q:** Say: 'The chicken is delicious, thank you.'
  **A:** El pollo está delicioso, gracias.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 test
*Student:* Take the unit test: restaurant listening, menu reading, venir, and ser vs. estar.

*How to teach:* Give the unit 1 test.

- **Q:** Translate: 'My friends come from the restaurant.'
  **A:** Mis amigos vienen del restaurante.

**Assessment:** Restaurant role-play rubric and unit 1 test.

**Answer key:** Role-play rubric (1–4 each): greeting and seating, ordering with polite requests and usted (¿Qué me recomienda? Quisiera… ¿Me trae…?), describing food with ser/estar, asking for and paying la cuenta, comprehensibility. Unit 1 test: restaurant listening, menu reading, venir conjugation, ser vs estar. Culture: tapas = small shared plates in Spain; comida corrida = affordable fixed-price multi-course lunch in Mexico; sobremesa = time spent talking at the table after a meal; main meal in Spain around 2–3 p.m., dinner around 9–10 p.m.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Two-point perspective and cityscapes

**Prepare:** Ruler, pencils, fine-tip markers. Pull up Raphael's 'School of Athens' (1509–1511) and a modern cityscape on Google Arts & Culture.

**Watch for:**
- Putting vanishing points too close together. Correct: place them far apart (near paper edges) to avoid distortion.
- Tilting vertical lines. Correct: in two-point perspective, verticals stay vertical.

### Day 1 (Monday): Two-point boxes
*Student:* Draw 6 boxes in two-point perspective above, at and below eye level.

*How to teach:* Draw 6 boxes in two-point perspective above, at and below eye level.

- **Q:** How many vanishing points in two-point perspective and where?
  **A:** Two, both on the horizon line, usually far apart.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Building a house
*Student:* Draw a house with roof, doors and windows in two-point perspective.

*How to teach:* Draw a house with roof, doors, windows.

- **Q:** How do you find the center of a wall to place a roof peak?
  **A:** Draw an X corner to corner on the wall; the crossing point is the perspective center.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Art history
*Student:* Analyze perspective in Raphael's 'School of Athens' and a modern cityscape on Google Arts & Culture.

*How to teach:* Analyze perspective in 'School of Athens.'

- **Q:** Where is the vanishing point in 'School of Athens'?
  **A:** Near the center, between Plato and Aristotle, drawing the viewer's eye to them.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cityscape
*Student:* Begin an original cityscape with at least 5 buildings.

*How to teach:* Begin a cityscape with at least 5 buildings.

- **Q:** How can you show some buildings are closer?
  **A:** Make them larger, lower on the page or overlapping others, with more detail.

### Day 5 (Friday): Finish
*Student:* Ink and shade the cityscape.

*How to teach:* Ink and shade.

- **Q:** Why use a consistent light source when shading?
  **A:** So all shadows fall the same way, making the scene believable.

**Assessment:** Two-point cityscape.

**Answer key:** Two-point cityscape: 4 = 5+ buildings converging correctly to two VPs, vertical lines vertical, overlap and detail create depth, clean inking, consistent shading; 3 = minor errors; 2 = frequent convergence errors; 1 = incomplete.

## Physical Education 8: Adventure challenges and problem solving

**Prepare:** Gather a tarp or old sheet, blindfold, cones, hula hoop, carpet squares or pool noodles. Recruit siblings, friends or co-op members (3+ people). Set boundaries and safety rules: walk only in blind maze, spotters ready.

**Watch for:**
- Leaders talking over others. Correct: good teams listen to all ideas and test them.
- Giving up after one failed attempt. Correct: plan, try, debrief, adjust.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tarp flip
*Student:* With a group (siblings, co-op), stand on a tarp and flip it over without stepping off; debrief what worked.

*How to teach:* Group stands on a tarp and flips it without anyone stepping off; debrief: Who led? What worked?

- **Q:** What strategy usually works for the tarp flip?
  **A:** Crowd onto one half, fold/roll the open half over, step onto the flipped side, and repeat — communicating each step.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Blind maze
*Student:* Guide a blindfolded partner through a cone maze using only verbal directions, then switch roles.

*How to teach:* Guide a blindfolded partner through a cone maze with words only; switch roles.

- **Q:** What makes verbal directions effective?
  **A:** Specific, short, calm cues with distances and directions ('Two small steps left, stop'), and checking that the partner understood.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Helium hoop
*Student:* Lower a hula hoop to the ground as a group using only fingertips; try three strategies.

*How to teach:* Helium hoop: everyone's fingertips under the hoop; lower it to the ground without losing contact. Try three strategies.

- **Q:** Why does the hoop rise?
  **A:** Everyone pushes up slightly to keep contact, so combined upward pressure lifts it; the group must coordinate and slow down together.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Raft crossing
*Student:* Cross a 10-meter 'river' using only four carpet squares or pool noodles as a group.

*How to teach:* Cross a 10 m 'river' with four squares as a group; if anyone touches the 'water,' restart.

- **Q:** What made your team successful or unsuccessful?
  **A:** Answers vary; strong answers name planning, roles, communication and encouragement.

### Day 5 (Friday): Debrief
*Student:* Write a reflection on which communication behaviors helped or hurt the group and one thing you will do differently.

*How to teach:* The child writes a debrief reflection.

- **Q:** Name two communication behaviors that help a team.
  **A:** Listening to every idea, using encouraging words, clear instructions, checking for agreement, staying calm.

**Assessment:** Written debrief reflection.

**Answer key:** Debrief reflection rubric: 4 = describes at least two challenges with specific helpful and unhelpful behaviors, explains their effect on the group, and states one personal change; 3 = specific but one example; 2 = general ('we worked together'); 1 = incomplete.

## Health Education 8: Suicide prevention and self-harm awareness

**Prepare:** Parental preview: this week covers suicide warning signs. Read 988lifeline.org 'Know the signs' first. Make myth/fact cards. Be ready to talk calmly; if your child discloses thoughts of suicide, stay with them, listen and call or text 988 or the child's doctor.

**Watch for:**
- Believing asking 'Are you thinking about suicide?' plants the idea. Correct: research shows asking directly does not increase risk and often brings relief.
- Thinking you should keep a friend's secret if they ask. Correct: safety comes before secrecy — always tell a trusted adult.

### Day 1 (Monday): Facts and myths
*Student:* Sort myth/fact cards about suicide (e.g., asking about suicide does not plant the idea).

*How to teach:* Read each card aloud; the child sorts it as myth or fact and explains. Include: asking does not plant the idea (fact); people who talk about suicide won't do it (myth); suicide is preventable (fact).

- **Q:** True or false: People who talk about suicide are just seeking attention.
  **A:** False (myth). Talking about suicide is a warning sign and must be taken seriously.
- **Q:** True or false: Asking someone directly about suicide can help.
  **A:** True. It shows you care and opens the door to getting help.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Warning signs
*Student:* Identify verbal, behavioral and situational warning signs.

*How to teach:* Make three columns: verbal, behavioral, situational. Fill them together from 988lifeline.org.

- **Q:** Give one example of each type of warning sign.
  **A:** Verbal: 'I wish I were dead,' 'No one would miss me.' Behavioral: giving away belongings, withdrawing, increased substance use, saying goodbye, searching for means. Situational: a recent loss, breakup, bullying, major failure or family crisis.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ask-Listen-Tell
*Student:* Practice with a parent how to ask a friend directly, listen and tell a trusted adult.

*How to teach:* Model Ask-Listen-Tell: 'Are you thinking about killing yourself?' / listen without judging / 'Let's tell ____ together.' Then switch roles so the child practices.

- **Q:** What are the three steps, and what do you do if a friend asks you to keep it a secret?
  **A:** Ask directly, Listen without judgment, Tell a trusted adult right away. Do not keep it secret; say you care too much to keep it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Resources
*Student:* Save 988 and the Crisis Text Line in your phone and list three trusted adults.

*How to teach:* Help the child save 988 (call or text) and the Crisis Text Line (text HOME to 741741) and write three trusted adults with phone numbers.

- **Q:** How do you reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline?
  **A:** Call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org, 24/7. In immediate danger, call 911.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Complete a wallet card with warning signs and help steps; discuss feelings with a parent.

*How to teach:* The child completes a wallet card (warning signs on one side, Ask-Listen-Tell and contacts on the other). Close with an open conversation about feelings.

- **Q:** If a friend posts 'I can't do this anymore, goodbye everyone' late at night, what should you do?
  **A:** Treat it as an emergency: contact the friend if safe, tell a parent or trusted adult immediately, and call 911 or 988 to get help.

**Assessment:** Help-seeking wallet card and role-play.

**Answer key:** Wallet card: 4 = at least 3 accurate warning signs across categories, Ask-Listen-Tell steps, 988 and Crisis Text Line (741741), 3 named trusted adults; 3 = minor omission; 2 = missing resources or steps; 1 = incomplete. Role-play: proficient if the child asks the question directly, listens without arguing, refuses secrecy and names who they will tell.
