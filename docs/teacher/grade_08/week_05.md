# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 5

## English Language Arts 8: Narrative writing workshop and unit assessment

**Prepare:** Revision and editing checklists. Print 'The Open Window' by Saki for Friday (do not let the child read it early).

**Watch for:**
- Revising only for spelling. Revision means changing content and structure; editing is fixing errors.
- Punctuating dialogue wrong: the comma or period goes inside the quotation marks.

### Day 1 (Monday): Finish drafting
*Student:* Complete the story draft with dialogue and a resolution of the conflict.

*How to teach:* Finish the draft with dialogue and a resolution. Finish by asking: "Punctuate: she said I'm coming" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Punctuate: she said I'm coming
  **A:** She said, "I'm coming."

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Revise for pacing
*Student:* Revise using a checklist: vary sentence length, add transitions that signal time shifts, and cut slow passages.

*How to teach:* Revise using the checklist. Before moving on, ask: "How do short sentences affect pacing?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** How do short sentences affect pacing?
  **A:** They speed it up and create tension.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Edit & publish
*Student:* Edit for verbals, dialogue punctuation and commas; type and publish the final draft.

*How to teach:* Edit and publish. Check understanding with: "Correct: 'Look out' he yelled." If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Correct: "Look out" he yelled.
  **A:** "Look out!" he yelled.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Read-aloud
*Student:* Read the story aloud to family with expression and ask listeners to identify the twist and its clues.

*How to teach:* Child reads aloud; listeners identify the twist and clues. To check, ask: "What should listeners be able to name?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What should listeners be able to name?
  **A:** The twist and at least one clue that foreshadowed it.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 test
*Student:* Cold-read assessment on an unfamiliar short story ('The Open Window' by Saki) with irony and point-of-view questions.

*How to teach:* Cold-read 'The Open Window' and answer questions (key). Finish by asking: "Who is the unreliable storyteller in 'The Open Window'?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Who is the unreliable storyteller in 'The Open Window'?
  **A:** Vera, the niece.

**Assessment:** Published narrative (rubric) and Unit 1 cold-read test.

**Answer key:** Published narrative rubric (4 levels: plot/conflict, pacing, dialogue, conventions). Cold-read test answers for 'The Open Window': 1) Vera (the niece, 15) tells Framton that Mrs. Sappleton's husband and two brothers went shooting three years ago and drowned in a bog, and the window is left open for their return. 2) When the men return, Framton flees in terror. 3) Vera then invents a story that he fears dogs because he was hunted by pariah dogs in a cemetery on the Ganges. 4) Irony: situational — the 'ghosts' are real men and the reader, like Framton, was fooled. 5) Point of view: third person; we don't learn Vera's lie until the end. 6) Last line: 'Romance at short notice was her speciality' — 'romance' means made-up stories.

## Mathematics 8: Dilations

**Prepare:** Gather ruler, compass, graph paper; open GeoGebra's Dilate tool. Draw a sample triangle and point P for Day 1.

**Watch for:**
- Measuring from a vertex of the figure rather than from the center of dilation. Every distance is measured from the center along a ray.
- Thinking dilations change angles. Have the child measure: angles stay the same, only lengths scale.

### Day 1 (Monday): Projecting and scaling
*Student:* Use a ruler to dilate a triangle from a point P with scale factor 2 and 1/2 by measuring along rays; compare side lengths and angles.

*How to teach:* Model: draw rays from P through each vertex, measure the distance, and mark the image at 2 times that distance (and at half for k = 1/2). The child compares side lengths and angles of the images to the original.

- **Q:** Vertex A is 3 cm from center P. Where is A' for scale factor 2?
  **A:** On ray PA, 6 cm from P.
- **Q:** A side is 4 cm. How long is its image for k = 1/2?
  **A:** 2 cm.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Circular grid
*Student:* Dilate shapes on a circular (polar) grid, then explain why dilations preserve angles but not lengths.

*How to teach:* Dilate shapes on a circular grid where circles are evenly spaced from the center. Ask the child to measure angles before and after and explain.

- **Q:** Why do dilations keep angles the same but change lengths?
  **A:** Each segment is scaled by the same factor and stays parallel to the original (or on the same line), so the directions of sides — and therefore the angles between them — don't change, while every length is multiplied by k.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dilations on a square grid
*Student:* Dilate polygons on a coordinate grid with center at the origin and write the rule (x, y) -> (kx, ky) for k = 3, 1/2 and 1.5.

*How to teach:* With center at the origin, the rule is (x, y) → (kx, ky). Do one example together, then the child dilates polygons for k = 3, 1/2 and 1.5.

- **Q:** Dilate (4, −2) with center at the origin by k = 1/2, 3 and 1.5.
  **A:** (2, −1), (12, −6) and (6, −3).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Scale factor reasoning
*Student:* Find the scale factor and center given a figure and image; solve 6 problems including scale factors less than 1.

*How to teach:* Show how to find the scale factor (image length ÷ original length) and the center (draw lines through corresponding points; they meet at the center). The child solves 6 problems.

- **Q:** A segment of length 6 has an image of length 4. What is the scale factor?
  **A:** 4/6 = 2/3 — a reduction since k < 1.
- **Q:** If k = 1, what does the dilation do?
  **A:** Nothing — the image is identical to the original.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: perform two dilations and find a missing scale factor and center.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (key below). Review & check day: have the child redo any missed problem in the notebook and explain the error in one sentence.

- **Q:** Is a dilation a rigid motion? Explain.
  **A:** No (unless k = 1) — it changes lengths, though it keeps angles.

**Assessment:** Quiz on dilations by hand and with coordinates.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Dilate A(2, 4), B(6, 2), C(4, 0) with center origin, k = 1/2 → A'(1, 2), B'(3, 1), C'(2, 0). 2) Same triangle, k = 3 → (6, 12), (18, 6), (12, 0). 3) A side of length 5 has an image of length 15 → k = 3; the center is found where lines through corresponding vertices (A to A', B to B') intersect. 4) A 12-unit side becomes 8 → k = 2/3 (a reduction). 3 points per item for correct image/value with work shown.

## Science 8 (Integrated): Engineering challenge: protect the egg

**Prepare:** Raw eggs (or water balloons), straws, tape, cardboard, cotton balls, rubber bands, plastic bags. Choose a 2-m drop site with easy cleanup. Prepare the Unit 1 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the goal is a stronger (harder) shell. Softer, crushable designs work better because they increase stopping time.
- Changing many things at once when iterating. Change one main feature so you know what helped.

### Day 1 (Monday): Define the problem
*Student:* Write criteria and constraints for a device that protects an egg in a 2-meter drop or a cart crash, using limited materials.

*How to teach:* Define criteria (must survive a 2-m drop; mass under 100 g) and constraints (only listed materials, 30 minutes to build). The child writes both lists.

- **Q:** Criteria vs constraints?
  **A:** Criteria are what a successful design must do; constraints are limits (materials, time, cost, size).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Design & decision matrix
*Student:* Sketch 3 designs and choose one with a decision matrix scoring cost, mass and expected protection.

*How to teach:* The child sketches 3 designs and scores them in a decision matrix (cost, mass, expected protection). Before moving on, ask: "Why use a decision matrix?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Why use a decision matrix?
  **A:** It compares designs fairly against the criteria instead of choosing by gut feeling.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Build & test
*Student:* Build the prototype and test it; record results and observe how the device increases collision time.

*How to teach:* Build and drop. Observe and record whether the egg survived and how the device changed during impact.

- **Q:** Why does padding protect the egg?
  **A:** It increases the time it takes the egg to stop, so the force on it is smaller.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Iterate
*Student:* Redesign based on test data, retest, and compare results with a family member's design.

*How to teach:* Redesign one feature based on data, retest, and compare with another family member's design. To check, ask: "Your egg cracked on the side that hit first. What one change makes sense?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Your egg cracked on the side that hit first. What one change makes sense?
  **A:** Add more crushable padding on that side (or a parachute to reduce speed).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 test
*Student:* Unit test on motion, forces and Newton's laws; submit engineering notebook pages.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 test and collect the notebook pages. Finish by asking: "A 4-kg object accelerates at 2 m/s². Net force?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** A 4-kg object accelerates at 2 m/s². Net force?
  **A:** 8 N.

**Assessment:** Engineering notebook rubric and Unit 1 test.

**Answer key:** Engineering notebook rubric (4 levels): criteria and constraints listed; 3 labeled sketches; decision matrix with scores; test data; redesign explained using evidence; reflection connects to force and collision time. Unit 1 test sample answers: speed = d/t (6 m in 3 s → 2 m/s); net force of 25 N right and 10 N left → 15 N right; F = ma (4 kg × 2 m/s² = 8 N); Newton's first law (inertia); third law pairs on different objects; crumple zones increase collision time to reduce force.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: The Constitutional Convention

**Prepare:** Constitution transcript; make delegate profile cards (Madison, Washington, Franklin, Sherman, Gouverneur Morris, James Wilson).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Three-Fifths Clause meant enslaved people were 'three-fifths of a person' with rights. It was a formula for counting population for representation and direct taxes; enslaved people had no rights under it and gained power for their enslavers.
- Believing all delegates agreed. The Convention was full of debate and compromise; some delegates refused to sign.

### Day 1 (Monday): Delegates
*Student:* Create profile cards for Madison, Washington, Franklin, Sherman, Morris and Wilson.

*How to teach:* Create profile cards for six delegates.

- **Q:** Why is Madison called the 'Father of the Constitution'?
  **A:** He drafted the Virginia Plan, took detailed notes of the debates and later championed ratification and the Bill of Rights.
- **Q:** What role did Gouverneur Morris play?
  **A:** He wrote the final wording of the Constitution, including the Preamble 'We the People of the United States.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Representation debate
*Student:* Role-play the Virginia vs New Jersey Plan debate and write the Great Compromise in your own words.

*How to teach:* Role-play Virginia vs New Jersey Plan; write the Great Compromise.

- **Q:** Explain the Great Compromise.
  **A:** Congress has two houses: the House of Representatives based on population and the Senate with equal representation (two per state).
- **Q:** Who proposed it?
  **A:** Roger Sherman (and Oliver Ellsworth) of Connecticut.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Slavery and the Constitution
*Student:* Analyze the Three-Fifths Clause, slave trade clause and fugitive slave clause; discuss how they contradicted the Declaration.

*How to teach:* Analyze the Three-Fifths, slave-trade and fugitive-slave clauses and how they contradicted the Declaration.

- **Q:** How did the Three-Fifths Clause benefit slaveholding states?
  **A:** Counting enslaved people (who couldn't vote) increased Southern seats in the House and electoral votes.
- **Q:** How did these clauses contradict the Declaration?
  **A:** The Declaration said all men are created equal with the right to liberty, yet the Constitution protected slavery.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Other debates
*Student:* Examine debates on the executive, the commerce clause and the status of American Indian nations.

*How to teach:* Examine debates on the executive, commerce and Native nations.

- **Q:** Why did delegates create a single executive?
  **A:** To provide energy and accountability (Wilson argued for it), while limiting power with checks; others feared a single executive would resemble a king.
- **Q:** How does the Constitution treat Indian nations?
  **A:** The commerce clause gives Congress power to regulate commerce 'with the Indian tribes,' treating them as separate nations dealt with by the federal government.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on compromises and their resolutions.

*How to teach:* Give the compromise quiz.

- **Q:** What did the Electoral College compromise resolve?
  **A:** How to choose the president — not directly by Congress nor purely by popular vote, but by electors chosen by the states.

**Assessment:** Compromise quiz.

**Answer key:** Compromise quiz key: Virginia Plan (Madison) — bicameral legislature, representation by population (favored large states); New Jersey Plan (Paterson) — one house, equal votes per state (small states); Great (Connecticut) Compromise (Roger Sherman) — House by population, Senate with two senators per state; Three-Fifths Compromise — three-fifths of enslaved people counted for representation and direct taxes; slave trade clause — Congress could not ban importation of enslaved people before 1808; fugitive slave clause — escaped enslaved people had to be returned; Electoral College — compromise between election by Congress and direct popular vote; commerce clause — Congress regulates trade with foreign nations, among the states and with Indian tribes.

## Spanish 1B: Mi casa y mi dormitorio

**Prepare:** Floor plan paper, sticky notes. Vocabulary: la cocina, la sala, el comedor, el dormitorio, el baño, el garaje, el sótano, el jardín, la escalera, el pasillo; furniture: la cama, la cómoda, el armario, la alfombra, el sofá, la lámpara, el escritorio, el estante, la mesita, el espejo, la cortina, el sillón, la silla, la mesa, el televisor.

**Watch for:**
- Colors not agreeing. Rojo/roja/rojos/rojas; azul/azules (no gender); marrón/marrones; naranja and rosa often don't change.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Colors

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mi dormitorio
*Student:* Write a 10-sentence description of your bedroom using hay, estar + prepositions, and colors.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Drawing unit project and critique

**Prepare:** Drawing materials; critique sheet (Describe, Analyze, Interpret, Evaluate — Feldman method).

**Watch for:**

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Draft
*Student:* Lightly sketch the chosen composition.

*How to teach:* Lightly sketch the chosen composition.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Render

*How to teach:* Add value and detail.

## Physical Education 8: Leading a group activity

**Prepare:** Cones, balls, a lesson-plan template (warm-up, activity, rules, safety, cool-down). Arrange a group of family or friends for Thursday.

**Watch for:**
- Explaining too long. Correct: explain in under a minute, demonstrate, then play and fix as you go.
- Planning games that eliminate players. Correct: choose games where everyone stays active (re-entry rules).

### Day 1 (Monday): Leadership styles
*Student:* Discuss what makes a good coach or team captain; list five leadership behaviors.

*How to teach:* Discuss good coaches; list five leadership behaviors (clear instructions, encouragement, fairness, listening, modeling effort).

- **Q:** Name three behaviors of a good team captain.
  **A:** Encourages teammates, communicates clearly, shows good sportsmanship, leads by example, includes everyone.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plan
*Student:* Write a 20-minute plan: warm-up, one tag or relay game with rules, and cool-down.

*How to teach:* Write a 20-minute plan with timing.

- **Q:** What three parts should every session have?
  **A:** Warm-up, main activity, cool-down.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Modify
*Student:* Write two modifications so a younger sibling or someone with limited mobility can play your game fully.

*How to teach:* Write two modifications (larger target, softer ball, walking rule, partner assistance, shorter distance).

- **Q:** Give a modification for someone with limited mobility in a tag game.
  **A:** Let them tag with a pool noodle, use a smaller area, or give them a role like 'freer' who can unfreeze players while seated or walking.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Lead
*Student:* Lead your planned session for family or a homeschool group, explaining rules and safety clearly.

*How to teach:* The child leads the session, explaining rules and safety.

- **Q:** What should you check before starting?
  **A:** Space is clear of hazards, boundaries are marked, equipment is safe, everyone knows the rules and stop signal.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluate
*Student:* Collect a quick thumbs-up/down from participants and self-evaluate your leadership on a rubric.

*How to teach:* Collect thumbs up/down; self-evaluate with the rubric.

- **Q:** What one thing would you change next time?
  **A:** Answers vary; strong answers are specific (e.g., shorter explanation, bigger boundary).

**Assessment:** Leadership rubric for the led session.

**Answer key:** Leadership rubric (4 levels): Planning (warm-up, game, cool-down timed); Communication (clear, brief rules with demo); Safety (boundaries, stop signal); Inclusion (modifications used, everyone active); Enthusiasm/feedback. Proficient = 3+ in every row.

## Health Education 8: Grief, loss and change

**Prepare:** Choose a picture book or article on grief (e.g., KidsHealth 'Death and Grief'). Be sensitive to any recent losses in the family; this week can bring up strong feelings.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking grief follows five stages in order. Correct: grief is not linear — feelings come and go, overlap and return, especially on anniversaries.
- Believing the best way to help is to say 'I know how you feel' or 'They're in a better place.' Correct: listen, acknowledge the loss and offer specific help.

### Day 1 (Monday): Kinds of loss
*Student:* List types of loss (death, moving, divorce, friendship ending, pet) and how each may feel.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm types of loss together and write one feeling each might bring (sadness, anger, confusion, relief, guilt).

- **Q:** Name three types of loss other than death.
  **A:** Examples: moving, parents' divorce, a friendship ending, losing a pet, injury that ends a sport, a change of school.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Grief reactions
*Student:* Learn that grief is not linear and identify common physical and emotional reactions.

*How to teach:* Read the article together. Draw a tangled 'scribble' line vs. a straight line to show grief is not linear. List physical (tiredness, appetite change) and emotional reactions.

- **Q:** Name one physical and one emotional reaction to grief.
  **A:** Physical: fatigue, trouble sleeping, headaches, stomachaches, appetite changes. Emotional: sadness, anger, guilt, numbness, anxiety, relief.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Supporting others
*Student:* Write what to say and not say to a grieving friend.

*How to teach:* Make a T-chart: helpful vs. unhelpful things to say. Practice saying one helpful line aloud.

- **Q:** Give one helpful and one unhelpful thing to say to a grieving friend.
  **A:** Helpful: 'I'm so sorry. I'm here if you want to talk,' 'Do you want company at lunch?' Unhelpful: 'At least…,' 'I know exactly how you feel,' 'You should be over it by now.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Coping plan
*Student:* Choose three healthy coping strategies (journaling, activity, talking) for times of change.

*How to teach:* The child picks three healthy coping strategies and writes when and how they'd use each. Point out unhealthy coping (isolating, substances) to avoid.

- **Q:** When should grief be shared with an adult or counselor?
  **A:** When it does not ease over time, interferes with sleep, school or eating, or includes hopelessness or thoughts of self-harm.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a letter of support to a fictional friend experiencing a loss.

*How to teach:* The child writes a letter of support to a fictional friend, using phrases from the helpful list and offering specific help.

- **Q:** What makes a support letter helpful?
  **A:** It acknowledges the loss, expresses care, avoids minimizing, offers specific help and reminds the friend they're not alone.

**Assessment:** Support letter.

**Answer key:** Support letter: 4 = names the loss, acknowledges feelings, no minimizing phrases, offers at least two specific forms of help, mentions a trusted adult or resource; 3 = caring and specific but missing one element; 2 = generic or includes an unhelpful phrase; 1 = incomplete.
