# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 21

## English Language Arts 8: Form and sound: Dunbar, Lazarus and Frost

**Prepare:** Print Dunbar's 'Sympathy' and 'We Wear the Mask,' Lazarus's 'The New Colossus' and Frost's 'The Road Not Taken.' Review the terms Petrarchan sonnet (octave + sestet, turn) and rhyme scheme.

**Watch for:**
- Reading 'The Road Not Taken' as simple advice to be a nonconformist. The poem says both roads were worn 'really about the same'; the speaker predicts he'll later tell it differently.
- Marking rhyme scheme by spelling instead of sound. Read the line endings aloud.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dunbar
*Student:* Read 'Sympathy' and 'We Wear the Mask'; analyze the caged-bird and mask metaphors and the effect of the refrains.

*How to teach:* Read both Dunbar poems aloud. Child explains the caged bird and mask metaphors and how the refrains build intensity.

- **Q:** Why does the caged bird sing in 'Sympathy'?
  **A:** Not from joy — 'It is not a carol of joy or glee, / But a prayer that he sends from his heart's deep core' — a plea for freedom.
- **Q:** What does the mask hide?
  **A:** Pain and suffering; people 'grin and lie' to survive in a world that won't see their tears.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): 'The New Colossus'
*Student:* Mark the rhyme scheme of the Petrarchan sonnet and explain how the turn changes the meaning.

*How to teach:* Child marks the rhyme scheme of 'The New Colossus' and finds the turn. Before moving on, ask: "How does the turn change the meaning?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** How does the turn change the meaning?
  **A:** The octave contrasts the statue with the conquering Colossus of Rhodes; the sestet gives her voice, welcoming 'your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): 'The Road Not Taken'
*Student:* Analyze the poem's structure and debate whether its ending is sincere or ironic.

*How to teach:* Analyze structure of 'The Road Not Taken'; debate sincere vs ironic. Check understanding with: "What evidence suggests irony?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** What evidence suggests irony?
  **A:** The roads were 'really about the same' and 'equally lay / In leaves no step had trodden black,' yet he predicts he'll say 'with a sigh' that he took 'the one less traveled by.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Structure comparison
*Student:* Write a comparison of how a sonnet and a rhymed lyric each shape meaning, citing two poems.

*How to teach:* Child writes the structure comparison citing two poems. To check, ask: "What is a Petrarchan sonnet?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** What is a Petrarchan sonnet?
  **A:** 14 lines: an octave (ABBAABBA) and a sestet (often CDCDCD), with a turn between them.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on poetic form and sound devices.

*How to teach:* Give the form and sound quiz. Finish by asking: "Give an alliteration from 'The New Colossus.'" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Give an alliteration from 'The New Colossus.'
  **A:** 'sea-washed, sunset gates' or 'world-wide welcome.'

**Assessment:** Structure comparison and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) 'The New Colossus' rhyme scheme: ABBAABBA CDCDCD (Petrarchan sonnet). 2) The turn comes at line 9, when the statue speaks: 'Keep, ancient lands, your storied pomp!' 3) 'Sympathy' refrain: 'I know what (why) the caged bird ...' 4) 'We Wear the Mask' — the mask that 'grins and lies' hides pain (Black Americans' suffering hidden from a hostile world). 5) 'The Road Not Taken' stanzas: 4 stanzas of 5 lines, ABAAB. 6) Define alliteration and personification with examples. Structure comparison rubric: explains how the sonnet's octave/sestet turn shifts from describing to the statue's voice, and how Frost's regular rhyme and the 'sigh' ending create ambiguity; cites both.

## Mathematics 8: Spheres and dimensions as functions

**Prepare:** A ball, an orange, rice, Desmos. Prepare the Unit 5 review set and test.

**Watch for:**
- Using r² instead of r³ for sphere volume. Volume is three-dimensional, so the unit is cubed.
- Thinking doubling the radius doubles the volume. For a cylinder it quadruples (r²); for a sphere it multiplies by 8.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sphere volume
*Student:* Compare a sphere to a cylinder of the same radius and height 2r to justify V = (4/3) pi r^3; find volumes of 4 balls.

*How to teach:* Explain that a sphere fills 2/3 of a cylinder with the same radius and height 2r: (2/3)(πr²·2r) = (4/3)πr³. Child measures and computes the volume of 4 balls.

- **Q:** Find the volume of a sphere with radius 3 cm.
  **A:** (4/3)π(27) = 36π ≈ 113.1 cm³.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Composite volumes
*Student:* Find volumes of composite solids like an ice-cream cone with a hemisphere on top and a grain silo.

*How to teach:* Model splitting a composite solid into parts and adding. Child does the ice-cream cone and silo.

- **Q:** Ice cream cone r = 3 cm, h = 10 cm, topped by a hemisphere r = 3 cm. Total volume?
  **A:** Cone 30π + hemisphere 18π = 48π ≈ 150.8 cm³.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Volume as a function
*Student:* Graph volume of a cylinder as a function of height (linear) and of radius (nonlinear) in Desmos; compare.

*How to teach:* In Desmos graph V = π(2²)h (r fixed at 2) and V = πr²(5) (h fixed at 5); compare shapes. Check understanding with: "Which graph is linear and why?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Which graph is linear and why?
  **A:** V = 4πh is linear (h to the first power); V = 5πr² is a curve because r is squared.
- **Q:** If radius doubles (h fixed), what happens to cylinder volume?
  **A:** It multiplies by 4.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit 5 review
*Student:* Review functions and volume with a 12-problem mixed set.

*How to teach:* Child completes the 12-problem mixed review; check and rework errors. To check, ask: "f(x) = −2x + 5. Find f(−3)." and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** f(x) = −2x + 5. Find f(−3).
  **A:** 11.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 test
*Student:* Unit test on functions and volume.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test. Finish by asking: "Find the volume of a hemisphere of radius 6." — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Find the volume of a hemisphere of radius 6.
  **A:** (1/2)(4/3)π(216) = 144π ≈ 452.4.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test suggested items: 1) Is {(0, 1), (1, 1), (2, 3)} a function? Yes. 2) f(x) = −2x + 5, f(−3) = 11. 3) Compare rates: table +4 per step vs y = 3x + 10 → table grows faster. 4) Cylinder r = 3, h = 7 → 63π ≈ 197.9. 5) Cone r = 6, h = 5 → 60π ≈ 188.5. 6) Sphere r = 3 → 36π ≈ 113.1. 7) Hemisphere r = 6 → 144π ≈ 452.4. 8) Composite ice cream: cone r = 3, h = 10 plus hemisphere r = 3 → 48π ≈ 150.8. 9) Is V = 4πh linear? Yes. Is V = 5πr²? No. 1–2 points each.

## Science 8 (Integrated): The geologic time scale

**Prepare:** 5 m of adding-machine tape or toilet paper, meter stick, markers; 100 coins or M&Ms and a container.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking humans and dinosaurs lived together. Non-avian dinosaurs died out about 66 million years ago; humans appeared about 300,000 years ago.
- Thinking half-life means half the time remains. It's the time for half the atoms to decay.

### Day 1 (Monday): Timeline to scale
*Student:* Make a 4.6 m timeline (1 mm = 1 million years) and mark major events like first life and dinosaurs.

*How to teach:* Make the 4.6-m timeline; mark events. Finish by asking: "Where is the dinosaur extinction on a 4.6-m timeline?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** Where is the dinosaur extinction on a 4.6-m timeline?
  **A:** 6.6 cm from the present end.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Eras and extinctions
*Student:* Research the 5 mass extinctions and add them to the timeline.

*How to teach:* Research the 5 mass extinctions and add them. Before moving on, ask: "Which was largest?" and have the child point to evidence or show work.

- **Q:** Which was largest?
  **A:** End-Permian, about 252 million years ago.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Absolute dating
*Student:* Model radioactive half-life by shaking coins and removing 'decayed' ones; graph the results.

*How to teach:* Shake coins; remove 'decayed' ones; repeat and graph. Check understanding with: "After 3 half-lives, what fraction remains?" If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** After 3 half-lives, what fraction remains?
  **A:** 1/8.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Explanation
*Student:* Write a scientific explanation of how rock-strata evidence is used to build the time scale.

*How to teach:* Write a scientific explanation of how strata build the time scale. To check, ask: "Relative vs absolute dating?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** Relative vs absolute dating?
  **A:** Relative orders events; absolute gives an age in years (radiometric).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on the geologic time scale and dating methods.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Finish by asking: "How old is Earth?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** How old is Earth?
  **A:** About 4.6 billion years.

**Assessment:** Timeline model and written explanation.

**Answer key:** Timeline: 4.6 m at 1 mm = 1 million years; first life ~3.5–3.8 billion years ago (3.5–3.8 m from present end); Cambrian explosion ~541 mya (54 cm); dinosaurs appear ~230 mya (23 cm); non-avian dinosaurs extinct ~66 mya (6.6 cm); Homo sapiens ~0.3 mya (0.3 mm). Five mass extinctions: end-Ordovician (~445 mya), Late Devonian (~372), end-Permian (~252, largest), end-Triassic (~201), end-Cretaceous (~66). Explanation rubric: rock strata order + index fossils give relative ages; radiometric dating gives absolute ages; major changes in fossils mark boundaries between eras and periods.

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: Compromises over slavery's expansion

**Prepare:** Maps of free and slave states in 1820 and 1850; Henry Clay speech excerpt (1850); Wilmot Proviso text; account of the Anthony Burns case (1854).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Missouri Compromise banned slavery everywhere north of 36°30'. It applied to the Louisiana Purchase territory (except Missouri).
- Believing the Compromise of 1850 settled the issue. Its stronger Fugitive Slave Act enraged Northerners and deepened sectional conflict.

### Day 1 (Monday): Missouri Compromise
*Student:* Color a map showing the 36°30' line and explain Henry Clay's role in balancing free and slave states.

*How to teach:* Color the 1820 map with the 36°30' line; explain Clay's role.

- **Q:** What did the Missouri Compromise do?
  **A:** Admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as free, keeping the Senate balanced, and banned slavery in the Louisiana Territory north of 36°30' (except Missouri).
- **Q:** Why was Henry Clay called the 'Great Compromiser'?
  **A:** He brokered the Missouri Compromise, the 1833 tariff compromise and the Compromise of 1850.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Wilmot Proviso
*Student:* Read the one-sentence Wilmot Proviso (1846) and explain why it divided Congress along sectional rather than party lines.

*How to teach:* Read the Wilmot Proviso; explain the sectional split.

- **Q:** Why did the Wilmot Proviso divide Congress by section rather than party?
  **A:** Northern Democrats and Whigs voted for banning slavery in the Mexican Cession while Southerners of both parties opposed, showing slavery outweighed party loyalty.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compromise of 1850
*Student:* Chart each part of the Compromise of 1850 (including California statehood) and score which side gained more.

*How to teach:* Chart the Compromise of 1850; score which side gained more.

- **Q:** What did the North gain in 1850?
  **A:** California admitted as a free state; slave trade ended in Washington, D.C.
- **Q:** What did the South gain?
  **A:** A stronger Fugitive Slave Act; possibility of slavery in New Mexico and Utah by popular sovereignty.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fugitive Slave Act
*Student:* Read accounts of the Anthony Burns case in Boston and explain how the act radicalized Northerners.

*How to teach:* Read about Anthony Burns; explain how the Act radicalized Northerners.

- **Q:** What happened in the Anthony Burns case (1854)?
  **A:** Burns, who had escaped from Virginia, was arrested in Boston; despite protests and a rescue attempt, federal troops marched him to a ship back to slavery while thousands watched — turning many Northerners against the law.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on compromises and a map-labeling task.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and map task.

- **Q:** What is popular sovereignty?
  **A:** Letting settlers of a territory vote on whether to allow slavery.

**Assessment:** Compromise quiz and map.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: Missouri Compromise (1820, Henry Clay) — Missouri admitted as a slave state, Maine as free (12–12 balance), slavery banned in the rest of the Louisiana Territory north of 36°30'; Wilmot Proviso (1846) — would have banned slavery in any land acquired from Mexico; passed the House, failed in the Senate; voting split North vs South rather than by party; Compromise of 1850 — California admitted free, slave trade (not slavery) banned in D.C., New Mexico and Utah territories decide by popular sovereignty, Texas–New Mexico boundary settled and Texas debt assumed, stronger Fugitive Slave Act (to South). Fugitive Slave Act — federal officials must help capture freedom seekers, no jury trial, citizens could be fined for helping. Map: free vs slave states labeled correctly for 1820 and 1850.

## Spanish 1B: Preterite of ir and ser

**Prepare:** Photo album. A Spanish Proficiency Exercises clip about a trip (UT Austin, e.g., 'Describe a trip').

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 'fue' always means 'went.' Ir and ser share preterite forms: 'Fue a Madrid' (went) vs 'Fue divertido' (was).
- Adding accents: fui, fue have no accent.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ir/ser preterite
*Student:* Learn that ir and ser share preterite forms and determine meaning from context in 15 sentences.

*How to teach:* Teach the shared forms; determine meaning in 15 sentences.

- **Q:** Translate: 'Fui al zoológico. Fue muy divertido.'
  **A:** I went to the zoo. It was very fun.
- **Q:** Conjugate ir (preterite) for nosotros.
  **A:** fuimos.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): ¿Adónde fuiste?
*Student:* Write where each family member went last summer and how it was: Fuimos a la playa; fue divertido.

*How to teach:* Write where family members went last summer and how it was.

- **Q:** Write: 'My parents went to Mexico; it was fantastic.'
  **A:** Mis padres fueron a México; fue fantástico.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Listening
*Student:* Listen to a native speaker describe a trip (Spanish Proficiency Exercises) and note where they went and what they did.

*How to teach:* Listen to a native speaker; note where they went and what they did.

- **Q:** If you hear 'Fuimos a las montañas y acampamos,' what did they do?
  **A:** They went to the mountains and camped.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Conversation
*Student:* Have an unscripted conversation with a parent about a past trip, asking at least five follow-up questions.

*How to teach:* Unscripted conversation with five follow-up questions.

- **Q:** Ask: 'Where did you go and with whom?'
  **A:** ¿Adónde fuiste y con quién?

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take an ir/ser preterite quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the ir/ser quiz.

- **Q:** Ask: 'How was the trip?'
  **A:** ¿Qué tal fue el viaje? (or ¿Cómo fue el viaje?)

**Assessment:** Ir/ser preterite quiz and conversation rubric.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: ir/ser — fui, fuiste, fue, fuimos, fuisteis, fueron. Context items: 'Fuimos a la playa' = we went; 'El viaje fue fantástico' = was; 'Ellos fueron al museo' = went; '¿Qué tal fue?' = how was it. Conversation rubric (1–4): describes a past trip in the preterite, asks/answers 5 follow-up questions (¿Adónde fuiste? ¿Con quién? ¿Qué hiciste? ¿Qué comiste? ¿Qué tal fue?), comprehensibility.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Monologue work

**Prepare:** Four age-appropriate public-domain monologues (e.g., Puck's epilogue from 'A Midsummer Night's Dream,' Portia's 'quality of mercy' speech from 'The Merchant of Venice,' a speech from 'The Importance of Being Earnest'). Highlighters.

**Watch for:**
- Memorizing the words without meaning. Correct: understand every line and paraphrase it first.
- Rushing. Correct: use pauses and emphasis tied to the beats.

### Day 1 (Monday): Selecting
*Student:* Read 4 age-appropriate monologues and choose one.

*How to teach:* Read four and choose one.

- **Q:** What makes a monologue a good choice?
  **A:** Age-appropriate, understandable, has a clear objective and emotional journey, right length (about 1 minute).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Research
*Student:* Research the play's story, playwright and period.

*How to teach:* Research play, playwright, period.

- **Q:** Who wrote 'A Midsummer Night's Dream' and when?
  **A:** William Shakespeare, around 1595–96.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Analysis
*Student:* Mark objectives, beats and emphasis.

*How to teach:* Mark objectives, beats, emphasis.

- **Q:** Why mark operative words?
  **A:** They are the key words to stress so meaning is clear.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Memorize
*Student:* Memorize using the line-by-line method.

*How to teach:* Memorize line by line (add one line at a time, repeat from the start).

- **Q:** Describe the line-by-line method.
  **A:** Learn line 1, then lines 1–2, then 1–3, always going back to the beginning.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform off-book for family.

*How to teach:* Perform off-book.

- **Q:** What does 'off-book' mean?
  **A:** Performing from memory without the script.

**Assessment:** Monologue off-book check.

**Answer key:** Off-book check: 4 = fully memorized (no more than 1 prompt), clear objective, beats show in shifts of voice/body, good projection and diction; 3 = 2–3 prompts; 2 = many prompts; 1 = not memorized.

## Physical Education 8: Archery-style and bowling target skills

**Prepare:** Bean bags, 10 plastic bottles (add a little water/sand), playground ball, target poster with three rings (e.g., 10/5/1 points), tape lines at 3 distances.

**Watch for:**
- Rushing shots under pressure. Correct: a consistent pre-shot routine improves accuracy.
- Using too much force for accuracy. Correct: controlled force and follow-through toward the target.

### Day 1 (Monday): Bocce/bean bag
*Student:* Play bocce-style target toss, using underhand toss and controlled force.

*How to teach:* Bocce-style toss: underhand, step with opposite foot, follow-through toward the jack.

- **Q:** How do you win a bocce frame?
  **A:** The team with the ball(s) closest to the target ball (pallino/jack) scores a point for each ball closer than the opponent's closest.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Bowling
*Student:* Bowl a playground ball at 10 plastic-bottle pins; learn approach and release.

*How to teach:* Bowl at pins: 4-step approach, swing arm like a pendulum, release at the ankle, follow-through.

- **Q:** How is a strike recorded and scored?
  **A:** X; 10 plus the pins from the next two balls.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Target throw
*Student:* Throw at a three-ring target from three distances and record scores.

*How to teach:* Target throws from three distances; record scores.

- **Q:** Why might accuracy drop at longer distances?
  **A:** More force is needed, small errors in release angle grow, and control decreases.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Routine
*Student:* Design a personal pre-shot routine and compare scores with and without it.

*How to teach:* Design a pre-shot routine (breath, look, picture, go); compare scores with and without.

- **Q:** Why use a pre-shot routine?
  **A:** It focuses attention, calms nerves and makes each attempt more consistent.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Compete in a target-skills triathlon (bowling, bocce, target throw) and record totals.

*How to teach:* Target triathlon: record totals.

- **Q:** What do all target games have in common?
  **A:** Accuracy toward a target with controlled force, aim and follow-through.

**Assessment:** Target triathlon score sheet.

**Answer key:** Triathlon score sheet: totals correct for bowling (frame scoring), bocce (points per frame) and target throw (sum of ring values). Proficient: correct scoring, uses routine on every attempt, and a brief note on which game was strongest.

## Health Education 8: Stimulants, inhalants, caffeine and cannabis potency

**Prepare:** Print NIDA for Teens pages on prescription stimulants, inhalants, caffeine and marijuana. Make a blank drug-facts chart (drug, effects, risks, CA law). Have energy-drink/soda labels for caffeine counts.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking prescription drugs are safe because a doctor prescribed them. Correct: safe only for the person prescribed at the right dose; misuse can cause heart problems, addiction and overdose.
- Believing cannabis today is the same as decades ago. Correct: THC potency has risen from about 4% in the 1990s to often 20%+ in flower and 60–90%+ in concentrates/vapes.

### Day 1 (Monday): Prescription stimulants
*Student:* Learn why 'study drugs' are risky when misused and the law on sharing prescriptions.

*How to teach:* Explain ADHD stimulants (Adderall, Ritalin) help people with ADHD but misuse can cause rapid heart rate, anxiety, insomnia and addiction; sharing or selling a prescription is illegal.

- **Q:** Do 'study drugs' improve grades for people without ADHD?
  **A:** Research shows no real improvement in grades; they carry risks such as heart problems, anxiety and dependence.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Inhalants
*Student:* Learn how household inhalants damage the brain and can cause sudden death.

*How to teach:* Read about inhalants (aerosols, glue, gasoline). Explain 'sudden sniffing death' can happen the first time from heart failure.

- **Q:** How can inhalants kill on first use?
  **A:** They can cause the heart to beat irregularly and stop (sudden sniffing death), or cause suffocation.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Caffeine
*Student:* Calculate your weekly caffeine intake and compare with recommended limits.

*How to teach:* The child lists all caffeine for a typical week (soda ~35–45 mg/12 oz, tea ~30–50 mg/8 oz, coffee ~95 mg/8 oz, energy drinks 80–300 mg) and compares to ≤100 mg/day.

- **Q:** A teen drinks two 12-oz colas (35 mg each) and one 8-oz coffee (95 mg) in a day. Is that over 100 mg?
  **A:** Yes: 35 + 35 + 95 = 165 mg, which is 65 mg over 100 mg.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cannabis potency
*Student:* Compare THC potency of modern vapes and edibles with the past and explain risks to teen brains.

*How to teach:* Compare THC levels then and now and discuss risks to the teen brain: memory, attention, mood, addiction (cannabis use disorder), psychosis risk with high-potency use; illegal under 21 in CA.

- **Q:** Why are high-potency vapes and edibles riskier for teens?
  **A:** Higher THC raises risk of addiction, anxiety, psychosis and impaired memory/learning; edibles take 30 minutes–2 hours to work, leading people to take too much.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Complete a drug-facts chart with effects, risks and laws.

*How to teach:* The child fills the drug-facts chart for stimulants, inhalants, caffeine and cannabis.

- **Q:** What is California's legal age for cannabis?
  **A:** 21 (18 with a medical recommendation).

**Assessment:** Drug-facts chart.

**Answer key:** Drug-facts chart key: Prescription stimulants – increase alertness/heart rate; risks: heart problems, anxiety, insomnia, addiction; sharing/selling is illegal. Inhalants – brief high; risks: brain damage, sudden sniffing death, suffocation; selling certain products to minors is restricted. Caffeine – stimulant; risks: jitters, insomnia, rapid heartbeat; teens ≤100 mg/day, avoid energy drinks. Cannabis – THC alters perception/memory; risks: addiction, impaired learning, psychosis; illegal under 21. Proficient: accurate in all four rows.
