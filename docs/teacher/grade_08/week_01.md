# Teacher's Guide — 8th Grade, Week 1

## English Language Arts 8: 'The Tell-Tale Heart': unreliable narrators

**Prepare:** Read 'The Tell-Tale Heart' yourself first (about 2,200 words; free on Project Gutenberg; LibriVox has an audio version). Parental preview: the narrator murders and dismembers an old man — described briefly but vividly. Print a reading-interest survey; help the child choose an independent reading book.

**Watch for:**
- Taking the narrator's claims of sanity at face value. Ask: does how he says it (frantic dashes, repetition, the obsession with the eye) match what he says?
- Confusing gerunds with present participles. Test: if the -ing word can be replaced by 'it' or 'something,' it is a gerund (noun).

### Day 1 (Monday): Launch & reading inventory
*Student:* Complete a reading interest survey, choose an independent reading book, and write a one-page 'reading life' reflection; read Poe's biography.

*How to teach:* Have the child complete the interest survey, pick an independent reading book (daily 20–30 minutes all year) and write a one-page 'reading life' reflection. Then read a short Poe biography together and ask what in his life (loss of his mother and wife, poverty) might show up in dark stories.

- **Q:** Name two facts about Edgar Allan Poe's life.
  **A:** Examples: he was orphaned young and raised by the Allan family; he lived 1809–1849; his young wife Virginia died of tuberculosis; he is credited with inventing the detective story and wrote 'The Raven.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Read 'The Tell-Tale Heart'
*Student:* Read the story aloud (or with the LibriVox recording) and annotate every place the narrator insists he is not mad.

*How to teach:* Read the story aloud (or play LibriVox) while the child follows and marks each place the narrator insists he is not mad. Pause after the first paragraph and ask what kind of person talks this way.

- **Q:** How does the story open, and what is the narrator trying to prove?
  **A:** 'True!—nervous—very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am; but why will you say that I am mad?' He is trying to prove he is sane by how calmly and carefully he planned the murder.
- **Q:** What is his motive?
  **A:** He says he loved the old man and wanted no money; it was the old man's eye — 'the eye of a vulture.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Narrator reliability
*Student:* Write a paragraph citing three pieces of evidence that the narrator is unreliable and explain how that builds suspense.

*How to teach:* Model the claim–evidence–explanation pattern with one example, then have the child write a paragraph with three pieces of evidence of unreliability and how it builds suspense. Check understanding with: "Give one piece of evidence the narrator is unreliable." If the answer misses the key idea, revisit today's model together.

- **Q:** Give one piece of evidence the narrator is unreliable.
  **A:** Examples: he says the disease 'sharpened my senses' and that he 'heard all things in the heaven and in the earth'; he murders over an eye; he hears a heartbeat after the man is dead.
- **Q:** How does unreliability build suspense?
  **A:** Because we can't trust his perceptions, we wait anxiously to see how his frantic mind will betray him — and it does when he confesses.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: gerunds
*Student:* Learn that gerunds are -ing verbals used as nouns; find 5 in the story and write 8 original sentences using gerunds as subject, object and object of preposition.

*How to teach:* Teach: a gerund is an -ing verb form used as a noun. Find 5 in the story, then the child writes 8 sentences using gerunds as subject, direct object and object of a preposition.

- **Q:** Identify the gerund and its job: 'Reading at night was the narrator's habit.'
  **A:** 'Reading' is a gerund used as the subject.
- **Q:** Is 'running' a gerund in 'The running dog barked'?
  **A:** No — it describes 'dog,' so it is a participle (adjective).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a reading quiz on the story and roots (acu-, dis-, -ous); share a 2-minute oral summary using precise vocabulary.

*How to teach:* Give the reading and roots quiz (see key), then have the child give a 2-minute oral summary using words such as 'unreliable narrator' and 'suspense.' Finish by asking: "What does 'acute' mean and what root does it contain?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** What does 'acute' mean and what root does it contain?
  **A:** Sharp or keen; acu- 'sharp' — the narrator claims his hearing is acute.

**Assessment:** Reading quiz plus evidence paragraph scored on claim, evidence and explanation.

**Answer key:** Reading quiz (1 point each): 1) What does the narrator hate about the old man? His 'vulture' eye — pale blue with a film over it. 2) How many nights does he look in before acting? Seven; he kills him on the eighth. 3) Where does he hide the body? Under the floorboards. 4) Who comes to the house? Police officers, after a neighbor heard a shriek. 5) Why does he confess? He believes he hears the old man's heart beating louder and louder and thinks the officers are mocking him. 6) acu- means sharp (acute, acupuncture); dis- means not/apart; -ous means full of (nervous, furious). Evidence-paragraph rubric (4 levels): 4 = clear, arguable claim; at least two accurate, embedded quotations; explanation that connects each quotation to the claim; 3 = claim and evidence with thin explanation; 2 = evidence summarized rather than quoted or loosely connected; 1 = opinion without evidence. Proficient claim example: 'The narrator's frantic insistence that he is calm proves he is not.'

## Mathematics 8: Moving figures: translations, rotations, reflections

**Prepare:** Print or copy a 15-problem Grade 7 readiness check (proportions, integers, expressions). Gather tracing paper, graph paper, a protractor, a ruler and a cardboard cutout (e.g., an L-shape or flag) that is not symmetric so flips are visible.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing rotation and reflection. Correct it by tracing the figure: if you must flip the tracing paper over to match, it is a reflection; if you only turn it, it is a rotation.
- Describing moves vaguely ('it moved over'). Require precise language: direction and distance for translations, center/angle/direction for rotations, and the line for reflections.

### Day 1 (Monday): Diagnostic & moves warm-up
*Student:* Take a 15-problem Grade 7 readiness check (proportions, integers, expressions); then use tracing paper to slide, turn and flip a paper cutout and describe each move in words.

*How to teach:* Give the readiness check untimed and note which skills (proportions, integer operations, expressions) need review in later warm-ups. Then hand the child the cutout on tracing paper and ask them to slide, turn and flip it, saying aloud exactly what each move did; model one precise sentence such as 'I turned it a quarter turn clockwise around its corner.'

- **Q:** Solve 3/4 = x/20.
  **A:** x = 15, because 20 ÷ 4 = 5 and 3 × 5 = 15.
- **Q:** Compute -7 + 12 - (-3).
  **A:** 8 (−7 + 12 = 5, and subtracting −3 adds 3).
- **Q:** You flip the cutout over so it faces the other way. Which move is that?
  **A:** A reflection (flip).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Translations
*Student:* Translate triangles on a grid using directed line segments; draw 4 images and label corresponding points A to A'.

*How to teach:* Model translating a triangle: draw a directed segment (arrow) and move every vertex the same distance in the same direction, labeling A→A', B→B', C→C'. The child draws 4 images; check that every vertex moved by the same arrow and that the image is the same shape facing the same way.

- **Q:** Point A(2, 3) is translated 4 units right and 5 units down. What are the coordinates of A'?
  **A:** A'(6, −2).
- **Q:** Does a translation change which way a figure faces?
  **A:** No. A translation slides every point the same distance and direction, so orientation and size stay the same.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rotations
*Student:* Rotate figures 90, 180 and 270 degrees clockwise and counterclockwise about a given center using tracing paper; record the center and angle for each.

*How to teach:* Show how to pin tracing paper at the center of rotation with a pencil tip, trace the figure, and turn it 90°, 180° or 270°, using a protractor or the grid corners to measure. Have the child record center, angle and direction (clockwise/counterclockwise) for each; check that a vertex's distance from the center is unchanged.

- **Q:** Rotate the point (3, 1) 90° clockwise about the origin.
  **A:** (1, −3).
- **Q:** Is a 270° clockwise rotation the same as some counterclockwise rotation?
  **A:** Yes — 270° clockwise is the same as 90° counterclockwise.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Reflections
*Student:* Reflect figures across horizontal, vertical and diagonal lines; complete IM Lesson 'Grid Moves' style practice of 8 problems mixing all three moves.

*How to teach:* Model reflecting across a vertical line by counting each vertex's distance to the line and placing the image the same distance on the other side; repeat for a horizontal line and the diagonal y = x (fold the paper on the line to check). The child completes the 8 mixed practice problems and names each move.

- **Q:** Reflect (4, −2) across the x-axis.
  **A:** (4, 2).
- **Q:** Reflect (2, 5) across the line y = x.
  **A:** (5, 2) — the coordinates swap.
- **Q:** A reflected point is 3 units left of the mirror line. Where is its image?
  **A:** 3 units right of the line, on a perpendicular to it.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: identify and perform 6 single transformations and write a precise description of each; correct errors in a math notebook.

*How to teach:* Give the 6-item quiz (see assessment key) without help, then have the child correct errors in the notebook in a different color, writing why the original was wrong. Review & check day: have the child redo any missed problem in the notebook and explain the error in one sentence.

- **Q:** Describe precisely the move that takes (1, 2) to (−1, −2).
  **A:** A rotation of 180° about the origin (equivalently, a reflection across the x-axis followed by one across the y-axis).

**Assessment:** Friday quiz on performing and describing single rigid motions (80% target).

**Answer key:** Use these 6 items on a grid with triangle A(1,2), B(4,2), C(1,4): 1) Translate 3 left, 2 up → A'(-2,4), B'(1,4), C'(-2,6). 2) Reflect over the x-axis → (1,-2),(4,-2),(1,-4). 3) Reflect over the y-axis → (-1,2),(-4,2),(-1,4). 4) Rotate 180° about the origin → (-1,-2),(-4,-2),(-1,-4). 5) Rotate 90° counterclockwise about the origin → (-2,1),(-2,4),(-4,1). 6) Identify a pictured move and describe it precisely (e.g., 'a reflection across the line y = x'). Each item: 1 point for correct image, 1 point for a precise description (type + direction/distance, center/angle/direction, or line). 80% = 10/12.

## Science 8 (Integrated): Describing motion

**Prepare:** Toy car, masking tape, meter stick, stopwatch/phone timer, graph paper; a composition notebook for the science notebook. Clear a 3-meter smooth floor path. Bookmark PhET 'Forces and Motion: Basics' (phet.colorado.edu).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking speed and velocity are the same. Velocity includes direction: 2 m/s east and 2 m/s west are the same speed but different velocities.
- Reading a distance-time graph as a picture of a hill. Steepness is speed; a flat segment means stopped.

### Day 1 (Monday): Science notebook & frames of reference
*Student:* Set up a science notebook; observe a toy car rolling past from two viewpoints and write how each observer would describe its motion.

*How to teach:* Help the child set up a notebook (table of contents, dated entries). Roll the car past while one person stands still and another walks alongside it; each describes the motion. Explain that motion is always described relative to a reference frame.

- **Q:** To you walking beside the car at the same speed, how does the car appear to move?
  **A:** It appears not to move (stationary) relative to you, though it is moving relative to the floor.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Measuring speed
*Student:* Mark 0.5 m intervals with tape, time a toy car over 3 m, and calculate average speed in m/s for 3 trials.

*How to teach:* Mark 0.5-m intervals over 3 m. The child releases or pushes the car, you time it, and the child calculates average speed = distance ÷ time for 3 trials.

- **Q:** The car covers 3 m in 2.4 s. What is its average speed?
  **A:** 3 ÷ 2.4 = 1.25 m/s.
- **Q:** Why do 3 trials?
  **A:** To reduce the effect of random errors like reaction time; average the results.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Distance-time graphs
*Student:* Graph your toy-car data and walking data; interpret steepness as speed and flat segments as stopping.

*How to teach:* The child graphs distance (y) vs time (x) for the car and for a walk that includes a stop. Ask what the steepness and flat parts mean.

- **Q:** What does a flat (horizontal) section mean on a distance-time graph?
  **A:** The object is stopped — time passes but distance doesn't change.
- **Q:** Which is faster: a steep line or a shallow line?
  **A:** The steep line.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Velocity and direction
*Student:* Use the PhET 'Forces and Motion: Basics' motion screen to compare speed and velocity; record 3 observations.

*How to teach:* Open the PhET motion screen; the child changes push direction and records speed vs velocity observations. To check, ask: "A car moves 5 m/s north, then 5 m/s south. Same speed? Same velocity?" and compare the answer with the model below.

- **Q:** A car moves 5 m/s north, then 5 m/s south. Same speed? Same velocity?
  **A:** Same speed (5 m/s), different velocity because direction changed.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on speed calculations and interpreting 4 distance-time graphs; write a one-paragraph summary of the lab.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (key) and have the child write the lab summary paragraph. Finish by asking: "At 4 m/s, how far does an object go in 6 s?" — look for the specific points in the model answer.

- **Q:** At 4 m/s, how far does an object go in 6 s?
  **A:** 24 m.

**Assessment:** Speed lab data table and graph plus Friday quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) A car travels 3 m in 1.5 s → 2 m/s. 2) A walker goes 100 m in 80 s → 1.25 m/s. 3) At 4 m/s, how far in 6 s? 24 m. 4) Graphs: steeper line = faster; flat line = stopped; straight line = constant speed; curve getting steeper = speeding up. Lab data/graph: table with distance, time for 3 trials, average speed (distance ÷ time) with units; labeled axes (time on x, distance on y); accurate plotting. Summary paragraph names question, method, result with a number, and one source of error (reaction time).

## U.S. History and Geography: Growth and Conflict: Foundations: English rights and Enlightenment ideas

**Prepare:** Set up a notebook and a long paper timeline marked 1750–1900. Print National Archives/Avalon Project excerpts: Magna Carta (clauses 12, 39, 40), English Bill of Rights (1689), Mayflower Compact (1620), Locke's Second Treatise (ch. 2 and 9 excerpts) and Montesquieu's Spirit of the Laws (Book XI, ch. 6).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Magna Carta created democracy for everyone. In 1215 it protected mainly barons and free men from King John; its lasting importance is the idea that the ruler is under the law.
- Believing Americans invented rights ideas from scratch. Colonists drew on English rights traditions and Enlightenment writers like Locke and Montesquieu.

### Day 1 (Monday): Course launch & historical thinking
*Student:* Set up an interactive notebook and timeline (1750–1900); practice sourcing a document by asking who, when, why and how reliable.

*How to teach:* Set up the notebook and timeline. Model sourcing on any document: Who wrote it? When? Why? How reliable? Your child sources a short document (e.g., the Mayflower Compact heading).

- **Q:** What four questions do historians ask when sourcing a document?
  **A:** Who created it, when, for what purpose/audience, and how reliable is it (was the author in a position to know, any bias)?

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Magna Carta & English Bill of Rights
*Student:* Read excerpts of the Magna Carta (1215) and English Bill of Rights (1689); list rights each limited the king's power over.

*How to teach:* Read the excerpts; list the limits each placed on the king.

- **Q:** Name one limit Magna Carta (1215) placed on King John.
  **A:** No free man could be imprisoned or lose property except by the lawful judgment of his peers or the law of the land; certain taxes required the 'common counsel' of the realm.
- **Q:** Name two rights in the English Bill of Rights (1689).
  **A:** The monarch cannot levy taxes or keep a peacetime standing army without Parliament's consent; free elections; free speech in Parliament; right to petition; no excessive bail or cruel and unusual punishments.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mayflower Compact
*Student:* Read the full Mayflower Compact (1620) and annotate examples of self-government and consent.

*How to teach:* Read the Mayflower Compact and annotate self-government and consent.

- **Q:** What did the Pilgrims agree to do in the Mayflower Compact?
  **A:** To 'combine ourselves together into a civil body politic' and make and obey 'just and equal laws' for the general good — self-government by consent.
- **Q:** Why was it written before landing?
  **A:** They were outside the Virginia Company's territory and wanted order and agreed rules before going ashore.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Enlightenment thinkers
*Student:* Read short excerpts from Locke's Second Treatise and Montesquieu's Spirit of the Laws; make a chart linking each idea to American government.

*How to teach:* Read Locke and Montesquieu; chart each idea and its American connection.

- **Q:** What were Locke's natural rights, and what could people do if government violated them?
  **A:** Life, liberty and property; people may alter or abolish a government that violates them (government rests on consent).
- **Q:** What did Montesquieu argue?
  **A:** Separating government into legislative, executive and judicial powers protects liberty — the basis of the three branches.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph answering 'Where did American ideas about rights come from?' citing two documents.

*How to teach:* Your child writes the document-based paragraph; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** What is the social contract?
  **A:** The idea that people agree to form a government and give it power in exchange for protection of their rights.

**Assessment:** Document-based paragraph scored for claim and evidence.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric (1–4): Claim — answers the question (e.g., 'American ideas about rights came from English legal tradition and Enlightenment philosophy'); Evidence — cites two documents accurately (e.g., Magna Carta's guarantee that no free man be imprisoned except by lawful judgment of his peers or the law of the land; Locke's natural rights to life, liberty and property and government by consent; the Mayflower Compact's 'civil body politic' making 'just and equal laws'); Explanation — connects each to American ideas (trial by jury, consent of the governed); Conventions. 4 = claim + 2 accurate cited documents + explanation.

## Spanish 1B: ¿Qué tal el verano? Diagnostic and restaurant vocabulary

**Prepare:** Pull out the 1A can-do checklist. Set a place setting (plate, glass, cup, fork, knife, spoon, napkin, bowl, tablecloth, salt and pepper). Write a 15-item 1A diagnostic quiz (ser, gustar, -ar/-er/-ir present, tener, ir a, dates/time).

**Watch for:**
- Saying 'el camarero' for women. Use la camarera (or el/la mesero/a in Mexico).
- Ordering with 'Yo quiero' only. In restaurants use '¿Me trae…?' or 'Quisiera…' — more polite.

### Day 1 (Monday): Diagnostic
*Student:* Hold a 3-minute conversation with a parent about your summer, family, and likes, then take a short 1A diagnostic quiz to find gaps to target.

*How to teach:* Hold a 3-minute conversation about summer, family and likes; give the diagnostic quiz and note weak areas.

- **Q:** Answer: ¿Qué te gusta hacer en el verano?
  **A:** En el verano me gusta ___ (infinitive), e.g., nadar y leer.
- **Q:** Answer: ¿Cuántos hermanos tienes?
  **A:** Tengo ___ hermanos (or No tengo hermanos).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Table setting
*Student:* Learn 12 table-setting words and set the table while naming each item aloud: Pongo el tenedor a la izquierda.

*How to teach:* Learn 12 table words; set the table naming items: Pongo el tenedor a la izquierda del plato.

- **Q:** Say: 'I put the knife to the right of the plate.'
  **A:** Pongo el cuchillo a la derecha del plato.
- **Q:** What is 'la servilleta'?
  **A:** The napkin.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Restaurant foods
*Student:* Learn main dishes, desserts, and drinks (el bistec, el arroz, el flan) and build a three-course menu.

*How to teach:* Learn main dishes, desserts and drinks (el bistec, el pollo asado, el arroz, la ensalada, el flan, el helado, el pastel, el agua mineral, el té helado); build a three-course menu.

- **Q:** What is 'el flan'?
  **A:** A caramel custard dessert.
- **Q:** How do you say 'main dish'?
  **A:** el plato principal.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Polite requests
*Student:* Learn me trae..., ¿me puede traer...?, ¿algo más?, la cuenta por favor and practice with a parent as camarero.

*How to teach:* Practice requests with you as camarero: ¿Me trae…?, ¿Me puede traer…?, ¿Algo más?, La cuenta, por favor.

- **Q:** Ask the waiter to bring you a spoon.
  **A:** ¿Me trae una cuchara, por favor? (or ¿Me puede traer una cuchara?)

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a restaurant vocabulary quiz and review the diagnostic results to set two personal goals.

*How to teach:* Give the vocabulary quiz; set two goals from the diagnostic.

- **Q:** Translate: 'Anything else?' 'No, thank you. The check, please.'
  **A:** ¿Algo más? —No, gracias. La cuenta, por favor.

**Assessment:** Restaurant vocabulary quiz and diagnostic conversation.

**Answer key:** Restaurant vocabulary quiz key: el plato (plate), el vaso (glass), la taza (cup), el tenedor (fork), el cuchillo (knife), la cuchara (spoon), la servilleta (napkin), el tazón (bowl), el mantel (tablecloth), la sal, la pimienta, el camarero/la camarera (waiter/waitress), el plato principal (main dish), el postre (dessert), la cuenta (check). Phrases: ¿Me trae…, por favor? (Can you bring me…?); ¿Algo más? (Anything else?). 80% proficient. Diagnostic conversation: 3 minutes using present tense, gustar and ir a; note gaps for goals.

## Visual and Performing Arts 8: Line, contour and observation

**Prepare:** Sketchbook, HB/2B/4B pencils, kneaded or white eraser, a timer and household objects (shoe, banana, mug). A family member or pet to model for gestures.

**Watch for:**
- Drawing what you think an object looks like (symbols) rather than what you see. Correct: look at the object far more than the paper.
- Pressing hard with one pencil. Correct: use light HB lines to start and softer B pencils for dark accents.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sketchbook setup
*Student:* Decorate a sketchbook cover and do 5 one-minute gesture drawings of a family member or pet.

*How to teach:* The child decorates the cover, then does five one-minute gesture drawings, capturing the whole pose with fast, loose lines.

- **Q:** What is the purpose of gesture drawing?
  **A:** To quickly capture movement, pose and overall proportion rather than details.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Blind contour
*Student:* Do three blind contour drawings of your hand without looking at the paper; write what you noticed.

*How to teach:* Three blind contour drawings of the hand — eyes on the hand only, pencil moving slowly as the eyes trace edges. Write observations.

- **Q:** Why draw without looking at the paper?
  **A:** It trains the eye and hand to coordinate and forces careful observation of edges instead of drawing from memory.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Contour drawing
*Student:* Draw a shoe in modified contour line, looking 80% at the object.

*How to teach:* Modified contour of a shoe: look about 80% at the shoe, 20% at the paper; include inner contours (laces, seams).

- **Q:** What is a contour line?
  **A:** A line that follows the edges (outer and inner) of a form.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cross-contour
*Student:* Add cross-contour lines to a drawing of a banana or mug to show form.

*How to teach:* Draw a banana or mug, then add cross-contour lines wrapping around the form like bands.

- **Q:** What do cross-contour lines show?
  **A:** The three-dimensional surface and curvature of a form, like lines of latitude on a globe.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Choose your best drawing and write a reflection on progress.

*How to teach:* The child chooses the best drawing and writes a reflection on progress.

- **Q:** Name two kinds of line drawing practiced this week.
  **A:** Any two: gesture, blind contour, modified contour, cross-contour.

**Assessment:** Sketchbook check: 8 drawings with reflection.

**Answer key:** Sketchbook check (8 drawings): 4 = all 8 complete (5 gestures, 3 blind contours, contour shoe, cross-contour object — at least 8 total), careful observation evident, reflection names a specific improvement and a technique; 3 = complete with brief reflection; 2 = missing 2–3 drawings; 1 = mostly incomplete.

## Physical Education 8: Health-related fitness check-in

**Prepare:** Download the FitnessGram PACER audio (20 m version) and measure a 20 m lane with cones; have a stopwatch, mat and grade 7 scores. Find the curl-up and push-up cadence audio (one rep every 3 seconds). Make a 12-inch-high box or use a step for sit-and-reach and have a yardstick.

**Watch for:**
- Pacing the PACER like a sprint early. Correct: start easy; the early levels are slow, and the beeps speed up about every minute.
- Bouncing during sit-and-reach. Correct: reach slowly and hold for 1 second; bouncing can strain muscles and inflates scores.

### Day 1 (Monday): Lead the warm-up
*Student:* Lead a family member or sibling through the 10-move dynamic warm-up learned in grade 7, then run 10 minutes of tag-style games.

*How to teach:* The child leads the 10-move dynamic warm-up (e.g., high knees, butt kicks, walking lunges, arm circles, inchworms, leg swings, carioca, skips, lateral shuffles, jog) with clear counts, then plays 10 minutes of tag.

- **Q:** Why use dynamic rather than static stretching before activity?
  **A:** Dynamic movements raise heart rate, muscle temperature and range of motion for the activity; long static holds before activity can briefly reduce power.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): PACER re-test
*Student:* Complete the 20-meter PACER (or timed mile) and record your score next to last year's.

*How to teach:* Run the PACER: the child runs 20 m, reaching the line before each beep; the test ends on the second missed beep. Record laps beside last year's.

- **Q:** When does the PACER end?
  **A:** The second time the runner fails to reach the line before the beep (first miss is a warning).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Strength re-test
*Student:* Complete curl-ups and 90-degree push-ups to cadence and record repetitions.

*How to teach:* Curl-ups: knees bent, feet flat, fingertips slide across a 4.5-inch measuring strip (FITNESSGRAM standard for ages 10+), one per 3 seconds; 90° push-ups: lower until elbows are at 90°, body straight, one per 3 seconds. Stop at the second form correction.

- **Q:** What does a 90° push-up test measure?
  **A:** Upper-body muscular strength and endurance.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Flexibility re-test
*Student:* Complete the back-saver sit-and-reach and trunk lift; record both sides.

*How to teach:* Back-saver sit-and-reach: one leg straight against box, other knee bent, reach four times, hold the fourth; test each side. Trunk lift: lie face down, lift upper body slowly up to 12 inches, hold, measure chin height.

- **Q:** Why test each leg separately in the back-saver version?
  **A:** It reduces strain on the lower back and shows any left-right imbalance in hamstring flexibility.

### Day 5 (Friday): Compare
*Student:* Graph grade 7 vs. grade 8 scores for each item and write which components improved or declined and why.

*How to teach:* Graph grade 7 vs. grade 8 per item; the child writes which improved or declined and plausible reasons (growth, training, activity level).

- **Q:** Name the five health-related fitness components.
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, body composition.

**Assessment:** Completed fitness comparison graph with written analysis.

**Answer key:** Graph and analysis rubric: 4 = all five tests graphed with labels and units, compares to grade 7 and to Healthy Fitness Zone, explains at least two changes with specific reasons and names a target component; 3 = graph complete, explanation general; 2 = partial graph; 1 = incomplete.

## Health Education 8: Resilience and protective factors

**Prepare:** Have the health journal ready. Find a KidsHealth for Teens article on resilience (search 'resilience' on kidshealth.org/en/teens) and preview it. Gather paper and markers for a support web.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking resilient people never feel upset. Correct: resilience is recovering and adapting after difficulty, not avoiding painful feelings.
- Believing resilience is a fixed trait you are born with. Correct: it is built through skills, relationships and practice — that is why protective factors matter.

### Day 1 (Monday): Grade 7 recap
*Student:* Review the stress-coping strategies from grade 7 and rate how often you use each.

*How to teach:* List grade 7 coping strategies together (deep breathing, exercise, talking to someone, journaling, time management, sleep). Have the child rate each 1–5 for how often they use it, then circle one to use more.

- **Q:** Name three healthy ways to cope with stress.
  **A:** Any three: slow deep breathing, physical activity, talking to a trusted person, journaling, breaking a task into steps, getting enough sleep, spending time on a hobby or outdoors.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Resilience
*Student:* Read a KidsHealth article on resilience and list the 'bounce back' skills it describes.

*How to teach:* Read the article together and stop at each skill it names. The child lists the 'bounce back' skills in the journal and puts a star next to one they already use.

- **Q:** What is resilience?
  **A:** The ability to cope with, adapt to and recover from stress, setbacks or hard experiences.
- **Q:** Give two resilience skills.
  **A:** Examples: problem solving, positive self-talk, asking for help, staying connected to others, managing emotions, keeping perspective, taking care of the body.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Protective factors
*Student:* Map your protective factors (people, activities, strengths) on a support web.

*How to teach:* Explain protective factors versus risk factors. The child draws themself in the center of a page and adds spokes for people, activities, places and personal strengths that support them.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a protective factor and a risk factor?
  **A:** A protective factor lowers the chance of negative outcomes (e.g., a caring adult, a team, good coping skills); a risk factor raises it (e.g., isolation, substance use, ongoing stress).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Growth mindset
*Student:* Rewrite five fixed-mindset statements as growth-mindset statements.

*How to teach:* Model one rewrite ('I'm bad at math' → 'I'm not good at this yet; I'll try a new strategy'). The child rewrites five fixed-mindset statements; check that each adds 'yet,' effort, strategy or learning from mistakes.

- **Q:** Rewrite 'I give up, I'll never get this' as a growth-mindset statement.
  **A:** Example: 'This is hard right now, but if I practice and try a different strategy or ask for help, I can improve.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a short reflection on a setback you overcame and which protective factors helped.

*How to teach:* The child writes a reflection of a real setback (lost game, bad grade, friendship problem), what they did and which protective factors from the web helped. Discuss it together.

- **Q:** Why do protective factors matter when facing a setback?
  **A:** They give support and resources — people to talk to, skills and activities — that help you recover faster and cope in healthy ways.

**Assessment:** Support web and reflection.

**Answer key:** Support web (4 pts each, total 8): includes at least 3 people, 2 activities/places and 2 personal strengths, all labeled. Reflection: 4 = names a specific setback, the action taken, at least two protective factors and explains how each helped; 3 = setback and one protective factor with some explanation; 2 = vague setback, factors listed without explanation; 1 = incomplete.
