# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 10

## ELA 3: Text features in informational texts

**Prepare:** Set up a free ReadWorks account and find a grade 3 passage on camouflage; borrow a National Geographic Kids animal book from the library. Make cards for change-y-to-i words (cry, fly, baby, try, puppy, berry, carry, study).

**Watch for:**
- Choosing an interesting detail as the main idea — the main idea is what most of the text is about; details support it.
- Skipping text features — headings, captions and bold words are tools that help readers find and understand information.

### Day 1 (Monday): Text feature hunt
*Student:* Find headings, captions, bold words, glossary and index in an animal nonfiction book and explain each feature's job.

*How to teach:* Page through the animal book together and hunt for a heading, caption, bold word, glossary and index. For each one, ask 'What job does this do for the reader?' and have the child record the feature and its job in a chart. Check by asking them to use the index to find one fact.

- **Q:** What is the job of a caption?
  **A:** To explain what a photo or picture shows.
- **Q:** How is an index different from a table of contents?
  **A:** An index is at the back, in alphabetical order, and lists pages for specific topics; a table of contents is at the front and lists chapters in order.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Main idea
*Student:* Read a ReadWorks passage on camouflage and identify the main idea and 3 supporting details.

*How to teach:* Read the camouflage passage aloud together. Model asking 'What is this mostly about?' and write the main idea in one sentence, then have the child find 3 details that support it. Check that each detail really proves the main idea.

- **Q:** What is camouflage?
  **A:** Colors, patterns or shapes that help an animal blend in with its surroundings so it can hide from predators or sneak up on prey.
- **Q:** Give an example of camouflage.
  **A:** Examples: a snowshoe hare turns white in winter; a stick insect looks like a twig; a chameleon changes color.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Adjectives
*Student:* Identify adjectives in animal descriptions and write 5 descriptive sentences; spell change-y-to-i words (cry/cries).

*How to teach:* Underline adjectives in a few animal descriptions and point out they tell what kind, how many or which one. The child writes 5 descriptive sentences about animals. Then teach: when a word ends in consonant + y, change y to i before adding -es or -ed.

- **Q:** Add -es to cry and fly.
  **A:** cries, flies.
- **Q:** Find the adjectives: The sleek, spotted leopard hid in the tall grass.
  **A:** sleek, spotted, tall.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Choose an animal
*Student:* Select an animal for an informational report and write 5 questions you want to answer.

*How to teach:* Help the child choose an animal they are curious about for a report. Model one good research question, then have them write 5 questions covering body, habitat, food and adaptations.

- **Q:** What makes a strong research question?
  **A:** It cannot be answered with yes or no and helps you learn something important, such as 'How does a camel survive without water for days?'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Answer text-feature questions on a new passage and take the spelling check.

*How to teach:* Give a new short passage and ask the text feature questions in the answer key, then give the 8-word spelling check. Review any missed feature by finding it again in the book.

- **Q:** Where would you look to find the meaning of a bold word?
  **A:** In the glossary (or in the sentence around it).

**Assessment:** Text feature check; main idea organizer

**Answer key:** Text features (match feature to job): heading → tells what a section is about; caption → explains a photo; bold word → important vocabulary; glossary → definitions of key words; index → page numbers for topics; table of contents → chapters/sections in order. Main idea organizer: proficient if the main idea covers the whole passage and 3 details come from the text. Spelling: cries, flies, babies, tries, puppies, berries, carried, studied — proficient 7+/8.

## Math 3: Multiplication and division word problems

**Prepare:** Tape diagram templates, a ruler and string, bake sale ideas. Write the Unit 2 test in the answer key.

**Watch for:**
- Choosing an operation from one word ('each' means multiply) — decide with a tape diagram whether the total is known or unknown.
- Dividing measurement amounts but dropping units (writing 8, not 8 feet) — always label the answer.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tape diagrams
*Student:* Draw tape diagrams for 6 multiplication and division word problems.

*How to teach:* Draw a tape split into equal sections; label the whole and one section. Show both 'find the total' (multiply) and 'find one section' (divide). The child diagrams and solves 6 problems.

- **Q:** 5 boxes hold 8 markers each. How many markers?
  **A:** 40 — a tape of 5 sections of 8.
- **Q:** 36 stickers fill 4 pages equally. How many on each page?
  **A:** 9.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Measurement problems
*Student:* Solve problems like 'A rope 32 feet long is cut into 4 equal pieces' using drawings.

*How to teach:* Act out cutting a length of string into equal pieces. The child draws a line split into equal parts for each measurement problem.

- **Q:** A rope 32 feet long is cut into 4 equal pieces. How long is each piece?
  **A:** 8 feet.
- **Q:** A ribbon was cut into 6 pieces, each 5 inches long. How long was the ribbon?
  **A:** 30 inches.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Which operation?
*Student:* Sort 10 word problems by operation and justify each choice to a parent.

*How to teach:* Put 10 problems on cards; the child sorts them into multiply or divide piles and justifies each choice to you. Ask 'Is the total known or unknown?' whenever the child hesitates.

- **Q:** There are 7 tables with 4 chairs each. Multiply or divide? Answer?
  **A:** Multiply — the number of groups and the group size are known, the total is unknown: 28 chairs.
- **Q:** 63 cookies go in bags of 9. Multiply or divide? Answer?
  **A:** Divide — the total is known: 7 bags.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Problem writing
*Student:* Write a multiplication and a division story about a bake sale and solve them.

*How to teach:* The child writes and solves a multiplication story and a division story about a bake sale, with a tape diagram for each. Have the child solve each story two ways (drawing and equation) to check.

- **Q:** 6 trays hold 5 muffins each. How many muffins?
  **A:** 30 muffins.
- **Q:** 24 brownies are packed 4 to a box. How many boxes?
  **A:** 6 boxes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 assessment
*Student:* Complete the Unit 2 test on the meaning of multiplication and division.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test without help. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** What two meanings can 18 ÷ 3 have?
  **A:** Sharing: 18 split into 3 groups gives 6 in each. Grouping: 18 split into groups of 3 makes 6 groups.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 test

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test: 1) 4 groups of 7 → 4 × 7 = 28 2) Array of 3 rows of 9 → 27 3) If 8 × 6 = 48 then 6 × 8 = 48 4) 30 ÷ 5 = 6 5) 7 × ? = 56 → 8 6) ? ÷ 3 = 9 → 27 7) 45 pencils shared by 9 kids → 5 each 8) 6 bags of 8 rolls → 48 rolls 9) A 40-inch board cut into 5 equal pieces → 8 inches each 10) Fact family for 4, 9, 36 → 4 × 9 = 36, 9 × 4 = 36, 36 ÷ 4 = 9, 36 ÷ 9 = 4. Proficient: 8+/10.

## Science 3: Animal life cycles: frogs, birds, mammals

**Prepare:** Life cycle picture cards (frog, chicken, human, dog), a raw egg and a small bowl, a hand lens.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a tadpole is a fish — it is a young frog that breathes with gills and later grows lungs and legs.
- Thinking all animals change form — mammals and birds look like small adults when young.

### Day 1 (Monday): Frogs
*Student:* Sequence the frog life cycle and describe changes from tadpole to adult.

*How to teach:* Sequence frog cards: egg, tadpole, tadpole with legs, froglet, adult frog. The child describes what changes at each stage.

- **Q:** Name two changes from tadpole to adult frog.
  **A:** It grows legs and loses its tail; gills are replaced by lungs so it can breathe air.
- **Q:** Where do frogs lay eggs?
  **A:** In water.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Birds
*Student:* Examine an egg (shell, membrane, yolk) and diagram a chicken's life cycle.

*How to teach:* Crack an egg into a bowl. Find the shell, membrane, yolk and white, discussing each part's job. The child diagrams the chicken life cycle: egg → chick → young chicken → adult.

- **Q:** What is the yolk's job?
  **A:** It provides food for the developing chick.
- **Q:** How long does a chicken egg take to hatch?
  **A:** About 21 days.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mammals
*Student:* Compare a human and a dog life cycle using a timeline.

*How to teach:* Make two timelines: human (baby, child, teen, adult) and dog (puppy, adult). The child compares how long each stage lasts.

- **Q:** How are human and dog life cycles alike?
  **A:** Both are born alive, drink milk from their mothers, grow into adults and can reproduce.
- **Q:** Which grows up faster?
  **A:** The dog — it is an adult in about 1–2 years.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compare
*Student:* Make a chart comparing life span and stages of 5 organisms.

*How to teach:* The child fills a chart comparing stages and approximate life spans of 5 organisms (e.g., butterfly, frog, chicken, dog, human).

- **Q:** About how long does a pet dog live?
  **A:** Usually about 10–13 years.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Answer questions about which stages all life cycles share.

*How to teach:* Ask questions about which stages all life cycles share.

- **Q:** What stages do all animal life cycles share?
  **A:** Birth (or hatching), growth, reproduction and death.

**Assessment:** Life cycle comparison chart

**Answer key:** Chart: proficient if 5 organisms have correct stages and reasonable life spans (e.g., monarch adult a few weeks to months, frog several years, chicken 5–10 years, dog 10–13 years, human 70–80 years). Shared stages: birth, growth, reproduction, death.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Tribal government today

**Prepare:** The government page of your local (or another California) tribe and a published tribal constitution excerpt (many are online). Chart paper for 3 columns.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking tribes are just groups or clubs. Federally recognized tribes are sovereign nations with their own governments, laws and courts.
- Thinking tribal members aren't U.S. citizens. Native Americans are citizens of their tribal nation, the United States and their state.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tribal council
*Student:* Read how a local tribe's council is elected and what decisions it makes.

*How to teach:* Read how the tribal council is elected and what it decides.

- **Q:** What is a tribal council?
  **A:** The elected leaders who govern a tribe and make decisions for it.
- **Q:** Who elects the council?
  **A:** The tribe's adult voting members.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Constitution
*Student:* Read short excerpts from a published tribal constitution and identify who can vote and what leaders do.

*How to teach:* Read short constitution excerpts; the child finds who can vote and what leaders do.

- **Q:** What is a constitution?
  **A:** A written document that sets up a government and its rules.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sovereignty
*Student:* Discuss what it means that tribes are sovereign nations with their own governments.

*How to teach:* Discuss sovereignty: tribes govern themselves, make laws and work government-to-government with the U.S.

- **Q:** What does it mean that tribes are sovereign?
  **A:** They have the right to govern themselves with their own laws and leaders.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Relationships
*Student:* Make a 3-column chart of tribal, state and federal government responsibilities.

*How to teach:* Make a 3-column chart of tribal, state and federal responsibilities.

- **Q:** Name one thing the federal government does.
  **A:** Prints money, runs the military, makes treaties, delivers the mail.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Answer 6 questions about tribal government and explain one in a complete paragraph.

*How to teach:* Ask the 6 questions; the child explains one fully in a paragraph.

- **Q:** Are tribal members U.S. citizens?
  **A:** Yes; they are citizens of their tribe, the United States and their state.

**Assessment:** Government chart and questions

**Answer key:** Government chart and questions key: Chart — Tribal: elects council, makes tribal laws, runs tribal programs (housing, health, education, culture), decides membership; State (California): state laws, state highways, state parks, education rules; Federal: national defense, money, treaties and government-to-government relations with tribes. Questions: 1) A tribal council is the elected government of a tribe. 2) Members of the tribe vote (eligible adult members). 3) The council makes laws and decisions for the tribe. 4) Sovereignty means the right to govern yourselves. 5) A constitution is a written plan of government. 6) Tribal members are citizens of their tribe, the U.S. and their state. Paragraph proficient = explains one with an example.

## Art & Music 3: Warm and cool colors

**Prepare:** Paints (red, orange, yellow, blue, green, violet, white), paper, magazine scraps or paint chips from a hardware store. Pull up Pablo Picasso's 'The Old Guitarist' (1903–04, Art Institute of Chicago website).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking warm and cool means the actual temperature of the paint. It describes how colors feel: reds/oranges/yellows remind us of fire and sun; blues/greens/violets of water, ice and shade.
- Thinking a color can only be one mood. Context matters, but warm colors often feel energetic or happy and cool colors calm, quiet or sad.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sort
*Student:* Sort paint chips or magazine scraps into warm and cool color groups.

*How to teach:* Sort chips or scraps into two piles: warm (reds, oranges, yellows) and cool (blues, greens, violets). Point out where they sit on the color wheel (two halves).

- **Q:** Name the warm colors and the cool colors.
  **A:** Warm: red, orange, yellow. Cool: blue, green, violet.
- **Q:** Why are they called warm and cool?
  **A:** Warm colors remind us of sun and fire; cool colors remind us of water, ice and sky.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Picasso
*Student:* Look at Picasso's 'The Old Guitarist' and describe the mood the blue colors create.

*How to teach:* Show 'The Old Guitarist.' Tell the child it is from Picasso's 'Blue Period,' when he painted mostly in blues during a sad time. Ask: What do you notice about the man? How do the blues make you feel?

- **Q:** What mood do the blue colors create in 'The Old Guitarist'?
  **A:** A sad, lonely, cold or quiet mood.
- **Q:** What is Picasso's 'Blue Period'?
  **A:** A time early in his career when he painted mostly in shades of blue, often of poor or lonely people.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Warm landscape
*Student:* Paint a simple landscape using only warm colors.

*How to teach:* Sketch a simple landscape (horizon, hills, a tree, sun or moon). Paint it using only warm colors.

- **Q:** Which color could you use for the sky in a warm painting?
  **A:** A warm color such as yellow, orange or red (e.g., a sunset sky).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cool landscape
*Student:* Paint the same landscape again using only cool colors.

*How to teach:* Sketch the same landscape again and paint it using only cool colors.

- **Q:** What could you use instead of yellow for the sun?
  **A:** A cool color such as pale blue, green or violet (or white mixed with a cool color).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Display both paintings and write 3 sentences comparing their moods.

*How to teach:* Display both paintings. The child writes 3 sentences comparing moods.

- **Q:** How is the mood different between your two paintings?
  **A:** Example: 'The warm painting feels hot and exciting like a desert afternoon; the cool painting feels calm and chilly like night.'

**Assessment:** Warm/cool landscape pair with comparison

**Answer key:** Warm/cool landscape pair rubric: 4 = same landscape painted twice, one with only warm and one with only cool colors, plus 3 sentences comparing moods with specific color words; 3 = one stray color or 2 sentences; 2 = colors mixed between groups, comparison vague; 1 = one painting missing.

## PE 3: Long rope and routine

**Prepare:** Get a long rope (about 14–16 feet) and two turners, or tie one end to a fence or post. Pick a song about 30–60 seconds long with a steady beat for the routine. Look up the traditional jump-rope chants 'Teddy Bear, Teddy Bear' and 'Cinderella' (short, public-domain playground rhymes).

**Watch for:**
- Running in at the wrong moment. Use the 'front door' (the rope comes over the top toward you): wait until the rope hits the ground in front of you, then follow it in.
- Standing near the end of the rope. Jump in the middle of the rope where the arc is highest.

### Day 1 (Monday): Long rope
*Student:* Enter, jump 5 times and exit a long rope turned by two family members (or rope tied to a fence).

*How to teach:* Turners swing the rope in big, steady circles. Teach 'front door' entry: stand beside a turner, watch the rope hit the ground, and run in to the middle just after it passes. Jump 5 times, then exit by running out diagonally toward the opposite turner's side right after a jump.

- **Q:** Where in the long rope should you jump?
  **A:** In the middle, where the rope is highest.
- **Q:** When do you run in?
  **A:** Right after the rope hits the ground and moves away from you, so you have time to get to the middle before it comes around.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rhymes
*Student:* Jump to traditional rhymes like 'Teddy Bear, Teddy Bear' and 'Cinderella.'

*How to teach:* Teach the rhymes and their actions (e.g., 'Teddy Bear' — turn around, touch the ground). Jump while chanting and doing each action on the beat. Turners keep a steady rhythm.

- **Q:** Why do we chant rhymes while jumping?
  **A:** The rhyme keeps a steady beat so the jumper and turners stay in time, and it adds actions to practice.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Routine plan
*Student:* Plan a 30-second single-rope routine to music using 4 tricks.

*How to teach:* Help the child pick 4 tricks (e.g., basic bounce, skier, bell, side swing) and decide how many jumps of each (e.g., 8 each) to fill about 30 seconds of music. Write the plan in order.

- **Q:** If you do 8 jumps of each of 4 tricks, how many jumps is your routine?
  **A:** 8 × 4 = 32 jumps.
- **Q:** Write your routine order.
  **A:** Any 4 tricks in order with counts, e.g., 8 basic, 8 skier, 8 bell, 8 alternate foot.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Practice
*Student:* Rehearse the routine 5 times, smoothing transitions between tricks.

*How to teach:* Rehearse the routine 5 times with the music. After each run, choose one transition to smooth (e.g., keep turning the rope while changing tricks).

- **Q:** How do you change tricks without stopping the rope?
  **A:** Keep the wrists turning at the same speed and change your feet on the next jump.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Perform the routine and note one trick to keep improving.

*How to teach:* The child performs the routine for family and then names one trick to keep improving and how.

- **Q:** What trick will you keep improving, and what will you practice?
  **A:** Any trick plus a specific fix, e.g., 'bell — keep feet together and jump smaller.'

**Assessment:** Jump rope routine performance

**Answer key:** Rubric for jump rope routine: 4 = 4 different tricks performed in planned order for about 30 seconds, mostly on the beat, with 2 or fewer misses and smooth restarts; 3 = 4 tricks with 3–4 misses or one long stop; 2 = 2–3 tricks or many misses; 1 = basic bounce only. Long rope: entered, jumped 5 and exited (yes/no).

## Health 3: Brain, nerves and senses

**Prepare:** A blindfold, 4–5 small food samples (check allergies), a 12-inch ruler, and a bike helmet. Senses chart template with columns: sense, organ, job, how to protect.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a helmet only matters on long or fast rides. Most head injuries happen close to home and at low speeds; always wear one.
- Thinking taste works alone. Much of flavor comes from smell, which is why food tastes bland with a stuffy nose.

### Day 1 (Monday): Brain
*Student:* Learn the brain's role as the control center and why helmets matter.

*How to teach:* Explain the brain is the control center sending messages through nerves. Show how a helmet absorbs impact (optional: egg in a padded cup vs. not). The child lists 4 activities that need a helmet.

- **Q:** What is the brain's job?
  **A:** It is the control center: it controls movement, thinking, feelings and the senses by sending and receiving messages through nerves.
- **Q:** Name two activities where you should wear a helmet.
  **A:** Biking, scootering, skateboarding, roller skating, skiing, horseback riding.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Reaction time
*Student:* Do the ruler-drop reaction test 5 times and record results.

*How to teach:* Hold a ruler at the top with the 0 end just above the child's open fingers; drop it without warning and note the cm/inch where they catch it. Repeat 5 times and record.

- **Q:** If you caught the ruler at 15 cm, then 12 cm, then 9 cm, what is happening?
  **A:** Your reaction time is getting faster (a smaller number means a quicker catch), likely from practice.
- **Q:** What carries the message from your eyes to your hand?
  **A:** Nerves carry signals from the eyes to the brain and from the brain to the hand muscles.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Senses test
*Student:* Do a blindfolded taste and smell test.

*How to teach:* Blindfold the child and give small tastes (apple, lemon, salty cracker). Then have them hold their nose and try again; discuss how smell helps taste.

- **Q:** Why does food taste different when you hold your nose?
  **A:** Smell is a big part of flavor; without smell, the brain gets less information about the food.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Protect senses
*Student:* List ways to protect eyes and ears (sunglasses, volume limits).

*How to teach:* List ways to protect eyes (sunglasses, goggles for sports or tools, never look at the sun) and ears (keep headphone volume at or below half, move away from loud sounds).

- **Q:** How can you protect your hearing when using headphones?
  **A:** Keep the volume low (about half or less) and take breaks.
- **Q:** Why should you never look directly at the sun?
  **A:** It can permanently damage the eyes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Make a senses chart with organ, job and protection.

*How to teach:* The child completes the senses chart for all 5 senses.

- **Q:** Which organ is used for touch?
  **A:** The skin.

**Assessment:** Senses chart

**Answer key:** Senses chart key: Sight – eyes – see light, color, shape – sunglasses, safety goggles, don't stare at sun; Hearing – ears – hear sounds – keep volume low, earplugs at loud events; Smell – nose – detect odors, warn of smoke/spoiled food – avoid sniffing chemicals; Taste – tongue – sweet, salty, sour, bitter, savory – don't taste unknown things; Touch – skin – feel pressure, heat, pain – gloves, sunscreen. Proficient: all 5 rows with correct organ and job and a reasonable protection.
