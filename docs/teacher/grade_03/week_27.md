# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 27

## ELA 3: The Wonderful Wizard of Oz, chapters 13–18

**Prepare:** The Wonderful Wizard of Oz chapters 13–18. Prepare a weak-word passage for Wednesday.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Wizard gave the friends their traits — they already showed brains, heart and courage on the journey.
- Misspelling schwa endings — the 'uh' sound in -al and -el (animal, travel) must be memorized.

### Day 1 (Monday): Chapters 13–15
*Student:* Read chapters 13–15 and discuss why the Wizard is a 'humbug.'

*How to teach:* Read chapters 13–15. Ask how the friends discover the truth about Oz and what a 'humbug' is.

- **Q:** How is the Wizard discovered?
  **A:** Toto knocks over a screen in the throne room, revealing a little old man who admits he isn't a real wizard.
- **Q:** Why does Oz call himself a humbug?
  **A:** He pretended to be a great and powerful wizard but has no real magic — he is a balloonist from Omaha.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Chapters 16–18
*Student:* Read chapters 16–18 and explain how the Wizard 'gives' each friend a gift.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 16–18. The child explains each 'gift' and whether it is real.

- **Q:** What does the Wizard give each friend?
  **A:** Scarecrow: bran mixed with pins and needles for brains; Tin Woodman: a silk heart stuffed with sawdust; Lion: a drink he calls courage.
- **Q:** Why can't Dorothy leave in the balloon?
  **A:** Toto runs after a kitten, Dorothy chases him, and the balloon's ropes break before she gets in.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Word choice
*Student:* Replace weak words with vivid ones in a passage and explain the effect; spell schwa words (-al, -el).

*How to teach:* Give a passage with weak words (nice, said, went). The child replaces them with vivid words and explains how the effect changes. Then spell schwa words.

- **Q:** Replace 'said' in 'The Lion said loudly.'
  **A:** Roared (or bellowed).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Theme
*Student:* Write a paragraph about the theme 'you already have what you need' with evidence.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph on the theme 'you already have what you need,' with one example for each friend. Model one example sentence citing a chapter before the child writes.

- **Q:** What evidence shows the Scarecrow already had brains?
  **A:** He came up with clever ideas, like cutting down a tree to make a bridge across the ditch.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Hold a book discussion and answer comprehension questions on chapters 13–18.

*How to teach:* Hold a book discussion and ask the comprehension questions in the key. Ask open-ended questions such as 'Which friend changed most, and how do you know?'

- **Q:** Who is chosen to rule the Emerald City after Oz leaves?
  **A:** The Scarecrow.

**Assessment:** Theme paragraph; discussion

**Answer key:** Comprehension: 1) How is Oz revealed? → Toto knocks over the screen 2) What is a humbug? → a fake/fraud 3) The three gifts → bran-and-pins brains, silk heart, courage drink 4) How does Oz leave? → hot-air balloon 5) Who rules the Emerald City? → Scarecrow. Proficient 4+/5. Theme paragraph: 4 = states theme with accurate examples for all three friends; 3 = two examples; 2 = theme with weak evidence; 1 = missing.

## Math 3: Liquid volume

**Prepare:** A 1-liter bottle, measuring cups, 5 household containers (mug, pot, vase …), water, a towel. Sketch beaker diagrams with scales counting by 1, 2 and 5 liters.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking taller containers always hold more — pour between a tall thin container and a short wide one.
- Misreading scales (counting each mark as 1 L when marks are 2 L apart) — find what each space is worth first.

### Day 1 (Monday): Liter benchmark
*Student:* Fill a 1-liter bottle and estimate which household containers hold more or less than 1 liter.

*How to teach:* Fill a 1-liter bottle. Show household containers and ask 'More or less than 1 liter?', then test by pouring.

- **Q:** Does a bathtub hold more or less than 1 liter?
  **A:** Much more.
- **Q:** Does a teacup hold more or less than 1 liter?
  **A:** Less.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Measure containers
*Student:* Measure the capacity of 5 containers with a marked liter bottle and record in a table.

*How to teach:* Use the liter bottle (marked at 1/2 and 1/4 if possible) to measure 5 containers; record results in a table. Have the child estimate before each measurement and record both numbers in the table.

- **Q:** A pot holds 3 full liter bottles of water. What is its capacity?
  **A:** 3 liters.
- **Q:** A vase holds about half a bottle. About how much is that?
  **A:** About 1/2 liter.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Read scales
*Student:* Read liquid levels on beaker diagrams with different scales.

*How to teach:* Read beaker diagrams: find the scale first, then read the level. Model one diagram aloud ('the marks go up by 2, so this line is 6'), then the child reads 6 more.

- **Q:** Marks go 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 L and the water is at the second mark above 0. How much water?
  **A:** 4 L.
- **Q:** Marks go by 5 L and the water is halfway between 10 and 15. About how much?
  **A:** About 12 1/2 L.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Volume problems
*Student:* Solve add/subtract/multiply word problems with liters (e.g., 4 jugs of 3 L).

*How to teach:* Solve liquid-volume word problems by drawing. Have the child draw jugs or bars for each problem and write an equation with units (L).

- **Q:** 4 jugs hold 3 L each. How much in all?
  **A:** 12 L.
- **Q:** A 20 L tank has 7 L used. How much is left?
  **A:** 13 L.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a liquid volume quiz and estimation challenge.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and an estimation challenge: the child guesses each container's capacity, then measures. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** A 9 L bucket is filled 3 L per trip. How many trips?
  **A:** 3.

**Assessment:** Liquid volume quiz

**Answer key:** 1) Spoon: more or less than 1 L → less; kitchen sink → more 2) Read a scale (marks by 2) showing 6 L → 6 L 3) 5 bottles × 2 L → 10 L 4) 18 L shared into 3 jugs → 6 L each 5) 15 L + 8 L → 23 L 6) 40 L − 26 L → 14 L. Proficient: 5+/6; estimation is proficient if the child uses the 1-liter benchmark and estimates are reasonable.

## Science 3: Seasonal weather patterns

**Prepare:** Graph paper, colored pencils, and average monthly temperature and rainfall for your city (NOAA, weather.gov climate data, or a weather website).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking an average means every day is like that — averages describe typical weather; actual days vary.
- Uneven graph scales — number the axis in equal steps.

### Day 1 (Monday): Local data
*Student:* Look up average monthly temperature and rainfall for your city and copy them into a table.

*How to teach:* Look up average monthly high temperature and rainfall for your city. The child copies them into a 12-row table.

- **Q:** What does 'average monthly rainfall' mean?
  **A:** The typical amount of rain that falls in that month, based on many years of records.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rainfall bar graph
*Student:* Draw a bar graph of average monthly rainfall with a labeled scale.

*How to teach:* The child draws a bar graph of average monthly rainfall with a title, labeled axes and an even scale.

- **Q:** Why use a bar graph for rainfall?
  **A:** It compares separate amounts for each month.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Temperature line graph
*Student:* Draw a line graph of average monthly high temperatures.

*How to teach:* The child draws a line graph of average monthly high temperatures.

- **Q:** Why use a line graph for temperature?
  **A:** It shows how temperature changes over time.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Interpret
*Student:* Use both graphs to describe the typical weather in winter, spring, summer and fall.

*How to teach:* Using both graphs, the child describes typical weather in each season.

- **Q:** In much of California, which season has the most rain?
  **A:** Winter — most rain falls from November to March, and summers are dry.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Predict next month's typical weather from the graphs and compare it to your log later.

*How to teach:* The child predicts next month's typical weather from the graphs and writes it in the log to compare later.

- **Q:** If next month's average high is 78 °F and rainfall is 0.1 inch, what weather is expected?
  **A:** Warm and mostly dry.

**Assessment:** Seasonal graphs and written interpretation

**Answer key:** Graphs: proficient if each has a title, labeled axes, even scale and correct data. Interpretation: 3 = describes all four seasons with numbers from the graphs; 2 = some seasons or no numbers; 1 = inaccurate.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): States and tribes in the federal system

**Prepare:** A U.S. map, a map of California tribal lands/reservations. Look up California's senators and your representative (California has 52 members in the House). Prepare the Unit 6 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking states and the federal government do the same jobs. The federal government handles national matters (military, money, treaties); states handle things like driver's licenses and schools.
- Thinking tribes are part of the state government. Tribes are sovereign and work government-to-government with the state and federal governments.

### Day 1 (Monday): Federal system
*Student:* Learn what the federal government does and what states do.

*How to teach:* Discuss what the federal government does vs. states.

- **Q:** Name a job of the federal government and one of the state government.
  **A:** Federal: military, printing money, mail, treaties. State: driver's licenses, state highways, education, state parks.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): California's role
*Student:* Find California's senators and representatives and explain how they represent us.

*How to teach:* Find California's senators and your representative and explain representation.

- **Q:** Why does California have more representatives than Wyoming?
  **A:** House seats are based on population, and California has far more people.
- **Q:** How many senators does each state have?
  **A:** Two.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tribal nations
*Student:* Learn how sovereign tribes govern themselves and work with state and federal governments.

*How to teach:* Learn how tribes govern themselves and work with state and federal governments.

- **Q:** How do tribes work with the state and federal governments?
  **A:** Government-to-government, through agreements and treaties, while governing themselves.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Map
*Student:* Label California, neighboring states and California tribal lands on a map.

*How to teach:* Label California, neighboring states and tribal lands.

- **Q:** Which states border California?
  **A:** Oregon, Nevada and Arizona (plus Mexico to the south).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 6 assessment
*Student:* Complete the unit test on government.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test.

- **Q:** Which branch includes the Supreme Court?
  **A:** The judicial branch.

**Assessment:** Unit 6 test

**Answer key:** Unit 6 test key: 1) Legislative branch makes laws. 2) Executive carries out laws. 3) Judicial interprets laws. 4) Congress = Senate + House. 5) Each state has 2 senators. 6) California has 52 representatives. 7) The Governor leads California; capital is Sacramento. 8) The city council makes local laws. 9) Federal job: print money/military; state job: driver's licenses/schools. 10) Tribes are sovereign nations. Map proficient: California, Oregon, Nevada, Arizona and Mexico labeled, plus at least 2 tribal lands. 8–10 = proficient.

## Art & Music 3: Model of an everyday place

**Prepare:** Cardboard (cereal boxes, a shoebox for the base), paper, glue, tape, markers, small bits of clay. Graph or plain paper for the map.

**Watch for:**
- Drawing a map from the side. A bird's-eye view shows what you see from directly above (tops of tables, beds as rectangles).
- Ignoring size relationships. Big things should be bigger in the model than small things (scale).

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose
*Student:* Choose a familiar place (bedroom, park, kitchen) and list its important features.

*How to teach:* The child picks a familiar place and lists its important features and where they are.

- **Q:** Why list features before building?
  **A:** So you remember the important parts and their locations.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Map
*Student:* Draw a bird's-eye-view map of the place with a key.

*How to teach:* Draw a bird's-eye-view map with symbols and a key (e.g., rectangle = bed, circle = rug).

- **Q:** What is a map key?
  **A:** A box that explains what the symbols on a map mean.
- **Q:** What does a table look like from a bird's-eye view?
  **A:** A rectangle or circle (its top).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Build
*Student:* Build a 3D cardboard model of the place based on your map.

*How to teach:* Build a 3D model in a box or on a cardboard base following the map. Fold paper into forms for furniture.

- **Q:** How do you turn a flat paper rectangle into a table form?
  **A:** Fold or attach legs (or fold the edges down) so it stands with height.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Details
*Student:* Add small details (furniture, trees, signs) from paper and clay.

*How to teach:* Add small details from paper and clay (lamps, trees, signs, books).

- **Q:** Name 2 details that make the model look like the real place.
  **A:** Answers vary, e.g., a rug pattern and a lamp on the desk.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 check
*Student:* Evaluate the model using criteria (accurate, detailed, sturdy) and give a tour.

*How to teach:* Evaluate with criteria (accurate to the map, detailed, sturdy) and give a tour.

- **Q:** Rate your model on 'sturdy' and give evidence.
  **A:** E.g., '3 out of 4 — everything is glued, but the bookcase wobbles.'

**Assessment:** Place model with criteria evaluation

**Answer key:** Place model criteria: Accurate (layout matches the bird's-eye map and real place), Detailed (at least 6 recognizable features plus small details), Sturdy (holds together when moved). 4 = strong in all 3 plus a clear tour; 3 = strong in 2; 2 = strong in 1; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 3: Creative dance

**Prepare:** Help the child choose a song (instrumental works well) about 1 minute long. Paper for the dance plan. Review vocabulary: levels (high/middle/low), pathways (straight/curved/zigzag), tempo, force (strong/light).

**Watch for:**
- Making each part different with no repetition. Repeating a phrase gives the dance structure, like a chorus in a song.
- Forgetting the beginning and ending shapes. A dance starts and ends with a still shape to frame it.

### Day 1 (Monday): Plan
*Student:* Choose a song and a theme (ocean, storm, animals) and list 4 movement ideas using levels and pathways.

*How to teach:* Choose a song and theme (ocean, storm, animals). Brainstorm 4 movement ideas that match the theme and name the level and pathway for each (e.g., waves = low, curved pathway).

- **Q:** How could you show a storm with force?
  **A:** Strong, sharp, fast movements (stamping, quick arm slashes) — and a calm part with light, slow movements.
- **Q:** Give a movement idea that uses a low level.
  **A:** E.g., crawling like a crab or rolling like a wave close to the floor.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Create
*Student:* Choreograph a 32-count dance with a beginning shape, 3 movement phrases and an ending shape.

*How to teach:* Build a 32-count dance: beginning shape, three 8-count phrases, and an ending shape (the shapes and phrases add up to 32 counts — e.g., 4 counts start shape, 3 × 8 phrases, 4 counts end shape). Write it in a chart.

- **Q:** How many 8-count phrases fit in 32 counts?
  **A:** 4 (32 ÷ 8 = 4).
- **Q:** If your start and end shapes take 4 counts each, how many counts are left for phrases?
  **A:** 32 − 8 = 24, which is 3 phrases of 8.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Practice
*Student:* Rehearse the dance 5 times, adding one change of tempo and one change of level.

*How to teach:* Rehearse 5 times. Add one tempo change (e.g., slow-motion phrase) and one level change.

- **Q:** What is a tempo change in dance?
  **A:** Moving faster or slower than before (e.g., switching to slow motion).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Perform
*Student:* Perform the dance for family and teach them one phrase.

*How to teach:* Perform for family, then teach them one phrase by demonstrating and counting.

- **Q:** How do you teach a phrase to someone?
  **A:** Show it, break it into small parts with counts, do it together, then let them try alone.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Describe how you used force, levels and pathways and what you would change.

*How to teach:* The child describes how they used force, levels and pathways and what they would change next time.

- **Q:** Describe one place in your dance you used a curved pathway.
  **A:** Answer should name a specific part, e.g., 'I skipped in a circle during phrase 2 to show swirling water.'

**Assessment:** Original dance

**Answer key:** Original dance rubric: 4 = 32 counts with beginning and ending shapes, 3 phrases matching the theme, uses all 3 levels, at least 2 pathways, a tempo change and clear force changes; child explains choices; 3 = most elements present, one missing; 2 = dance is short or unclear and few elements; 1 = no planned dance.

## Health 3: Refusal skills and drug-free choices

**Prepare:** Scenario cards about being offered a vape or alcohol; poster board and markers. Prepare the Unit 5 quiz below.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking peer pressure is always bad. Positive peer pressure (friends encouraging you to try out for a team or study) can help.
- Thinking you need a long excuse to refuse. A firm 'No thanks' is enough.

### Day 1 (Monday): Peer pressure
*Student:* Define peer pressure and list positive and negative examples.

*How to teach:* Define peer pressure (influence from people your age). List positive examples and negative ones.

- **Q:** Give an example of positive peer pressure.
  **A:** A friend encouraging you to join a team, study, try a new healthy food, or be kind to someone.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Refusal strategies
*Student:* Learn 5 ways to say no (say no firmly, give a reason, change the subject, walk away, suggest something else).

*How to teach:* Teach the 5 ways to say no; model each with a different voice and body language.

- **Q:** Name the 5 refusal strategies.
  **A:** Say no firmly, give a reason, change the subject, walk away, suggest something else.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Role-play
*Student:* Role-play refusing a vape or alcohol offer in 3 scenarios.

*How to teach:* Role-play 3 scenarios where an older kid offers a vape or alcohol. Coach a firm voice and leaving.

- **Q:** An older kid offers you a vape and says 'Everyone does it.' What could you say?
  **A:** Example: 'No thanks, I don't want to get addicted. Let's go shoot hoops instead.' Then walk away and tell a trusted adult.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Poster
*Student:* Design a drug-free poster with a positive message for kids.

*How to teach:* The child designs a drug-free poster with a positive message.

- **Q:** Why use a positive message on a poster?
  **A:** Positive messages show the benefits of healthy choices and are more inspiring than scare tactics.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 check
*Student:* Complete a quiz on medicines, tobacco, alcohol and refusal skills.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 quiz.

- **Q:** What is the addictive drug in vapes?
  **A:** Nicotine.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 quiz; drug-free poster

**Answer key:** Unit 5 quiz key: 1) A medicine treats or prevents illness; take only from a trusted adult. 2) Never take someone else's prescription. 3) Poison Control 1-800-222-1222. 4) Nicotine is the addictive drug in tobacco and vapes. 5) Vape aerosol is not harmless water vapor. 6) Secondhand smoke harms people nearby. 7) Alcohol is a depressant that slows the brain. 8) Legal age for alcohol and tobacco: 21. 9) Five refusal strategies: say no firmly, give a reason, change the subject, walk away, suggest something else. 10) Peer pressure can be positive or negative. Poster proficient: clear positive message, accurate fact, eye-catching. 8–10 = proficient.
