# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 4

## ELA 3: Publishing a personal narrative

**Prepare:** Editing checklist (capitals, end marks, spelling of plurals), dictionary, recording device (phone). Choose a new fable for Friday ('The North Wind and the Sun').

**Watch for:**
- Thinking abstract nouns aren't nouns because you can't touch them — they name ideas and feelings (courage, honesty).
- Mixing un- and re- meanings — un- means 'not' or 'opposite of' (unlock); re- means 'again' (retell).

### Day 1 (Monday): Abstract nouns
*Student:* Learn abstract nouns (kindness, courage, friendship) and identify the abstract noun each fable teaches about.

*How to teach:* Explain concrete nouns (you can see/touch) vs. abstract nouns (ideas). The child names the abstract noun each fable teaches about.

- **Q:** What abstract noun fits 'The Lion and the Mouse'?
  **A:** Kindness (or gratitude).
- **Q:** Is 'courage' concrete or abstract?
  **A:** Abstract.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Edit
*Student:* Edit your narrative for capitals, end punctuation and plurals; practice prefixes un- and re- in spelling.

*How to teach:* The child reads the narrative aloud and edits with the checklist, one item at a time. Then spell un- and re- words and tell what each means.

- **Q:** What does 'unhappy' mean? 'Rewrite'?
  **A:** Not happy; write again.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ending
*Student:* Write a satisfying ending that reflects on the lesson learned, then publish a final copy.

*How to teach:* Show endings that reflect: 'From then on, I always…' or 'I learned that…'. The child writes the ending and copies a neat final draft.

- **Q:** What should a satisfying ending in a personal narrative do?
  **A:** Wrap up the event and tell what the writer learned or how they felt afterward.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fluency
*Student:* Practice reading a fable aloud 3 times for expression and record yourself.

*How to teach:* Model reading a fable aloud with expression — pausing at commas, changing voice for characters. The child practices 3 times and records the last reading.

- **Q:** What should change in your voice when a character speaks?
  **A:** Use a different voice or tone that fits the character and the feeling.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 assessment
*Student:* Read a new fable and answer questions on moral and character, take the un-/re- spelling test, and read your narrative aloud.

*How to teach:* Read 'The North Wind and the Sun' and ask the questions in the key; give the un-/re- spelling test; the child reads the published narrative aloud. Score the narrative with the rubric afterward and celebrate the finished piece.

- **Q:** Who wins the contest in 'The North Wind and the Sun'?
  **A:** The Sun.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 reading assessment; published narrative scored with rubric

**Answer key:** 'The North Wind and the Sun': 1) What is the contest? → who can make a traveler take off his coat 2) What does the Wind do, and what happens? → blows hard; the man pulls his coat tighter 3) What does the Sun do? → shines gently and warmly until the man gets hot and takes the coat off 4) Moral → gentle persuasion works better than force. Spelling (10): unlock, unkind, unhappy, untie, unsafe, redo, reread, retell, rewrite, refill — proficient 8+/10. Narrative rubric (4 levels): 4 = clear beginning that sets up the moment, events in order with temporal words, details/dialogue showing feelings, ending that reflects on the lesson, few errors; 3 (proficient) = all parts present, some details, mostly correct capitals and punctuation; 2 = events listed with few details or no real ending; 1 = unclear or incomplete.

## Math 3: Subtraction within 1,000

**Prepare:** Base-ten blocks, open number line paper, an addition table (0–10) for Thursday. Write the quiz in the answer key.

**Watch for:**
- Subtracting the smaller digit from the larger in each column (52 − 38 = 26) — model with blocks that you cannot take 8 units from 2 without trading a ten.
- Errors across zeros (603 − 278) — show trading a flat for 10 rods, then a rod for 10 units: 603 becomes 5 hundreds, 9 tens, 13 ones.

### Day 1 (Monday): Counting up
*Student:* Find differences like 603 − 278 by counting up on an open number line.

*How to teach:* Model 603 − 278 by counting up: 278 + 2 = 280, + 20 = 300, + 300 = 600, + 3 = 603; the jumps total 2 + 20 + 300 + 3 = 325. The child solves 8 problems this way, adding the jumps carefully.

- **Q:** Find 603 − 278 by counting up.
  **A:** 325.
- **Q:** Find 500 − 347 by counting up.
  **A:** 347 + 3 = 350, + 50 = 400, + 100 = 500; 3 + 50 + 100 = 153.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Compensation
*Student:* Use friendly-number strategies (e.g., 502 − 199 = 503 − 200) for 10 problems.

*How to teach:* Show that adding the same amount to both numbers keeps the difference the same: 502 − 199 = 503 − 200 = 303. The child solves 10 problems by making the number being subtracted friendly, then checks one with blocks.

- **Q:** Use compensation for 502 − 199.
  **A:** 503 − 200 = 303.
- **Q:** Use compensation for 341 − 98.
  **A:** 343 − 100 = 243.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Standard algorithm
*Student:* Model decomposing a hundred with blocks, then subtract with the algorithm, including across zeros.

*How to teach:* Build 425 with blocks and subtract 168: trade a rod for 10 units, then a flat for 10 rods, recording each trade in the algorithm. Practice across zeros with 700 − 254. The child does 10 problems.

- **Q:** Solve 425 − 168.
  **A:** 257.
- **Q:** Solve 700 − 254.
  **A:** 446 (700 is regrouped as 6 hundreds, 9 tens, 10 ones).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Check with addition
*Student:* Solve 8 subtraction problems and check each with the inverse addition; find patterns in an addition table.

*How to teach:* The child solves 8 subtraction problems and checks each by adding the answer to the number subtracted. Then look at an addition table together and notice that each row and column goes up by 1 and the table is symmetric.

- **Q:** Solve 812 − 457 and check with addition.
  **A:** 355; check 355 + 457 = 812.
- **Q:** What pattern do you see in the doubles 1 + 1, 2 + 2, 3 + 3 … on the addition table?
  **A:** The sums 2, 4, 6, 8 … are all even and go up by 2.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a subtraction quiz with word problems and choose the most efficient strategy for each.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. For each problem, ask which strategy is most efficient (counting up when numbers are close, compensation near a hundred, the algorithm otherwise).

- **Q:** Which strategy fits 401 − 398 best, and why?
  **A:** Counting up — the numbers are close: 398 + 3 = 401, so the answer is 3.

**Assessment:** Subtraction quiz including across-zero problems

**Answer key:** 1) 568 − 235 = 333 2) 603 − 278 = 325 3) 900 − 456 = 444 4) 702 − 199 = 503 5) 431 − 287 = 144 6) 805 − 367 = 438 7) 650 tickets, 384 sold → 266 left 8) A 1,000-step goal, 625 done → 375 steps more. Proficient: 6+/8 with across-zero problems correct.

## Science 3: Forces and motion design challenge

**Prepare:** Cardboard, paper towel tubes, straws, tape, marbles, a cup, a meter stick. Write the Unit 1 quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Blaming 'bad luck' when a design fails — identify the force (gravity, friction, a collision) causing it.
- Changing several things at once while improving — change one thing, then retest.

### Day 1 (Monday): Marble run plan
*Student:* Plan a cardboard marble run that makes a marble travel 1 meter and stop in a cup.

*How to teach:* Present the challenge: a marble must travel at least 1 meter along the run and stop in a cup. The child sketches a plan with labels and lists materials.

- **Q:** What force pulls the marble down the run?
  **A:** Gravity.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Build
*Student:* Build the marble run using tape and cardboard tubes.

*How to teach:* The child builds the run from the sketch, taping tubes and folded cardboard to a wall, box or chair. Help with cutting but let them make the design choices.

- **Q:** Why must the run slope downward?
  **A:** So gravity keeps pulling the marble forward along the run.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Test
*Student:* Test and record where the marble fails; identify the force causing each failure.

*How to teach:* Test the run 3 times and record where the marble fails (falls off, stops, misses the cup). For each failure, the child names the force involved.

- **Q:** The marble flies off at a sharp turn. What is happening?
  **A:** It is moving too fast to change direction; there isn't enough sideways push from the wall to turn it, so it needs a taller wall or a gentler curve.
- **Q:** The marble stops halfway. Why?
  **A:** Friction slowed it and the slope wasn't steep enough for gravity to keep it moving.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Improve
*Student:* Modify the design and retest, recording changes.

*How to teach:* The child changes one part of the design to fix the biggest problem, retests and records the result.

- **Q:** Why change only one thing at a time?
  **A:** So you know which change made the difference.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 assessment
*Student:* Complete the unit quiz on balanced/unbalanced forces and motion patterns.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 quiz and review the testing log. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** What happens to a moving object when forces are balanced?
  **A:** It keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction (or stays still if it was still).

**Assessment:** Unit 1 quiz; marble run testing log

**Answer key:** Unit 1 quiz: 1) A force is → a push or pull 2) Balanced forces → equal and opposite, no change in motion 3) Unbalanced forces → cause a change in motion (start, stop, speed up, turn) 4) Friction → slows moving objects 5) Longer pendulum → swings more slowly (fewer swings) 6) Bouncing ball → each bounce lower 7) Draw arrows for a tug-of-war that is balanced. Proficient 6+/7. Testing log: proficient if failures, the force involved, the change made and the retest result are recorded.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): People change the land

**Prepare:** Search Calisphere (calisphere.org) for historic photos of your town or region; take or find current photos of the same places. Read a short page on the Los Angeles Aqueduct, Hetch Hetchy (O'Shaughnessy) Dam or Shasta Dam.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all changes to the land are bad or all are good. Most have both benefits (water, farms, electricity) and costs (lost habitat, flooded valleys, dried lakes).
- Thinking the Central Valley was always farmland. Before irrigation, much of it was grassland, wetlands and seasonal lakes like Tulare Lake.

### Day 1 (Monday): Then and now
*Student:* Compare historic and current photos of your region from Calisphere.

*How to teach:* Compare a historic and current photo of the same area. The child lists what changed.

- **Q:** What is one change people made to the land in your photos?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; e.g., new roads, buildings, farms or a dam where there was open land.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dams and aqueducts
*Student:* Learn about a California dam or aqueduct and its effects.

*How to teach:* Read about one dam or aqueduct. Discuss its purpose and effects.

- **Q:** What is an aqueduct?
  **A:** A channel or pipe that carries water a long distance, often from mountains to cities or farms.
- **Q:** Name one benefit and one cost of building a dam.
  **A:** Benefit: stores water, prevents floods or makes electricity. Cost: floods a valley, harms fish like salmon, displaces people.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Farming
*Student:* Explore how irrigation changed the Central Valley.

*How to teach:* Explain irrigation: bringing water to crops through canals. Look at a map of Central Valley canals.

- **Q:** How did irrigation change the Central Valley?
  **A:** It turned dry grassland and wetland areas into one of the world's most productive farming regions.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cause and effect
*Student:* Make a chart of human changes to the land and their effects.

*How to teach:* Make a cause-and-effect chart with 4 human changes and their effects.

- **Q:** Cause: a city builds a freeway. What is one effect?
  **A:** Faster travel and more businesses, but also more traffic, pollution, and homes or habitat removed.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write about one positive and one negative environmental change.

*How to teach:* The child writes about one positive and one negative change.

- **Q:** Can one change be both positive and negative? Explain.
  **A:** Yes; a dam can provide water for people but also flood land and harm fish.

**Assessment:** Cause/effect chart and paragraph

**Answer key:** Cause/effect chart key: at least 4 rows, e.g., Dam built → reservoir stores water and makes electricity, but a valley floods and fish runs are blocked; Aqueduct built (Los Angeles Aqueduct, 1913) → city grows, but Owens Lake dried up; Irrigation → Central Valley becomes major farmland, but wetlands and Tulare Lake disappeared; Roads and buildings → easier travel and homes, but less habitat. Paragraph rubric: 4 = one positive and one negative change, each with a specific example and effect; 3 = both present but one vague; 2 = only one; 1 = incomplete.

## Art & Music 3: Imaginative drawing

**Prepare:** Sketchbook, pencil, eraser, colored pencils (sharpened). Optional: animal books or photos for reference.

**Watch for:**
- Starting with details before the basic shapes. Sketch big basic shapes lightly first (circles, ovals, rectangles), then refine with contour lines and details.
- Pressing hard with colored pencils right away. Layer lightly from light to dark so colors can blend and build.

### Day 1 (Monday): Brainstorm
*Student:* Combine two animals into an imaginary creature and list where it lives, what it eats and its special power.

*How to teach:* The child picks two animals and combines them (e.g., an owl-cat). They list where it lives, what it eats and its special power, and give it a name.

- **Q:** What two animals did you combine and what features came from each?
  **A:** Answers vary; e.g., 'The owl-cat has owl wings and eyes and a cat's body and tail.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sketch
*Student:* Sketch the creature using basic shapes first, then contour lines.

*How to teach:* Model sketching with light basic shapes (oval body, circle head, triangle ears), then drawing contour lines over the shapes and erasing extra lines.

- **Q:** Why sketch with basic shapes first?
  **A:** They help get the size and position of body parts right before adding details.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Elaborate
*Student:* Add a detailed habitat background and at least 3 patterns or textures.

*How to teach:* Add a detailed habitat background (fits where the creature lives) and at least 3 patterns or textures (scales, fur, feathers, stripes, bark).

- **Q:** Name 3 patterns or textures you added.
  **A:** Answers vary, e.g., feathers on the wings, stripes on the tail, bumpy bark on the tree.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Final
*Student:* Finish the drawing with colored pencil, layering light to dark.

*How to teach:* Color with colored pencil: light layers first, then add darker layers for shadows. Show how to blend two colors by layering.

- **Q:** How do you make a color darker with colored pencils?
  **A:** Add more layers and press a little harder, or layer a darker color on top in the shadow areas.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 check
*Student:* Self-assess with a line/shape/detail checklist and clap the syllable rhythm of your creature's name.

*How to teach:* The child uses a checklist (uses different lines; basic shapes then contour; 3+ patterns/textures; background habitat; light-to-dark color) and marks each item. Then they clap the syllables of the creature's name.

- **Q:** Clap your creature's name. How many syllables?
  **A:** The child claps one clap per syllable correctly, e.g., 'Ow-li-cat' = 3.

**Assessment:** Imaginative drawing with self-assessment checklist

**Answer key:** Imaginative drawing and checklist: proficient drawing (4) has a clear invented creature made of two animals, a habitat background, at least 3 patterns/textures, varied lines and layered colored pencil from light to dark; checklist completed honestly; syllables clapped correctly. 3 = one checklist item missing; 2 = 2–3 missing; 1 = drawing unfinished.

## PE 3: Locomotor transitions

**Prepare:** Set up a 'yard' lane about 20–30 yards long and 6–8 cones for pathways; have upbeat music ready. Preview the definitions: hop = one foot to the same foot; leap = one foot to the other with a long flight; skip = step-hop alternating; gallop = same foot leads forward; slide = sideways gallop.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing a hop and a jump. A hop takes off and lands on the same one foot; a jump takes off and lands on two feet (or two to one / one to two).
- Galloping and sliding look the same. In a gallop you face forward with one foot always leading; in a slide you move sideways, chest facing forward, without crossing the feet.

### Day 1 (Monday): Locomotor review
*Student:* Practice walk, run, hop, skip, gallop, slide and leap across a yard.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate each locomotor skill and have the child do each one across the yard: walk, run, hop (each foot), skip, gallop (each lead foot), slide (both directions) and leap. Give one cue per skill (e.g., skip: 'step-hop, swing arms').

- **Q:** What is the difference between a hop and a leap?
  **A:** A hop takes off and lands on the same foot; a leap takes off on one foot and lands on the other with a long stretch in the air.
- **Q:** In a slide, which way do you face?
  **A:** You face forward (chest to the front) while moving sideways, feet not crossing.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pathways
*Student:* Move in straight, curved and zigzag pathways around cones.

*How to teach:* Lay cones in a straight line, a curve and a zigzag. Have the child travel each pathway with a different skill (walk the straight, skip the curved, slide the zigzag). Then call out a pathway and a skill and let them choose the cones.

- **Q:** Name the 3 pathways we practiced.
  **A:** Straight, curved and zigzag.
- **Q:** Draw or describe a zigzag pathway.
  **A:** A path of straight lines with sharp turns back and forth, like the letter Z repeated.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Transitions
*Student:* Combine run-to-leap and skip-to-slide sequences.

*How to teach:* Model run-to-leap: 3–4 running steps, then take off on one foot and land on the other, then keep running. Then skip-to-slide: skip 4 times, turn sideways and slide 4 times without stopping. Practice each 5 times, cueing 'no stopping between skills.'

- **Q:** What does a smooth transition mean?
  **A:** Changing from one movement to the next without stopping or stumbling.
- **Q:** When you run into a leap, which foot do you land on?
  **A:** The opposite foot from the one you took off on.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Obstacle course
*Student:* Complete an obstacle course with transitions.

*How to teach:* Build an obstacle course with 4–5 parts (e.g., zigzag run through cones, leap over a pool noodle, hop to a cone, slide to the finish). The child completes it 3 times, trying to make each change smooth.

- **Q:** Which skill did you use to get over the noodle, and why?
  **A:** A leap (or jump), because it carries you up and over the object.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Show a 4-skill locomotor sequence.

*How to teach:* The child designs and performs a 4-skill sequence (e.g., skip → gallop → slide → leap), saying it aloud first, then performing it twice. Score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Tell me your 4 skills in order.
  **A:** Any 4 correct locomotor skills named in the order performed, e.g., 'skip, gallop, slide, leap.'

**Assessment:** Locomotor sequence

**Answer key:** Rubric for 4-skill locomotor sequence: 4 = performs 4 different locomotor skills with correct form and smooth transitions (no stops), repeats it the same way; 3 = 4 correct skills with one pause or stumble; 2 = 2–3 skills correct or several stops; 1 = cannot show a sequence of skills.

## Health 3: Food advertising

**Prepare:** Two cereal boxes with cartoon characters or prizes; find 2 short food commercials on YouTube to preview (choose kid-targeted ones). Paper and markers for the ad.

**Watch for:**
- Believing ads just give information. Ads are made to persuade you to buy; they show the best side and leave out things like sugar content.
- Thinking that if a famous character or athlete likes a food, it must be good for you. Characters and celebrities are paid or used to make you want the product.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ad tricks
*Student:* Analyze 2 cereal boxes for tricks (cartoon characters, prizes, bright colors).

*How to teach:* Look at a cereal box with the child and hunt for 'tricks': cartoon characters, prizes, bright colors, words like 'fun' or 'power,' placement at kid eye level. List them, then check the label to see what's really inside.

- **Q:** Why do cereal boxes for kids often have cartoon characters?
  **A:** To catch kids' attention and make them want the cereal, even if it is high in sugar.
- **Q:** Name two other tricks on food packaging.
  **A:** Prizes or toys, bright colors, games on the box, health words like 'made with whole grain,' or celebrity pictures.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Commercials
*Student:* Watch 2 food commercials and identify who the target audience is and what they promise.

*How to teach:* Watch 2 commercials. After each, ask: Who is this for? What does it promise (fun, friends, energy)? What doesn't it tell you? The child records answers in a 3-column chart.

- **Q:** How can you tell a commercial is aimed at kids?
  **A:** It uses kids as actors, cartoons, toys, games, music and fun or excitement instead of facts.
- **Q:** What is something a food ad might leave out?
  **A:** How much sugar, salt or fat it has, or the serving size.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Healthy ad
*Student:* Design an ad that makes a vegetable appealing.

*How to teach:* The child designs a poster or storyboard that makes a vegetable exciting, using the same tricks ads use. Encourage a slogan and a true fact about the vegetable.

- **Q:** What ad technique could you use to make broccoli appealing?
  **A:** Any: a cartoon character, a catchy slogan, bright colors, a promise like 'grow strong,' or showing kids having fun eating it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Share
*Student:* Present the ad and explain the techniques used.

*How to teach:* The child presents the ad to the family and names each technique and who the audience is. Family members ask one question each.

- **Q:** Who is the target audience of your ad and how do you know?
  **A:** Answer names a specific audience (e.g., kids ages 6–9) and points to features aimed at them (cartoons, fun colors, games).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write how ads might change what a kid wants to eat and how to think it through.

*How to teach:* Ask the child to write 3–5 sentences: how ads can change what kids want, and what questions to ask before believing them (What are they selling? What's left out? What does the label say?).

- **Q:** What questions should you ask yourself when you see a food ad?
  **A:** Who made this and what are they selling? What is it promising? What isn't it telling me? What does the label say?

**Assessment:** Healthy food ad and explanation

**Answer key:** Healthy food ad rubric: 4 = ad makes a vegetable appealing using at least 2 named techniques (bright colors, catchy slogan, character, 'fun' or 'strong' promise) and includes 1 true health fact; the explanation names each technique and the target audience; 3 = ad and explanation with 1–2 techniques named; 2 = ad present but techniques not explained; 1 = incomplete.
