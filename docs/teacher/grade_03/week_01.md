# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 1

## ELA 3: Reading routines and Aesop's fables

**Prepare:** Print or bookmark Aesop's Fables on Project Gutenberg ('The Lion and the Mouse', 'The Tortoise and the Hare', 'The Fox and the Crow', and 'The Dog and His Reflection' for Friday). Set up a reading log and spelling notebook; visit the library for an independent reading book.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing the moral with the plot ('The moral is the hare fell asleep') — the moral is the lesson for people, stated as advice: 'Slow and steady wins the race.'
- Splitting syllables in the wrong place — teach closed syllables end in a consonant and have a short vowel (rab-bit), open syllables end in a vowel and have a long vowel (ti-ger).

### Day 1 (Monday): Reading launch
*Student:* Choose a library book for a 20-minute independent reading routine and start a reading log; read 'The Lion and the Mouse' and discuss its moral.

*How to teach:* Set a timer for 20 minutes of independent reading and show how to log title, pages and one sentence. Then read 'The Lion and the Mouse' aloud, stopping to ask what each character does. Ask the child to state the moral in their own words.

- **Q:** In 'The Lion and the Mouse,' how does the mouse repay the lion?
  **A:** When the lion is caught in a hunter's net, the mouse gnaws through the ropes and frees him.
- **Q:** What is the moral?
  **A:** Kindness is never wasted; even a small friend can be a great help.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Recount a fable
*Student:* Read 'The Tortoise and the Hare,' retell it in 5 sentences using first/next/then/finally, and spell closed vs. open syllable words (rab-bit, ti-ger).

*How to teach:* Read 'The Tortoise and the Hare' and model a 5-sentence retell with First/Next/Then/After that/Finally. The child retells aloud, then writes it. Then sort and spell closed and open syllable words, clapping syllables.

- **Q:** Why did the hare lose the race?
  **A:** He was so sure he would win that he stopped to nap, and the tortoise kept going steadily and passed him.
- **Q:** Split 'tiger' and 'rabbit' into syllables and name each first syllable type.
  **A:** ti-ger (open, long i); rab-bit (closed, short a).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sentence types
*Student:* Learn the four sentence types and end punctuation; find each type in 'The Fox and the Crow' and write one of each about the fable.

*How to teach:* Teach statement (.), question (?), command (. or !) and exclamation (!). Read 'The Fox and the Crow' and find an example of each type. The child writes one of each about the fable.

- **Q:** Why did the crow drop the cheese?
  **A:** The fox flattered her and asked her to sing; when she opened her beak to sing, the cheese fell and the fox grabbed it.
- **Q:** What type of sentence is 'Do not trust flatterers.'?
  **A:** A command.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Writing a memory
*Student:* Brainstorm small-moment memories and draft a half-page narrative about a time you learned a lesson.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm 3–4 small moments when the child learned a lesson (a mistake, a fall, a kindness). Help them pick one and tell it aloud first, then draft half a page in first person with events in order.

- **Q:** What makes a good 'small moment' topic?
  **A:** One short event (minutes or an hour) the child remembers clearly, not a whole day or trip.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a spelling check on syllable types, answer 4 text-based questions on a new fable, and discuss its lesson aloud using complete sentences.

*How to teach:* Give a spelling check of 10 syllable-type words and read a new fable ('The Dog and His Reflection'), then ask the 4 questions in the key. The child discusses the lesson aloud in complete sentences.

- **Q:** What lesson does 'The Dog and His Reflection' teach?
  **A:** Being greedy can make you lose what you already have.

**Assessment:** Fable comprehension questions, spelling check, narrative draft

**Answer key:** 'The Dog and His Reflection': 1) What is the dog carrying? → a piece of meat (a bone in some versions) 2) What does he see in the water? → his own reflection, which he thinks is another dog with a bigger piece of meat 3) What happens when he snaps at it? → he drops his own meat into the water and loses it 4) Moral → greed can cost you what you have. Spelling (10 words such as rabbit, tiger, napkin, robot, basket, music, kitten, paper, picnic, open): proficient 8+/10. Narrative draft: check that it is one small moment told in order (full rubric in Week 4).

## Math 3: Place value to 1,000

**Prepare:** Gather base-ten blocks (or paper flats/rods/units), a place value chart, 3 dice and digit cards 0-9. Write the 12 quiz items in the answer key on a sheet before Friday.

**Watch for:**
- Reading 305 as 'thirty-five' or writing 3005 for three hundred five — use a place value chart and point out that the 0 holds the tens place empty.
- Comparing by the last digit (thinking 298 > 301 because 8 > 1) — always compare hundreds first, then tens, then ones.

### Day 1 (Monday): Build numbers
*Student:* Build 3-digit numbers rolled on dice with base-ten blocks and record each in standard, expanded and word form.

*How to teach:* Roll 3 dice, build the number with blocks (flats = hundreds, rods = tens, units = ones), then say it aloud. Model one: 'I rolled 4, 2, 6 — 426 is 4 flats, 2 rods, 6 units = 400 + 20 + 6.' The child builds and records 6 numbers in all three forms; check each block model matches the digits.

- **Q:** Write 538 in expanded form and word form.
  **A:** 500 + 30 + 8; five hundred thirty-eight.
- **Q:** What number is 7 hundreds, 0 tens, 4 ones?
  **A:** 704 (the zero shows there are no tens).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Number line placement
*Student:* Place 10 three-digit numbers on an open number line from 0 to 1,000 and explain which benchmarks helped.

*How to teach:* Draw a long line labeled 0 and 1,000 and mark 500 first, then the other hundreds as benchmarks. Model placing 750 halfway between 700 and 800; the child places 10 numbers and tells which benchmark helped. Check that numbers are in order and roughly spaced correctly.

- **Q:** Between which two hundreds is 462, and is it closer to 400 or 500?
  **A:** Between 400 and 500; closer to 500 because 462 is past the halfway point, 450.
- **Q:** Where does 250 go on a 0–1,000 line?
  **A:** Halfway between 200 and 300.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compare and order
*Student:* Compare pairs of 3-digit numbers with <, >, = and order a set of 6 numbers from least to greatest.

*How to teach:* Model comparing by lining numbers up in a place value chart and checking hundreds, then tens, then ones. Remind the child the open side of < or > faces the greater number. The child compares 10 pairs and orders 6 numbers, saying which place decided each comparison.

- **Q:** Compare 647 and 674 with <, > or =.
  **A:** 647 < 674 — the hundreds are equal, and 4 tens is less than 7 tens.
- **Q:** Order from least to greatest: 509, 590, 95, 950.
  **A:** 95, 509, 590, 950.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Place value game
*Student:* Play 'Greatest Number' with digit cards, then write 5 numbers that are 10 more, 10 less, 100 more and 100 less.

*How to teach:* Play 'Greatest Number': each player draws 3 digit cards and arranges them to make the largest number; the greater number wins. Then show that 10 more/less changes the tens digit and 100 more/less changes the hundreds digit, using blocks for 395 → 405. The child writes 5 numbers with all four related numbers.

- **Q:** With the digits 3, 8, 1, what are the greatest and least 3-digit numbers?
  **A:** Greatest 831; least 138.
- **Q:** What is 10 more than 495? 100 less than 312?
  **A:** 505 (9 tens + 1 ten = 10 tens, so the hundreds go up); 212.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a 12-item place value quiz (forms, comparing, 10/100 more or less) and correct errors with blocks.

*How to teach:* Give the 12-item quiz in the answer key without help. Afterward, rebuild any missed item with blocks and have the child explain the fix aloud.

- **Q:** What is the value of the 6 in 862?
  **A:** 6 tens, or 60.

**Assessment:** 12-item place value quiz; observation of block models

**Answer key:** 1) 472 in expanded form → 400 + 70 + 2. 2) 305 in words → three hundred five. 3) 600 + 40 + 8 → 648. 4) six hundred nineteen → 619. 5) 527 __ 572 → <. 6) 890 __ 809 → >. 7) 400 + 30 + 6 __ 436 → =. 8) 10 more than 395 → 405. 9) 10 less than 702 → 692. 10) 100 more than 846 → 946. 11) 100 less than 1,000 → 900. 12) Order 318, 381, 183, 813 → 183, 318, 381, 813. Proficient: 10+/12 and block models match digits.

## Science 3: Pushes, pulls and balanced forces

**Prepare:** Science notebook, a rope or towel, a toy car, several books. Preview a Mystery Science (free lessons) or similar video on balanced and unbalanced forces.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a still object has no forces on it — a book on a table is pushed down by gravity and up by the table; the forces are balanced.
- Thinking a bigger object always wins a push — what matters is which force is stronger (unbalanced).

### Day 1 (Monday): Forces around us
*Student:* List 20 pushes and pulls seen at home and sort them in a science notebook T-chart.

*How to teach:* Explain that a force is a push or a pull. Walk through the house together naming pushes and pulls (opening a drawer = pull, closing a door = push). The child records 20 in a T-chart and you check that each is sorted correctly.

- **Q:** Is opening a refrigerator door a push or a pull?
  **A:** A pull.
- **Q:** What is a force?
  **A:** A push or a pull on an object.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tug of war
*Student:* Play a gentle towel tug-of-war with a family member and draw force arrows for balanced and unbalanced pulls.

*How to teach:* Play a gentle towel tug-of-war: first pull equally so nothing moves, then let one person pull harder. The child draws arrows for each pull — equal-length arrows for balanced, a longer arrow for the stronger pull.

- **Q:** When both people pull equally and the towel doesn't move, what kind of forces are these?
  **A:** Balanced forces — equal in strength and opposite in direction.
- **Q:** What happens when one side pulls harder?
  **A:** The forces are unbalanced, so the towel moves toward the stronger pull.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Book stack
*Student:* Push a book with equal force from both sides, then unequal force, and record what happens to its motion.

*How to teach:* Push a book from both sides with equal force (it stays still), then push harder on one side (it slides). The child records what happens each time and labels the forces balanced or unbalanced.

- **Q:** Predict: if you push a book from the left harder than from the right, which way will it move?
  **A:** To the right — the stronger push from the left moves it away from the left side.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Video and model
*Student:* Watch a Mystery Science forces lesson and draw arrow diagrams for a stationary and a moving object.

*How to teach:* Watch a short video on forces. The child draws arrow diagrams for a book sitting on a table (gravity down, table pushing up — equal arrows) and a cart being pushed (a longer arrow in the direction it moves).

- **Q:** What forces act on a book sitting still on a table?
  **A:** Gravity pulls it down and the table pushes it up; the forces are balanced.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain in writing with diagrams why an object starts moving only when forces are unbalanced.

*How to teach:* The child writes an explanation with diagrams of why an object starts moving only when forces are unbalanced, using an example from the week. Check that the arrows match the explanation.

- **Q:** Why does a soccer ball on the ground start rolling when kicked?
  **A:** The kick is a force that makes the forces unbalanced, so the ball changes its motion.

**Assessment:** Force diagram explanation

**Answer key:** Explanation rubric: 4 = defines balanced and unbalanced forces, gives a home example, draws correct arrows (equal/opposite for balanced; one stronger for unbalanced) and states that motion changes only when forces are unbalanced; 3 (proficient) = correct idea with one diagram; 2 = partly correct; 1 = incorrect (e.g., says still objects have no forces).

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Maps and globes

**Prepare:** A printed local street map, a world map, a California map and a globe. Find your town on each ahead of time. Prepare a 6-question map quiz from the local map (see key).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking north always means 'up the hill' or 'forward.' North is a direction on Earth; on most maps it is at the top, shown by the compass rose.
- Confusing the map key and the compass rose. The key (legend) explains symbols; the compass rose shows directions.

### Day 1 (Monday): Map parts
*Student:* Identify title, compass rose, map key and scale on a printed local map.

*How to teach:* Point out the four map parts on a printed local map. The child finds each and tells what it does.

- **Q:** What does a map key (legend) do?
  **A:** It explains what the symbols and colors on the map stand for.
- **Q:** What does the compass rose show?
  **A:** Directions: north, south, east and west (and often in-between directions like northeast).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Where in the world
*Student:* Locate your continent, country, state and city on a globe and make a 'nested circles' address.

*How to teach:* On a globe, find your continent, country, state and city. The child draws nested circles (biggest = planet Earth, then North America, United States, California, county, city, street).

- **Q:** What continent and country do you live in?
  **A:** North America; the United States.
- **Q:** Put in order from largest to smallest: city, state, country, continent.
  **A:** Continent, country, state, city.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Grid maps
*Student:* Use a grid to locate places on a town map and give directions using cardinal directions.

*How to teach:* Show how grid letters and numbers locate places (like B4). Give 4 places to find and 2 sets of directions using north, south, east, west.

- **Q:** On a grid with letters across the top (left to right, west to east) and numbers down the side, the park is in B3. Which square is one square east of it?
  **A:** C3.
- **Q:** The sun rises in which direction?
  **A:** East.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Draw a map
*Student:* Draw a map of your neighborhood with a key and compass rose.

*How to teach:* The child draws their neighborhood with a title, compass rose, key and at least 4 symbols.

- **Q:** Why do maps use symbols instead of drawings of everything?
  **A:** Symbols save space and make maps easier to read.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a map skills quiz and explain your neighborhood map.

*How to teach:* Give the map skills quiz and have the child explain their neighborhood map, including directions to one place.

- **Q:** How would you tell someone to get from your house to the nearest park?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; the child should use street names and cardinal directions, e.g., 'Go north on Oak Street two blocks, then turn east.'

**Assessment:** Map skills quiz; neighborhood map

**Answer key:** Map skills quiz key: 1) The title tells what the map shows. 2) The compass rose shows directions: north, south, east, west. 3) The map key/legend explains what the symbols mean. 4) The scale shows how distance on the map compares to real distance. 5) Grid location of a named place (answer from your map, e.g., 'the library is in C3'). 6) Direction from home to a place (e.g., 'the park is east of our house'). Neighborhood map rubric: 4 = title, compass rose, key with 4+ symbols, streets and landmarks in roughly correct positions, and the child explains how to get from home to one place using cardinal directions; 3 = missing one element; 2 = missing two; 1 = a drawing without map elements.

## Art & Music 3: Kinds of line

**Prepare:** Gather a sketchbook, pencils, fine and thick markers. Pull up a good-quality image of Vincent van Gogh's 'The Starry Night' (1889; MoMA's website has one). Choose 3–4 short instrumental music clips with different moods (calm, angry, excited, sleepy). Pick a simple echo song (e.g., a call-and-response song you know).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a line is only straight. Lines can be straight, curved, zigzag, wavy, spiral, dotted, dashed, thick, thin, broken, vertical, horizontal and diagonal.
- Thinking lines can't show feelings. Smooth, wavy horizontal lines feel calm; jagged zigzag lines feel angry or excited; drooping lines can feel sleepy or sad.

### Day 1 (Monday): Line hunt
*Student:* Find 10 kinds of lines in your home and draw each in a sketchbook line chart.

*How to teach:* Make a line chart in the sketchbook with 10 boxes. Walk around the house looking for lines (stairs = diagonal, blinds = horizontal, cord = curved, plant tendril = spiral) and draw and label one kind in each box.

- **Q:** Name 5 kinds of lines.
  **A:** Any 5 of: straight, curved, zigzag, wavy, spiral, dotted, dashed, thick, thin, vertical, horizontal, diagonal, broken.
- **Q:** Where did you find a diagonal line at home?
  **A:** Answers vary, e.g., a staircase rail, a slanted roof, a leaning broom.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Line in art
*Student:* Look at a Van Gogh painting ('The Starry Night') and describe how the lines show movement.

*How to teach:* Show 'The Starry Night' and ask the child to trace the lines in the air with a finger. Ask: Which lines are curved or swirling? Where are lines straight or vertical (the cypress tree, church steeple)? How do the lines make the sky feel?

- **Q:** How do the lines in the sky of 'The Starry Night' show movement?
  **A:** The sky is painted with curving, swirling lines and short brushstrokes that make the wind, stars and moon seem to spin and flow.
- **Q:** Who painted 'The Starry Night'?
  **A:** Vincent van Gogh.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Feelings in lines
*Student:* Draw lines to show calm, angry, excited and sleepy to music.

*How to teach:* Play each music clip for about 1 minute. The child draws lines that match how it feels: calm, angry, excited, sleepy. Afterward, label each box and ask why they chose those lines.

- **Q:** What kind of line would show 'angry'? 'calm'?
  **A:** Angry: sharp, jagged zigzags, thick and dark. Calm: smooth horizontal or gently wavy lines.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Line design
*Student:* Create a full-page line design using at least 8 line types.

*How to teach:* The child fills a full page with a line design using at least 8 different line types (can divide the page into sections or let lines overlap). Encourage varying thickness and color.

- **Q:** Point to and name 8 line types in your design.
  **A:** The child points to 8 different labeled line types, e.g., zigzag, wavy, spiral, dotted, dashed, thick, thin, diagonal.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & sing
*Student:* Share the line design and sing a warm-up echo song keeping a steady beat.

*How to teach:* The child shows the design and describes 3 line types and the feeling they create. Then sing an echo song while patting a steady beat on the knees (you sing a line, they echo it).

- **Q:** What is a steady beat?
  **A:** The even, regular pulse in music that stays the same, like a heartbeat or a ticking clock.
- **Q:** Which line in your design shows the most energy, and why?
  **A:** Answers vary but should connect a line type to a feeling, e.g., 'the zigzags, because they are sharp and jumpy.'

**Assessment:** Line design and description

**Answer key:** Line design and description rubric: 4 = full page with 8+ different line types, variety of thickness/direction, and the child names the line types and explains the feeling at least 3 of them create; 3 = 6–7 line types and a basic description; 2 = 4–5 types or page mostly empty; 1 = fewer than 4 types and no description.

## PE 3: Warm-up, cool-down and safety

**Prepare:** Clear an open, flat play area free of furniture, rocks and holes; set 4 cones as boundaries. Make sure the child has sneakers and a water bottle. Write 4–5 PE safety rules on a card (stay in boundaries, keep personal space, stop on the signal, use equipment only as taught, tell an adult if hurt) and pick a stop signal (whistle or 'Freeze!').

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a warm-up means static stretching cold muscles. A warm-up should be moving (dynamic) to raise heart rate and body temperature; hold-still stretches belong in the cool-down.
- Thinking the cool-down is optional or means sitting down right away. Slowing down gradually and then stretching helps the heart rate come down and muscles relax.

### Day 1 (Monday): Safety rules
*Student:* Learn PE safety rules and personal space with a 5-minute bubble-space tag game.

*How to teach:* Read the safety rules card aloud and have the child repeat each rule. Show 'personal space' by having them spin slowly with arms out (their bubble). Play 5 minutes of bubble-space tag: everyone moves with arms out and must not pop anyone's bubble; freeze on your signal and check that they stop right away.

- **Q:** What is personal space?
  **A:** The space around your body you can reach with arms out (your 'bubble'); in PE you keep your bubble from touching anyone else's.
- **Q:** What do you do when you hear the stop signal?
  **A:** Stop moving right away, freeze, and look and listen to the adult.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dynamic warm-up
*Student:* Learn a 6-move dynamic warm-up (jog, high knees, arm circles, skips, lunges, butt kicks).

*How to teach:* Demonstrate each of the 6 moves (jog, high knees, arm circles, skips, lunges, butt kicks) once, then do them together for about 30 seconds each. Watch that lunges keep the front knee over the ankle and that the child moves under control. Ask how their body feels at the end (warmer, breathing faster).

- **Q:** Name 3 moves in our dynamic warm-up.
  **A:** Any 3 of: jog, high knees, arm circles, skips, lunges, butt kicks.
- **Q:** Why do we warm up before exercise?
  **A:** To raise heart rate and body temperature and loosen muscles and joints so they work better and are less likely to be injured.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cool-down
*Student:* Practice a static stretch cool-down and explain why muscles need it.

*How to teach:* After a short jog, lead 4–5 static stretches (quad stretch, hamstring reach, calf stretch against a wall, arm-across-chest, triceps), holding each 15–20 seconds with no bouncing. Have the child breathe slowly and say 'stretch, not pain.' Ask them to explain why we cool down.

- **Q:** How long do you hold a cool-down stretch, and should you bounce?
  **A:** About 15–30 seconds, holding still and breathing slowly — no bouncing.
- **Q:** Why do muscles need a cool-down?
  **A:** It lets the heart rate and breathing slow down gradually and helps warm muscles relax and stay flexible, which reduces stiffness.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Active game
*Student:* Play 15 minutes of freeze tag using the warm-up and cool-down routine.

*How to teach:* Have the child lead the warm-up, then play 15 minutes of freeze tag inside the cones (tagged players freeze until a free player tags them). Remind them of tagging gently on the shoulders or back. Finish with the cool-down stretches.

- **Q:** Where is a safe place to tag someone?
  **A:** A gentle touch on the shoulder, arm or back — never a push, grab or tag on the head or face.
- **Q:** What comes first and last in our PE session?
  **A:** Warm-up first; cool-down last.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Lead the family through the warm-up and name 3 safety rules.

*How to teach:* The child leads the family through all 6 warm-up moves, calling out each one and showing correct form. Then they name 3 safety rules without the card. Use the assessment rubric below.

- **Q:** Name 3 PE safety rules.
  **A:** Any 3, e.g., stay inside the boundaries, keep personal space, stop on the signal, use equipment the right way, tell an adult if you are hurt, wear sneakers.

**Assessment:** Leads warm-up correctly

**Answer key:** Rubric for leading the warm-up: 4 = leads all 6 moves (jog, high knees, arm circles, skips, lunges, butt kicks) in order with correct form, clear voice and about 30 seconds each, and names 3 safety rules; 3 = leads 5–6 moves with minor form slips and names 3 rules; 2 = needs prompts for several moves or names only 1–2 rules; 1 = cannot lead the warm-up without the adult doing it.

## Health 3: MyPlate food groups

**Prepare:** Print or open the MyPlate Kids page (myplate.gov); gather grocery flyers, scissors, glue and a paper plate divided into MyPlate sections (half fruits and vegetables, a bit more vegetables than fruit; grains and protein on the other half; dairy as a cup on the side). Prepare a simple food-log sheet with columns for each group.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a food belongs to only the group of its main look (e.g., pizza is 'grains'). Mixed foods count in several groups: crust = grains, sauce = vegetables, cheese = dairy, toppings may be protein or vegetables.
- Thinking beans are only vegetables or that eggs are dairy. Beans and peas can count as protein or vegetables; eggs are in the protein group, not dairy.

### Day 1 (Monday): Five food groups
*Student:* Explore the MyPlate Kids page and name the 5 food groups with 3 examples each.

*How to teach:* Look at the MyPlate Kids page together. Point to each section and have the child name it; then make a chart with the 5 groups and have the child write 3 foods in each. Check that every example is in the right group.

- **Q:** What are the five MyPlate food groups?
  **A:** Fruits, vegetables, grains, protein and dairy.
- **Q:** Name one food from the grains group and one from the protein group.
  **A:** Grains: bread, rice, oatmeal, pasta or tortillas. Protein: chicken, fish, beans, eggs, nuts or tofu.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Food group sort
*Student:* Cut pictures from grocery flyers and sort them onto a paper-plate MyPlate.

*How to teach:* Have the child cut 15–20 food pictures from flyers and glue each into the right section of the paper-plate MyPlate. Ask them to justify any tricky ones (cheese, beans, juice) before gluing.

- **Q:** Which group does cheese go in? Which group do beans go in?
  **A:** Cheese is dairy. Beans go in protein (they can also count as a vegetable).
- **Q:** Which half of MyPlate should be filled with fruits and vegetables?
  **A:** Half of the plate should be fruits and vegetables, with a little more vegetables than fruit.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Nutrients
*Student:* Learn one job of each nutrient (protein builds muscles, calcium builds bones) and match foods to jobs.

*How to teach:* Explain that nutrients are the parts of food the body uses, and give one job each: protein builds and repairs muscles, calcium builds bones and teeth, carbohydrates give energy, vitamins and minerals help the body work, fiber helps digestion, water carries things through the body. Child matches food cards to jobs.

- **Q:** Which nutrient builds strong bones, and name a food that has it?
  **A:** Calcium; milk, yogurt, cheese, fortified soy milk or leafy greens.
- **Q:** What is protein's job? Give a food with protein.
  **A:** Protein builds and repairs muscles and other body parts; chicken, fish, eggs, beans, nuts or tofu.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Food log
*Student:* Record everything you eat for one day and tally servings from each group.

*How to teach:* Give the child the food-log sheet to fill in through the day (help with breakfast so they see how). In the evening, put a tally mark in each group for every food and add them up.

- **Q:** Your sandwich had bread, turkey, lettuce and cheese. Which groups does it count for?
  **A:** Bread = grains, turkey = protein, lettuce = vegetables, cheese = dairy (four groups).
- **Q:** Why do we keep a food log?
  **A:** To see what we actually eat so we can notice which groups we eat a lot of and which we are missing.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Identify which food group you ate least yesterday and name 2 foods to add.

*How to teach:* Review the tallies with the child and have them circle the group with the fewest marks. They choose 2 foods from that group they would enjoy and plan when to eat them.

- **Q:** Why does eating a variety of foods matter?
  **A:** Different foods give different nutrients; no single food has everything the body needs, so eating from all groups keeps the body healthy.
- **Q:** If you ate almost no vegetables yesterday, name 2 you could add.
  **A:** Any two vegetables, e.g., carrot sticks at lunch and broccoli at dinner.

**Assessment:** Food log analysis

**Answer key:** Rubric for the food log analysis: 4 = log is complete for the whole day, every food is placed in the correct group, servings are tallied for all 5 groups, and the child names the group eaten least plus 2 specific foods to add from that group; 3 = log is mostly complete with 1–2 sorting errors and a correct 'least' group with 2 foods; 2 = incomplete log or several mis-sorted foods, only 1 suggestion; 1 = log missing most of the day and no analysis.
