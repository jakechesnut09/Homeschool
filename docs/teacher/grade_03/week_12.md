# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 12

## ELA 3: Writing an informational report

**Prepare:** Gather the note cards, a report template (introduction, 3 topic paragraphs, conclusion), an envelope, and a zoo's mailing address (e.g., the San Diego Zoo or a local zoo).

**Watch for:**
- Leaving out commas in a series ('lions tigers and bears') — put a comma after each item before 'and'.
- Writing paragraphs without topic sentences — each paragraph should begin with a sentence that tells its topic.

### Day 1 (Monday): Report plan
*Student:* Organize notes into an outline with an introduction, 3 topic paragraphs and conclusion.

*How to teach:* Lay out the sorted note cards and show how each category can become one paragraph. Help the child write an outline: introduction, three topic paragraphs (e.g., body, habitat, food/adaptations) with 3–4 notes each, and a conclusion.

- **Q:** What goes in an introduction?
  **A:** A hook to interest the reader and a sentence naming the animal and what the report will teach.
- **Q:** How do you decide what goes in each paragraph?
  **A:** Each paragraph covers one category of notes, such as habitat or food.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Diagrams
*Student:* Study a labeled diagram of an animal and explain what it adds to the words; spell -tion and -sion words.

*How to teach:* Look at a labeled diagram of an animal and ask what it shows that words alone don't. Have the child explain it aloud. Then sort and spell -tion and -sion words (motion, action, vision, television).

- **Q:** What does a labeled diagram add that words alone can't?
  **A:** It shows exactly where body parts are and what they look like.
- **Q:** Spell the word that means 'the ability to see' (ends in -sion).
  **A:** vision.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft
*Student:* Draft introduction and first two paragraphs using linking words (also, another, and, but).

*How to teach:* Model writing a topic sentence and turning notes into full sentences joined with linking words (also, another, and, but). The child drafts the introduction and the first two topic paragraphs.

- **Q:** Name three linking words for adding information.
  **A:** Also, another, and (also 'in addition', 'for example').

### Day 4 (Thursday): Commas
*Student:* Practice commas in a series and in addresses by writing a letter to a zoo asking a question.

*How to teach:* Teach commas between items in a series and between a city and state. The child writes a short friendly letter to a zoo asking a question about their animal, with at least one series and a full address.

- **Q:** Add commas: Pandas eat bamboo fruit and small animals.
  **A:** Pandas eat bamboo, fruit, and small animals.
- **Q:** Punctuate this address line: San Diego CA 92101
  **A:** San Diego, CA 92101.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Finish the report draft and read it to a family member for feedback on clarity.

*How to teach:* The child finishes the draft (third paragraph and conclusion) and reads it aloud to a family member. The listener gives one compliment and tells one place that was confusing or needs more information.

- **Q:** What question should the listener answer?
  **A:** Was anything confusing or missing, and what did you learn?

**Assessment:** Report draft; letter with correct comma use

**Answer key:** Report draft: proficient if it has an introduction, 3 topic paragraphs each starting with a topic sentence and containing facts, and a conclusion. Letter: date, greeting, body with a question, closing and signature; commas between city and state and in any series — proficient with 0–1 comma errors.

## Math 3: Facts of 3, 4, 6 and the distributive property

**Prepare:** Grid paper, colored pencils, a deck of playing cards (face cards removed; ace = 1) for fact war. Write the quiz in the answer key.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 3 × 7 is 2 × 7 + 1 (adding 1 instead of one more group of 7) — show the extra row in an array.
- Splitting an array but multiplying only one part (6 × 7 = 5 × 7 = 35) — color both parts and add them.

### Day 1 (Monday): Threes
*Student:* Use 2s facts plus one more group to solve 3s facts with arrays.

*How to teach:* Draw a 2 × 6 array, then add one more row: 3 × 6 = 2 × 6 + 6 = 12 + 6 = 18. The child solves all the 3s facts this way and records them.

- **Q:** Use 2 × 8 to find 3 × 8.
  **A:** 2 × 8 = 16; add one more 8 to get 24.
- **Q:** What is 3 × 7?
  **A:** 21.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Fours as doubles
*Student:* Double 2s facts to find 4s facts and record in a table.

*How to teach:* Show that 4 × 6 is double 2 × 6 (12 + 12 = 24). The child makes a table of 2s facts and doubles each to get the 4s.

- **Q:** Find 4 × 7 by doubling.
  **A:** 2 × 7 = 14; double 14 is 28.
- **Q:** Find 4 × 9.
  **A:** 36 (double 18).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Break apart arrays
*Student:* Split arrays such as 6 × 7 into 5 × 7 + 1 × 7 and color each part.

*How to teach:* Draw a 6 × 7 array and draw a line after 5 rows; color 5 × 7 = 35 and 1 × 7 = 7, total 42. Name this the distributive property. The child splits 4 arrays.

- **Q:** Split 6 × 7 into easier facts.
  **A:** 5 × 7 + 1 × 7 = 35 + 7 = 42.
- **Q:** Split 4 × 8 into two equal parts.
  **A:** 2 × 8 + 2 × 8 = 16 + 16 = 32.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sixes
*Student:* Use 5s plus one group and 3s doubled to learn 6s facts; play a fact war card game.

*How to teach:* Teach 6s as 5s plus one group (6 × 8 = 40 + 8 = 48) or as 3s doubled. Play fact war: each player flips 2 cards and multiplies; the larger product wins.

- **Q:** Find 6 × 8 using 5s.
  **A:** 5 × 8 = 40, plus 8 more = 48.
- **Q:** Find 6 × 6.
  **A:** 36 (5 × 6 = 30, plus 6).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Solve a mixed 3s/4s/6s quiz and explain one fact using the distributive property.

*How to teach:* Give the mixed quiz; then the child explains one fact with the distributive property in writing, with a split array. Look for a split array drawn and both partial products added.

- **Q:** Explain 4 × 9 with the distributive property.
  **A:** 4 × 9 = 4 × 5 + 4 × 4 = 20 + 16 = 36 (or 2 × 9 + 2 × 9 = 18 + 18 = 36).

**Assessment:** Mixed facts quiz with distributive explanation

**Answer key:** 3 × 4 = 12, 3 × 6 = 18, 3 × 9 = 27, 4 × 4 = 16, 4 × 6 = 24, 4 × 8 = 32, 6 × 3 = 18, 6 × 7 = 42, 6 × 9 = 54, 6 × 6 = 36, 24 ÷ 4 = 6, 18 ÷ 3 = 6, 42 ÷ 6 = 7, 36 ÷ 4 = 9, 27 ÷ 3 = 9. Proficient: 13+/15. Distributive explanation: 3 = correct split, both parts multiplied and added, with drawing; 2 = correct split with a minor error; 1 = no valid split.

## Science 3: Why animals live in groups

**Prepare:** Short videos of wolf packs, fish schools and honeybee colonies (e.g., National Geographic Kids), simple articles on animal groups, an evidence chart with columns: hunting, defense, raising young, staying warm.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking animals group up only because they are 'friends' — groups help them survive (finding food, staying safe).
- Thinking every animal benefits from groups — some animals (like leopards) usually live alone.

### Day 1 (Monday): Group examples
*Student:* Watch videos of wolf packs, fish schools and bee colonies and list benefits.

*How to teach:* Watch short videos of each group. After each, ask 'How does being in a group help?' and the child lists benefits.

- **Q:** How does hunting in a pack help wolves?
  **A:** They can work together to catch large prey that one wolf couldn't catch alone.
- **Q:** How does a school help fish?
  **A:** It's harder for a predator to pick out one fish; many eyes watch for danger.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ant observation
*Student:* Observe ants outdoors (or a video) and record how they work together.

*How to teach:* Watch ants outdoors (or a video) for 10 minutes. The child records what different ants are doing and how they cooperate.

- **Q:** How do ants work together?
  **A:** They divide jobs (gathering food, caring for young, defending the nest) and follow scent trails to food.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Evidence chart
*Student:* Read articles and fill an evidence chart: hunting, defense, raising young, staying warm.

*How to teach:* Read 2–3 short articles and add evidence to the chart under hunting, defense, raising young and staying warm.

- **Q:** How do emperor penguins use their group to survive?
  **A:** They huddle together to stay warm in Antarctic cold, taking turns on the cold outside.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fish school model
*Student:* Model a fish school vs. a lone fish in a predator tag game and record results.

*How to teach:* Play predator tag: one 'predator' tries to tag a lone 'fish' and then a 'school' of people moving together. Record how long each takes.

- **Q:** Why might the school last longer?
  **A:** The group confuses the predator and it's hard to single one out.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Summarize evidence for one animal group.

*How to teach:* The child writes a summary of evidence for one animal group.

- **Q:** What is one benefit of honeybees living in a colony?
  **A:** They share jobs — workers collect food, care for young and protect the hive, so the colony survives.

**Assessment:** Evidence chart

**Answer key:** Evidence chart: proficient if each of the 4 columns has at least one accurate example (e.g., wolves hunt in packs; fish schools/musk oxen circles for defense; elephants raise calves together; penguins huddle for warmth). Summary: 3 = 3 accurate benefits for one group.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Explorers along the California coast

**Prepare:** Search the Library of Congress for 'California as an island' maps (17th century). Read short biographies of Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo and Gaspar de Portolá, and an excerpt of Father Juan Crespí's diary.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking explorers 'discovered' empty land. Native peoples already lived there and often met and helped explorers.
- Thinking old mapmakers were careless. They drew from limited reports; some early maps showed California as an island.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cabrillo
*Student:* Read about Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo's 1542 voyage and trace his route along the California coast.

*How to teach:* Read about Cabrillo's 1542 voyage and trace his route on a map from Mexico up the California coast.

- **Q:** When did Cabrillo sail along the California coast and for which country?
  **A:** 1542, for Spain.
- **Q:** What California bay did Cabrillo enter?
  **A:** San Diego Bay.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Portolá and Crespí
*Student:* Read about the 1769 Portolá expedition and an excerpt from Father Crespí's diary describing the land.

*How to teach:* Read about the 1769 Portolá expedition and an excerpt from Crespí's diary; list what the diary describes.

- **Q:** Why did Spain send the Portolá expedition in 1769?
  **A:** To claim and settle Alta California and start missions and presidios before other countries could.
- **Q:** What famous bay did Portolá's party see in 1769?
  **A:** San Francisco Bay.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Early map
*Student:* Examine an early map of California from the Library of Congress and note what mapmakers got right and wrong (e.g., California as an island).

*How to teach:* Examine an early map that shows California as an island; compare to a modern map.

- **Q:** What did some early mapmakers get wrong about California?
  **A:** They drew it as an island.
- **Q:** Why might they have made that mistake?
  **A:** They relied on incomplete reports from sailors and had not explored the whole coast and Gulf of California.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Explorer card
*Student:* Make an explorer trading card with dates, route, goals and effects on local people.

*How to teach:* The child makes an explorer trading card.

- **Q:** How did the explorers affect local people?
  **A:** They claimed Native land for Spain and led the way for missions and settlers who changed and harmed Native ways of life.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Present the explorer card and answer questions about the explorer's impact.

*How to teach:* The child presents the card and answers questions.

- **Q:** Which came first, Cabrillo or Portolá?
  **A:** Cabrillo (1542); Portolá came 227 years later (1769).

**Assessment:** Explorer trading card

**Answer key:** Explorer trading card key: Cabrillo — 1542; sailed for Spain north from Mexico; first European to explore the California coast, entered San Diego Bay; goal: find riches and a sea route; died during the voyage (1543). Portolá — 1769; led Spain's land expedition to settle Alta California with Father Junípero Serra; reached San Diego and Monterey area; his scouts were the first Europeans known to see San Francisco Bay; effects: led to missions and presidios that greatly changed Native life. Rubric: 4 = dates, route, goals and effects on Native people accurate; 3 = one missing; 2 = two missing; 1 = incomplete.

## Art & Music 3: Color and musical mood

**Prepare:** Paints, large paper, a recording of Bedřich Smetana's 'The Moldau' (Vltava) from 'Má vlast'; one calm piece (e.g., Debussy's 'Clair de Lune') and one energetic piece (e.g., Rimsky-Korsakov's 'Flight of the Bumblebee'). A simple song to sing loud and soft.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking forte means fast. Forte means loud; tempo (fast/slow) is separate from dynamics (loud/soft).
- Thinking any color matches any music. Choose colors, strokes and values that match the music's dynamics and tempo.

### Day 1 (Monday): Listen and paint
*Student:* Paint freely while listening to Smetana's 'The Moldau,' letting colors follow the river music.

*How to teach:* Explain 'The Moldau' follows a river in what is now the Czech Republic from two small springs to a big river. Play it while the child paints freely, letting the colors and strokes follow the music (small trickles at first, wider sweeping strokes as it grows).

- **Q:** What does 'The Moldau' describe?
  **A:** The Vltava (Moldau) river flowing from two small springs, growing bigger, and passing through the countryside to the city of Prague.
- **Q:** Who composed it?
  **A:** Bedřich Smetana, a Czech composer.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dynamics
*Student:* Learn forte (loud) and piano (soft) and sing a song using both.

*How to teach:* Teach forte (f, loud) and piano (p, soft). Sing a familiar song, switching between forte and piano when you hold up a card.

- **Q:** What does the symbol 'p' mean in music?
  **A:** Piano — play or sing softly.
- **Q:** What does 'f' mean?
  **A:** Forte — loud.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Contrast
*Student:* Paint two small pieces to a calm piece and an energetic piece of music.

*How to teach:* Paint two small pieces: one to the calm piece and one to the energetic piece.

- **Q:** What colors and brushstrokes might match the energetic piece?
  **A:** Bright warm colors and quick, short, zigzag or jabbing strokes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compare
*Student:* Explain how colors in each painting match the music's dynamics and tempo.

*How to teach:* The child explains how the colors in each painting match the music's dynamics and tempo.

- **Q:** How did your painting show soft, slow music?
  **A:** E.g., 'I used light cool colors and long smooth strokes.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 check
*Student:* Choose your best painting and score it on a color-use rubric.

*How to teach:* The child chooses their best painting and scores it on a color rubric: colors match the mood; uses warm/cool on purpose; uses tints and shades; brushwork matches the tempo.

- **Q:** Which rubric point is strongest in your painting? Explain.
  **A:** Answers vary but should point to specific evidence in the painting.

**Assessment:** Paintings with rubric self-evaluation

**Answer key:** Color-use rubric (self- and parent score): Colors match the music's mood (1–4), warm/cool colors chosen on purpose (1–4), tints and shades used (1–4), brushstrokes match tempo/dynamics (1–4). Proficient = average of 3 or more, with a written or spoken explanation for each score.

## PE 3: Catching

**Prepare:** Gather a beanbag, a soft playground ball and a tennis ball. Make 'reach and give' and 'thumbs/pinkies' cue cards. Open space for partner catch and jogging.

**Watch for:**
- Trapping the ball against the chest. Catch with the hands, reaching out and 'giving' (pulling the ball in) to soften it.
- Using the same hand position for every catch. Above the waist: thumbs together; below the waist: pinkies together.

### Day 1 (Monday): Self-toss
*Student:* Self-toss and catch a beanbag 20 times, then a ball, calling 'reach and give.'

*How to teach:* Self-toss a beanbag a little above head height and catch with hands, saying 'reach and give' (reach out to the object, pull it in softly). Do 20, then repeat with a ball.

- **Q:** What does 'reach and give' mean?
  **A:** Reach your hands out toward the ball, then bend your elbows and pull it in to soften the catch.
- **Q:** Where should your eyes be when catching?
  **A:** On the ball all the way into your hands.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Levels
*Student:* Catch tosses above the head (thumbs together) and below the waist (pinkies together).

*How to teach:* Toss to the child at different levels. High balls: hands up, thumbs together. Low balls: hands down, pinkies together. Mix levels and call out the hand position after each catch.

- **Q:** A ball is coming at your knees. How do you hold your hands?
  **A:** Pinkies together, palms up/forward.
- **Q:** A ball is coming above your head. How do you hold your hands?
  **A:** Thumbs together, fingers up.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Partner
*Student:* Play partner catch, taking a step back after each 3 successful catches.

*How to teach:* Play partner catch starting 5 steps apart; after each 3 successful catches in a row, the child takes one step back. Count the farthest distance reached.

- **Q:** Why do you step back only after 3 good catches?
  **A:** It makes sure you are successful at one distance before making it harder.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Moving
*Student:* Jog and catch tosses from a partner on the move.

*How to teach:* The child jogs across the yard while you toss the ball slightly ahead of them. They catch without stopping. Switch roles.

- **Q:** When catching on the move, where should the ball be thrown?
  **A:** Slightly ahead of the runner so they can catch it without stopping.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Record your best streak of consecutive partner catches.

*How to teach:* Play partner catch and count the best streak of consecutive catches in 3 tries. Record the best.

- **Q:** Streaks of 12, 18 and 15. What is your best streak?
  **A:** 18.

**Assessment:** Catching streak record

**Answer key:** Catching streak record: proficient = best streak of 10 or more consecutive partner catches at about 10 feet using hands (not trapping), with correct thumbs/pinkies hand position at high and low levels. 4 = 15+ catches and correct hand positions; 3 = 10–14; 2 = 5–9 or frequent trapping; 1 = fewer than 5.

## Health 3: Identifying feelings

**Prepare:** A feelings chart (search 'feelings wheel for kids') and a journal. Model your own feelings honestly during the week.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking some feelings are 'bad.' All feelings are normal; what matters is how we express them.
- Thinking an I-message blames: 'I feel mad because you're mean.' A real I-message describes the action, not the person: 'I feel mad when you take my pencil without asking because I need it.'

### Day 1 (Monday): Feelings words
*Student:* Build a feelings word bank of 20 emotions.

*How to teach:* Together, list 20 feeling words (happy, proud, nervous, frustrated, lonely, excited, embarrassed, jealous, calm…). The child acts out 5 for you to guess.

- **Q:** Give a 'stronger' word for 'mad' and for 'happy.'
  **A:** Mad: furious or angry. Happy: thrilled, joyful or ecstatic.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Body signals
*Student:* Identify where you feel anger, worry and excitement in your body.

*How to teach:* On a body outline, the child colors where they feel anger (hot face, clenched fists), worry (stomach ache, tight chest) and excitement (racing heart, energy).

- **Q:** What might your body do when you are angry?
  **A:** Face gets hot, fists clench, heart beats fast, muscles tighten.
- **Q:** Why is it useful to notice body signals?
  **A:** They are early warnings that help you recognize a feeling and calm down before acting.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Expressing
*Student:* Sort healthy and unhealthy ways to express anger.

*How to teach:* Make two columns: healthy and unhealthy ways to express anger. Sort cards (yell at someone, hit a pillow, take deep breaths, slam a door, talk to an adult, draw).

- **Q:** Is hitting a person a healthy way to show anger? What could you do instead?
  **A:** No. Instead: take deep breaths, walk away, talk to a trusted adult, use an I-message, or squeeze a stress ball.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Journal
*Student:* Write a feelings journal entry about your day.

*How to teach:* The child writes a journal entry: 2–3 feelings from today and what caused them.

- **Q:** Can you feel two feelings at once? Give an example.
  **A:** Yes, e.g., excited and nervous before a game or a first day.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Role-play expressing a feeling using an I-message.

*How to teach:* Teach the I-message frame. Role-play: a sibling grabbed your book. The child says a complete I-message calmly.

- **Q:** Turn 'You're so annoying!' into an I-message.
  **A:** Example: 'I feel frustrated when you interrupt me because I lose my place.'

**Assessment:** Feelings journal and role-play

**Answer key:** Feelings journal and role-play rubric: 4 = journal uses at least 2 specific feeling words and explains what caused them; the role-play uses a complete I-message ('I feel ___ when ___ because ___') in a calm voice; 3 = journal and I-message with one missing part; 2 = vague words ('good/bad') and incomplete I-message; 1 = not attempted.
