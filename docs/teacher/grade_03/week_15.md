# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 15

## ELA 3: Myths: King Midas and Arachne

**Prepare:** Print 'The Golden Touch' from Hawthorne's A Wonder-Book (Gutenberg) or use a Midas retelling; find an Arachne retelling (D'Aulaires' or online). Make a three-column chart: wish / action / consequence.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Midas's wish was a great idea — trace the consequences: his food and his daughter turn to gold.
- Pronoun mismatches ('Arachne said they was the best') — a pronoun must match the noun it stands for.

### Day 1 (Monday): King Midas
*Student:* Read 'The Golden Touch' and chart Midas's wishes, actions and consequences.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Golden Touch' aloud in parts. Pause after each event to fill the wish / action / consequence chart. Ask: When did Midas realize his wish was a curse?

- **Q:** What does Midas wish for?
  **A:** That everything he touches will turn to gold.
- **Q:** How is the golden touch undone?
  **A:** He is told to bathe in a river and sprinkle its water on what he turned to gold; this restores his daughter Marygold.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Arachne
*Student:* Read the myth of Arachne and Athena and describe Arachne's pride with evidence.

*How to teach:* Read the Arachne myth. Model finding evidence for a trait. The child writes 2 pieces of evidence that show Arachne's pride.

- **Q:** How does Arachne show pride?
  **A:** She boasts that her weaving is better than Athena's and refuses to admit her skill came from the goddess, even when warned.
- **Q:** What happens to Arachne?
  **A:** After the contest, Athena turns her into a spider who will weave forever.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pronouns
*Student:* Practice pronoun-antecedent agreement in sentences about both myths; spell diphthongs oi/oy, ou/ow.

*How to teach:* Model fixing pronoun–antecedent mistakes in sentences about the myths. Then sort and spell oi/oy and ou/ow words (coin, boy, cloud, crown).

- **Q:** Fix: Midas loved their gold.
  **A:** Midas loved his gold.
- **Q:** Which usually comes at the end of a word, oi or oy?
  **A:** oy (boy, toy).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Opinion plan
*Student:* Plan an opinion paragraph: Which character learned the hardest lesson? Give 2 reasons.

*How to teach:* Ask: Which character learned the hardest lesson, Midas or Arachne? The child chooses and fills an organizer with an opinion and 2 reasons with evidence from the myth.

- **Q:** What does an opinion paragraph need?
  **A:** A clear opinion, at least two reasons with evidence, linking words, and a concluding sentence.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write the opinion paragraph with linking words and discuss it in a book talk.

*How to teach:* The child writes the paragraph using linking words (because, also, for example), then shares it in a short book talk while family members ask a question. Listen for at least one reason backed by a detail from the myth.

- **Q:** Give a strong reason that Midas learned the hardest lesson.
  **A:** He turned his own daughter into gold and couldn't eat, so he learned that love matters more than wealth.

**Assessment:** Opinion paragraph about a myth

**Answer key:** Opinion paragraph rubric: 4 = clear opinion, two reasons with accurate evidence from the myth, linking words, conclusion; 3 (proficient) = opinion and two reasons, mostly accurate; 2 = one reason or weak evidence; 1 = no clear opinion.

## Math 3: Understanding area

**Prepare:** Square tiles (or 1-inch paper squares), sticky notes, centimeter and inch grid paper, a 12-inch square of paper for a square-foot template, a ruler.

**Watch for:**
- Leaving gaps or overlapping units — units must cover the shape with no gaps or overlaps; show that a messy covering gives a wrong count.
- Confusing area with perimeter (counting edges) — area counts the squares inside.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cover it
*Student:* Cover book covers and placemats with sticky notes and count to find area in improvised units.

*How to teach:* Cover a book with sticky notes edge to edge, count them, and say 'The area is 9 sticky notes.' Try a placemat. Discuss why different-sized units give different counts.

- **Q:** Why might a book measure 6 sticky notes but 20 smaller squares?
  **A:** The units are different sizes; smaller units need more to cover the same area.
- **Q:** What does area measure?
  **A:** How much flat space a shape covers.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Square units
*Student:* Measure shapes on grid paper by counting square centimeters and square inches.

*How to teach:* Count squares inside shapes on centimeter and inch grid paper; write answers in square centimeters or square inches. Model counting row by row so no square is missed or counted twice, and point out half-squares are not used yet.

- **Q:** A rectangle on centimeter grid paper covers 3 rows of 5 squares. What is its area?
  **A:** 15 square centimeters.
- **Q:** Is a square inch bigger or smaller than a square centimeter?
  **A:** Bigger (an inch is about 2 1/2 centimeters).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compare areas
*Student:* Build three different shapes with 12 tiles each and explain why they have the same area.

*How to teach:* The child arranges 12 tiles into three different shapes (e.g., 3 × 4, 2 × 6, an L shape). Ask why they all have the same area.

- **Q:** Do a 2 × 6 rectangle and a 3 × 4 rectangle have the same area?
  **A:** Yes — both are 12 square units because both use 12 tiles.
- **Q:** Can shapes look different but have equal areas?
  **A:** Yes, if they cover the same number of square units.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Square feet
*Student:* Make a square-foot paper template and estimate then measure the area of a rug.

*How to teach:* Make a 12 in × 12 in square-foot template. Estimate how many fit on a rug, then measure by laying the template down and marking.

- **Q:** A rug is 3 square-foot templates long and 2 wide. What is its area?
  **A:** 6 square feet.
- **Q:** Would you measure a room in square inches or square feet?
  **A:** Square feet — square inches are too small.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Find the areas of 10 shapes on grid paper and explain why units must not overlap.

*How to teach:* Give 10 grid shapes to measure (see answer key). The child writes why units must not overlap or leave gaps.

- **Q:** Why can't area units overlap?
  **A:** Overlapping squares get counted twice and make the area too big; gaps leave space uncounted.

**Assessment:** Area by counting square units check

**Answer key:** Draw on grid paper: 1) 2 × 3 rectangle → 6 sq units 2) 4 × 4 → 16 3) 1 × 7 → 7 4) 3 × 5 → 15 5) L shape made of a 3 × 2 and a 1 × 2 → 8 6) 2 × 6 → 12 7) 5 × 2 → 10 8) 3 × 3 → 9 9) T shape: 3 × 1 top plus 1 × 2 stem → 5 10) 4 × 2 → 8. Proficient: 9+/10 and an explanation that mentions both gaps and overlaps.

## Science 3: Variation within a group

**Prepare:** 20 peanuts in shells, 15 leaves from one tree, a ruler, line plot paper, pictures of different dog breeds.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all members of a species look identical — individuals vary in size, color and shape.
- Thinking variation means different species — all dog breeds are the same species.

### Day 1 (Monday): Peanut variation
*Student:* Measure 20 peanut shells and record lengths.

*How to teach:* The child measures each peanut shell to the nearest quarter inch (or centimeter) and records.

- **Q:** Did all peanuts have the same length?
  **A:** No — they vary.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Line plot
*Student:* Make a line plot of the lengths and describe the variation.

*How to teach:* Make a line plot of the lengths. The child describes the shortest, longest and most common length.

- **Q:** What does the line plot show about variation?
  **A:** Peanuts from the same kind of plant have a range of lengths, with most near the middle.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Leaf variation
*Student:* Collect 15 leaves from one tree and compare size, shape and color.

*How to teach:* Compare 15 leaves from one tree for size, shape and color.

- **Q:** Are all leaves on one tree identical?
  **A:** No — they vary in size and color even though they are the same species.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Dogs
*Student:* Compare different dog breeds and discuss variation within a species.

*How to teach:* Look at pictures of dog breeds. Discuss how they differ but are all the same species.

- **Q:** Are a Chihuahua and a Great Dane the same species?
  **A:** Yes — both are dogs; they show variation within one species.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a conclusion about variation with evidence from your data.

*How to teach:* The child writes a conclusion about variation using numbers from the peanut data.

- **Q:** What is variation?
  **A:** Differences in traits among individuals of the same kind of organism.

**Assessment:** Variation conclusion with data

**Answer key:** Conclusion rubric: 3 = states that individuals of the same species vary, cites the range (shortest–longest) and most common value from data, and mentions leaves or dogs; 2 = general statement with little data; 1 = inaccurate.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Gold Rush and new settlers

**Prepare:** Gold Rush photographs and letters from Calisphere or the Library of Congress. Prepare the Unit 3 test below.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking most miners got rich. Most found little gold; many who prospered sold supplies or services.
- Thinking only Americans came. People arrived from China, Mexico, Chile, Europe, Hawaii and elsewhere; free and enslaved African Americans came too.

### Day 1 (Monday): Gold Rush
*Student:* Learn how the 1848 gold discovery brought people from around the world to California.

*How to teach:* Read about James Marshall's discovery of gold at Sutter's Mill in Coloma in January 1848 and the 1849 rush.

- **Q:** Where and when was gold discovered?
  **A:** At Sutter's Mill in Coloma, January 1848.
- **Q:** What were the people who came in 1849 called?
  **A:** Forty-niners.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Immigrant communities
*Student:* Read about Chinese, Mexican, Chilean and African American Gold Rush communities and their contributions.

*How to teach:* Read about Chinese, Mexican, Chilean and African American communities and their contributions.

- **Q:** Name one contribution of Chinese immigrants in Gold Rush California.
  **A:** Mining, running businesses like restaurants and laundries, and later building the transcontinental railroad.
- **Q:** Were newcomers always treated fairly?
  **A:** No. Many, such as Chinese and Mexican miners, faced unfair taxes (the Foreign Miners' Tax) and discrimination.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Primary source
*Student:* Analyze a miner's letter or daguerreotype and write 3 observations and 2 questions.

*How to teach:* Analyze a miner's letter or daguerreotype: 3 observations, 2 questions.

- **Q:** What is a daguerreotype?
  **A:** An early type of photograph made on a metal plate.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Timeline
*Student:* Build a timeline of newcomers to your region from first peoples to today.

*How to teach:* Build a timeline of newcomers to your region.

- **Q:** When did California become a state?
  **A:** 1850.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 assessment
*Student:* Complete the unit test on explorers, missions, ranchos and settlers.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 test.

- **Q:** Who found gold at Sutter's Mill?
  **A:** James Marshall.

**Assessment:** Unit 3 test; newcomer timeline

**Answer key:** Unit 3 test key: 1) Cabrillo explored the coast in 1542 for Spain. 2) Portolá's 1769 expedition began Spanish settlement. 3) Mission, presidio, pueblo = church/farm, fort, town. 4) 21 missions. 5) Missions forced great changes on Native people: labor, religion, disease. 6) Mexico ruled California from 1821. 7) Ranchos raised cattle for hides and tallow. 8) Gold was found at Sutter's Mill (Coloma) in January 1848 by James Marshall. 9) Gold Rush newcomers came from around the world (China, Mexico, Chile, etc.). 10) California became a state in 1850. Timeline proficient: first peoples → Spanish explorers (1542) → missions (1769) → Mexican era (1821) → Gold Rush (1848) → statehood (1850) → later local arrivals. 8–10 = proficient.

## Art & Music 3: The treble staff

**Prepare:** Staff paper, pencil, keyboard app. Make flash cards for treble staff notes (E4 to F5). Mnemonics: lines E-G-B-D-F 'Every Good Boy Does Fine'; spaces F-A-C-E spell 'FACE.'

**Watch for:**
- Counting lines and spaces from the top. Count from the bottom: the bottom line is line 1 (E).
- Mixing up line notes and space notes. A line note has the line through its middle; a space note sits between lines.

### Day 1 (Monday): Staff
*Student:* Learn the 5 lines and 4 spaces and draw a treble clef 5 times.

*How to teach:* Show the 5 lines and 4 spaces of the staff, numbered from the bottom. Teach drawing the treble clef starting on the second line (it curls around G). Draw 5 treble clefs.

- **Q:** How many lines and spaces are on a staff?
  **A:** 5 lines and 4 spaces.
- **Q:** Why is the treble clef also called the G clef?
  **A:** Its curl wraps around the second line, which is the note G.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Note names
*Student:* Learn E-G-B-D-F (lines) and F-A-C-E (spaces) with a mnemonic.

*How to teach:* Teach line notes E-G-B-D-F and space notes F-A-C-E with the mnemonics. Quiz with flash cards.

- **Q:** Name the line notes from bottom to top.
  **A:** E, G, B, D, F.
- **Q:** Name the space notes from bottom to top.
  **A:** F, A, C, E.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Write
*Student:* Write 10 notes on the staff from letter names.

*How to teach:* The child writes 10 notes on the staff from letter names (e.g., G, E, C, A, F (top line), D, B, F (first space), E (top space), C).

- **Q:** Where does C go on the staff (in the spaces)?
  **A:** In the third space from the bottom.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Read and play
*Student:* Read and play 'Hot Cross Buns' (E-D-C) on a keyboard app from notation.

*How to teach:* Show 'Hot Cross Buns' written as E-D-C (E on the bottom line, D below the staff, C on a ledger line). Find E, D, C on the keyboard and play it from notation.

- **Q:** What 3 notes are in 'Hot Cross Buns'?
  **A:** E, D, C.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Complete a 15-note naming quiz.

*How to teach:* 15-note naming quiz with flash cards or a worksheet.

- **Q:** A note sits on the middle (third) line. What is it?
  **A:** B.

**Assessment:** Note-name quiz

**Answer key:** 15-note naming quiz key (write these on the staff, child names them): 1) bottom line E 2) 2nd line G 3) 3rd line B 4) 4th line D 5) top line F 6) 1st space F 7) 2nd space A 8) 3rd space C 9) top space E 10) 2nd line G 11) 3rd space C 12) 3rd line B 13) 2nd space A 14) 4th line D 15) bottom line E. Proficient = 13 of 15.

## PE 3: Hand dribbling

**Prepare:** Use a playground ball or junior basketball (size 5) inflated to bounce to about waist height. Flat surface such as a driveway; set 6 cones about 5 feet apart for Friday.

**Watch for:**
- Slapping the ball with the palm. Push the ball down with the finger pads, wrist and fingers relaxed.
- Looking at the ball. Keep eyes up so you can see where you are going and where others are.

### Day 1 (Monday): Stationary
*Student:* Dribble 30 times with each hand using fingertips at waist height.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate dribbling: knees bent, push the ball down with the finger pads at waist height, ball beside (not in front of) the body. Dribble 30 times with the right hand, then 30 with the left.

- **Q:** What part of your hand pushes the ball?
  **A:** The finger pads (fingertips), not the palm.
- **Q:** How high should the ball bounce?
  **A:** About waist high or lower.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Walking
*Student:* Dribble while walking a straight line and back, switching hands at the turn.

*How to teach:* Dribble walking to a cone 20 feet away, turn, switch hands and walk back. Repeat 4 times. Cue 'ball slightly ahead and to the side.'

- **Q:** Why switch hands at the turn?
  **A:** To keep the ball on the side away from where you are turning and to practice both hands.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Running
*Student:* Dribble while jogging the length of a driveway or court.

*How to teach:* Dribble while jogging the length of the driveway. The ball is pushed out in front a little more and bounces a bit higher than walking. Jog back and repeat 5 times.

- **Q:** How is a running dribble different from a stationary one?
  **A:** You push the ball a little farther in front of you so you can move forward without kicking it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Eyes up
*Student:* Dribble while calling out the number of fingers a family member holds up.

*How to teach:* While the child dribbles in place, hold up fingers; they call out the number without stopping. Then do the same while walking.

- **Q:** Why practice dribbling with your eyes up?
  **A:** So you can see teammates, defenders and where you're going while still controlling the ball.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Dribble through a 6-cone course without losing control.

*How to teach:* Set the 6-cone course. The child dribbles around each cone and back without losing control. Time it if desired.

- **Q:** What counts as losing control?
  **A:** The ball bouncing away, being caught/held with two hands, or stopping the dribble.

**Assessment:** Dribble course

**Answer key:** Dribble course rubric: 4 = completes 6-cone course with finger-pad dribble, ball at waist height or lower, eyes mostly up, no loss of control; 3 = one loss of control or eyes mostly down; 2 = two or more losses; 1 = cannot dribble continuously.

## Health 3: Communication and conflict resolution

**Prepare:** Write 6–8 scenario cards of everyday conflicts (shared toy, who goes first, being interrupted, borrowed item broken). Index card for the pocket card.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking conflict resolution means someone wins. The goal is a solution both people can accept.
- Trying to solve a conflict while still angry. Step 1 is always to cool down.

### Day 1 (Monday): Active listening
*Student:* Practice active listening: partner tells a story, you repeat back the main points and a feeling.

*How to teach:* You tell a short story; the child listens with eyes on you and then repeats the main points and names a feeling you had. Switch roles.

- **Q:** What does an active listener do?
  **A:** Looks at the speaker, doesn't interrupt, nods, then repeats back the main points and the feeling.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): I-messages
*Student:* Write I-messages ('I feel ___ when ___ because ___') for 5 common conflicts.

*How to teach:* The child writes I-messages for 5 conflicts using 'I feel ___ when ___ because ___.'

- **Q:** Write an I-message for when someone cuts in line.
  **A:** Example: 'I feel upset when you cut in front of me because I was waiting my turn.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Conflict steps
*Student:* Learn 5 steps (cool down, talk, listen, brainstorm, agree) and make a pocket card.

*How to teach:* Teach the 5 steps: cool down, talk (I-message), listen, brainstorm solutions, agree. The child makes a pocket card.

- **Q:** What are the 5 conflict-resolution steps in order?
  **A:** Cool down, talk, listen, brainstorm, agree.
- **Q:** Why do you cool down first?
  **A:** When you're very angry it's hard to think clearly or listen.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Role-play
*Student:* Role-play solving a sibling conflict over a shared toy using the steps.

*How to teach:* Role-play a sibling conflict over a shared toy using the pocket card. Pause to coach each step.

- **Q:** Name two fair solutions for two kids who want the same toy.
  **A:** Take turns with a timer, play with it together, or choose a different activity and switch later.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain the conflict-resolution steps and when you would use them.

*How to teach:* The child explains the steps and gives 2 real situations where they could use them.

- **Q:** When should you get an adult instead of solving it yourself?
  **A:** When someone is getting hurt, it is bullying, or you've tried the steps and it isn't working.

**Assessment:** Role-play and pocket card

**Answer key:** Role-play and pocket card rubric: 4 = pocket card lists the 5 steps in order (cool down, talk, listen, brainstorm, agree); in the role-play the child cools down, uses an I-message, listens and repeats back, offers 2+ ideas and agrees on a fair solution; 3 = all steps but one weak; 2 = skips cooling down or listening; 1 = argues without the steps.
