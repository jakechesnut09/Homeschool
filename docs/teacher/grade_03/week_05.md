# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 5

## ELA 3: Charlotte's Web, chapters 1–5

**Prepare:** Get Charlotte's Web by E.B. White and a reader's notebook. Preview chapters 1–5. Note: chapter 1 opens with Mr. Arable going to kill the runt pig; reassure sensitive children that Fern saves him.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Fern's father is mean — he believes the runt won't survive; Fern's argument about fairness changes his mind.
- Writing questions with yes/no answers — model 'Why…?' and 'How…?' questions answered with evidence.

### Day 1 (Monday): Chapters 1–2
*Student:* Read chapters 1–2 and describe Fern's character with evidence; discuss whether Fern's father was fair.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 1–2 aloud or together. Ask the child to find words that show Fern's character, then debate: was Mr. Arable fair?

- **Q:** How does Fern show she is brave and caring in chapter 1?
  **A:** She runs after her father, grabs the ax and argues it is unfair to kill the pig just because it is small, so he lets her raise it.
- **Q:** What does Fern name the pig, and how does she care for him?
  **A:** Wilbur; she feeds him milk from a baby bottle and treats him like a baby.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Chapter 3–4
*Student:* Read chapters 3–4 and write 3 questions and answers about Wilbur's loneliness; spell dis- and pre- words.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 3–4. The child writes 3 questions and answers about Wilbur's loneliness. Then spell dis- (not/opposite) and pre- (before) words.

- **Q:** Why is Wilbur lonely in chapter 4?
  **A:** It is a rainy day, Fern doesn't come, and the lamb, Templeton and the goose all refuse to play with him.
- **Q:** What does 'preheat' mean?
  **A:** To heat before.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Verbs
*Student:* Identify action and linking verbs in a passage from chapter 4 and write 6 sentences about the barn.

*How to teach:* Teach action verbs (run, spin) and linking verbs (is, seems, feels). The child finds both in a chapter 4 passage and writes 6 sentences about the barn.

- **Q:** In 'Wilbur was lonely,' what kind of verb is 'was'?
  **A:** A linking verb — it links Wilbur to a word that describes him.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Chapter 5
*Student:* Read chapter 5 and describe first impressions of Charlotte using a character web.

*How to teach:* Read chapter 5. The child makes a character web of Charlotte with words and evidence and notes Wilbur's first impressions.

- **Q:** How does Charlotte first greet Wilbur?
  **A:** She says 'Salutations!' and explains it is a fancy way of saying hello.
- **Q:** Why does Wilbur have doubts about Charlotte at first?
  **A:** She traps and drinks the blood of flies and bugs, which seems cruel to him, though she explains it is how spiders must live.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a chapters 1–5 quiz and a prefix spelling check (dis-, pre-, mis-).

*How to teach:* Give the chapters 1–5 quiz and the prefix spelling check. Afterward, have the child find the page that answers any missed question.

- **Q:** Where does Wilbur go to live after Fern raises him?
  **A:** To her uncle Homer Zuckerman's barn.

**Assessment:** Chapters 1–5 quiz; prefix spelling check

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Why was Mr. Arable going to kill the pig? → it was a runt 2) Who saves Wilbur? → Fern 3) Why is Wilbur sold? → he gets too big to keep and Mr. Arable insists (he is sold to Uncle Homer Zuckerman) 4) Who tells Wilbur to escape? → the goose 5) How is Wilbur lured back? → Lurvy/Mr. Zuckerman uses a pail of slops 6) Who becomes Wilbur's friend? → Charlotte, a spider. Spelling (dis-, pre-, mis-): disagree, dislike, disappear, preview, preheat, prepay, misspell, mistake, misread, misplace — proficient 8+/10.

## Math 3: Add/subtract problem solving

**Prepare:** Bar model templates; a family trip mileage chart (e.g., Home→Zoo 128 mi, Zoo→Beach 245 mi, Beach→Home 310 mi). Write the Unit 1 test in the answer key.

**Watch for:**
- Using a 'key word' rule (always adding for 'in all') — draw the bar model and decide which piece is unknown instead.
- In start-unknown problems, adding when they should subtract — write the equation with a letter (n + 125 = 400) and use the inverse operation.

### Day 1 (Monday): Bar models
*Student:* Draw bar models for 6 add/subtract word problems (result, change and start unknown).

*How to teach:* Model a bar model: a long bar for the whole and smaller pieces for the parts, with '?' on the unknown. Do one each of result unknown, change unknown and start unknown. The child draws bars for 6 problems and solves.

- **Q:** Ana had some stickers. She got 125 more and now has 400. How many did she start with?
  **A:** 275 (400 − 125).
- **Q:** A store had 512 apples and sold 238. How many are left?
  **A:** 274.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Letters for unknowns
*Student:* Write equations with a letter for the unknown (e.g., 425 + n = 700) and solve.

*How to teach:* Turn each bar model into an equation with a letter for the unknown, such as 425 + n = 700. Solve using the inverse operation and substitute back to check.

- **Q:** Solve 425 + n = 700.
  **A:** n = 275 (700 − 425).
- **Q:** Solve m − 160 = 340.
  **A:** m = 500 (340 + 160).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Two-step problems
*Student:* Solve 5 two-step add/subtract problems about a library book drive, estimating first.

*How to teach:* Estimate first by rounding, then solve the two steps in order, labeling Step 1 and Step 2. The child solves 5 book-drive problems and compares to the estimates.

- **Q:** The drive collected 214 books Monday and 189 Tuesday, then gave 250 to a school. How many are left?
  **A:** 214 + 189 = 403; 403 − 250 = 153 books. Estimate 200 + 200 − 250 = 150, so it is reasonable.
- **Q:** They want 600 books and have 403. How many more do they need?
  **A:** 197.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Write your own
*Student:* Write and solve two original word problems using data from a family trip mileage chart.

*How to teach:* Using the mileage chart, the child writes two original problems (one one-step, one two-step) and solves them; you solve them too and compare. Check that each problem has enough information and that the child's answers match yours.

- **Q:** Home→Zoo is 128 mi and Zoo→Beach is 245 mi. How far from Home to the Beach through the Zoo?
  **A:** 373 miles.
- **Q:** Beach→Home is 310 mi. How much longer is that than Zoo→Beach (245 mi)?
  **A:** 65 miles.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 assessment
*Student:* Complete the Unit 1 test on rounding and adding/subtracting within 1,000.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 test without help. Review errors the next day with blocks or bar models.

- **Q:** Round 649 to the nearest hundred and to the nearest ten.
  **A:** 600; 650.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test: 1) Round 76 to the nearest 10 → 80 2) Round 845 to the nearest 100 → 800 3) Round 350 to the nearest 100 → 400 4) 456 + 278 = 734 5) 390 + 415 = 805 6) 702 − 348 = 354 7) 600 − 199 = 401 8) 300 + n = 825 → n = 525 9) A farm had 438 hens, bought 126 more, then sold 200 → 364 10) Estimate 489 + 312 to the nearest hundred → 500 + 300 = 800. Proficient: 8+/10.

## Science 3: Magnetic forces

**Prepare:** Refrigerator and bar magnets, paper clips, 15 household objects (spoon, foil, coins, nail, eraser, key, plastic toy …), paper, cardboard, a cup of water.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking magnets attract all metals — magnets attract iron, steel, nickel and cobalt, but not aluminum, copper or most coins.
- Thinking magnetic force needs contact — it acts at a distance and passes through many materials.

### Day 1 (Monday): Magnet hunt
*Student:* Test 15 household objects with a magnet and sort into attracted / not attracted.

*How to teach:* The child predicts whether each object will be attracted, then tests it with a magnet. Sort into attracted / not attracted and look for a pattern.

- **Q:** Will a magnet pick up aluminum foil?
  **A:** No — aluminum is not magnetic.
- **Q:** What do the attracted objects have in common?
  **A:** They contain iron or steel (or nickel).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Poles
*Student:* Explore north and south poles and record which combinations attract or repel.

*How to teach:* Bring the ends of two bar magnets together in all combinations and record which attract and which repel.

- **Q:** What happens when two north poles face each other?
  **A:** They repel (push apart).
- **Q:** What happens when north faces south?
  **A:** They attract (pull together).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Through materials
*Student:* Test whether magnetic force works through paper, cardboard, water and plastic.

*How to teach:* Test whether a magnet can move a paper clip through paper, cardboard, plastic and water (put the clip in a cup of water and move the magnet outside the cup).

- **Q:** Can magnetic force pass through paper and water?
  **A:** Yes — the magnet still pulls the paper clip through them.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Distance
*Student:* Measure how far a paper clip can be from a magnet before it is pulled, using different numbers of paper sheets.

*How to teach:* Stack sheets of paper between a magnet and a paper clip, adding sheets until the clip no longer jumps. Record the number of sheets for two different magnets.

- **Q:** What happens to magnetic force as distance increases?
  **A:** It gets weaker.
- **Q:** How could you compare two magnets' strength?
  **A:** See which one can pull the clip through more sheets of paper.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write cause-and-effect sentences explaining magnetic interactions.

*How to teach:* The child writes 4 cause-and-effect sentences ('Because the poles are the same, the magnets repel').

- **Q:** Complete: When a magnet is moved closer to a paper clip, …
  **A:** the pull gets stronger and the clip may jump to the magnet.

**Assessment:** Cause/effect explanation

**Answer key:** Cause/effect explanation: proficient (3) = 4 accurate sentences covering attraction of iron/steel, like poles repel/unlike attract, force through materials, and force weakens with distance; 2 = 2–3 accurate; 1 = misconceptions (e.g., all metals attracted).

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Geography field study

**Prepare:** Choose a nearby park, river or hill for the field study; bring a notebook, pencils and a camera or phone. Prepare the Unit 1 test below.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up physical and human features. Physical features are natural (hills, rivers, trees); human features are made by people (roads, bridges, buildings).

### Day 1 (Monday): Field trip
*Student:* Visit a local park, river or hill and sketch landforms and plants.

*How to teach:* At the site, the child sketches landforms and plants and labels them.

- **Q:** Name two physical features you saw.
  **A:** Answers vary by community; e.g., a hill, a creek, oak trees, a pond.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Human features
*Student:* Identify roads, buildings and bridges in the area.

*How to teach:* Find human features: roads, paths, buildings, bridges, fences.

- **Q:** Is a bridge a physical or human feature?
  **A:** A human feature, built by people.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Field map
*Student:* Make a map of the field study site.

*How to teach:* The child draws a map of the site with title, key and compass rose.

- **Q:** What direction is the main path from the parking lot?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; answer should use a cardinal direction checked with a compass or phone.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Present
*Student:* Present findings with photos.

*How to teach:* The child presents findings with photos to the family.

- **Q:** How did people change this place?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; e.g., built trails, planted lawns, added a bridge or parking lot.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 assessment
*Student:* Complete the unit test on geography.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 test.

- **Q:** Name California's four main regions.
  **A:** Coast, Central Valley, Sierra Nevada (mountains), desert.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test; field map

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test key: 1) Compass rose shows directions. 2) Map key explains symbols. 3) Order: continent, country, state, city. 4) Island = land surrounded by water. 5) Peninsula = land surrounded by water on three sides. 6) California regions: coast, Central Valley, Sierra Nevada, desert. 7) Climate = usual weather over many years. 8) Natural resource example (water, soil, timber). 9) Where your water comes from (local answer). 10) One positive and one negative effect of a dam. Field map rubric: 4 = title, compass rose, key, and at least 3 physical and 2 human features placed accurately; 3 = missing one element; 2 = missing several; 1 = sketch only. 8–10 = proficient.

## Art & Music 3: Steady beat and rhythm

**Prepare:** A drum, upside-down bucket or oatmeal container and a wooden spoon or hands. Recordings of 'Yankee Doodle' (a traditional American song) and 3 instrumental tracks at slow, medium and fast tempos. A list of 10 four-beat rhythms to clap (e.g., ta ta ti-ti ta).

**Watch for:**
- Confusing beat and rhythm. The beat is the steady pulse that never changes; the rhythm is the pattern of long and short sounds (often the words).
- Speeding up when excited. Keep the beat steady; pat it with the music and listen.

### Day 1 (Monday): Beat vs. rhythm
*Student:* Pat the steady beat on your knees while singing 'Yankee Doodle,' then clap the rhythm of the words.

*How to teach:* Sing 'Yankee Doodle' while patting the steady beat on the knees. Then sing again clapping every syllable of the words (the rhythm). Compare: which pattern stayed even?

- **Q:** What is the difference between beat and rhythm?
  **A:** The beat is the steady, even pulse; the rhythm is the pattern of long and short sounds, like the syllables of the words.
- **Q:** When you clapped the words, was it always even?
  **A:** No — the rhythm has some quick sounds and some longer sounds; the beat stays even.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Body percussion
*Student:* Perform a 4-beat body percussion pattern (stomp, pat, clap, snap) to a steady beat.

*How to teach:* Teach a 4-beat body percussion pattern: stomp (1), pat (2), clap (3), snap (4). Repeat it to a steady beat, then speed it up and slow it down.

- **Q:** What is the order of our body percussion pattern?
  **A:** Stomp, pat, clap, snap — one per beat.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tempo
*Student:* Walk, jog and tiptoe to recorded music at slow, medium and fast tempos.

*How to teach:* Play the tracks. The child walks to slow music, jogs to medium and tiptoes quickly to fast music, matching the beat.

- **Q:** What is tempo?
  **A:** The speed of the beat — fast or slow.
- **Q:** Which movement matched the fast tempo?
  **A:** Quick tiptoeing (fast steps).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Echo
*Student:* Echo-clap 10 four-beat rhythm patterns played by a parent or recording.

*How to teach:* Clap 10 four-beat rhythms; the child echoes each immediately. Repeat missed ones.

- **Q:** What does 'echo' mean in music?
  **A:** Repeating exactly what you just heard.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Perform the beat on a bucket drum while a family member claps the rhythm, then switch.

*How to teach:* The child plays a steady beat on the bucket drum while a family member claps the rhythm of 'Yankee Doodle' (or another song); then switch roles.

- **Q:** If you keep the beat and I clap the rhythm, who has more claps?
  **A:** Usually the rhythm, because some beats have two or more sounds.

**Assessment:** Beat and rhythm performance

**Answer key:** Beat and rhythm performance: 4 = keeps a steady beat on the drum for the whole song without speeding up, claps the word rhythm accurately when roles switch, and echoes 9–10 of 10 patterns; 3 = steady beat with small drift, echoes 7–8; 2 = beat wanders, echoes 4–6; 1 = cannot keep a beat.

## PE 3: Jumping and landing

**Prepare:** Gather tape or chalk for a start line, a measuring tape, chalk for the vertical jump (or sticky notes), and draw a hopscotch grid (1–10). Jump on grass or a soft surface.

**Watch for:**
- Landing with straight, locked legs. Land on the balls of the feet and bend hips, knees and ankles to absorb force ('land like a spring').
- Keeping arms still. Swinging the arms back then forward and up adds force and distance.

### Day 1 (Monday): Landing
*Student:* Practice soft landings with bent knees.

*How to teach:* Teach 'quiet landings': jump in place and land softly on both feet with knees, hips and ankles bent, as if landing on eggshells. Practice 10 small jumps and 5 jumps off a low step, checking that you can barely hear the landing.

- **Q:** How do you land safely after a jump?
  **A:** On both feet (balls of the feet first), with knees, hips and ankles bent to absorb the force, arms forward for balance.
- **Q:** Why does a quiet landing mean a good landing?
  **A:** It means your legs bent and absorbed the force instead of slamming down.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Standing long jump
*Student:* Measure 3 standing long jumps.

*How to teach:* Teach the standing long jump: feet shoulder-width behind the line, crouch and swing arms back, swing arms forward and jump out, land on both feet. Measure from the line to the back of the heel closest to the line. Do 3 jumps and record each.

- **Q:** Where do you measure a long jump to?
  **A:** From the start line to the back of the heel (the landing mark closest to the line).
- **Q:** Jumps of 42, 47 and 45 inches — which is your best?
  **A:** 47 inches.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Vertical jump
*Student:* Measure vertical jump with chalk on a wall.

*How to teach:* Have the child stand sideways to a wall, reach up with chalky fingers and mark standing reach. Then crouch, swing arms and jump, marking the highest point. Measure between the two marks. Do 3 tries.

- **Q:** Your reach mark is 54 inches and your jump mark is 63 inches. What is your vertical jump?
  **A:** 63 − 54 = 9 inches.
- **Q:** Should you take a running start for this test?
  **A:** No — jump from standing with two feet, using a crouch and arm swing.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Hopscotch
*Student:* Play hopscotch with variations.

*How to teach:* Play hopscotch: toss a marker, hop on one foot on single squares and land on two feet on side-by-side squares, skipping the marker square, then turn and come back. Add variations: hop only on the left foot, or jump with feet crossed.

- **Q:** In hopscotch, when do you land on two feet?
  **A:** On the squares that are side by side (and at the turn-around).
- **Q:** Is a single-square move a hop or a jump?
  **A:** A hop — one foot to the same foot.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Explain how arms help create force.

*How to teach:* Review the jump log. Have the child do 2 long jumps — one without arms, one with a full arm swing — measure both and explain the difference.

- **Q:** How do your arms help you jump farther?
  **A:** Swinging the arms back and then forcefully forward and up adds momentum and force that carries the body farther.
- **Q:** What should you do with your legs when you land?
  **A:** Bend the knees, hips and ankles to absorb the force.

**Assessment:** Jump measurements

**Answer key:** Jump measurements check: proficient record shows 3 standing long jumps measured to the nearest inch from line to heel with the best circled, a vertical jump calculated as jump mark minus reach mark, and soft two-foot landings with bent knees on every trial. 4 = all measurements correct and safe landings; 3 = one measurement error; 2 = missing a test or landings stiff; 1 = record incomplete.

## Health 3: Physical activity and health

**Prepare:** Activity log sheet and a timer. Know the guideline: children need at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity every day. Prepare a SMART goal template (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) and a 7-day tracker.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking only sports count as exercise. Walking, biking, dancing, playing tag, swimming and chores like raking all count.
- Writing a vague goal like 'exercise more.' A SMART goal says exactly what, how much and when: 'I will ride my bike 20 minutes after lunch 5 days this week.'

### Day 1 (Monday): Benefits
*Student:* List ways activity helps the heart, muscles, bones, mood and sleep.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm with the child how activity helps: heart gets stronger, muscles and bones get stronger, better mood, better sleep, healthy weight. They draw a body and label 5 benefits.

- **Q:** Name three ways physical activity helps your body.
  **A:** Any three: strengthens the heart and lungs, builds muscles, strengthens bones, improves mood, helps you sleep better, keeps a healthy weight.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Activity log
*Student:* Log all physical activity minutes for a day and compare to the 60-minute goal.

*How to teach:* The child logs every activity and its minutes today. Add the total and compare to 60 minutes.

- **Q:** How many minutes of activity do kids need each day?
  **A:** At least 60 minutes.
- **Q:** You walked 15 minutes, played tag 20 minutes and biked 10 minutes. How many more minutes to reach 60?
  **A:** 15 more minutes (15 + 20 + 10 = 45; 60 − 45 = 15).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Screen time
*Student:* Track screen time and brainstorm active swaps.

*How to teach:* Track screen time for the day. The child lists 5 'active swaps' (instead of a show, dance to 2 songs; instead of a game, shoot hoops).

- **Q:** Why does too much screen time make it harder to be healthy?
  **A:** Sitting a long time replaces active play and can disturb sleep.
- **Q:** Give one active swap for screen time.
  **A:** Any active choice, e.g., a bike ride, jump rope, a walk, dancing or playing outside.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Goal setting
*Student:* Set a one-week SMART goal to add activity or a fruit/vegetable serving.

*How to teach:* Explain SMART with an example. The child writes a one-week SMART goal to add activity or a fruit/vegetable serving and sets up a 7-day tracker.

- **Q:** Is 'I will be healthier' a SMART goal? Fix it.
  **A:** No, it is not specific or measurable. Example fix: 'I will eat one vegetable at lunch every day for 7 days.'
- **Q:** What does the M in SMART stand for?
  **A:** Measurable: you can count or check it.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Check day 1 of the goal tracker and plan supports.

*How to teach:* Check day 1 of the tracker together. Ask what made it easy or hard and plan 2 supports (a reminder, a family helper, putting fruit on the counter).

- **Q:** What is a 'support' for a goal?
  **A:** Something or someone that helps you reach it, like a reminder, a family member who joins you, or setting things up ahead of time.

**Assessment:** SMART goal and tracker

**Answer key:** SMART goal and tracker rubric: 4 = goal is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound (e.g., 'I will play outside for 30 extra minutes every day this week'), tracker has 7 days with daily checks, and child lists at least 2 supports; 3 = goal missing one SMART part; 2 = vague goal but tracker started; 1 = no clear goal.
