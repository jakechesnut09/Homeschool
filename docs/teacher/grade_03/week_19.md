# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 19

## ELA 3: Imagery and word choice

**Prepare:** Copies of 'Who Has Seen the Wind?' by Christina Rossetti and 'April Rain Song' by Langston Hughes (poetry anthology or Poetry Foundation website). Word cards for shades of meaning.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking imagery is only about sight — images can appeal to any of the five senses.
- Treating related words as identical — 'whisper' and 'shout' both mean speaking but at very different strengths.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rossetti
*Student:* Read 'Who Has Seen the Wind?' and sketch the images the poet creates.

*How to teach:* Read 'Who Has Seen the Wind?' twice. Ask how we know the wind is there if we can't see it. The child sketches the two images the poet creates.

- **Q:** How does the poem show the wind if no one can see it?
  **A:** Through its effects: the leaves 'hang trembling' and the trees 'bow down their heads.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Hughes
*Student:* Read 'April Rain Song' and list sensory words; spell VCV words (cab-in, ti-ger).

*How to teach:* Read 'April Rain Song' aloud slowly. The child lists every sensory word and which sense it uses. Then divide and spell VCV words (ca-bin, ti-ger).

- **Q:** What sensory images does Hughes use?
  **A:** Rain kissing you, beating on your head with 'silver liquid drops,' singing a lullaby, making pools, and playing a sleep-song on the roof.
- **Q:** How does the speaker feel about rain?
  **A:** He loves it — the poem ends 'And I love the rain.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Shades of meaning
*Student:* Order related words on a line (whisper, say, shout; think, believe, know).

*How to teach:* Lay word cards on a line from weakest to strongest and discuss small differences. The child orders several sets and uses one word from each in a sentence.

- **Q:** Order from quietest to loudest: shout, say, whisper.
  **A:** whisper, say, shout.
- **Q:** Order from least sure to most sure: know, think, believe.
  **A:** think, believe, know.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Free verse
*Student:* Write a free-verse poem about weather using sensory words.

*How to teach:* Explain free verse (no regular rhyme or rhythm). The child writes a free-verse poem about weather using at least 4 sensory words.

- **Q:** What is free verse?
  **A:** Poetry without a regular rhyme or beat pattern.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain in writing how one poet's word choice creates a feeling.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph explaining how one poet's word choice creates a feeling, quoting at least one word or phrase. Then give the shades-of-meaning check.

- **Q:** How does Hughes's word choice create a feeling?
  **A:** Words like 'kiss' and 'lullaby' make rain feel gentle and comforting instead of gloomy.

**Assessment:** Word choice response; shades of meaning check

**Answer key:** Word choice response: 4 = names poem and poet, quotes 2 words/phrases and explains the feeling each creates; 3 (proficient) = one quoted word explained; 2 = feeling named without evidence; 1 = off-topic. Shades of meaning: whisper–say–shout; tiny–small–big–huge; think–believe–know — proficient 2+/3.

## Math 3: Arithmetic patterns

**Prepare:** Addition table (0–10) and multiplication table (0–10), colored pencils. Write the Unit 5 test and a 20-fact mixed check.

**Watch for:**
- Believing odd × odd is even — test examples: 3 × 5 = 15 is odd.
- Naming an input/output rule from only one pair — check the rule against every row.

### Day 1 (Monday): Even and odd
*Student:* Investigate whether sums and products of evens and odds are even or odd.

*How to teach:* The child tests many examples and fills a chart for even + even, even + odd, odd + odd, then the same for products, and states the rules. Ask the child to explain why the rule works using pairs (an odd number always has one left over).

- **Q:** Is odd + odd even or odd? Give an example.
  **A:** Even; 3 + 5 = 8.
- **Q:** Is even × odd even or odd?
  **A:** Even; 4 × 3 = 12.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Table patterns
*Student:* Find and color patterns in the addition table and write rules.

*How to teach:* Color patterns in the addition table (equal sums along diagonals, rows increasing by 1). The child writes 3 rules.

- **Q:** What happens moving down one column of the addition table?
  **A:** Each sum increases by 1.
- **Q:** Along a diagonal from bottom-left to top-right, what stays the same?
  **A:** The sums are all equal (3 + 5, 4 + 4, 5 + 3 all equal 8).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Input/output
*Student:* Complete input-output tables and name the rule.

*How to teach:* Fill in input/output tables and name each rule (× 4, + 7, ÷ 5 …). Check the rule on every pair.

- **Q:** In: 2, 3, 5 → Out: 8, 12, 20. What is the rule, and what is the output for 7?
  **A:** × 4; 28.
- **Q:** In: 10, 15, 20 → Out: 2, 3, 4. What is the rule?
  **A:** ÷ 5.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Explain it
*Student:* Write an explanation of why 4 times any number is always even.

*How to teach:* The child writes why 4 times any number is even: 4 × n = 2 × (2 × n), so it is a double, and doubles are always even. Ask them to test the rule with three numbers, including an odd one, before writing.

- **Q:** Why is 4 × 7 even?
  **A:** 4 × 7 = 2 × 14 — a double — and doubles are even, so 28 is even.
- **Q:** Is 4 × 9 even?
  **A:** Yes, 36.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 assessment
*Student:* Complete a short test on two-step problems and patterns plus a mixed fact check.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test plus the 20-fact mixed check. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** Is odd × odd even or odd?
  **A:** Odd (e.g., 3 × 7 = 21).

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test: 1) 4 packs of 6 juice boxes, 5 drunk → 19 2) $40 − 3 × $8 → $16 3) Equation with a letter for #2 → m = 40 − 3 × 8 4) 7 + 9 even or odd → even 5) 5 × 7 even or odd → odd 6) In 1, 2, 3 → Out 6, 12, 18: rule × 6, output for 5 = 30 7) Why is 2 × any number even? → it is a double. Proficient: 6+/7 and 18+/20 facts.

## Science 3: Fossils as evidence of past environments

**Prepare:** Fossil picture cards (fish, fern, shark tooth, palm leaf, mammoth tooth, seashell), bread and sandwich fillings for a rock-layer model, a simple rock-layer diagram.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the place a fossil is found has always looked the same — land changes over very long times.
- Thinking the top rock layers are the oldest — in undisturbed layers the bottom layers formed first and are oldest.

### Day 1 (Monday): Fossil clues
*Student:* Examine fossil cards (fish, fern, shark tooth) and infer past environments.

*How to teach:* Hand the child one fossil card at a time and ask: What did this organism need to live? What does that tell us about this place long ago? Record each inference in a two-column chart.

- **Q:** What does a fish fossil tell us about the place where it was found?
  **A:** That the area was once underwater.
- **Q:** What does a fern or palm fossil suggest?
  **A:** That the area was once warm and moist.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rock layers
*Student:* Build a layered sandwich model of rock layers and discuss older vs. newer layers.

*How to teach:* Build a 'rock layer' sandwich one layer at a time, naming each as it goes down. Ask which layer went down first and which is youngest, then relate it to a real rock-layer diagram.

- **Q:** In undisturbed rock layers, which layers are oldest?
  **A:** The bottom layers, because they were laid down first.
- **Q:** If a trilobite fossil is in a lower layer than a dinosaur fossil, which lived earlier?
  **A:** The trilobite.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Desert ocean
*Student:* Investigate why marine fossils are found in deserts and mountains.

*How to teach:* Show photos of seashell fossils found in deserts and high mountains. Discuss how land can rise or seas can dry up over millions of years, and have the child explain it in their own words.

- **Q:** Why are seashell fossils found on mountaintops?
  **A:** The land was once under the sea; over millions of years it was pushed up to form mountains.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Data table
*Student:* Complete a data table matching fossils to environments.

*How to teach:* The child completes a table matching each fossil card to its likely past environment (ocean, swamp/forest, cold grassland) with a reason.

- **Q:** Shark tooth fossil → which environment, and why?
  **A:** Ocean — sharks live in the sea.
- **Q:** Mammoth tooth fossil → which environment?
  **A:** A cold grassland (Ice Age).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a claim about a past environment using fossil evidence.

*How to teach:* The child writes a claim about a past environment using at least two fossils as evidence and explains the reasoning.

- **Q:** Write a model claim for a desert where fish and shell fossils are found.
  **A:** This desert was once covered by water, because fish and shell fossils are found here and those animals can only live in water.

**Assessment:** Fossil evidence claim

**Answer key:** Fossil evidence claim: 3 = claim about a past environment, 2 fossils as evidence, reasoning about what those organisms needed; 2 = one piece of evidence or weak reasoning; 1 = no evidence. Data table: proficient if each fossil matches a sensible environment with a reason.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Early economy and entrepreneurs

**Prepare:** Local business histories (historical society, chamber of commerce 'history' pages, company websites), historic photos of early stores, mills, canneries or farms.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a business needs no risk. Entrepreneurs risk their own money and time with no guarantee of success.

### Day 1 (Monday): Early businesses
*Student:* Research 3 early businesses in your town (general store, mill, farm, cannery).

*How to teach:* Research 3 early businesses in town.

- **Q:** What did a general store do?
  **A:** Sold many kinds of goods (food, tools, cloth) to the community in one place.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Entrepreneur
*Student:* Read about a local entrepreneur and the risks they took to start a business.

*How to teach:* Read about one local entrepreneur and the risks taken.

- **Q:** What is an entrepreneur?
  **A:** A person who starts a business and takes a risk to make a profit.
- **Q:** Name one risk an entrepreneur takes.
  **A:** Losing the money they invested if the business fails.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Private property
*Student:* Discuss why owning land and businesses encouraged settlers to build and invest.

*How to teach:* Discuss private property: when people own land and businesses, they have reasons to build, improve and invest.

- **Q:** Why would owning land encourage a settler to build a house and plant orchards?
  **A:** The settler keeps the benefits of the work and can pass the property on or sell it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Then and now
*Student:* Compare early industries with today's major employers in a T-chart.

*How to teach:* Make a T-chart of early industries vs. today's major employers.

- **Q:** Name an early industry and a modern one in your town.
  **A:** Answers vary by community; e.g., early: farming, mining, lumber; today: health care, technology, tourism, schools.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain in writing how one early business shaped today's local economy.

*How to teach:* The child writes how one early business shaped today's economy.

- **Q:** How can one early business affect a town for a long time?
  **A:** It brings jobs, roads and other businesses that serve it, and the town grows around it.

**Assessment:** Economy explanation

**Answer key:** Economy explanation rubric: 4 = names an early business, explains what it produced or sold, gives 2 ways it shaped today's economy (jobs, roads, related industries, town growth) and compares with a modern employer; 3 = one connection; 2 = describes the business only; 1 = inaccurate.

## Art & Music 3: Pattern and printmaking

**Prepare:** Clean foam meat trays (cut off the rims), dull pencils, tempera paint (slightly thick), a brayer/roller or sponge, a flat tray for rolling paint, paper. A potato and a butter knife or sponges for stamps (an adult cuts potatoes). Phone or sketchbook for pattern hunting.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a print comes out the same direction as the plate. A print is a mirror image, so letters must be drawn backward.
- Pressing too lightly on the foam. Lines must be pressed deep, or paint fills them in and they disappear.

### Day 1 (Monday): Patterns
*Student:* Photograph or sketch 5 patterns found in fabrics, tiles and nature.

*How to teach:* Find and sketch or photograph 5 patterns in fabrics, tiles and nature (pinecone, honeycomb, plaid shirt). Name the repeating unit in each.

- **Q:** What is a pattern?
  **A:** A design where lines, shapes or colors repeat in a planned way.
- **Q:** What is an alternating pattern?
  **A:** A pattern where two (or more) elements take turns, like circle-square-circle-square.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Stamp prints
*Student:* Carve potato or sponge stamps and print an alternating pattern border.

*How to teach:* An adult cuts a potato in half; the child draws a simple shape and the adult carves away the background (or cut sponge shapes). Print an alternating pattern border around a page (e.g., star, circle, star, circle).

- **Q:** What part of a stamp prints?
  **A:** The raised part (the part left standing); carved-away areas stay blank.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Foam plate
*Student:* Draw a design firmly into a foam tray with a pencil to make a print plate.

*How to teach:* Draw a design on paper first, then trace it firmly into the foam with a dull pencil, pressing deep lines. Remind: words must be written backward.

- **Q:** Will the lines you press into foam print in color or stay white?
  **A:** They stay the color of the paper (white) because the paint sits on the raised surface, not in the grooves.
- **Q:** Why write letters backward on the plate?
  **A:** The print is a mirror image, so backward letters print the right way.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Print edition
*Student:* Roll paint onto the plate and print an edition of 3 prints, signing and numbering each.

*How to teach:* Roll a thin, even layer of paint onto the plate, place paper on top, rub with the palm, peel off. Make 3 prints. Sign in pencil under each print and number them 1/3, 2/3, 3/3.

- **Q:** What does '2/3' written under a print mean?
  **A:** It is the 2nd print in an edition of 3 prints.
- **Q:** What is a print edition?
  **A:** A set of matching prints made from the same plate.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Choose your best print and explain why using criteria (clear lines, even ink).

*How to teach:* Choose the best print and explain using criteria: clear lines, even ink, clean edges, centered on paper.

- **Q:** Why might a print look blotchy?
  **A:** Too much or uneven paint, or the paper moved while rubbing.

**Assessment:** Print edition

**Answer key:** Print edition rubric: 4 = 3 prints from the same plate with clear deep lines, even ink and clean edges, each signed and numbered (1/3, 2/3, 3/3); best print chosen with 2+ criteria; 3 = 3 prints with one blotchy or minor numbering slip; 2 = fewer than 3 prints or lines unclear; 1 = no edition. Pattern border: alternating pattern repeated correctly around the page.

## PE 3: Small-sided games

**Prepare:** Ball (soccer ball or playground ball), 4 cones for a small field and 2 more for goals. Invite a sibling, parent or friend for 2v1 and the mini game. Paper for Friday's reflection.

**Watch for:**
- Everyone chasing the ball ('beehive soccer'). Teammates spread out into open space so they can receive a pass.
- Facing the ball and standing still on defense. Use a ready stance (knees bent, on balls of feet) and stay between the attacker and the goal.

### Day 1 (Monday): Open space
*Student:* Practice moving to open space to receive a pass in a 2-person keep-away.

*How to teach:* Play 2-person keep-away in a square with one defender (parent). The child practices moving to open space — a spot where the passer has a clear lane — and calling for the ball. Freeze play occasionally and ask 'Where is the open space?'

- **Q:** What is open space?
  **A:** An area away from defenders where a teammate can pass you the ball without it being intercepted.
- **Q:** Why move after you pass?
  **A:** To get open again for a return pass.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Guarding
*Student:* Practice defensive stance and staying between the attacker and the goal.

*How to teach:* Teach the defensive stance: feet apart, knees bent, weight on balls of feet, arms out. The child shuffles to stay between the attacker and the goal as you dribble slowly.

- **Q:** Where should a defender stand?
  **A:** Between the attacker and the goal they are protecting.
- **Q:** Describe a good defensive stance.
  **A:** Feet shoulder-width apart, knees bent, on the balls of the feet, arms out, eyes on the attacker's body/ball.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): 2v1
*Student:* Play 2v1 games where attackers use passes to beat one defender.

*How to teach:* Play 2v1: two attackers try to score past one defender. Teach 'draw and pass': dribble at the defender, and when they come to you, pass to your open teammate.

- **Q:** In 2v1, when should you pass?
  **A:** When the defender commits to you, pass to your teammate who is now open.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mini game
*Student:* Play a mini soccer or basketball game with family or friends.

*How to teach:* Play a mini soccer or basketball game (2v2 or 3v3) for 15–20 minutes with simple rules. Pause to praise spacing, passing and fair play.

- **Q:** What does fair play look like in a game?
  **A:** Following the rules, being honest about calls, no rough play, encouraging teammates and congratulating opponents.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Write about one strategy you used and one fair-play moment.

*How to teach:* The child writes 3–5 sentences: one strategy they used (e.g., moving to open space, draw and pass, staying goal-side) and one fair-play moment.

- **Q:** Give an example of a strategy and how it helped.
  **A:** E.g., 'I moved to open space so my brother could pass to me, and I scored.'

**Assessment:** Game play observation and reflection

**Answer key:** Game play observation and reflection rubric: 4 = during play the child moves to open space, uses a defensive stance goal-side, and passes when a teammate is open; reflection names a specific strategy, how it helped, and a specific fair-play moment; 3 = shows 2 of the 3 skills and reflection names a strategy and fair-play moment with less detail; 2 = mostly follows the ball, reflection vague; 1 = no reflection or unsafe play.

## Health 3: Pedestrian, bike and passenger safety

**Prepare:** Bike helmet (check it fits and isn't cracked), bicycle, a planned short walking route with a crosswalk. Know California law: children under 8 ride in a car seat or booster in the back seat; children under 8 who are 4'9" or taller may use a seat belt; kids under 13 should ride in the back seat. Bike helmets are required by law for riders under 18 in California.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the green walk light means it's always safe to go. Still look left-right-left and watch for turning cars.
- Wearing the helmet tipped back on the forehead. It should sit level, about 2 fingers above the eyebrows.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pedestrian
*Student:* Walk a neighborhood route with an adult practicing stop-look-listen and crosswalk rules.

*How to teach:* Walk a route with a crosswalk. At each corner the child stops at the curb, looks left-right-left, listens, and crosses only when clear.

- **Q:** What do you do before crossing a street?
  **A:** Stop at the curb, look left, right and left again, listen for cars, and cross when it's clear, preferably at a crosswalk.
- **Q:** If there is no sidewalk, which way do you walk?
  **A:** Facing traffic, on the left side, as far from the road as possible.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Helmet fit
*Student:* Fit your helmet using the eyes-ears-mouth test from Safe Kids.

*How to teach:* Fit the helmet: Eyes — look up and see the rim (2 fingers above brows); Ears — straps make a V under each ear; Mouth — open wide and the helmet hugs the head; buckle snug with only 1–2 fingers under the strap.

- **Q:** What is the eyes-ears-mouth test?
  **A:** Eyes: you can see the helmet rim when looking up (about 2 fingers above eyebrows). Ears: straps form a V just under the ears. Mouth: opening wide makes the helmet hug your head.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Bike rules
*Student:* Learn bike hand signals and ride a safe route, signaling turns.

*How to teach:* Teach hand signals (left turn: left arm straight out; right turn: right arm straight out or left arm bent up; stop: left arm bent down). Ride a short safe route signaling turns.

- **Q:** Show the hand signal for stopping.
  **A:** Left arm bent down at the elbow, palm facing back.
- **Q:** Which side of the road do bikes ride on?
  **A:** The right side, in the same direction as traffic.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Car safety
*Student:* Learn California booster seat rules and the 5-step seat belt fit test.

*How to teach:* Explain booster seat rules and do the 5-step seat belt test in the car.

- **Q:** What are the 5 steps of the seat-belt fit test?
  **A:** Back against the seat; knees bend at the seat edge; lap belt low on the upper thighs/hips; shoulder belt across the middle of the chest and shoulder (not the neck); can stay seated like that the whole trip.
- **Q:** Where should kids under 13 sit in the car?
  **A:** In the back seat.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a pedestrian, bike and passenger safety checklist.

*How to teach:* The child completes the checklist and explains each item.

- **Q:** Why wear bright clothes when walking or biking?
  **A:** So drivers can see you, especially at dusk or in bad weather.

**Assessment:** Safety checklist

**Answer key:** Safety checklist key: Pedestrian – stop at the curb, look left-right-left, listen, cross at crosswalks, walk facing traffic if no sidewalk, make eye contact with drivers. Bike – helmet fits (eyes-ears-mouth), ride on the right with traffic, use hand signals, obey stop signs, bright clothing. Passenger – booster seat until the seat belt fits (usually 4'9"), back seat under 13, buckle every ride, 5-step test passes. Proficient: child can explain each item.
