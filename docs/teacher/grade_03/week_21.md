# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 21

## ELA 3: Biography: Helen Keller

**Prepare:** Chapter 4 of Helen Keller's The Story of My Life (Project Gutenberg — the water-pump scene) and a children's biography of Helen Keller from the library. Timeline paper.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing biography and autobiography — 'auto' means self; an autobiography is written by the person about her own life.
- Thinking Helen was born deaf and blind — an illness when she was about 19 months old caused it.

### Day 1 (Monday): Biography basics
*Student:* Compare biography and autobiography; start a timeline of Helen Keller's life.

*How to teach:* Explain biography (written by someone else) vs. autobiography (written by the person). Read the first chapter of the library biography and start a timeline with the earliest dates.

- **Q:** Is The Story of My Life a biography or an autobiography?
  **A:** An autobiography — Helen Keller wrote it herself.
- **Q:** When and where was Helen Keller born?
  **A:** In 1880, in Tuscumbia, Alabama.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Primary account
*Student:* Read the water-pump excerpt from 'The Story of My Life' and discuss how she felt.

*How to teach:* Read the water-pump passage from chapter 4 aloud. Ask how Helen felt before and after, and have the child find the words that show it.

- **Q:** What happened at the water pump?
  **A:** Anne Sullivan spelled w-a-t-e-r into one of Helen's hands while cool water flowed over the other, and Helen suddenly understood that words name things.
- **Q:** How did Helen feel afterward?
  **A:** Joyful and eager — she wrote that the 'living word awakened my soul' and she wanted to learn the name of everything.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Conjunctions
*Student:* Combine sentences about Helen Keller using and, but, or, so; spell over-, under-, sub- words.

*How to teach:* Model combining two short sentences with and, but, or, so and a comma. The child combines 6 pairs about Helen Keller. Then spell over-, under- and sub- words.

- **Q:** Combine: Helen could not see. She learned to read.
  **A:** Helen could not see, but she learned to read (braille).
- **Q:** What does 'submarine' mean, using sub-?
  **A:** Under the sea — a boat that travels underwater.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Main idea
*Student:* Identify the main idea of a biography chapter and its key details.

*How to teach:* Read another chapter of the biography. The child writes the main idea in one sentence and lists 3 key details that support it.

- **Q:** Who was Anne Sullivan?
  **A:** Helen's teacher, who arrived in 1887 and taught her words by spelling them into her hand.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete the timeline and write a summary of Helen Keller's achievements.

*How to teach:* The child completes the timeline and writes a summary paragraph of Helen Keller's achievements. Check dates against the biography and have the child correct any errors.

- **Q:** Name one of Helen Keller's great achievements.
  **A:** She graduated from Radcliffe College in 1904, wrote books, and worked her whole life to help people with disabilities.

**Assessment:** Timeline and summary

**Answer key:** Timeline: 1880 born in Tuscumbia, Alabama → 1882 illness leaves her deaf and blind → 1887 Anne Sullivan arrives; the water-pump breakthrough → 1903 publishes The Story of My Life → 1904 graduates from Radcliffe College → lifelong advocate for people with disabilities; died 1968. Summary rubric: 3 = main idea plus 3 accurate achievements in own words; 2 = 1–2 achievements; 1 = inaccurate.

## Math 3: Non-unit fractions

**Prepare:** Fraction strips from last week, pattern blocks (or printed paper ones), 8 counters in two colors.

**Watch for:**
- Reading 3/4 as '3 and 4' — say '3 fourths,' meaning 3 pieces of size 1/4.
- Writing the unshaded count as the numerator — the numerator counts the parts being described (shaded).

### Day 1 (Monday): Building fractions
*Student:* Build 3/4, 5/6 and 2/3 by joining unit fraction strips.

*How to teach:* Join three 1/4 strips and say '1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 is 3 fourths, 3/4.' Build 5/6 and 2/3 the same way.

- **Q:** How many 1/6 pieces make 5/6?
  **A:** 5.
- **Q:** What is 2/3 made of?
  **A:** Two 1/3 pieces.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pattern blocks
*Student:* Use pattern blocks with the hexagon as one whole to show halves, thirds and sixths.

*How to teach:* Use the yellow hexagon as 1 whole: trapezoid = 1/2, blue rhombus = 1/3, triangle = 1/6. Build fractions like 2/3 (2 rhombuses).

- **Q:** How many triangles cover the hexagon, and what fraction is one triangle?
  **A:** 6; 1/6.
- **Q:** What fraction of the hexagon do 2 rhombuses cover?
  **A:** 2/3.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fractions of a set
*Student:* Explore fractions of a group of 8 counters, e.g., 3/8 are red.

*How to teach:* Line up 8 counters with 3 red: 3/8 are red. Change colors to show other fractions of the set.

- **Q:** 8 counters, 3 red. What fraction are red? Not red?
  **A:** 3/8 red; 5/8 not red.
- **Q:** 4 of 8 counters are blue. What fraction is blue?
  **A:** 4/8 (also equal to 1/2).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Pizza problems
*Student:* Solve fraction stories like 'Ana ate 3 of 8 equal slices; what fraction did she eat?'

*How to teach:* Draw pizzas and chocolate bars to solve fraction stories. Ask the child to say what the whole is and how many equal parts it has before writing each fraction.

- **Q:** Ana ate 3 of 8 equal slices. What fraction did she eat? What fraction is left?
  **A:** 3/8 eaten; 5/8 left.
- **Q:** A bar has 6 equal parts and Ben ate 4. What fraction did he eat?
  **A:** 4/6.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write fractions for 12 shaded models and draw models for 4 fractions.

*How to teach:* Give 12 shaded models to name and 4 fractions to draw. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** Draw 2/5.
  **A:** A whole split into 5 equal parts with 2 shaded.

**Assessment:** Fraction models check

**Answer key:** Name: 1) 3 of 4 shaded → 3/4 2) 2 of 3 → 2/3 3) 5 of 6 → 5/6 4) 3 of 8 → 3/8 5) 1 of 2 → 1/2 6) 4 of 6 → 4/6 7) 7 of 8 → 7/8 8) 2 of 4 → 2/4 9) 1 of 3 → 1/3 10) 5 of 8 → 5/8 11) 6 of 6 → 6/6 = 1 12) 0 of 4 → 0/4 = 0. Draw: 13) 3/4 14) 2/6 15) 5/8 16) 2/3 with equal parts. Proficient: 14+/16.

## Science 3: Habitats meet needs

**Prepare:** Outdoor space (yard or park), habitat picture cards (desert, ocean, forest, wetland) and organism cards, a shoebox, damp and dry soil, about 10 pill bugs (roly-polies, found under logs or rocks — handle gently and return them).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking organisms can live anywhere — each survives well, less well or not at all depending on whether its needs are met.
- Harming animals during tests — handle pill bugs gently, keep them shaded, and return them where you found them.

### Day 1 (Monday): Backyard habitat
*Student:* Survey a backyard or park and list organisms and their needs.

*How to teach:* Explore a yard or park for 20 minutes. The child lists organisms they find and what each needs (food, water, shelter, air, space) and where it gets them.

- **Q:** What do all organisms need to survive?
  **A:** Food (or sunlight for plants), water, air, shelter and space.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Habitat cards
*Student:* Match organisms to habitats (desert, ocean, forest, wetland).

*How to teach:* The child matches organism cards to habitat cards and explains each match using the organism's needs or features.

- **Q:** Which habitat fits a camel?
  **A:** The desert.
- **Q:** Which habitat fits a sea otter?
  **A:** The ocean (kelp forest).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Survive well, less well
*Student:* Sort organisms by how well they would survive in a desert.

*How to teach:* Take the desert card and sort organisms into 'survives well', 'survives less well' and 'cannot survive', explaining each choice.

- **Q:** Would a frog survive well in a desert?
  **A:** Poorly — frogs need water and moist skin.
- **Q:** Would a lizard survive in a desert?
  **A:** Well — many lizards have scaly skin that holds water and can handle heat.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Pill bug choice
*Student:* Test whether pill bugs prefer damp or dry soil in a shoebox choice chamber.

*How to teach:* Put damp soil at one end of a shoebox and dry soil at the other. Place pill bugs in the middle, cover, wait 10 minutes, and count where they are. Repeat 3 times.

- **Q:** Which side do pill bugs usually choose, and why?
  **A:** The damp side — they breathe through gill-like parts that must stay moist.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write an argument for which habitat suits a cactus best.

*How to teach:* The child writes an argument (claim, evidence, reasoning) for the habitat that suits a cactus best.

- **Q:** Why does a cactus suit the desert?
  **A:** It stores water in its thick stem, has spines instead of broad leaves to lose less water, and has wide shallow roots to soak up rain quickly.

**Assessment:** Habitat argument; pill bug data

**Answer key:** Habitat argument — CER rubric: 4 = clear claim, 3 accurate pieces of evidence, reasoning that links each piece to the claim with science ideas; 3 (proficient) = claim, 2+ pieces of evidence, reasoning for most; 2 = claim with evidence but little reasoning; 1 = claim only or inaccurate. Pill bug data: proficient if counts for damp and dry are recorded for 3 trials and the conclusion matches the data (usually damp).

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Why we have rules and laws

**Prepare:** A list of your family rules, a few local ordinance examples (city website: leash laws, noise hours, speed limits), a ball.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking rules and laws are the same. Rules apply to a family, school or group; laws are made by governments and apply to everyone in an area.
- Thinking consequences are only punishment. They are also meant to protect people, teach and repair harm.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rules at home
*Student:* List family rules and explain the reason behind each one.

*How to teach:* List family rules and the reason for each.

- **Q:** Why do families have rules?
  **A:** To keep everyone safe, healthy and treated fairly, and to help the home run smoothly.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rules vs. laws
*Student:* Compare rules and laws and sort examples (bedtime, speed limits, library fines).

*How to teach:* Sort examples into rules and laws.

- **Q:** Is a speed limit a rule or a law?
  **A:** A law, made by the government.
- **Q:** Is bedtime a rule or a law?
  **A:** A family rule.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Consequences
*Student:* Discuss what consequences for breaking laws are meant to do (protect, teach, repair).

*How to teach:* Discuss the purposes of consequences: protect, teach, repair.

- **Q:** What is the purpose of a fine for littering?
  **A:** To discourage littering, keep places clean and pay for cleanup.

### Day 4 (Thursday): No-rules game
*Student:* Play a ball game with no rules, then add rules and discuss how the game changed.

*How to teach:* Play a ball game with no rules, then add rules and play again; discuss.

- **Q:** What happened when there were no rules?
  **A:** It became confusing and unfair, and people argued; rules made it fair and fun.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph about why communities need rules and laws.

*How to teach:* The child writes the paragraph.

- **Q:** Who makes laws in a city?
  **A:** The city council.

**Assessment:** Rules and laws paragraph

**Answer key:** Rules and laws paragraph rubric: 4 = topic sentence; explains that rules and laws keep people safe, protect rights and property, and help people get along fairly; gives one rule and one law example with reasons; concluding sentence; 3 = 2 reasons with one example; 2 = one reason; 1 = incomplete.

## Art & Music 3: Instrument families and timbre

**Prepare:** Listen ahead to Benjamin Britten's 'The Young Person's Guide to the Orchestra' (about 17 minutes); Classics for Kids (classicsforkids.com) has instrument pages and audio. Make 4 cards: Strings, Woodwinds, Brass, Percussion. Shoebox (lid off), rubber bands of different thicknesses, 2 pencils as bridges.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all woodwinds are made of wood. Flutes are usually metal and saxophones are brass but are woodwinds because of how sound is made (air across an edge or through a reed).
- Thinking only drums are percussion. Percussion includes anything struck, shaken or scraped, like xylophones, triangles, cymbals and timpani.

### Day 1 (Monday): Families
*Student:* Learn the 4 orchestra families and how each makes sound.

*How to teach:* Teach the 4 orchestra families and how each makes sound: strings (vibrating strings, bowed or plucked: violin, viola, cello, double bass, harp), woodwinds (air blown across an edge or through a reed: flute, clarinet, oboe, bassoon), brass (buzzing lips into a cup-shaped mouthpiece: trumpet, French horn, trombone, tuba), percussion (struck, shaken or scraped: drums, timpani, xylophone, cymbals).

- **Q:** How do brass instruments make sound?
  **A:** The player buzzes their lips into a cup-shaped mouthpiece.
- **Q:** Name the 4 instrument families of the orchestra.
  **A:** Strings, woodwinds, brass and percussion.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Britten
*Student:* Listen to Britten's 'The Young Person's Guide to the Orchestra' and raise a card for each family.

*How to teach:* Play the Britten piece. The child raises the matching family card as each family plays the theme and as each instrument gets its variation.

- **Q:** Whose tune did Britten use as the theme?
  **A:** A tune by the English composer Henry Purcell.
- **Q:** Which family plays the very lowest notes in the piece?
  **A:** Answers vary; e.g., the tuba (brass), double bass (strings) or bassoon (woodwinds) — all low instruments.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Timbre game
*Student:* Identify 10 instruments by sound alone in a listening game.

*How to teach:* Play 10 instrument sound clips; the child names the instrument and family by timbre (tone color).

- **Q:** What is timbre?
  **A:** The special quality or 'color' of a sound that lets you tell instruments or voices apart.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Make an instrument
*Student:* Build a shoebox rubber-band guitar and explore how band thickness changes pitch.

*How to teach:* Stretch rubber bands of different thicknesses over an open shoebox, lift them with pencils as bridges, and pluck. Compare pitches.

- **Q:** Which rubber band makes the lowest pitch?
  **A:** The thickest (or loosest) one.
- **Q:** What happens to pitch when you stretch a band tighter?
  **A:** The pitch goes higher.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Complete an instrument family quiz.

*How to teach:* Instrument family quiz (oral or written).

- **Q:** Is a clarinet a woodwind or brass instrument?
  **A:** Woodwind (it uses a reed).

**Assessment:** Instrument family quiz

**Answer key:** Instrument family quiz key (10): 1) violin — strings 2) trumpet — brass 3) flute — woodwind 4) timpani — percussion 5) cello — strings 6) oboe — woodwind 7) trombone — brass 8) xylophone — percussion 9) harp — strings 10) French horn — brass. Bonus: brass makes sound by buzzing lips. Proficient = 8 of 10.

## PE 3: Forehand striking

**Prepare:** Paddle and ball, a wall, a rope or line on the ground as a 'net', and 2 hula hoops (or chalk circles) placed near and far.

**Watch for:**
- Swinging down at the ball (chopping). Swing low to high to lift the ball over the net.
- Facing the net. Turn the non-paddle side toward the target before swinging, just like the overhand throw.

### Day 1 (Monday): Forehand cues
*Student:* Learn the forehand cues (side to target, swing low to high, follow through) and shadow 20 swings.

*How to teach:* Teach forehand cues: 1) side to target (non-paddle shoulder toward the target), 2) swing low to high, 3) follow through toward the target and up. Shadow 20 swings, saying the cues.

- **Q:** Name the 3 forehand cues.
  **A:** Side to target, swing low to high, follow through.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Drop hit
*Student:* Drop a ball, let it bounce and hit a forehand to a wall 20 times.

*How to teach:* Drop the ball beside the front foot, let it bounce, and hit a forehand to the wall 20 times. Count good hits that reach the wall above a chalk line.

- **Q:** Where do you drop the ball for a drop hit?
  **A:** Out to the side and slightly in front of your body, on your paddle side.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Partner rally
*Student:* Rally with a partner over a rope or line, counting hits.

*How to teach:* Rally over a rope or line with a partner, using forehands and counting hits in a row.

- **Q:** Why swing low to high when hitting over a net?
  **A:** It lifts the ball up so it clears the net.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Target
*Student:* Hit forehands to hula hoop targets near and far, adjusting force.

*How to teach:* Place hoops near and far. Hit forehands into each target, adjusting force: softer swing for near, firmer for far.

- **Q:** How do you hit the far target?
  **A:** Use a bigger, firmer swing with more follow-through (more force).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Complete a forehand check scored on the 3 cues.

*How to teach:* Forehand check: observe 5 forehands and score each on the 3 cues.

- **Q:** Which foot is forward when a right-hander hits a forehand?
  **A:** The left foot (non-paddle side toward the target).

**Assessment:** Forehand check

**Answer key:** Forehand check: 5 hits × 3 cues = 15 points (side to target, low-to-high swing, follow through). 4 = 13–15 points; 3 = 10–12; 2 = 6–9; 1 = 0–5.

## Health 3: Emergencies and 911

**Prepare:** A toy or unplugged phone, the home first aid kit (bandages, gauze, antiseptic wipes, gloves). Make sure the child knows the full home address. Know: for a nosebleed, sit up, lean forward and pinch the soft part of the nose for 10 minutes.

**Watch for:**
- Tilting the head back for a nosebleed. Lean forward and pinch the soft part of the nose so blood doesn't run down the throat.
- Thinking 911 is for any problem. 911 is for emergencies when someone is hurt, in danger, or there's a fire or crime; for small problems, tell an adult.
- Hanging up on 911 first. Stay on the line until the dispatcher says to hang up.

### Day 1 (Monday): Emergency or not
*Student:* Sort 12 situations into emergencies (call 911) and non-emergencies (tell an adult).

*How to teach:* The child sorts 12 situations: emergencies (fire, someone not breathing, car crash, someone badly bleeding) vs. non-emergencies (scraped knee, lost toy, power out).

- **Q:** Is a scraped knee a 911 emergency?
  **A:** No. Tell an adult and clean and bandage it.
- **Q:** Name two situations where you should call 911.
  **A:** Any two: fire, someone unconscious or not breathing, serious injury or bleeding, a crime or someone breaking in, a car crash.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Calling 911
*Student:* Practice a pretend 911 call stating your name, full address and what happened.

*How to teach:* Practice a pretend 911 call: stay calm, say your name, full address, what happened, answer questions, and don't hang up.

- **Q:** What three things should you tell the 911 dispatcher first?
  **A:** Your name, your address (where you are), and what happened.
- **Q:** When can you hang up?
  **A:** Only when the dispatcher tells you to.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): First aid
*Student:* Learn basic first aid for small cuts, scrapes and nosebleeds and tour the home first aid kit.

*How to teach:* Show how to clean a small cut (wash with soap and water, press with gauze if bleeding, bandage). Practice nosebleed care. Tour the first aid kit.

- **Q:** What do you do for a nosebleed?
  **A:** Sit up, lean forward, and pinch the soft part of the nose for about 10 minutes.
- **Q:** How do you care for a small scrape?
  **A:** Wash it with soap and water, gently pat dry, and cover with a bandage; tell an adult.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Earthquake
*Student:* Practice drop, cover and hold on in 3 rooms and identify safe spots.

*How to teach:* Practice drop, cover and hold on in 3 rooms; find safe spots (under a sturdy table, away from windows and tall furniture).

- **Q:** What are the three steps during an earthquake?
  **A:** Drop to your hands and knees, cover your head and neck (under a sturdy table if you can), and hold on until the shaking stops.
- **Q:** Where is NOT a safe spot in an earthquake?
  **A:** Near windows, under heavy things that could fall, or in a doorway (modern advice is to get under a table).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Make an emergency contact card with address, phone numbers and a trusted neighbor.

*How to teach:* The child makes and laminates (or tapes) an emergency contact card.

- **Q:** What is the number for Poison Control?
  **A:** 1-800-222-1222.

**Assessment:** Emergency contact card

**Answer key:** Emergency contact card key: child's full name, full home address with city and ZIP, parent/caregiver names and cell numbers, one trusted neighbor or relative with phone number, '911 for emergencies,' and Poison Control 1-800-222-1222. Proficient: all items present and the child can read/say them without help.
