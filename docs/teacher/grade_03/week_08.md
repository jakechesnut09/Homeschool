# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 8

## ELA 3: Charlotte's Web, chapters 16–19

**Prepare:** Charlotte's Web chapters 16–19, a revision checklist. Preview the fair chapters — Charlotte is weakening.

**Watch for:**
- Putting the end punctuation outside quotation marks — periods and commas go inside: "Hello," said Wilbur.
- Not doubling consonants (hoping vs. hopping) — double the final consonant in short-vowel CVC words (hop → hopping).

### Day 1 (Monday): Chapters 16–17
*Student:* Read chapters 16–17 (the fair) and describe Templeton's motivation.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 16–17. Discuss why Templeton goes to the fair.

- **Q:** What is Templeton's motivation for going to the fair?
  **A:** Food — the old sheep tells him a fair is full of leftover food to eat.
- **Q:** Who is 'Uncle'?
  **A:** A huge pig at the fair who looks like he will win the prize.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dialogue punctuation
*Student:* Copy dialogue from the novel and learn quotation mark and comma rules; spell words with -ed and -ing using the doubling rule.

*How to teach:* Copy dialogue lines from the book and learn: quotes around spoken words, comma before or after, capital to start. Then spell -ed/-ing with the doubling rule.

- **Q:** Punctuate: you are radiant said Charlotte
  **A:** "You are radiant," said Charlotte.
- **Q:** Add -ing to hop and to hope.
  **A:** hopping, hoping.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Chapters 18–19
*Student:* Read chapters 18–19 and chart how Wilbur has changed since chapter 1.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 18–19. Chart how Wilbur has changed since chapter 1.

- **Q:** What does Charlotte make in chapter 18–19?
  **A:** An egg sac — she calls it her magnum opus (greatest work).
- **Q:** How has Wilbur changed?
  **A:** He was helpless and frightened; now he is calmer, more confident and cares for others.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revise
*Student:* Revise your story's dialogue for correct punctuation and temporal words (later, meanwhile).

*How to teach:* The child revises story dialogue for punctuation and adds temporal words (later, meanwhile). Have them read the dialogue aloud to hear whether it sounds like real talk.

- **Q:** Name three temporal words.
  **A:** Later, meanwhile, after that (also then, finally).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a dialogue punctuation check and discuss Charlotte's sacrifice in a family book talk.

*How to teach:* Give the dialogue punctuation check and hold a family book talk about Charlotte's sacrifice. During the book talk, ask 'Why do you think Charlotte did this?' and expect evidence.

- **Q:** What did Charlotte sacrifice?
  **A:** She spent her last strength helping Wilbur at the fair even though she was tired and near the end of her life.

**Assessment:** Dialogue punctuation check; book talk participation

**Answer key:** Dialogue check (5 sentences): 1) "Let's go," said Fern. 2) Wilbur asked, "Are you awake?" 3) "I am tired," Charlotte whispered. 4) "Look!" shouted Lurvy. 5) Templeton said, "I want food." Proficient 4+/5. Book talk: proficient if the child cites at least one specific event and listens/responds to others.

## Math 3: Meaning of division

**Prepare:** 24 counters, 8 cups, grapes or crackers (20+) and small bags, number line paper. Write the quiz in the answer key.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking division only means 'sharing' — show the other meaning, how many groups of a given size (20 ÷ 5 = 4 bags).
- Writing the numbers in the wrong order (3 ÷ 24) — the total being split always comes first.

### Day 1 (Monday): Fair sharing
*Student:* Share 24 counters equally among 3, 4, 6 and 8 cups and write division equations for each.

*How to teach:* Deal 24 counters one at a time into 3 cups and count one cup: 24 ÷ 3 = 8. Repeat with 4, 6 and 8 cups. The child writes a division equation each time.

- **Q:** Share 24 counters among 4 cups. How many in each?
  **A:** 6; 24 ÷ 4 = 6.
- **Q:** Share 24 among 8 cups.
  **A:** 3; 24 ÷ 8 = 3.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): How many groups
*Student:* Make groups of a given size (e.g., 20 grapes in bags of 5) and record the number of groups.

*How to teach:* Now the group size is known: put 20 grapes into bags of 5 and count the bags (4). The child records several 'how many groups' problems.

- **Q:** 20 grapes go in bags of 5. How many bags?
  **A:** 4 bags; 20 ÷ 5 = 4.
- **Q:** 18 socks are put in pairs. How many pairs?
  **A:** 9; 18 ÷ 2 = 9.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Division on a number line
*Student:* Show division as repeated subtraction by jumping backward on a number line.

*How to teach:* Start at 15 on a number line and jump back by 3s until you reach 0; count the jumps (5). The child shows 4 problems this way.

- **Q:** Show 12 ÷ 4 on a number line.
  **A:** Jump back 4s: 12 → 8 → 4 → 0 is 3 jumps, so 12 ÷ 4 = 3.
- **Q:** How many jumps of 5 from 30 to 0?
  **A:** 6; 30 ÷ 5 = 6.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Story problems
*Student:* Solve 6 sharing and grouping word problems and label which kind each is.

*How to teach:* For each story ask 'Do we know the number of groups or the size of each group?' The child solves 6 problems and labels each 'sharing' or 'grouping'.

- **Q:** 28 cards are shared by 4 friends. How many each? Which kind?
  **A:** 7 each; sharing (number of groups known).
- **Q:** 30 kids make teams of 6. How many teams? Which kind?
  **A:** 5 teams; grouping (group size known).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a division meaning quiz and write one sharing and one grouping problem.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz; then the child writes one sharing and one grouping problem and solves them. Have the child read their problems to you and label which is sharing and which is grouping.

- **Q:** Write a grouping problem for 16 ÷ 2.
  **A:** Example: 16 shoes are put into pairs. How many pairs? 8 pairs.

**Assessment:** Division meaning quiz

**Answer key:** 1) 12 ÷ 3 = 4 2) 20 ÷ 4 = 5 3) 18 ÷ 6 = 3 4) 35 ÷ 5 = 7 5) 40 ÷ 10 = 4 6) 24 cookies shared by 6 kids → 4 each (sharing) 7) 21 flowers, 3 per vase → 7 vases (grouping) 8) 16 ÷ 4 on a number line → 4 jumps of 4. Written problems: proficient if both are correctly modeled and solved. Proficient overall: 7+/8.

## Science 3: Plant life cycles

**Prepare:** Dry bean seeds (lima beans are easiest to open), paper towels, zip bags, a ruler, a flower (lily or alstroemeria from a store shows parts clearly), a hand lens. Soak a few beans overnight before Tuesday.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking seeds need soil to sprout — germination needs water, air and the right temperature; the seed has its own food.
- Thinking flowers are just for decoration — flowers make seeds.

### Day 1 (Monday): Germination bag
*Student:* Plant beans in wet paper towels in a zip bag and tape to a window; predict what will happen.

*How to teach:* Wet a paper towel, place 3 beans in a zip bag against it, and tape the bag to a window. The child predicts what will happen and draws day 1.

- **Q:** What do seeds need to germinate?
  **A:** Water, air and warmth; they use stored food inside the seed.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Seed dissection
*Student:* Soak a bean and open it to find the seed coat, embryo and food supply; draw and label.

*How to teach:* Open a soaked bean along its seam with a fingernail. The child finds and labels the seed coat, the two halves of stored food (cotyledons) and the tiny embryo (baby plant).

- **Q:** What is the job of the seed coat?
  **A:** To protect the seed.
- **Q:** What is the embryo?
  **A:** The tiny baby plant inside the seed.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Flower parts
*Student:* Dissect a flower from the garden or store and identify parts used for reproduction.

*How to teach:* Gently take apart a flower and lay the parts on paper. The child labels petals, sepals, stamens (make pollen) and the pistil (where seeds form).

- **Q:** Which part of a flower makes pollen?
  **A:** The stamen (the anther at its tip).
- **Q:** Where do seeds form?
  **A:** In the ovary at the base of the pistil.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Measure growth
*Student:* Measure and record bean sprout growth and draw daily observations.

*How to teach:* Measure the root and sprout in the germination bag with a ruler and draw it. Record date and length in a table.

- **Q:** What grows first from a germinating bean?
  **A:** The root.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draw a circular model of a flowering plant's life cycle with labels.

*How to teach:* The child draws a circular life-cycle diagram: seed → germination/seedling → adult plant → flower → pollination → fruit with seeds → back to seed.

- **Q:** Why is a plant life cycle drawn as a circle?
  **A:** Because the adult plant makes seeds that start the cycle again.

**Assessment:** Plant life cycle model

**Answer key:** Life cycle model: 4 = all stages in a correct circle (seed, seedling, adult, flower, pollination, fruit/seed) with arrows and descriptions; 3 (proficient) = stages in order with arrows; 2 = missing stages or wrong order; 1 = incomplete.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Customs, beliefs and traditions

**Prepare:** Borrow a collection of California Indian stories from the library (Coyote stories are common to many California tribes; note which tribe each comes from) and an Aesop fable. Look at a museum's California basketry page (e.g., California Indian Heritage Center or a university museum).

**Watch for:**
- Calling Native stories 'just myths.' Oral traditions teach history, values and lessons and are respected parts of living cultures.
- Thinking traditions stopped long ago. Many ceremonies, dances, basketry and storytelling are practiced today.

### Day 1 (Monday): Oral tradition
*Student:* Read or listen to a California Indian story (e.g., a Coyote story) and discuss its lesson.

*How to teach:* Read a California Indian story aloud and discuss what lesson it teaches.

- **Q:** What lesson does the story teach?
  **A:** Answer should be drawn from the story read, e.g., Coyote's greed or trickery gets him into trouble, teaching not to be greedy or boastful.
- **Q:** Why are stories passed down orally?
  **A:** Many cultures shared history and lessons by speaking and remembering them, teaching each new generation.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ceremonies
*Student:* Learn about a traditional ceremony and its meaning.

*How to teach:* Read about one traditional ceremony (e.g., an acorn harvest celebration or a Big Time gathering) and its meaning.

- **Q:** Why did communities hold ceremonies?
  **A:** To give thanks, mark seasons or harvests, celebrate, pray, and bring people together.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Basketry
*Student:* Study California Indian basketry and its significance.

*How to teach:* Look at basketry photos: patterns, materials (willow, sedge root, bulrush), and uses. Discuss that weavers today still gather materials.

- **Q:** Why are California Indian baskets considered so important?
  **A:** They were essential tools and are also works of art that carry family and cultural knowledge, still made today.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compare
*Student:* Compare a California Indian story to an Aesop fable.

*How to teach:* Read an Aesop fable (e.g., The Tortoise and the Hare) and compare to the Native story in a Venn diagram.

- **Q:** How are the two stories alike?
  **A:** Both use animal characters and teach a lesson or moral.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write about a tradition still practiced today.

*How to teach:* The child writes about a tradition still practiced today.

- **Q:** Name a California Indian tradition practiced today.
  **A:** Basket weaving, dances and ceremonies, storytelling, Big Time gatherings, or Chumash tomol canoe crossings.

**Assessment:** Tradition writing

**Answer key:** Tradition writing rubric: 4 = names a specific tradition still practiced today (e.g., basket weaving, Big Times gatherings, Bear Dance, tomol canoe paddling by the Chumash), explains what it is, why it matters, and cites a source; 3 = tradition and meaning but vague; 2 = tradition named only; 1 = inaccurate.

## Art & Music 3: Rhythm composition

**Prepare:** Open Chrome Music Lab 'Rhythm' (musiclab.chromeexperiments.com) on a tablet or computer. Gather pots, pans, plastic containers and wooden spoons. Staff paper or plain paper for stick notation.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking an 'answer' must copy the 'question.' The answer should be different but fit the same number of beats and feel like it finishes the idea.
- Forgetting that each measure must add up to 4 beats in 4/4. Count the beats in every measure.

### Day 1 (Monday): Improvise
*Student:* Answer a parent's 4-beat clapped 'question' with your own improvised 4-beat 'answer' 10 times.

*How to teach:* Clap a 4-beat 'question'; the child immediately claps a different 4-beat 'answer' that keeps the beat. Do 10 rounds.

- **Q:** How long should your answer be?
  **A:** The same length as the question — 4 beats.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ostinato
*Student:* Create a repeating ostinato on a pot or bucket to accompany 'Row, Row, Row Your Boat.'

*How to teach:* Sing 'Row, Row, Row Your Boat' (traditional). The child invents a short repeating rhythm (ostinato, e.g., ta ta ti-ti ta) on a pot and plays it over and over while you sing.

- **Q:** What is an ostinato?
  **A:** A short musical pattern that repeats over and over.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compose
*Student:* Compose a 4-measure rhythm piece in 4/4 time using stick notation and Chrome Music Lab Rhythm.

*How to teach:* The child composes 4 measures of 4/4 in stick notation (ta, ti-ti, ta-a, rests), then enters it in Chrome Music Lab Rhythm to hear it.

- **Q:** How many total beats are in 4 measures of 4/4?
  **A:** 16 beats.
- **Q:** Check a measure: ti-ti ta-a Z. Does it equal 4 beats?
  **A:** Yes: 1 + 2 + 1 = 4.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rehearse
*Student:* Assign household percussion to each line and rehearse your piece 3 times.

*How to teach:* Assign a household instrument to each line or measure (pot, container, pan lid). Rehearse 3 times, keeping a steady tempo.

- **Q:** Why rehearse with a steady tempo?
  **A:** So all the parts fit together and the piece doesn't speed up or slow down.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 check
*Student:* Perform the composition for family and explain how you organized the rhythms.

*How to teach:* Perform the composition for family and explain how it is organized (e.g., measure 1 and 3 repeat, the ending has a rest).

- **Q:** How did you organize your rhythms?
  **A:** Answers vary but should mention repetition, contrast, or a clear ending.

**Assessment:** Rhythm composition performance

**Answer key:** Rhythm composition rubric: 4 = 4 measures of correct 4/4 stick notation (each measure equals 4 beats), uses at least 3 note/rest types, shows organization (repetition or pattern) and is performed steadily; child explains organization; 3 = one measure miscounted or small tempo drift; 2 = several measures wrong or unsteady; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 3: Single rope basics

**Prepare:** Get a jump rope (beaded or PVC rope works well). Size it: stand on the middle with one foot; handles should reach the armpits. Jump on a flat, non-slip surface (driveway, gym floor). Prepare a jump log and pick a slow and a faster song.

**Watch for:**
- Turning the rope with big arm circles. Elbows stay close to the sides and the rope turns from the wrists.
- Jumping too high or double-bouncing. Jump only an inch or two off the ground, on the balls of the feet, one jump per turn.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sizing and turning
*Student:* Size the rope to armpit height and practice turning it from the wrists without jumping.

*How to teach:* Size the rope (stand on the middle; handles to the armpits). Hold both handles in one hand and swing the rope at your side to feel the rhythm from the wrist. Then hold one in each hand and turn the rope over the head and let it stop in front of the toes, step over, repeat 10 times.

- **Q:** How do you know your rope is the right size?
  **A:** Standing on the middle with one foot, the handles reach about to your armpits.
- **Q:** What part of your arm turns the rope?
  **A:** The wrists (with elbows close to your sides).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Basic bounce
*Student:* Practice the two-foot basic bounce in 5 sets of 20-second tries, resting between sets.

*How to teach:* Practice the basic bounce: feet together, small jump on the balls of the feet as the rope passes under. Do 5 sets of 20-second tries with 30-second rests. Cue 'small jumps, quiet feet, wrists turn.'

- **Q:** How high should you jump in the basic bounce?
  **A:** Just an inch or two — enough for the rope to pass under.
- **Q:** Why do we rest between sets?
  **A:** Jump rope is hard work for the heart and legs; rest lets you recover so you can keep good form.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Count
*Student:* Count consecutive jumps in 3 trials and record the best in a jump log.

*How to teach:* Do 3 trials of counting consecutive jumps without a miss. Record each number and circle the best in the jump log.

- **Q:** Trials were 14, 22 and 19 jumps. What is your best? What is the difference between best and worst?
  **A:** Best is 22; 22 − 14 = 8.
- **Q:** What counts as 'consecutive' jumps?
  **A:** Jumps in a row without the rope stopping or a miss.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rhythm practice
*Student:* Jump to a slow-tempo song, then a faster one, keeping time with the beat.

*How to teach:* Jump to a slow-tempo song, trying to land one jump per beat. Then switch to a faster song. Talk about which was easier and why.

- **Q:** What do you need to change when the music gets faster?
  **A:** Turn the rope faster with the wrists and make smaller, quicker jumps to stay on the beat.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Record a new personal best and explain how practice changed your count.

*How to teach:* Warm up, then do 3 trials to try for a new personal best and record it. Ask the child to compare this week's first count to the best.

- **Q:** How did practice change your count?
  **A:** Answer should compare numbers, e.g., 'I went from 14 to 30 because I kept my jumps small and turned with my wrists.'

**Assessment:** Personal best jumps in log

**Answer key:** Jump log check: proficient log lists 3 counted trials on Day 3 with the best circled, a Day 5 personal best, and a sentence comparing them using numbers and a reason (e.g., better timing, wrist turning, smaller jumps). Form check: rope sized to armpits, elbows in, wrists turning, small two-foot jumps. 4 = all present; 3 = log complete but reason vague; 2 = missing trials; 1 = no log.

## Health 3: Digestive system

**Prepare:** Zip-top bag, a few crackers, water, a 20-foot piece of string or yarn, and a digestive-system diagram (KidsHealth has a kid version).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking food goes straight from the stomach out of the body. Most nutrients are absorbed in the small intestine; the large intestine absorbs water before waste leaves.
- Thinking digestion starts in the stomach. It starts in the mouth with chewing and saliva.

### Day 1 (Monday): Digestion path
*Student:* Trace food from mouth to intestines on a diagram.

*How to teach:* Trace the path on a diagram with the child's finger: mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine. Say one job for each.

- **Q:** What is the tube that carries food from the mouth to the stomach?
  **A:** The esophagus.
- **Q:** Where does digestion begin?
  **A:** In the mouth, where teeth chew and saliva starts breaking food down.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Stomach model
*Student:* Mash crackers with water in a zip bag to model the stomach.

*How to teach:* Put crackers in a zip bag ('teeth' crush them), add water ('stomach juices'), seal and squish ('stomach muscles churn'). Discuss what it models.

- **Q:** What did squishing the bag represent?
  **A:** The stomach's muscles churning and mixing food with digestive juices.
- **Q:** What did the water represent?
  **A:** Saliva and stomach juices that soften and break down food.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Intestine length
*Student:* Measure a string as long as the small intestine (about 20 feet) and lay it out.

*How to teach:* Lay out a 20-foot string in a hallway or yard and talk about how the small intestine is coiled to fit in the belly. Explain that nutrients pass into the blood here.

- **Q:** About how long is the small intestine of an adult?
  **A:** About 20 feet (about 6 meters).
- **Q:** What happens in the small intestine?
  **A:** Food is broken down more and nutrients are absorbed into the blood.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fiber and water
*Student:* Discuss how fiber and water help digestion.

*How to teach:* Explain that fiber (in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, beans) and water help food move smoothly through the intestines. The child names 3 high-fiber foods they like.

- **Q:** How do fiber and water help digestion?
  **A:** Fiber adds bulk and water softens waste so it moves easily through the intestines and prevents constipation.
- **Q:** Name two high-fiber foods.
  **A:** Any two: apples, berries, beans, broccoli, oatmeal, whole-wheat bread, carrots.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write the digestion steps in order.

*How to teach:* The child writes the digestion steps in order with one sentence per organ, using the diagram only if stuck.

- **Q:** What does the large intestine do?
  **A:** It absorbs water from leftover food and forms waste.

**Assessment:** Digestion sequence

**Answer key:** Digestion sequence key: 1) Mouth – teeth chew, saliva starts breaking down food; 2) Esophagus – a tube that squeezes food down to the stomach; 3) Stomach – muscles churn food with acid and juices into mush; 4) Small intestine – food is broken down further and nutrients pass into the blood; 5) Large intestine – water is absorbed and waste forms; 6) Waste leaves the body. Proficient: all organs in correct order with a correct job for at least 4.
