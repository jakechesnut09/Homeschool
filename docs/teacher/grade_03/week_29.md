# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 29

## ELA 3: Oz narrative and book vs. film

**Prepare:** The Oz adventure plan; optional clip of the 1939 film (e.g., the Emerald City or melting scene; check for scary content). Write the Unit 7 test.

**Watch for:**
- Putting two speakers' dialogue in the same paragraph — start a new paragraph each time the speaker changes.
- Assuming the film must match the book — filmmakers make choices (ruby slippers, Oz as a dream).

### Day 1 (Monday): Draft
*Student:* Draft the new Oz adventure with dialogue and temporal words.

*How to teach:* The child drafts the adventure from the plan, including dialogue and temporal words to move through time. Remind them to start a new paragraph each time the speaker changes.

- **Q:** Name a temporal word or phrase to start a paragraph.
  **A:** Meanwhile, later, the next morning, after that.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Revise
*Student:* Revise for word choice and correct dialogue punctuation; spell -ous and -ive words.

*How to teach:* Revise for vivid words and correct dialogue punctuation. Then spell -ous and -ive words (famous, active).

- **Q:** Punctuate: Where are we asked Dorothy
  **A:** "Where are we?" asked Dorothy.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compare
*Student:* Compare a scene from the book to a film version and discuss differences.

*How to teach:* Watch a short scene from the 1939 film and reread the same scene in the book. The child charts what is the same and different and discusses why the filmmakers might have changed things.

- **Q:** What is one major difference between the book and the 1939 film?
  **A:** In the film the shoes are ruby; in the book they are silver. (Also: the film makes Oz a dream, but in the book it is real.)

### Day 4 (Thursday): Publish
*Student:* Publish the narrative with an illustration.

*How to teach:* The child publishes the narrative neatly with an illustration of an important scene. Have the child read the final version aloud once before the illustration is added.

- **Q:** What makes a strong illustration for your story?
  **A:** It shows an important moment and matches the details in the text.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 assessment
*Student:* Complete the Unit 7 test and share your Oz story aloud.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test. The child reads their Oz story aloud to the family.

- **Q:** What is a theme of The Wonderful Wizard of Oz?
  **A:** You already have what you need inside you; there's no place like home.

**Assessment:** Unit 7 test; narrative rubric

**Answer key:** Unit 7 test: 1) Each friend's wish → brains, heart, courage 2) Oz's demand → kill the Wicked Witch of the West 3) What is a humbug? → a fraud 4) How does Dorothy get home? → silver shoes 5) Theme → you already have what you need 6) Possessive: the hats of the Munchkins → the Munchkins' hats 7) Contraction: they are → they're 8) Book vs. film → silver vs. ruby shoes. Proficient 6+/8. Narrative rubric (4 levels): 4 = clear beginning that sets up the moment, events in order with temporal words, details/dialogue showing feelings, ending that reflects on the lesson, few errors; 3 (proficient) = all parts present, some details, mostly correct capitals and punctuation; 2 = events listed with few details or no real ending; 1 = unclear or incomplete.

## Math 3: Scaled picture graphs and bar graphs

**Prepare:** Grid paper, a survey tally sheet, a sample picture graph where one symbol = 5. Draw the pets bar graph from the answer key for Friday.

**Watch for:**
- Counting symbols instead of using the key (3 stars = 3 instead of 15) — always read the key first.
- Bar graph scales that skip unevenly — number the axis by the same amount at every line.

### Day 1 (Monday): Read scaled graphs
*Student:* Read picture graphs where one symbol equals 5 and answer questions.

*How to teach:* Show a picture graph with ★ = 5 votes. Ask how much each row is worth, skip counting by 5s for each star. The child answers 6 questions.

- **Q:** ★ = 5. Apples has 4 stars. How many votes?
  **A:** 20.
- **Q:** Bananas has 2 stars and apples has 4. How many more votes for apples?
  **A:** 10.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Survey
*Student:* Survey family and friends about favorite fruits and tally the results.

*How to teach:* The child surveys 10–20 family members and friends about their favorite fruit and records tallies. Show how to make a tally chart with one row per fruit and remind them to ask each person only once.

- **Q:** What number does a bundle of 5 tally marks plus 3 more show?
  **A:** 8.
- **Q:** Why do we group tallies in 5s?
  **A:** It makes counting faster — you can count by 5s.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Make a bar graph
*Student:* Draw a scaled bar graph (scale of 2) of the survey data.

*How to teach:* Draw a bar graph with a scale of 2 (axis labeled 0, 2, 4, 6 …). Bars for odd numbers end halfway between lines. Add a title and labels.

- **Q:** On a scale of 2, where does a bar for 7 end?
  **A:** Halfway between 6 and 8.
- **Q:** What does a bar graph need?
  **A:** A title, category labels, a numbered scale and axis labels.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compare data
*Student:* Answer one- and two-step 'how many more/fewer' questions from your graph.

*How to teach:* Ask one- and two-step comparison questions from the child's graph. Have the child write an equation for each answer (12 − 7 = 5).

- **Q:** Apples 12, grapes 7, bananas 5. How many more chose apples than grapes?
  **A:** 5.
- **Q:** How many fewer chose bananas than apples and grapes together?
  **A:** 19 − 5 = 14.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Interpret a new scaled graph and create a picture graph with a key.

*How to teach:* The child interprets the new graph in the answer key and makes a picture graph with a key from their survey. Check that the picture graph's key is used correctly for every row.

- **Q:** If ● = 4, how many does ●●● show?
  **A:** 12.

**Assessment:** Graphing check

**Answer key:** Graph (bar graph, scale of 2): Pets — dogs 8, cats 6, fish 4, birds 2. 1) Most popular → dogs 2) Number of cats → 6 3) How many more dogs than fish → 4 4) Total → 20 5) How many more dogs and cats together than fish and birds together → 14 − 6 = 8. Picture graph: proficient if it has a title, a key, and the right number of symbols for each category.

## Science 3: California climates

**Prepare:** California map, monthly climate data for San Diego, Sacramento, South Lake Tahoe and Death Valley (NOAA or a climate website), graph paper.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all of California is sunny and warm — mountains are snowy and the coast is cool and foggy.
- Forgetting elevation — higher places are usually cooler.

### Day 1 (Monday): CA regions
*Student:* Mark the coast, Central Valley, Sierra Nevada and desert regions on a California map.

*How to teach:* Mark the coast, Central Valley, Sierra Nevada and desert regions on a California map.

- **Q:** Where is the Central Valley?
  **A:** The long, flat valley in the middle of California between the Coast Ranges and the Sierra Nevada.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): City data
*Student:* Collect monthly temperature and rainfall for San Diego, Sacramento, South Lake Tahoe and Death Valley.

*How to teach:* Collect monthly temperature and rainfall for the four cities into a table.

- **Q:** Which city gets the most snow?
  **A:** South Lake Tahoe (high in the Sierra Nevada).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Graph and compare
*Student:* Graph the 4 cities' July highs and yearly rainfall on bar graphs and compare.

*How to teach:* Graph July highs and yearly rainfall for the 4 cities and compare.

- **Q:** Which city has the hottest July?
  **A:** Death Valley — average July highs are well over 110 °F.
- **Q:** Which has the mildest July?
  **A:** San Diego — cooled by the ocean.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Travel guide
*Student:* Write a one-page travel guide describing one region's climate and what to pack in each season.

*How to teach:* The child writes a one-page travel guide describing one region's climate and what to pack each season.

- **Q:** What should you pack for South Lake Tahoe in winter?
  **A:** Warm coat, boots, hat and gloves for snow.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Share the travel guide and explain how elevation and the ocean affect California climates.

*How to teach:* Share the travel guide; explain how elevation and the ocean affect California climates.

- **Q:** How does the ocean affect coastal climate?
  **A:** It keeps the coast cooler in summer and milder in winter.
- **Q:** How does elevation affect climate?
  **A:** Higher places are usually colder and get more snow.

**Assessment:** California climate travel guide

**Answer key:** Travel guide: 4 = region located, accurate seasonal climate data, packing advice for each season, explanation of ocean/elevation effects; 3 (proficient) = most elements accurate; 2 = general; 1 = inaccurate.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Landmarks and essential documents

**Prepare:** A Declaration of Independence image (archives.gov), California symbols: state flag (Bear Flag with a grizzly bear, a red star and 'California Republic'), state animal (California grizzly bear), state flower (California poppy), motto 'Eureka' (Greek for 'I have found it'). Research a local landmark.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing the Declaration of Independence (1776, announced independence) with the Constitution (1787, set up the government).
- Thinking 'unalienable rights' can be given or taken by the government. They belong to people naturally.

### Day 1 (Monday): Declaration
*Student:* Examine the Declaration of Independence and discuss 'all men are created equal' and 'unalienable rights.'

*How to teach:* Look at the Declaration; read and discuss 'all men are created equal' and 'unalienable rights… Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness.'

- **Q:** When was the Declaration of Independence adopted?
  **A:** July 4, 1776.
- **Q:** What are the three unalienable rights it names?
  **A:** Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): U.S. Capitol
*Student:* Learn the history of the U.S. Capitol and what happens there.

*How to teach:* Learn about the U.S. Capitol in Washington, D.C., where Congress meets.

- **Q:** What happens in the U.S. Capitol?
  **A:** Congress (the Senate and House) meets and makes laws.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): California symbols
*Student:* Learn the California flag, grizzly bear, poppy and state motto 'Eureka.'

*How to teach:* Learn California symbols.

- **Q:** What does California's motto 'Eureka' mean?
  **A:** 'I have found it' (connected to the discovery of gold).
- **Q:** What animal is on California's flag?
  **A:** A grizzly bear.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Local landmark
*Student:* Research a local landmark (historic building, statue or park) and its history.

*How to teach:* Research a local landmark's history.

- **Q:** Why is your landmark important?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; answer explains its connection to an event, person or the community's history.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Present the local landmark with a drawing and 5 facts.

*How to teach:* The child presents the landmark with a drawing and 5 facts.

**Assessment:** Landmark presentation

**Answer key:** Landmark presentation rubric: 4 = drawing plus 5 accurate facts (name, location, when built, why it is important, a person or event connected) with a source and a clear spoken presentation; 3 = 4 facts; 2 = 2–3 facts; 1 = incomplete.

## Art & Music 3: Composing a song

**Prepare:** Staff paper or plain paper, pencil, Chrome Music Lab Song Maker (set to Major, C, 4 beats per bar). The favorite improvisation idea from week 28.

**Watch for:**
- Putting the strong word syllables on weak beats. Say the words aloud and line up stressed syllables on beat 1 or strong beats.
- Ending a melody on a random note. Ending on do (C) gives a finished feeling.

### Day 1 (Monday): Lyrics
*Student:* Write 4 lines of lyrics about a topic you care about.

*How to teach:* The child writes 4 lines of lyrics on a topic they care about (pet, season, family). Each line should have about the same number of syllables; rhyming is optional.

- **Q:** Why should lines have similar syllable counts?
  **A:** So each line fits the same rhythm pattern and the song feels balanced.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rhythm
*Student:* Set the lyrics to rhythms using quarter, eighth and half notes.

*How to teach:* Speak the lyrics rhythmically and write rhythms above the words using ta, ti-ti and ta-a (one syllable per note). Check each measure has 4 beats.

- **Q:** Rhythm for 'I love my dog' (4 syllables) written ti-ti ta ta-a: how many beats?
  **A:** 1 + 1 + 2 = 4 beats — one full measure of 4/4, with one note per syllable.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Melody
*Student:* Create a melody for the rhythm in Song Maker, ending on do.

*How to teach:* Enter the rhythm into Song Maker and create a melody that moves mostly by steps, has one leap, and ends on do.

- **Q:** Why end on do?
  **A:** It is the home note, so the song sounds finished.
- **Q:** What is a step vs. a leap?
  **A:** A step moves to the next note up or down (C to D); a leap skips one or more notes (C to G).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Notate
*Student:* Write the song in standard or iconic notation.

*How to teach:* Write the song in standard notation on the staff (or iconic notation: dashes high/low with rhythm marks), with lyrics under the notes.

- **Q:** Where do lyrics go in written music?
  **A:** Under the notes, one syllable lined up under each note.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Share the draft and explain how the music fits the words.

*How to teach:* Share the draft and explain how the music fits the words (e.g., high notes for an exciting word, a long note for an important word).

- **Q:** Give one example of how your melody matches a word.
  **A:** Answers vary, e.g., 'I put "sky" on the highest note.'

**Assessment:** Song draft

**Answer key:** Song draft rubric: 4 = 4 lines of original lyrics, rhythms that fit natural speech with 4 beats per measure, melody that ends on do, written in notation with lyrics aligned, and an explanation of how music fits words; 3 = one measure miscounted or notation incomplete; 2 = melody or rhythm missing; 1 = lyrics only.

## PE 3: Tag games and strategy

**Prepare:** Belt flags or scarves tucked into waistbands, cones for boundaries and lanes, two 'flags' (bandanas) for capture the flag; invite friends or siblings if possible.

**Watch for:**
- Running straight at a tagger. Use fakes, quick changes of direction and open space.
- Thinking an honest call loses the game. Honesty keeps the game fair and fun, and others will trust you.

### Day 1 (Monday): Flag tag
*Student:* Play flag tag with belt flags or tucked scarves, using open space to avoid taggers.

*How to teach:* Tuck a flag in each side of the waistband. Players try to pull others' flags while protecting their own, using open space. A pulled flag means do 5 jumping jacks and rejoin.

- **Q:** How do you avoid taggers in flag tag?
  **A:** Move into open space away from them, keep your eyes up and change direction quickly.
- **Q:** Is it fair to hold your flag with your hand?
  **A:** No — you protect it by moving, not by holding or blocking it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dodging
*Student:* Practice dodging skills (fake, pivot, change speed) in a cone-lane challenge.

*How to teach:* Set a cone lane. A defender stands in the middle; the child gets past using a fake (lean one way, go the other), a pivot, or a change of speed.

- **Q:** Name 3 dodging moves.
  **A:** Fake, pivot (turn on one foot), change of speed (and change of direction).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Capture the flag
*Student:* Play capture the flag with friends, planning offense and defense roles.

*How to teach:* Play capture the flag: two territories, a flag at the back of each. Plan roles: defenders guard, attackers try to grab the opposing flag. Tagged players go to 'jail' until freed.

- **Q:** Why have both offense and defense players?
  **A:** So you can try to capture the other flag while still protecting your own.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fair play
*Student:* Role-play honest calls ('I was tagged') and discuss why honesty keeps games fun.

*How to teach:* Role-play situations: you feel a tag but no one saw; the score is close and you step out of bounds. Practice saying 'I was tagged' or 'I was out.'

- **Q:** Why does honesty keep games fun?
  **A:** Everyone trusts the game is fair, there are fewer arguments, and people want to keep playing together.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Write a short reflection on which strategy helped your team most.

*How to teach:* Write a 3–5 sentence reflection: which strategy helped your team most and why, with an example.

- **Q:** Give an example of a team strategy in capture the flag.
  **A:** E.g., two players run at once so defenders can't guard both; one gets the flag.

**Assessment:** Strategy reflection

**Answer key:** Strategy reflection rubric: 4 = names a specific strategy (e.g., fake, use open space, split offense/defense), explains why it worked with an example from play, and mentions fair play; 3 = names a strategy with a basic reason; 2 = vague ("we ran fast"); 1 = no reflection.

## Health 3: Dental health

**Prepare:** Toothbrush, floss, a hand mirror, two hard-boiled eggs (white shells), dark soda, water, two cups. Set up the egg experiment on Day 2 and check on Day 3. Find a Mouth Healthy Kids brushing video (mouthhealthykids.org, American Dental Association).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking baby teeth don't matter because they fall out. They hold space for adult teeth and cavities in them can hurt and cause infections.
- Brushing hard and fast. Use gentle small circles for 2 minutes.

### Day 1 (Monday): Teeth
*Student:* Learn tooth parts and types (incisors, canines, molars) using a mirror.

*How to teach:* Use a mirror to find incisors (front, cutting), canines (pointed, tearing), premolars and molars (back, grinding). Name the parts: enamel (hard outer layer), dentin, pulp, root, gums.

- **Q:** What do molars do?
  **A:** Grind and crush food.
- **Q:** What is the hardest outer layer of a tooth called?
  **A:** Enamel.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Egg experiment
*Student:* Soak one hard-boiled egg in soda and one in water overnight and compare stains on the 'enamel.'

*How to teach:* Place one egg in soda and one in water overnight. The child writes a prediction.

- **Q:** Why use an egg?
  **A:** The shell is made of calcium like tooth enamel, so it models how drinks affect teeth.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Brushing
*Student:* Watch a Mouth Healthy Kids video and practice brushing for 2 minutes with a timer.

*How to teach:* Remove the eggs and compare. Watch the brushing video, then brush for 2 minutes with a timer: small gentle circles, all surfaces, tongue too.

- **Q:** What happened to the egg in soda and why?
  **A:** It got stained (and may be softer) because the soda's color and acid affect the calcium shell, like teeth.
- **Q:** How long and how often should you brush?
  **A:** 2 minutes, twice a day, with fluoride toothpaste.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Flossing
*Student:* Practice flossing technique on all teeth.

*How to teach:* Model flossing: about 18 inches of floss, wrap on middle fingers, slide gently between teeth and curve in a C shape around each tooth.

- **Q:** Why floss?
  **A:** It removes food and plaque between teeth where the toothbrush can't reach.
- **Q:** How often should you floss?
  **A:** Once a day.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Start a 2-week brushing and flossing tracker.

*How to teach:* Start the 2-week brushing and flossing tracker and post it in the bathroom.

- **Q:** Name two foods or drinks that are bad for teeth and one that's good.
  **A:** Bad: soda, candy, sticky sweets, juice. Good: water, milk, cheese, crunchy vegetables.

**Assessment:** Egg experiment notes; brushing tracker

**Answer key:** Egg experiment notes key: the egg in soda is stained brown and may feel rougher; the egg in water is unchanged. Conclusion: soda's color and acid can stain and weaken enamel, like teeth. Brushing tracker: proficient = brushing twice a day for 2 minutes and flossing once a day recorded for 14 days, with at least 12 days complete.
