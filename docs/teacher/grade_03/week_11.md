# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 11

## ELA 3: How and why: animal adaptations

**Prepare:** Print 'How the Leopard Got His Spots' from Kipling's Just So Stories (Project Gutenberg). It is long and old-fashioned, so plan to read aloud and paraphrase; the original also uses dated, insensitive language about the Ethiopian character — preview it and consider a modern retelling or explain that such wording is no longer acceptable. Find a nonfiction leopard passage (ReadWorks, CommonLit or a library book) and get note cards.

**Watch for:**
- Treating pourquoi ('why') tales as facts — they are made-up explanations; science gives the real reason.
- Copying whole sentences onto note cards — write short notes in your own words.

### Day 1 (Monday): Just So Story
*Student:* Read Kipling's 'How the Leopard Got His Spots' and discuss how a pourquoi tale explains nature.

*How to teach:* Explain that a pourquoi tale is a made-up story that tells why something in nature is the way it is. Read the story aloud in parts, pausing to paraphrase, and ask the child what nature fact it explains. Have them retell the main events in order.

- **Q:** In Kipling's story, how does the Leopard get his spots?
  **A:** The Ethiopian presses his fingertips all over the Leopard's coat, leaving spots so he can hide in the shadowy forest.
- **Q:** Why do the Leopard and the Ethiopian need to change?
  **A:** The other animals moved into the forest and became striped and blotchy, so the hunters could no longer find or catch them.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Real reasons
*Student:* Read a nonfiction passage on leopard camouflage and complete a cause/effect chart.

*How to teach:* Read the nonfiction leopard passage. Model one cause/effect pair ('Because leopards have spots, they blend into shadows'), then have the child complete a chart with 3 more pairs from the text.

- **Q:** What is the real reason leopards have spots?
  **A:** Their spots (rosettes) are camouflage that blends into the patchy light and shadows of grass and trees, helping them sneak up on prey.
- **Q:** Name a cause and effect from the passage.
  **A:** Example: Because leopards are strong climbers (cause), they can drag prey up into trees away from other predators (effect).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Comparatives
*Student:* Use comparative and superlative adjectives (faster, fastest) to compare animal features; spell -able and -ible words.

*How to teach:* Teach comparative adjectives (-er or more, comparing two) and superlative adjectives (-est or most, comparing three or more). The child writes sentences comparing animal features. Then spell -able and -ible words and talk about their meaning ('able to be').

- **Q:** Use the correct form: A cheetah is (fast) than a leopard.
  **A:** faster.
- **Q:** Use the correct form: The blue whale is the (big) animal on Earth.
  **A:** biggest.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Note taking
*Student:* Take notes on your report animal from 2 sources, sorting facts into body, habitat, food and adaptations.

*How to teach:* Model turning a sentence from a source into a short note in your own words. The child takes notes on their report animal from 2 sources, one fact per card, and sorts the cards into body, habitat, food and adaptations, writing the source title on each.

- **Q:** Turn this into a note: 'Polar bears have black skin under their fur, which helps them absorb heat from the sun.'
  **A:** Example note: black skin under fur → soaks up heat.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph comparing the fiction and nonfiction explanations of the leopard's spots.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph comparing Kipling's explanation with the scientific explanation, using linking words like 'but' and 'also'. Check the notes for own-words wording and correct sorting.

- **Q:** How are the two explanations different?
  **A:** Kipling's is imaginative fiction about fingerprints; the nonfiction explains that spots are camouflage that help leopards hide and hunt. Both connect the spots to hiding in shadows.

**Assessment:** Fiction vs. nonfiction paragraph; notes check

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric: 4 = names both texts, explains Kipling's fictional cause and the real cause (camouflage for hunting), notes a similarity (both link spots to hiding in shadows), uses linking words; 3 (proficient) = both explanations stated correctly; 2 = only one explanation; 1 = off-topic. Notes check: proficient = 8+ notes in own words, sorted into the 4 categories, with source titles.

## Math 3: Facts of 2, 5, 10, 0 and 1

**Prepare:** Hundreds chart, fact cards for 0, 1, 2, 5 and 10, an analog clock and some nickels.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 8 × 0 = 8 (mixing up with addition) — 8 groups of nothing is nothing; show 8 empty plates.
- Confusing × 1 with + 1 (6 × 1 = 7) — one group of 6 is 6.

### Day 1 (Monday): Twos and tens
*Student:* Find doubles and tens patterns on a hundreds chart and practice 2s and 10s facts.

*How to teach:* Color multiples of 2 and of 10 on a hundreds chart; notice 2s products are even and 10s end in 0. Connect 2s facts to doubles (2 × 7 = 7 + 7). Practice with cards for 10 minutes.

- **Q:** What is 2 × 9? How do doubles help?
  **A:** 18 — it is 9 + 9.
- **Q:** What is 10 × 6?
  **A:** 60.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Fives
*Student:* Use clock faces and nickels to practice 5s facts and notice the 0/5 ending pattern.

*How to teach:* Count by 5s around a clock face (each number is 5 minutes) and with nickels. Point out that every 5s product ends in 0 or 5.

- **Q:** What is 7 × 5? Use the clock.
  **A:** 35 — the minute hand on the 7 means 35 minutes.
- **Q:** How much are 9 nickels worth?
  **A:** 45 cents.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Zero and one
*Student:* Explore 0 and 1 rules with counters and write why 8 × 0 = 0 in a sentence.

*How to teach:* Show 0 plates with 5 counters, then 5 plates with 0 counters — both make 0. Show that any number × 1 is itself. The child writes why 8 × 0 = 0 in a sentence.

- **Q:** Why does 8 × 0 = 0?
  **A:** 8 groups of 0 is nothing at all, so the product is 0.
- **Q:** What is 1 × 47?
  **A:** 47.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Pattern explain
*Student:* Explain why 5s products are half of 10s products using arrays.

*How to teach:* Build a 10 × 4 array and cover half of it; the half showing is 5 × 4. The child explains in words why 5s products are half of 10s products for 3 facts.

- **Q:** If 10 × 8 = 80, what is 5 × 8, and why?
  **A:** 40 — 5 groups is half of 10 groups, and half of 80 is 40.
- **Q:** Use the pattern to find 5 × 6.
  **A:** Half of 10 × 6 = 60, so 30.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a timed-free fact check on 2, 5, 10, 0 and 1 and record facts to practice.

*How to teach:* Give an untimed 20-fact check (2s, 5s, 10s, 0s, 1s, with some division). The child records missed facts on a practice card.

- **Q:** What is 5 × 9? What is 20 ÷ 2?
  **A:** 45; 10.

**Assessment:** Fact check of 2s, 5s, 10s, 0s, 1s

**Answer key:** 2 × 6 = 12, 2 × 8 = 16, 5 × 7 = 35, 5 × 9 = 45, 10 × 4 = 40, 10 × 9 = 90, 0 × 6 = 0, 1 × 9 = 9, 7 × 0 = 0, 5 × 5 = 25, 2 × 9 = 18, 10 × 7 = 70, 5 × 3 = 15, 1 × 1 = 1, 18 ÷ 2 = 9, 40 ÷ 10 = 4, 35 ÷ 5 = 7, 6 ÷ 1 = 6, 16 ÷ 2 = 8, 45 ÷ 5 = 9. Proficient: 18+/20; list missed facts for daily practice.

## Science 3: Life cycle models

**Prepare:** Paper plates (2 per model), markers, brads (paper fasteners), library books or kid-safe websites about sea turtles, salmon or oak trees, potting soil and a pot. Write the Unit 3 quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking life cycles end at adulthood — the adult reproduces, which restarts the cycle.
- Mixing up plant and animal stages (calling a seedling a larva).

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose an organism
*Student:* Research a chosen organism's life cycle (sea turtle, salmon, oak tree).

*How to teach:* The child chooses an organism and researches its stages, writing each on a note card.

- **Q:** What are the stages of a salmon's life?
  **A:** Egg (in a freshwater stream) → alevin → fry → smolt (moves to the ocean) → adult that returns to its home stream to spawn.
- **Q:** What are the stages of an oak tree?
  **A:** Acorn (seed) → seedling → sapling → mature tree that makes acorns.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Build model
*Student:* Build a spinning paper-plate life cycle model.

*How to teach:* Divide one plate into sections and draw a stage in each. Cut a window in the top plate and join them with a brad so turning shows one stage at a time.

- **Q:** How many sections should a sea turtle model have?
  **A:** About 4: egg, hatchling, juvenile, adult.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Present
*Student:* Present the model and explain each stage.

*How to teach:* The child presents the model, turning to each stage and explaining what happens.

- **Q:** What happens to sea turtle hatchlings after they hatch?
  **A:** They crawl across the beach to the ocean.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Bean wrap-up
*Student:* Transplant bean sprouts to soil and describe the next stages expected.

*How to teach:* Transplant bean sprouts into soil. The child predicts and writes the next stages (leaves, flowers, pods with seeds).

- **Q:** What will the bean plant make after it flowers?
  **A:** Bean pods containing seeds.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 assessment
*Student:* Complete the unit quiz on life cycles.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 quiz. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** What does every life cycle include?
  **A:** Birth, growth, reproduction and death.

**Assessment:** Unit 3 quiz; life cycle model

**Answer key:** Unit 3 quiz: 1) Order bean stages → seed, seedling, adult plant, flower, pod/seeds 2) Butterfly stages → egg, larva, pupa, adult 3) Grasshopper → egg, nymph, adult 4) Frog → egg, tadpole, froglet, adult 5) What part of a seed is the baby plant → embryo 6) What do all life cycles share → birth, growth, reproduction, death 7) Which flower part makes pollen → stamen. Proficient 6+/7. Model: 3 = all stages in order, accurate, explained.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Newcomers meet first peoples

**Prepare:** Short excerpts from a settler account (e.g., a diary or letter from the mission or Gold Rush era) and a Native account or oral history about the same period (many museum and tribal sites publish these). A timeline strip. Prepare the Unit 2 test. Parent preview: the arrival of newcomers brought disease, loss of land and violence; discuss honestly at a level fit for an 8-year-old.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking newcomers arrived in empty land. California was home to many Native nations for thousands of years.
- Thinking the story ended with loss. Native nations have survived and continue to revive languages and traditions.

### Day 1 (Monday): Arrival
*Student:* Read about the arrival of Spanish, Mexican and American newcomers and list changes each brought.

*How to teach:* Read about the arrival of Spanish (1769 onward), Mexican (1821–1848) and American (1848 onward) newcomers; list changes each brought.

- **Q:** Put in order: American, Spanish, Mexican.
  **A:** Spanish, Mexican, American.
- **Q:** Name two changes Spanish newcomers brought.
  **A:** Missions and Christianity, cattle and horses, new crops like wheat, the Spanish language, and diseases.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Perspectives
*Student:* Compare a settler's account and a Native account of the same period and note differences.

*How to teach:* Read the two accounts and list differences in a T-chart.

- **Q:** Why might two people describe the same event differently?
  **A:** They had different experiences, beliefs and points of view.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Changes
*Student:* Chart changes to land, food, language and daily life for local nations.

*How to teach:* Chart changes to land, food, language and daily life for local nations.

- **Q:** How did grazing cattle change the land?
  **A:** Cattle ate the native grasses and plants that people gathered, making traditional food harder to find.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Resilience
*Student:* Learn how local nations have kept languages, ceremonies and basketry alive today.

*How to teach:* Read about present-day efforts: language classes, basket weavers, ceremonies.

- **Q:** What is resilience?
  **A:** The ability to keep going and recover after hard times.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 assessment
*Student:* Complete the unit test on local American Indian nations and present one fact you will remember.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test; the child presents one fact they'll remember.

- **Q:** Name one way local nations keep their culture alive today.
  **A:** Language classes, basket weaving, ceremonies and dances, cultural centers, storytelling.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 test

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test key: 1) Name of local nation (local answer). 2) Acorns were leached to remove bitter tannins. 3) Tule houses were built near marshes. 4) Baskets: storing, cooking, carrying. 5) Coastal people traded shells/shell beads; inland people traded obsidian. 6) Tribes are sovereign nations. 7) A tribal council is the elected government. 8) Newcomers (Spanish, Mexican, American) brought new animals, crops, religion, laws and diseases. 9) Different accounts can describe the same events differently because of perspective. 10) One way tribes keep culture alive today (language classes, basketry, ceremonies). 8–10 = proficient; plus one fact presented clearly.

## Art & Music 3: Tints, shades and value

**Prepare:** Paint in one color plus white and black, brushes, palette, thick paper. Pull up images of Georgia O'Keeffe's flower paintings (e.g., 'Red Canna' or 'Jimson Weed/White Flower No. 1'). Draw a 7-box strip for the value scale.

**Watch for:**
- Adding a lot of black at once. Black is strong; add a tiny bit at a time for shades (and add color to white, not white to color, for tints).
- Thinking value means price. In art, value means how light or dark a color is.

### Day 1 (Monday): Value scale
*Student:* Paint a 7-step value scale by adding white (tints) and black (shades) to one color.

*How to teach:* Paint the pure color in the middle box. Add a little white for each box going one way (tints) and a little black for each box going the other way (shades), making 7 steps from light to dark.

- **Q:** What is a tint? A shade?
  **A:** A tint is a color plus white (lighter); a shade is a color plus black (darker).
- **Q:** What is value?
  **A:** How light or dark a color is.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Monochromatic
*Student:* Paint a small monochromatic picture of a tree using only one color plus white and black.

*How to teach:* Paint a small tree using only one color plus its tints and shades (monochromatic).

- **Q:** What does monochromatic mean?
  **A:** Using only one color (hue) with its tints and shades.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): O'Keeffe
*Student:* Look at Georgia O'Keeffe's close-up flower paintings and discuss why she painted them so large.

*How to teach:* Look at O'Keeffe's flowers. Explain she painted flowers enormous and up close so busy people would stop and really look at them. Ask how the size changes how we see a flower.

- **Q:** Why did O'Keeffe paint flowers so large?
  **A:** So people would notice the small details and take time to really look at the flower, as if seen up close.
- **Q:** Where did O'Keeffe often paint (later in life)?
  **A:** New Mexico (the American Southwest).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Flower painting
*Student:* Paint a large close-up flower that fills the page, using at least 4 tints and shades.

*How to teach:* Paint a large close-up flower that fills the whole page and runs off the edges, using at least 4 tints and shades to show curved petals.

- **Q:** How can value make a petal look curved?
  **A:** Use lighter tints where light hits it and darker shades where it curves away or is in shadow.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Explain where you used light and dark values and how they create form.

*How to teach:* The child points to where they used light and dark values and explains how that makes the flower look 3D (form).

- **Q:** Where should the darkest values go?
  **A:** In shadowed areas — deep in the center of the flower and where petals overlap or curve away from the light.

**Assessment:** Value scale and O'Keeffe-inspired flower painting

**Answer key:** Value scale and flower rubric: 4 = 7-step scale that moves evenly from lightest tint to darkest shade; flower fills the page with at least 4 values placed to show form; the child explains light and dark placement; 3 = scale has 1–2 uneven jumps or flower uses 3 values; 2 = scale incomplete or flower flat; 1 = little work.

## PE 3: Overhand throw cues

**Prepare:** Gather 10–20 tennis balls (or rolled socks indoors). Tape a target about 2 feet square on a garage or fence wall with the center about 3 feet high; mark a throw line 15 feet away. Find an open field for distance throws. Make a 4-cue checklist.

**Watch for:**
- Stepping with the same foot as the throwing arm. Step with the opposite foot (right-handers step with the left).
- Facing the target squarely and pushing the ball. Start with the non-throwing side toward the target so the hips and shoulders can rotate.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cues
*Student:* Learn the cues (side to target, arm back, step with opposite foot, follow through) and shadow-throw 20 times.

*How to teach:* Teach the 4 cues: 1) side to the target, 2) arm way back, 3) step with the opposite foot, 4) follow through across the body. Have the child say the cues and shadow-throw (no ball) 20 times while you watch each cue.

- **Q:** If you throw with your right hand, which foot steps forward?
  **A:** The left foot (the opposite foot).
- **Q:** Name the 4 overhand throwing cues.
  **A:** Side to target, arm back, step with opposite foot, follow through.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Wall target
*Student:* Throw 20 tennis balls at a taped wall target from 15 feet and tally hits.

*How to teach:* From the 15-foot line, the child throws 20 tennis balls at the wall target using all cues. Tally hits on paper.

- **Q:** You hit the target 7 times out of 20. How many misses?
  **A:** 20 − 7 = 13 misses.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Distance
*Student:* Throw for distance in an open field using a big step and full follow-through; mark best throw.

*How to teach:* In an open field, throw for distance: run-up optional, big step with the opposite foot, full follow-through. Mark the best throw with a cone and pace or measure it.

- **Q:** What should you change to throw farther?
  **A:** Take a bigger step, rotate hips and shoulders, and throw with more force with a full follow-through (aim a little upward).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Partner check
*Student:* Watch a partner's throws and check off each cue on a checklist.

*How to teach:* Partners take turns: one throws 5 times while the other checks each cue on the checklist. Then switch and discuss.

- **Q:** Your partner stepped with the same foot. What cue do you give?
  **A:** 'Step with the opposite foot' — the foot on the other side from the throwing arm.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Explain the cues and throw 10 at the target to compare accuracy with Monday.

*How to teach:* The child explains all 4 cues, then throws 10 at the target and compares the hit rate with Day 2.

- **Q:** Monday: 7 of 20 hits. Today: 5 of 10 hits. Which day was more accurate?
  **A:** Today — 5 of 10 is half; 7 of 20 is less than half (10 would be half).

**Assessment:** Throw cue checklist and target tally

**Answer key:** Throw cue checklist (score each throw yes/no): side to target, arm back, opposite-foot step, follow through. Proficient = all 4 cues on at least 4 of 5 observed throws, plus a target tally recorded for Day 2 and Day 5 with a correct comparison. 4 = all 4 cues consistently; 3 = 3 cues consistently; 2 = 2 cues; 1 = 0–1 cues.

## Health 3: Growing and changing

**Prepare:** Baby photos of the child, a height chart or measuring tape. Preview a check-up description (e.g., KidsHealth 'Going to the Doctor'). Prepare the Unit 2 quiz below.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking being shorter or taller than friends means something is wrong. Kids grow at different rates because of genes, nutrition and sleep; this is normal.
- Thinking doctors are only for when you're sick. Check-ups help keep you healthy and catch problems early.

### Day 1 (Monday): Growth
*Student:* Compare baby photos to now and measure your height.

*How to teach:* Look at baby photos and compare to now. Measure the child's height and mark it on the chart; list things they can do now that they couldn't as a baby.

- **Q:** Name two ways your body has changed since you were a baby.
  **A:** Any two: taller, heavier, more teeth (adult teeth coming in), can walk/run, can talk and read.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Different rates
*Student:* Discuss why kids grow at different rates and that it is normal.

*How to teach:* Discuss that friends and siblings grow at different speeds and that genes, food, sleep and activity affect growth.

- **Q:** Why do kids the same age grow at different rates?
  **A:** Growth depends on genes from parents, nutrition, sleep and activity; everyone has their own timetable.
- **Q:** What can you do to help your body grow healthy?
  **A:** Eat a variety of healthy foods, get 9–12 hours of sleep, and be active.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Trusted adults
*Student:* List trusted adults and professionals to ask about body questions.

*How to teach:* The child lists trusted adults (parents, grandparents, doctor, school nurse) they can ask about body questions.

- **Q:** Who could you ask if you have a question about your body?
  **A:** A parent or caregiver, doctor, nurse or another trusted adult.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Doctor visit
*Student:* Learn what happens at a check-up.

*How to teach:* Describe a check-up: height, weight, heart and lungs with a stethoscope, eyes and ears, reflexes, maybe vaccines. Let the child ask questions and list one to ask at their next visit.

- **Q:** What does the doctor use to listen to your heart and lungs?
  **A:** A stethoscope.
- **Q:** Why do we go to the doctor when we're not sick?
  **A:** For check-ups to make sure we're growing well, get vaccines, and catch any problems early.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 check
*Student:* Complete a quiz on body systems and growth.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 quiz orally or in writing.

- **Q:** Which organ is the control center of the body?
  **A:** The brain.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 quiz

**Answer key:** Unit 2 quiz key: 1) Skull protects the brain. 2) Calcium makes bones hard. 3) Elbow = hinge joint; shoulder = ball-and-socket. 4) Muscles work in pairs because they can only pull. 5) Digestion order: mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine. 6) Nutrients are absorbed in the small intestine. 7) The heart is a muscle that pumps blood. 8) Lungs take in oxygen. 9) Brain = control center. 10) Kids grow at different rates and that is normal. 8–10 correct = proficient.
