# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 17

## ELA 3: Writing a modern myth

**Prepare:** Story map, a myth word bank (gods, heroes, natural events such as moon phases, rainbows, thunder, volcanoes). Write the Unit 4 test.

**Watch for:**
- Writing a myth that doesn't explain anything — the myth must explain why something in nature happens or teach a lesson.
- Overusing weak verbs (went, said) — replace with strong verbs (soared, whispered).

### Day 1 (Monday): Myth plan
*Student:* Plan an original myth explaining a natural event (why the moon changes shape) with a story map.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm natural events and pick one. Model a story map: god or hero, problem, events, and how the ending explains the natural event. The child completes their map.

- **Q:** What must every myth include?
  **A:** Characters such as gods or heroes, a problem, and an explanation of something in nature or a lesson.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Draft
*Student:* Draft the myth using a god or hero character and dialogue; spell irregular words (said, laugh, though).

*How to teach:* The child drafts the myth from the map, including at least 3 lines of dialogue. Then practice spelling irregular words.

- **Q:** Spell the words: said, laugh, though.
  **A:** said, laugh, though.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Revise
*Student:* Revise the myth for a clear lesson and strong verbs.

*How to teach:* Read the draft together, circling weak verbs and replacing them with stronger ones. Check that the lesson or explanation is clear in the ending.

- **Q:** Give a stronger verb for 'went' in 'The goddess went across the sky.'
  **A:** Raced, soared or streaked.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Present
*Student:* Read the myth aloud with expression to an audience.

*How to teach:* Model reading a dramatic passage with expression. The child practices, then reads the myth aloud to an audience.

- **Q:** How should you read a dramatic part?
  **A:** Slow down, change your voice to fit the character, and pause for effect.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 assessment
*Student:* Complete the Unit 4 test on myths, agreement and capitalization.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 4 test and score the myth with the rubric. Score the myth separately with the rubric and share one strength with the child.

- **Q:** Why did ancient Greeks tell myths?
  **A:** To explain nature and to teach lessons and values.

**Assessment:** Unit 4 test; original myth rubric

**Answer key:** Unit 4 test: 1) What is a myth? → a traditional story that explains nature or teaches values 2) What remained in Pandora's box? → Hope 3) Midas's lesson → greed; love matters more than gold 4) What happened to Arachne? → turned into a spider for her pride 5) What does Persephone's myth explain? → the seasons 6) Why did Icarus fall? → flew too close to the sun; wax melted 7) Agreement: The gods (live) on Olympus 8) Capitalize: the golden touch → The Golden Touch. Proficient 6+/8. Myth rubric: 4 = explains a natural event, god/hero character, dialogue, clear lesson, strong verbs; 3 = most elements; 2 = story but no explanation; 1 = incomplete.

## Math 3: Area of composite figures

**Prepare:** Grid paper, colored pencils. Draw several L- and T-shaped figures with labeled sides. Write the Unit 4 test in the answer key.

**Watch for:**
- Counting a section twice when splitting shapes — color each rectangle differently so no square belongs to two parts.
- Missing an unlabeled side on an L shape — find it by subtracting from the opposite side.

### Day 1 (Monday): Decompose shapes
*Student:* Split L-shaped figures into rectangles and add their areas.

*How to teach:* Draw an L shape and split it with a line into two rectangles; color each, find each area, then add. The child does 5.

- **Q:** An L shape splits into a 4 × 3 and a 2 × 2 rectangle. What is its area?
  **A:** 12 + 4 = 16 square units.
- **Q:** Why do we add the two areas?
  **A:** The whole shape is made of the two parts with no overlap.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Two ways
*Student:* Find the area of the same figure two different ways and compare.

*How to teach:* Split the same figure horizontally and then vertically, or find a big rectangle and subtract the missing corner. Both answers should match.

- **Q:** A 6 × 5 rectangle has a 2 × 2 corner cut out. What is its area?
  **A:** 30 − 4 = 26 square units.
- **Q:** If two methods give different answers, what does that mean?
  **A:** One method has an error — a part was missed or counted twice.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Room design
*Student:* Design a dream bedroom floor plan on grid paper and calculate each area.

*How to teach:* The child designs a dream bedroom on grid paper (1 square = 1 sq ft) with a bed, desk and rug drawn as rectangles, and labels each area. Remind the child to split any L-shaped space into rectangles before multiplying, and check two of their areas with a calculator.

- **Q:** A bed is 4 ft by 7 ft. What is its area?
  **A:** 28 sq ft.
- **Q:** The room is 10 ft by 12 ft. What is its area?
  **A:** 120 sq ft (10 × 10 + 10 × 2).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Garden problems
*Student:* Solve area word problems about garden plots and patios.

*How to teach:* Solve garden and patio area problems by drawing and splitting. Model the first problem, then let the child do the rest while you check that every part of each shape is counted once.

- **Q:** An L-shaped patio is made of a 5 × 4 ft and a 3 × 2 ft rectangle. What is its area?
  **A:** 20 + 6 = 26 sq ft.
- **Q:** A garden plot is 8 m by 6 m. What is its area?
  **A:** 48 sq m.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 assessment
*Student:* Complete the Unit 4 test on area concepts and composite figures.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 4 test. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** What units is area measured in?
  **A:** Square units (sq cm, sq in, sq ft, sq m).

**Assessment:** Unit 4 test

**Answer key:** Unit 4 test: 1) Shape of 3 rows of 4 squares → 12 sq units 2) 6 × 4 rectangle → 24 3) 9 m × 5 m → 45 sq m 4) Area 42, one side 6 → 7 5) 8 × 7 = 8 × 5 + 8 × 2 → 40 + 16 = 56 6) L shape of 5 × 3 and 2 × 2 → 19 7) 8 × 6 rectangle minus a 2 × 3 corner → 42 8) Why can't units overlap? → overlaps count the same space twice. Proficient: 7+/8.

## Science 3: Variation and survival advantage

**Prepare:** Paper 'insects' (small squares) in green, brown, red, yellow — 10 of each; a lawn or green and brown backgrounds; a timer. Write the Unit 5 quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking animals choose to change color to survive — individuals with helpful variations are more likely to survive and pass on traits.
- Thinking the 'best' trait is always the same — it depends on the environment.

### Day 1 (Monday): Camouflage game
*Student:* Scatter colored paper 'insects' on grass and time how many a family member finds; record which colors survive.

*How to teach:* Scatter all paper insects on the grass. A family member 'bird' collects as many as possible in 30 seconds. Count which colors were found and which 'survived'.

- **Q:** Which colors survived best on grass?
  **A:** Green (and often brown) — they were camouflaged.
- **Q:** Which were found first?
  **A:** Bright colors like red and yellow.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Analyze
*Student:* Graph results and explain the advantage of camouflage.

*How to teach:* The child graphs results and explains the advantage of camouflage.

- **Q:** What advantage does camouflage give?
  **A:** Predators have a harder time finding the animal, so it is more likely to survive and reproduce.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Other advantages
*Student:* Research how longer beaks or thicker fur help individuals survive.

*How to teach:* Research examples: finches with different beaks, thicker fur in cold places.

- **Q:** How might a longer beak help a bird?
  **A:** It can reach food deep inside flowers or bark that shorter beaks can't reach.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Write
*Student:* Explain how a variation could help an animal find a mate or food.

*How to teach:* The child writes how a variation could help an animal find a mate or food.

- **Q:** Give an example of a variation that helps find a mate.
  **A:** A peacock with brighter, larger tail feathers is more likely to attract a mate.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 assessment
*Student:* Complete the unit quiz on traits and variation.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 quiz. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** What happens to animals with a helpful variation over time?
  **A:** They are more likely to survive and reproduce, so the trait becomes more common.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 quiz; camouflage data analysis

**Answer key:** Unit 5 quiz: 1) Inherited trait example → eye color 2) Environmental influence example → plant in dark is pale 3) What is variation → differences among individuals 4) Why do green insects survive on grass → camouflage 5) Flamingo pink comes from → diet 6) How a thick coat helps → staying warm in cold. Proficient 5+/6. Data analysis: graph plus explanation that camouflaged colors survived more.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Primary sources: photos and newspapers

**Prepare:** Chronicling America (chroniclingamerica.loc.gov) search for your town or county newspaper (choose a short article ahead). 3 historic photos from Calisphere. A camera for the then-and-now photo. Ten source cards.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a textbook is a primary source. Primary sources were made by people at the time (diaries, letters, photos); secondary sources are written later about the past.

### Day 1 (Monday): Primary vs. secondary
*Student:* Sort 10 sources (diary, textbook, photo, encyclopedia, letter) into primary and secondary.

*How to teach:* Sort 10 sources into primary and secondary.

- **Q:** Is a diary written in 1850 a primary or secondary source?
  **A:** Primary.
- **Q:** Is an encyclopedia article about the Gold Rush primary or secondary?
  **A:** Secondary.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Photo analysis
*Student:* Analyze 3 historic photos of your town using a see-think-wonder chart.

*How to teach:* Analyze 3 historic photos with see-think-wonder.

- **Q:** What clues in a photo help you guess when it was taken?
  **A:** Clothing, cars or wagons, buildings, signs, technology and whether it is black-and-white.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Newspapers
*Student:* Read a short historic newspaper article about your area on Chronicling America.

*How to teach:* Read a short historic newspaper article together and summarize it.

- **Q:** What can an old newspaper tell us?
  **A:** What happened and what people thought was important at that time, plus ads showing prices and goods.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Then and now
*Student:* Take a photo of the same spot as a historic picture and compare them.

*How to teach:* Go to the spot of a historic photo and take a matching photo.

- **Q:** Why take the photo from the same spot?
  **A:** So the two photos can be compared fairly to see what changed.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write what changed and what stayed the same, citing both photos.

*How to teach:* The child writes what changed and stayed the same, citing both photos.

- **Q:** Name something that often stays the same over 100 years.
  **A:** Hills, rivers or mountains, and sometimes old buildings or street layouts.

**Assessment:** Then-and-now comparison

**Answer key:** Then-and-now comparison rubric: 4 = new photo taken from nearly the same spot; writing names at least 2 changes and 1 thing that stayed the same, pointing to specific details in each photo; 3 = 1 change and 1 sameness; 2 = general statements; 1 = incomplete. Sort key for Day 1: diary, letter, photo, newspaper from the time, map from the time = primary; textbook, encyclopedia, biography written later, documentary = secondary.

## Art & Music 3: Melody performance

**Prepare:** A phone or tablet to record, the chosen song sheet, and Chrome Music Lab 'Song Maker' set to a pentatonic scale (Settings: Scale = Pentatonic, Start on C).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking criticism means 'bad.' Feedback names specific things to keep and to fix.
- Thinking audiences can talk or move during a performance. Audiences listen quietly and clap at the end.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rehearse
*Student:* Rehearse a chosen song, recording yourself once.

*How to teach:* The child rehearses a chosen song from this unit twice, then records one full performance.

- **Q:** Why record yourself?
  **A:** So you can hear what you sound like and find things to improve.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Feedback
*Student:* Listen to the recording with a parent and choose 2 things to fix (pitch, words, dynamics).

*How to teach:* Listen to the recording together. Pick 2 specific things to fix (pitch in a certain line, forgotten words, more dynamics). Practice just those parts.

- **Q:** Name one thing you will fix and how.
  **A:** Answers vary, e.g., 'I went flat on the high part, so I will practice it slowly with the piano.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compose
*Student:* Compose an 8-beat melody using do-re-mi-sol-la in Chrome Music Lab Song Maker.

*How to teach:* In Song Maker (pentatonic: do-re-mi-sol-la), compose an 8-beat melody that ends on do. Play it back and adjust.

- **Q:** Which note should your melody end on to sound finished?
  **A:** Do (the home note).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Etiquette
*Student:* Discuss performer and audience etiquette (bow, listen quietly, applaud).

*How to teach:* Discuss etiquette: performers walk on, wait for quiet, perform, then bow; audiences listen quietly, don't talk or use phones, and applaud at the end.

- **Q:** What does the performer do at the end?
  **A:** Bows to thank the audience.
- **Q:** Name two things a good audience does.
  **A:** Listens quietly, stays seated, and claps at the end.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 check
*Student:* Perform the song and your melody for family using proper concert etiquette.

*How to teach:* Concert: the child introduces and performs the song and the melody for family using concert etiquette.

- **Q:** Why do audiences wait until the end to clap?
  **A:** So they don't interrupt the music and everyone can hear the whole piece.

**Assessment:** Song and melody performance

**Answer key:** Song and melody performance rubric: 4 = song performed with accurate pitch and words, 2 planned fixes improved, 8-beat pentatonic melody that ends on do, and proper concert etiquette (introduction, bow); 3 = minor errors in one area; 2 = several errors or no original melody; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 3: Foot dribbling

**Prepare:** Soccer ball (size 3 or 4) and 6–8 cones; open grass. A stopwatch for Friday's timed course.

**Watch for:**
- Kicking the ball far ahead when dribbling. Use small touches so the ball stays within a step of your feet.
- Using only the toes. Use the inside and outside of the foot for control; toe kicks are hard to control.

### Day 1 (Monday): Touches
*Student:* Practice 50 inside-foot 'toe taps' and inside-foot touches back and forth.

*How to teach:* Teach toe taps (lightly tap the top of the ball with alternating soles, 50 times) and inside-foot touches (pass the ball between the insides of both feet, 50 times). Keep on the balls of the feet.

- **Q:** Which parts of the foot do you use to dribble a soccer ball?
  **A:** The inside and outside of the foot (and the sole to stop); small touches, not toe pokes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cones
*Student:* Dribble through a cone line using both feet and small touches.

*How to teach:* Dribble through a line of cones using small touches with both feet, keeping the ball close. Go 4 times through.

- **Q:** How far should the ball be from your feet while dribbling?
  **A:** Close — within about one step so you can control it.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Stop
*Student:* Dribble and stop the ball with the sole of the foot on a signal.

*How to teach:* Dribble in an open space; when you shout 'Stop!' the child puts the sole of the foot on top of the ball to stop it. Practice 10 times.

- **Q:** How do you stop the ball quickly?
  **A:** Step on top of it lightly with the sole of your foot.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Soccer tag
*Student:* Play soccer dribble tag, protecting the ball from a defender.

*How to teach:* Play soccer dribble tag: the child dribbles in a marked square while a defender tries to touch or kick the ball out. Teach shielding: body between the defender and the ball.

- **Q:** What is shielding?
  **A:** Keeping your body between the defender and the ball to protect it.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Complete a timed foot-dribble course and record your time.

*How to teach:* Set the cone course. Time the child dribbling through and back; do 2–3 trials and record the best time.

- **Q:** Trial times were 28, 25 and 26 seconds. What is your best time?
  **A:** 25 seconds (lowest time is best).

**Assessment:** Timed foot-dribble course

**Answer key:** Timed foot-dribble course: record best of 3 times. Form rubric: 4 = small touches with both feet, ball stays close, weaves every cone without missing; 3 = mostly controlled, one missed cone or ball runs away once; 2 = big kicks or several missed cones; 1 = cannot complete course with the ball.

## Health 3: Personal goals and growth mindset

**Prepare:** Goal worksheet and a 2-week calendar. Prepare the Unit 3 quiz below.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking people are either smart or not. Brains grow with practice and effort; mistakes help you learn.
- Setting a goal without steps. Big goals need small steps and support.

### Day 1 (Monday): Growth mindset
*Student:* Turn 6 fixed-mindset statements ('I can't do this') into growth-mindset statements ('I can't do this yet').

*How to teach:* Read 6 fixed-mindset statements; the child rewrites each as growth mindset.

- **Q:** Change 'I'm bad at math' into a growth-mindset statement.
  **A:** Example: 'Math is hard for me right now, but I'm getting better with practice.'
- **Q:** Why does the word 'yet' matter?
  **A:** It shows you can't do it now but can learn with effort.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Choose a goal
*Student:* Set a personal goal (learn to ride without training wheels, read a chapter book, make a new friend).

*How to teach:* The child chooses a personal goal that matters to them and writes it down.

- **Q:** What makes a good personal goal?
  **A:** It is specific, something you care about, and possible to reach with effort.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Steps and supports
*Student:* List 4 steps toward the goal and the people who can support you.

*How to teach:* List 4 small steps and names of supporters for each.

- **Q:** Goal: read a chapter book. Give a first step.
  **A:** Example: pick a book at the library and read 1 chapter each night.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Track
*Student:* Track progress on a 2-week calendar and note obstacles.

*How to teach:* Set up the 2-week calendar; the child checks off each day and notes any obstacle.

- **Q:** What should you do when you hit an obstacle?
  **A:** Figure out what went wrong, try a new strategy, ask for help, and keep going.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 check
*Student:* Complete a quiz on feelings, stress, friendship, conflict and bullying.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 quiz.

- **Q:** What's one sign of a good friend?
  **A:** Kind, honest, listens, includes you, respects you.

**Assessment:** Unit 3 quiz; goal worksheet

**Answer key:** Unit 3 quiz key: 1) All feelings are normal. 2) I-message frame: I feel ___ when ___ because ___. 3) Two calming strategies: belly breathing, 5-finger breathing, movement, calm-down kit. 4) Two friend qualities (kind, honest, listens, includes). 5) Conflict steps: cool down, talk, listen, brainstorm, agree. 6) Bullying = repeated, on purpose, power imbalance. 7) Four types: physical, verbal, social, cyber. 8) Upstander action: speak up, stay with, walk away together, get help. 9) Reporting is to keep someone safe. 10) Growth mindset example: 'I can't do it yet.' Goal worksheet proficient = clear goal, 4 steps, named supporters. 8–10 = proficient.
