# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 32

## ELA 3: Drafting the research report

**Prepare:** Sorted notes, a map of California, a research rubric (intro, organized body paragraphs, facts in own words, conclusion, map with caption, source list).

**Watch for:**
- Mixing categories within a paragraph — each paragraph covers one category.
- Adding a map with no caption — the caption tells the reader what the map shows.

### Day 1 (Monday): Outline
*Student:* Organize notes into a report outline.

*How to teach:* Turn the sorted piles into an outline: introduction, one body paragraph per category, conclusion. Model ordering the cards within each pile so the paragraph flows logically.

- **Q:** How many body paragraphs should the report have?
  **A:** One for each category — usually 3 or 4.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Draft
*Student:* Draft introduction and two body paragraphs.

*How to teach:* Model a hook and topic sentence. The child drafts the introduction and two body paragraphs from the outline.

- **Q:** What goes in an introduction?
  **A:** A hook that grabs the reader and a sentence stating the topic of the report.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft
*Student:* Finish the body and conclusion; add a map with caption.

*How to teach:* The child drafts the remaining body paragraph(s) and the conclusion, then draws or prints a map showing the location with a caption. Check that the conclusion restates the main idea and does not add brand-new facts.

- **Q:** What should the map caption say?
  **A:** What the map shows, e.g., 'Yosemite National Park is in the Sierra Nevada mountains of eastern California.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Affixes
*Student:* Determine meanings of words formed with affixes in the report (unprotected, rebuild); review homographs.

*How to teach:* Find words in the report with prefixes and suffixes and determine their meanings. Then review homographs (same spelling, different meanings).

- **Q:** What does 'unprotected' mean? 'Rebuild'?
  **A:** Not protected; build again.
- **Q:** Give a homograph and two meanings.
  **A:** Bat — a flying animal or a stick used in baseball (also: wind, bark, park).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Self-check the draft against the research rubric.

*How to teach:* The child checks the draft against each line of the rubric and marks what still needs work. Have the child highlight evidence for each rubric line in the draft.

- **Q:** Name one item on the research rubric.
  **A:** Example: facts written in my own words.

**Assessment:** Report draft with rubric self-check

**Answer key:** Draft proficient: introduction, organized body paragraphs (one category each), conclusion, captioned map; rubric self-check completed honestly with at least one revision goal.

## Math 3: Perimeter

**Prepare:** Ruler, string, grid paper, a book, a picture frame, a table to measure. Write the perimeter quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing perimeter with area — perimeter is the distance around (add the sides), not the squares inside.
- Forgetting unlabeled sides of rectangles — opposite sides are equal, so add all 4.

### Day 1 (Monday): Measure around
*Student:* Use string and a ruler to find the perimeter of books, a table and a picture frame.

*How to teach:* Wrap string around a book, mark it, and measure the string. Then measure each side and add to check.

- **Q:** A book is 9 in by 6 in. What is its perimeter?
  **A:** 9 + 6 + 9 + 6 = 30 inches.
- **Q:** What does perimeter measure?
  **A:** The distance around a shape.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Polygons
*Student:* Find perimeters of polygons on grid paper by adding side lengths.

*How to teach:* Count unit lengths around polygons on grid paper and add side lengths. Remind the child to mark a starting corner so no side is skipped or counted twice.

- **Q:** A triangle has sides of 5, 7 and 8 cm. What is its perimeter?
  **A:** 20 cm.
- **Q:** A square has 6 m sides. What is its perimeter?
  **A:** 24 m.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Unknown side
*Student:* Find a missing side length when the perimeter is known.

*How to teach:* Subtract the known sides from the perimeter to find a missing side. Model one problem by writing the known sides on a sketch, then the child solves 5 more.

- **Q:** A rectangle has perimeter 20 cm and one side of 6 cm. How long is the other side?
  **A:** 4 cm (6 + 6 = 12; 20 − 12 = 8; 8 ÷ 2 = 4).
- **Q:** A triangle has perimeter 18 in with sides of 5 in and 7 in. Third side?
  **A:** 6 in.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fence problems
*Student:* Solve fencing word problems for a dog pen and garden.

*How to teach:* Solve fencing word problems by drawing the pen or garden and labeling every side. Ask 'Are we going around or covering?' to confirm it is a perimeter problem.

- **Q:** A dog pen is 8 ft by 5 ft. How much fence is needed?
  **A:** 26 ft.
- **Q:** A square garden has 4 m sides. How much fence?
  **A:** 16 m.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a perimeter quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the perimeter quiz. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** What is the perimeter of a 10 by 3 rectangle?
  **A:** 26 units.

**Assessment:** Perimeter quiz

**Answer key:** 1) 7 × 4 rectangle → 22 2) Square with side 9 → 36 3) Triangle with sides 4, 6, 9 → 19 4) Pentagon with all sides 3 → 15 5) Rectangle with P = 30 and one side 10 → other side 5 6) Hexagon with sides 2, 3, 4, 2, 3, 4 → 18 7) Fence for a 12 ft by 6 ft garden → 36 ft. Proficient: 6+/7.

## Science 3: Design solutions for hazards

**Prepare:** A baking pan, sand or soil, clay, water in a pitcher, small blocks for 'houses', articles on levees, lightning rods, storm shelters and fire breaks. Do the flood model outdoors or over a tray.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a solution has no downsides — levees can fail and push water downstream.
- Testing a model only once — repeat to be sure.

### Day 1 (Monday): Solutions
*Student:* Read about levees, lightning rods, storm shelters and fire breaks and how each reduces damage.

*How to teach:* Read about four hazard solutions; the child writes how each reduces damage.

- **Q:** How does a levee work?
  **A:** It is a raised wall of earth along a river that keeps floodwater in the channel.
- **Q:** What does a lightning rod do?
  **A:** It gives lightning a safe metal path to the ground so it doesn't damage the building.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Compare
*Student:* Compare 3 flood solutions in a table by cost, effectiveness and effects on nature.

*How to teach:* Compare 3 flood solutions (levee, dam/reservoir, wetland restoration) in a table by cost, how well they work, and effects on nature.

- **Q:** What is a benefit of restoring wetlands for flood control?
  **A:** Wetlands soak up floodwater like a sponge and also provide habitat.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Flood model
*Student:* Build a sand-and-pan river model, pour water to flood a 'town,' then test a clay levee.

*How to teach:* Shape a river channel in sand, place block 'houses' beside it, pour water to flood them, then build a clay levee and pour again. Record results.

- **Q:** What happened with the levee?
  **A:** Houses stayed drier, though water may rise higher or flood elsewhere downstream.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Claim
*Student:* Write a claim about which flood solution has the most merit for a riverside town, with evidence from the model.

*How to teach:* The child writes a claim about the best flood solution for a riverside town with evidence from the model and reading.

- **Q:** Write a model claim.
  **A:** A levee has the most merit for this town because in our model it kept the houses dry in 3 out of 3 trials.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Present the claim and answer a family member's questions about trade-offs.

*How to teach:* The child presents the claim and answers questions about trade-offs.

- **Q:** What is a trade-off of building a levee?
  **A:** It protects one area but can send more water downstream and is costly to maintain.

**Assessment:** Claim about hazard solution with model evidence

**Answer key:** Claim — CER rubric: 4 = clear claim, 3 accurate pieces of evidence, reasoning that links each piece to the claim with science ideas; 3 (proficient) = claim, 2+ pieces of evidence, reasoning for most; 2 = claim with evidence but little reasoning; 1 = claim only or inaccurate. Model evidence must include results from trials with and without the levee.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Resources and production

**Prepare:** Resource picture cards (trees, water, workers, tractors, ovens). Arrange a visit to a local farm, bakery or factory or find a video tour.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing capital resources with money. Capital resources are tools, machines and buildings used to make goods.
- Thinking services aren't real work. Services (teaching, haircuts, medical care) are work people do for others.

### Day 1 (Monday): Resources
*Student:* Sort picture cards into natural, human and capital resources.

*How to teach:* Sort cards into natural, human and capital resources.

- **Q:** Is a tractor a natural, human or capital resource?
  **A:** Capital resource.
- **Q:** Is a farmer's knowledge a natural, human or capital resource?
  **A:** Human resource.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Goods and services
*Student:* Sort 20 local jobs into producers of goods and providers of services.

*How to teach:* Sort 20 local jobs into goods producers and service providers.

- **Q:** Is a dentist a producer of goods or a provider of services?
  **A:** A service provider.
- **Q:** Is a baker a producer of goods or services?
  **A:** Goods (bread and baked foods).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Local producer
*Student:* Visit or research a local farm, bakery or factory and list the resources it uses.

*How to teach:* Visit or research a local producer; list resources used.

- **Q:** What natural resources does a farm use?
  **A:** Soil, water, sunlight, seeds.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Production chart
*Student:* Make a flow chart showing how a local product is made from resources to store.

*How to teach:* Make a flow chart from resource to store.

- **Q:** What is the first step in making bread?
  **A:** Growing wheat.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain how a local producer uses all three kinds of resources, past and present.

*How to teach:* The child explains how the producer uses all three resources, past and present.

- **Q:** How has the producer's work changed over time?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; e.g., machines replaced hand tools, but workers' skills are still needed.

**Assessment:** Production flow chart

**Answer key:** Production flow chart key (example — bread at a local bakery): wheat grown on farm (natural resource: soil, water) → harvested with combines (capital) → milled into flour → baker (human resource) mixes and bakes in ovens (capital) → packaged → delivered by truck → sold at store. Explanation proficient: names at least one natural, one human and one capital resource the producer uses, then and now (e.g., hand tools in the past, machines today).

## Art & Music 3: Art tells stories

**Prepare:** Images of a Diego Rivera mural (e.g., the 'Detroit Industry Murals,' 1932–33, Detroit Institute of Arts; or 'Pan American Unity,' San Francisco). Chumash rock art images from Calisphere or Chumash Painted Cave State Historic Park. Pencils, sketch paper.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking we know exactly what Chumash rock paintings mean. Their meanings are connected to Chumash beliefs and ceremonies and are not fully known to outsiders; we should not guess as if certain.
- Thinking it's harmless to touch ancient rock art. Oils from hands, chalk, graffiti and rubbing damage it permanently; look but don't touch.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rivera murals
*Student:* Look at a Diego Rivera mural and identify the story it tells about workers.

*How to teach:* Show the Detroit Industry murals. Ask: What are the workers doing? What machines do you see? What story does the mural tell about workers and industry?

- **Q:** What story does Rivera's Detroit Industry mural tell?
  **A:** It shows factory workers building cars and working together with huge machines, honoring workers and industry.
- **Q:** Where was Diego Rivera from?
  **A:** Mexico.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Chumash rock art
*Student:* View images of Chumash rock paintings and discuss what they may mean and why they must be protected.

*How to teach:* View Chumash rock paintings (red, black and white circles, figures, sun-like designs). Explain the Chumash are a California Indian people of the Central and Southern Coast whose ancestors made these paintings; they are sacred and still important to Chumash people today. Discuss why they must be protected.

- **Q:** Why must rock art be protected?
  **A:** It is irreplaceable and sacred to the Chumash people; touching, graffiti and pollution can destroy it.
- **Q:** Where did the Chumash live?
  **A:** Along the central and southern California coast (around Santa Barbara, Ventura, San Luis Obispo) and the Channel Islands.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Context
*Student:* Discuss how knowing when and where an artwork was made changes your response to it.

*How to teach:* Discuss how context (when and where art was made, by whom and why) changes our response. Compare viewing the mural knowing it was painted during the Great Depression about Detroit factories.

- **Q:** Why does knowing the context of an artwork matter?
  **A:** It helps us understand why it was made and what it meant to the people of that time and place.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Plan mural
*Student:* Sketch a mural plan showing important people and places in your community.

*How to teach:* Sketch a mural plan showing important people and places in your community (library, park, farmers market, neighbors, helpers).

- **Q:** What story will your mural tell?
  **A:** Answers vary; should name a clear community story or theme.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Share the mural sketch and get feedback.

*How to teach:* Share the sketch with family and ask for feedback; note 1 change.

- **Q:** What feedback did you get, and what will you change?
  **A:** Answers vary; the child names a specific change.

**Assessment:** Mural sketch

**Answer key:** Mural sketch rubric: 4 = sketch tells a clear community story with at least 3 important people/places, uses the whole page with a planned composition (foreground/background), and the child explains its message and one change from feedback; 3 = clear story, less detail; 2 = unclear story; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 3: Fitness reassessment

**Prepare:** Fitness log with baseline scores from week 3, tape measure, sit-and-reach box, mat, stopwatch, the same half-mile route or PACER lane and audio. Use the same procedure and cadence as week 3 so results compare fairly.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a lower score means failure. Growth in any test or effort matters; compare to your own baseline, not to others.
- Skipping the warm-up to save energy. Warming up gives more accurate, safer results.

### Day 1 (Monday): Strength
*Student:* Retest curl-ups and 90-degree push-ups and record in the fitness log.

*How to teach:* Warm up. Retest curl-ups and 90-degree push-ups using the week 3 cadence and form. Record and compare to baseline.

- **Q:** Baseline curl-ups: 12. Today: 19. How many more?
  **A:** 19 − 12 = 7 more.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Flexibility
*Student:* Retest sit-and-reach and shoulder stretch after a proper warm-up.

*How to teach:* Warm up with dynamic moves, then retest back-saver sit-and-reach (each leg) and the shoulder stretch. Record.

- **Q:** Why must the warm-up be the same as in week 3?
  **A:** So the test conditions are the same and the comparison is fair.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Endurance
*Student:* Retest the timed half-mile walk/run or PACER.

*How to teach:* Retest the half-mile walk/run or PACER, pacing steadily. Record time or laps and cool down.

- **Q:** Baseline half-mile 6:10; today 5:40. Did you improve, and by how much?
  **A:** Yes — 30 seconds faster (6:10 − 5:40 = 0:30).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compare
*Student:* Graph baseline vs. current scores for each test.

*How to teach:* Make a double bar graph: for each test, a bar for baseline and a bar for current. Label axes and title.

- **Q:** For a timed run, is a taller bar better?
  **A:** No — for time, a shorter (lower) bar means faster, which is better. For counts like curl-ups, taller is better.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Write whether you met your fitness goal and what helped most.

*How to teach:* The child writes whether they met their SMART goal, using numbers, and what helped most (practice, warm-up, effort).

- **Q:** Did you meet your goal? Show with numbers.
  **A:** Answer should compare goal and result, e.g., 'My goal was 15 curl-ups and I did 19, so I met it.'

**Assessment:** Fitness progress comparison

**Answer key:** Fitness progress comparison: proficient work includes all retest scores recorded beside baseline, differences computed correctly (e.g., 12 → 19 curl-ups = +7; run time decreases are improvement), a labeled double bar graph, and a written statement of whether the SMART goal was met with numbers and a reason. 4 = all parts; 3 = one error; 2 = missing graph or statement; 1 = scores only.

## Health 3: Environment and health

**Prepare:** AirNow website (airnow.gov) bookmarked. Gloves, a bathroom scale or kitchen scale, 3 bins for trash audit. Know AQI levels: 0–50 green/good, 51–100 yellow/moderate, 101–150 orange/unhealthy for sensitive groups, 151–200 red/unhealthy, 201–300 purple/very unhealthy, 301+ maroon/hazardous.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking pollution is only what you can see. Some air pollution (fine particles, ozone) is invisible.
- Thinking recycling is the only answer. Reducing and reusing come first because they prevent waste.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pollution
*Student:* Learn how air and water pollution can affect breathing and health.

*How to teach:* Explain how air pollution (car exhaust, smoke from wildfires, factories) can irritate lungs and trigger asthma, and how water pollution can make people sick.

- **Q:** How can air pollution affect health?
  **A:** It can irritate the lungs, cause coughing and trouble breathing, and trigger asthma attacks.
- **Q:** Name two sources of air pollution.
  **A:** Car exhaust, wildfire smoke, factories, power plants, burning trash.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Air quality
*Student:* Check the daily Air Quality Index on AirNow for a week and decide which days are good for outdoor play.

*How to teach:* Each day, check AirNow for your ZIP code, record the AQI number and color, and decide if it's a good outdoor-play day.

- **Q:** The AQI is 160 (red). Should you play hard outside?
  **A:** No. The air is unhealthy for everyone; stay inside or keep outdoor activity light and short.
- **Q:** What AQI color is best?
  **A:** Green (0–50), good.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Trash audit
*Student:* With gloves, sort one day of household trash into landfill, recycling and compost and weigh each.

*How to teach:* With gloves, sort a day's trash into landfill, recycling and compost and weigh each.

- **Q:** Which bin does a banana peel go in?
  **A:** Compost.
- **Q:** Which bin does a clean plastic bottle go in?
  **A:** Recycling.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Reduce plan
*Student:* Write a family plan to reduce, reuse and recycle 3 items.

*How to teach:* The family chooses 3 items to reduce, reuse or recycle and writes a plan.

- **Q:** What do reduce, reuse and recycle mean?
  **A:** Reduce: use less. Reuse: use things again. Recycle: turn used materials into new products.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on how a clean environment keeps people healthy.

*How to teach:* The child writes the paragraph on how a clean environment keeps people healthy.

- **Q:** Why does clean water matter for health?
  **A:** Dirty water can carry germs and chemicals that make people sick.

**Assessment:** AQI log and environment paragraph

**Answer key:** AQI log and paragraph key: log has 7 days of AQI values with colors and a correct decision (play outside for green/yellow; limit hard outdoor play at orange for sensitive people; stay inside or keep play light at red or above). Paragraph rubric: 4 = topic sentence, at least 2 specific ways a clean environment protects health (clean air helps breathing/asthma, clean water prevents illness, less trash means fewer germs and pests), a concluding sentence; 3 = 1 specific way; 2 = vague; 1 = incomplete.
