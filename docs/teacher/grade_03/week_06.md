# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 6

## ELA 3: Charlotte's Web, chapters 6–10

**Prepare:** Charlotte's Web chapters 6–10 and a story map template. Preview chapter 7 — the sheep tells Wilbur he will be killed at Christmas; discuss gently.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing tenses within a sentence ('Wilbur runs and cried') — check every verb matches the time.
- Retelling chapters as separate events — show how each chapter causes the next (bad news → plan).

### Day 1 (Monday): Chapters 6–7
*Student:* Read chapters 6–7 and explain how Wilbur learns his fate and how it changes the plot.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 6–7. Ask how Wilbur learns his fate and how this changes the story's problem.

- **Q:** Who tells Wilbur he will be killed, and when?
  **A:** The old sheep tells him the Zuckermans are fattening him to kill at Christmas.
- **Q:** What does Charlotte promise?
  **A:** She says 'I am going to save you.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Verb tenses
*Student:* Write sentences about the novel in past, present and future tense; spell -ful and -less words.

*How to teach:* Model past (Wilbur ran), present (Wilbur runs), future (Wilbur will run). The child writes 3 sentences of each about the novel. Spell -ful and -less words.

- **Q:** Write 'Charlotte spins a web' in past and future.
  **A:** Charlotte spun a web. Charlotte will spin a web.
- **Q:** What does 'fearless' mean?
  **A:** Without fear.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Chapters 8–9
*Student:* Read chapters 8–9 and explain how each chapter builds on the last in a plot chart.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 8–9 and add to a plot chart, noting how each chapter builds on the last. Ask the child to draw an arrow from each event to the event it causes.

- **Q:** What worries Mrs. Arable in chapter 8?
  **A:** Fern says the animals talk, and her mother worries about her.
- **Q:** What happens when Wilbur tries to spin a web in chapter 9?
  **A:** He jumps with a string tied to his tail and falls; Charlotte explains he doesn't have spinnerets.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Story plan
*Student:* Plan an original animal story with a story map (characters, setting, problem, events, solution).

*How to teach:* The child plans an animal story with a story map: characters, setting, problem, 3 events, solution. Model filling in one box from a familiar story, then let the child fill theirs.

- **Q:** What is the most important part of a story plan?
  **A:** A clear problem that the events build toward solving.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on how Charlotte's plan begins and take a verb tense quiz.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph about how Charlotte's plan begins, then takes the tense quiz. Check that the paragraph has a topic sentence and at least two details from the book.

- **Q:** How does Charlotte's plan begin?
  **A:** She thinks it over and decides she must play a trick on Zuckerman, which leads to writing words in her web.

**Assessment:** Verb tense quiz; plot paragraph

**Answer key:** Tense quiz: write in past/present/future — jump → jumped/jumps/will jump; play → played/plays/will play; eat → ate/eats/will eat; spin → spun/spins/will spin; go → went/goes/will go. Proficient 13+/15. Plot paragraph proficient: states the problem (Wilbur will be killed), Charlotte's promise, and that she plans a trick on Zuckerman.

## Math 3: Equal groups and multiplication

**Prepare:** Counters (beans or cereal), cups and paper plates, a hundreds chart, and real items or photos with equal groups (egg carton, window panes).

**Watch for:**
- Confusing the number of groups with the number in each group — always say '3 groups of 7' while pointing to the plates, then the counters.
- Thinking any groups count — multiplication needs equal groups; show plates with unequal amounts and ask whether 3 × 4 describes them.

### Day 1 (Monday): Equal groups
*Student:* Make equal groups with counters on plates and write repeated addition and multiplication sentences.

*How to teach:* Put out 4 plates with 3 counters each and say '4 groups of 3 is 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12, and we write 4 × 3 = 12.' The child makes 6 arrangements and writes both the repeated addition and the multiplication sentence.

- **Q:** Write repeated addition and multiplication for 5 plates with 2 counters each.
  **A:** 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 10; 5 × 2 = 10.
- **Q:** What does 3 × 6 mean?
  **A:** 3 groups of 6, which is 18.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Groups in pictures
*Student:* Find equal groups in photos (egg cartons, window panes) and write a multiplication equation for each.

*How to teach:* Look at photos or objects with equal groups and ask 'How many groups? How many in each?' The child writes a multiplication equation for each picture.

- **Q:** An egg carton has 2 rows of 6 eggs. Write an equation.
  **A:** 2 × 6 = 12.
- **Q:** A window has 4 rows of 3 panes. How many panes?
  **A:** 4 × 3 = 12.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Skip counting
*Student:* Skip count by 2s, 5s and 10s on a hundreds chart and connect skip counts to products.

*How to teach:* Skip count by 2s, 5s and 10s on a hundreds chart, coloring the numbers you land on. Show that the 6th number landed on when counting by 5s is 6 × 5 = 30.

- **Q:** Skip count by 5s to find 7 × 5.
  **A:** 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35 — so 35.
- **Q:** What is 8 × 10?
  **A:** 80.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Story problems
*Student:* Draw pictures to solve 6 equal-group story problems like '4 bags with 6 apples each.'

*How to teach:* Read each story and ask 'What are the groups and what is in each one?', then draw circles with dots. The child solves 6 problems and writes an equation for each.

- **Q:** 4 bags have 6 apples each. How many apples?
  **A:** 4 × 6 = 24 apples.
- **Q:** There are 5 cars with 4 wheels each. How many wheels?
  **A:** 5 × 4 = 20 wheels.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Match 10 pictures to equations and write a multiplication story for 3 × 7.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-picture matching check. Then the child writes and draws a story for 3 × 7.

- **Q:** Write a story for 3 × 7.
  **A:** Example: 3 boxes each hold 7 crayons, so there are 21 crayons. (It must show 3 equal groups of 7 and the answer 21.)

**Assessment:** Picture-equation matching check

**Answer key:** Draw these 10 pictures and match: 2 groups of 5 → 2 × 5 = 10; 3 of 4 → 3 × 4 = 12; 4 of 2 → 8; 5 of 5 → 25; 6 of 3 → 18; 2 of 9 → 18; 4 of 4 → 16; 3 of 10 → 30; 7 of 2 → 14; 1 of 8 → 8. Proficient: 9/10 matched, and the 3 × 7 story shows 3 equal groups of 7 = 21.

## Science 3: Static electricity

**Prepare:** Balloons, tissue paper torn into small bits, a plastic comb, an empty aluminum can, and the free PhET 'Balloons and Static Electricity' simulation (phet.colorado.edu). Note: static works best on dry days.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking static electricity is the same as magnetism — static comes from electric charges; it can attract paper and aluminum, which magnets cannot.
- Thinking rubbing 'creates' electricity — rubbing moves tiny negative charges (electrons) from one object to another.

### Day 1 (Monday): Balloon charge
*Student:* Rub a balloon on hair and test whether it attracts tissue paper bits.

*How to teach:* Rub a balloon on dry hair for 10 seconds and hold it near tissue bits. The child records what happens, then tests whether it still works after touching the balloon to a metal doorknob.

- **Q:** What happens when a rubbed balloon is held near tissue bits?
  **A:** The tissue jumps up and sticks to the balloon — it is attracted.
- **Q:** Why did rubbing change the balloon?
  **A:** Rubbing moved negative charges from the hair onto the balloon, giving it a static charge.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rolling can
*Student:* Use a charged balloon to roll an empty aluminum can without touching it.

*How to teach:* Lay an empty can on its side on a smooth floor. Hold a charged balloon close (without touching) and slowly move it away; the can rolls after it. Try racing the can.

- **Q:** Why does the can roll toward the balloon?
  **A:** The charged balloon attracts the can with electric force, even without touching.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Two balloons
*Student:* Charge two balloons and observe repulsion; record observations.

*How to teach:* Charge two balloons hung from strings and bring them near each other. The child records and explains what happens.

- **Q:** What happens when two balloons with the same charge come close?
  **A:** They push apart (repel) because like charges repel.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Simulation
*Student:* Explore the PhET 'Balloons and Static Electricity' simulation and draw charges.

*How to teach:* Explore the PhET simulation: rub the balloon on the sweater and watch the negative charges move. The child draws the charges on the sweater and balloon before and after.

- **Q:** In the simulation, what moves from the sweater to the balloon?
  **A:** Negative charges (electrons).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write 3 testable cause-and-effect questions about static electricity and answer one.

*How to teach:* The child writes 3 testable cause-and-effect questions (e.g., 'Does rubbing longer make the balloon pick up more tissue?') and tests one, recording results.

- **Q:** Give an example of a testable question about static electricity.
  **A:** Does the number of rubs change how many tissue bits a balloon picks up?

**Assessment:** Questions and observation log

**Answer key:** Observation log: proficient if it records attraction (tissue, can) and repulsion (two balloons) with drawings. Questions: 3 = three testable cause/effect questions and one tested with recorded results and a conclusion; 2 = questions not testable or untested; 1 = missing.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): First peoples of our region

**Prepare:** Find your region's original people on a California Indian territories map (e.g., at the California Indian Heritage Center or a native-land map) and visit that tribe's official website; note correct names and present-day facts. Check whether a language resource (word list or audio) is published by the tribe.

**Watch for:**
- Speaking about California Indians only in the past tense. Native nations are still here today with governments, languages, schools and businesses.
- Thinking all American Indians lived the same way. California alone had well over 100 distinct tribes with different languages and ways of life.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tribal map
*Student:* Locate your region's original peoples on a California Indian territories map (e.g., Ohlone, Chumash, Miwok, Tongva, Kumeyaay, Yokuts).

*How to teach:* Locate your region on a tribal territories map and name the people (e.g., Ohlone in the Bay Area, Chumash on the central coast, Tongva in the Los Angeles basin, Kumeyaay in San Diego, Miwok and Yokuts in the Central Valley and Sierra foothills).

- **Q:** Whose homeland is your town in?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; the name of the local nation from the map, e.g., Tongva (Los Angeles) or Kumeyaay (San Diego).
- **Q:** Were there many different tribes in California?
  **A:** Yes; California had more than 100 tribes speaking many different languages.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Land acknowledgment
*Student:* Learn whose land you live on and write a land acknowledgment.

*How to teach:* Explain a land acknowledgment: a statement recognizing the Indigenous people of a place. The child writes 2–3 sentences in their own words.

- **Q:** What is the purpose of a land acknowledgment?
  **A:** To recognize and respect the Native people who have lived on the land for thousands of years and still live here.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Living cultures
*Student:* Visit a local tribe's official website to learn about its present-day community.

*How to teach:* Explore the tribe's official website together and list 3 things the community does today.

- **Q:** Name one thing the local tribe does today.
  **A:** Answers vary by community; e.g., runs a tribal government, holds cultural events or powwows, teaches its language, operates businesses or protects sacred sites.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Language
*Student:* Learn a few words from a local tribal language if available.

*How to teach:* If the tribe publishes language resources, learn and practice 3–5 words; note who shared them.

- **Q:** Why do many tribes work to teach their languages today?
  **A:** Many languages were nearly lost when children were forced to stop speaking them; teaching them keeps the culture alive.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write facts about the local nation then and now.

*How to teach:* The child writes 5 facts: some then and some now.

- **Q:** Write one 'then' fact and one 'now' fact.
  **A:** Answers vary by community; a 'then' fact about traditional life (food, homes) and a 'now' fact about the present community.

**Assessment:** Facts summary

**Answer key:** Facts summary rubric: 4 = names the local nation correctly, gives at least 3 accurate facts about traditional life (homeland, food, homes or crafts) and 2 about the present-day community (tribal government, cultural programs, language revival, events) with the source named; 3 = 3–4 facts total; 2 = only past facts or 1–2 facts; 1 = inaccurate or incomplete.

## Art & Music 3: Reading rhythms

**Prepare:** Make flash cards in stick notation (quarter note = a stick, 'ta'; paired eighth notes = two sticks joined at the top, 'ti-ti'; quarter rest = a 'Z' squiggle). Have a whiteboard or paper and the bucket drum. Prepare 5 rhythms to clap for dictation and 6 cards for Friday's quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a rest means stopping the beat. A rest is a beat of silence — the beat keeps going even though no sound is made.
- Clapping ti-ti as two slow sounds. Paired eighth notes are two equal sounds in one beat.

### Day 1 (Monday): Notes
*Student:* Learn quarter notes ('ta') and paired eighth notes ('ti-ti') and read 8 flash cards aloud.

*How to teach:* Teach ta (quarter note: one sound on one beat) and ti-ti (two eighth notes: two even sounds on one beat). Read and clap 8 flash cards while patting the beat with your foot.

- **Q:** How many sounds are in one ti-ti?
  **A:** Two equal sounds on one beat.
- **Q:** Read: ta ti-ti ta ta. How many beats?
  **A:** 4 beats (ti-ti fills one beat).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rests
*Student:* Add the quarter rest and clap rhythms where the rest is a silent open-hand gesture.

*How to teach:* Add the quarter rest (one beat of silence). On rests, open hands outward silently. Clap rhythms like ta ta Z ta and ti-ti Z ti-ti ta.

- **Q:** How long is a quarter rest?
  **A:** One beat of silence.
- **Q:** Clap ta Z ti-ti ta. How many sounds do you clap?
  **A:** 4 sounds (ta, ti, ti, ta), with one silent beat.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Read
*Student:* Read and clap 8 four-beat rhythm cards, then play them on a bucket drum.

*How to teach:* Read and clap 8 four-beat rhythm cards, then play each on the bucket drum.

- **Q:** Card: ti-ti ti-ti ta Z. How many beats and how many sounds?
  **A:** 4 beats; 5 sounds.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Dictation
*Student:* Listen to 5 clapped rhythms and write each one with stick notation.

*How to teach:* Clap 5 four-beat rhythms; the child writes each in stick notation. Check together.

- **Q:** If you hear 'short-short, long, long, long,' how do you write it?
  **A:** ti-ti ta ta ta (two joined sticks, then three single sticks).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Clap a 6-card rhythm quiz with no mistakes.

*How to teach:* The child claps a 6-card rhythm quiz (one card at a time) while keeping a steady beat.

- **Q:** Which note gets one beat: ta or ti?
  **A:** Ta (quarter note); one ti is only half a beat.

**Assessment:** Rhythm reading and dictation quiz

**Answer key:** Rhythm reading and dictation quiz: Dictation (5 points) — 1 point for each rhythm written exactly as clapped. Example key if you clap these: 1) ta ta ti-ti ta 2) ti-ti ta ta ta 3) ta Z ta ta 4) ti-ti ti-ti ta ta 5) ta ti-ti Z ta. Reading (6 points) — 1 point per card clapped correctly with a steady beat and silent rests. Proficient = 9 of 11.

## PE 3: Balance

**Prepare:** Make a 10-foot tape line on the floor (or use a curb or low 2x4 beam no higher than a few inches). Lay cushions or a mat nearby for falls. Have a stopwatch to time 5-second holds.

**Watch for:**
- Holding the breath and looking down. Keep breathing, look at a fixed point ahead, and tighten the belly to stay steady.
- Thinking fewer body parts touching makes a balance easier. Fewer points of contact (a smaller base of support) make it harder; a wide base is more stable.

### Day 1 (Monday): Static balance
*Student:* Hold balances on 1, 2, 3 and 4 body parts for 5 seconds.

*How to teach:* Call out balances on different body parts: 4 points (hands and knees), 3 points (lift one arm), 2 points (one hand, one foot, or two feet), 1 point (one foot, arms out). Hold each 5 seconds while the child counts. Teach 'eyes on a spot, tight tummy.'

- **Q:** Which is harder to hold: a balance on 4 body parts or on 1? Why?
  **A:** On 1, because the base of support is smaller, so it is easier to tip over.
- **Q:** What helps you hold a balance?
  **A:** Looking at one spot, tightening your stomach muscles, arms out to the sides, and breathing steadily.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Line walk
*Student:* Walk forward, backward and sideways on a tape line or curb.

*How to teach:* Walk forward heel-to-toe along the line, then backward (toe reaching back), then sideways with slide steps. Add arms out to the side. Finish each pass with a controlled dismount: step off and land with bent knees.

- **Q:** Where should your eyes be when walking the line?
  **A:** Looking ahead at the end of the line or a spot in front, not down at your feet.
- **Q:** How do you dismount safely?
  **A:** Step or jump off with control and land on both feet with bent knees.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Partner balance
*Student:* Create partner counterbalances.

*How to teach:* Show counterbalance (partners face each other, hold wrists, lean back together) and counter-tension; also try back-to-back sitting down and standing up together. Stress slow movement and talking to each other.

- **Q:** In a counterbalance, why do you both need to lean equally?
  **A:** Each partner's weight holds the other up; if one leans more or lets go, both lose balance.
- **Q:** What should partners do before letting go?
  **A:** Agree and come back to standing slowly together, then release.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Balance sequence
*Student:* Create a 3-balance sequence.

*How to teach:* The child chooses 3 balances (e.g., stork stand, V-sit, 3-point bridge) and links them with smooth transitions. Have them write or draw the sequence, then practice it 3 times.

- **Q:** Name your 3 balances and how many body parts touch in each.
  **A:** Answers vary; e.g., stork stand (1 foot), V-sit (seat), bridge (2 hands, 2 feet = 4).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Perform the sequence.

*How to teach:* The child performs the 3-balance sequence, holding each balance still for 5 seconds with smooth transitions. Score with the rubric.

- **Q:** What is a base of support?
  **A:** The body parts touching the ground that hold you up.

**Assessment:** Balance sequence

**Answer key:** Rubric for balance sequence: 4 = 3 different balances, each held still for 5 seconds, smooth transitions, controlled start and end; 3 = all 3 balances but one wobble or brief pause; 2 = holds only 1–2 balances for 5 seconds; 1 = cannot hold balances.

## Health 3: Planning balanced meals

**Prepare:** A simple recipe for the planned dinner, kitchen tools, and grocery money or flyers for price estimates. Review kitchen safety: wash hands, adult handles knives and hot stove/oven, pot handles turned in. Prepare the Unit 1 quiz below.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a balanced meal needs exactly equal amounts of every food. MyPlate shows about half fruits and vegetables, a quarter grains and a quarter protein, with dairy on the side.
- Forgetting food safety while cooking. Wash hands before and after touching raw meat, and use separate cutting boards for raw meat and produce.

### Day 1 (Monday): Meal plan
*Student:* Plan a balanced dinner for your family using all 5 food groups.

*How to teach:* The child plans a dinner for the family that includes all 5 food groups, drawing it on a MyPlate outline and labeling each food's group.

- **Q:** Plan a dinner with all 5 groups.
  **A:** Example: baked chicken (protein), brown rice (grains), green beans (vegetables), sliced oranges (fruit), a glass of milk (dairy).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Shopping list
*Student:* Write a shopping list and estimate cost.

*How to teach:* The child writes a shopping list for the meal with quantities and uses flyers or a store website to estimate each cost and the total.

- **Q:** If chicken costs $8, rice $3 and green beans $2, what is the total?
  **A:** $13.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cook
*Student:* Help prepare the planned meal, practicing safe kitchen habits.

*How to teach:* Cook together. Before starting, the child lists 3 kitchen safety rules; supervise any knife or heat use.

- **Q:** Name three kitchen safety habits.
  **A:** Wash hands with soap first and after touching raw meat; an adult handles hot stoves and sharp knives; turn pot handles inward; wipe spills; use oven mitts.
- **Q:** Why use a different cutting board for raw chicken and vegetables?
  **A:** Raw chicken can carry germs (like salmonella) that could spread to food that won't be cooked.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Family message
*Student:* Make a poster encouraging your family to eat a rainbow of fruits and vegetables.

*How to teach:* The child makes a 'Eat a Rainbow' poster with fruits and vegetables of different colors and one sentence encouraging the family.

- **Q:** Why eat fruits and vegetables of many colors?
  **A:** Different colors have different vitamins and nutrients, so eating a variety gives the body more of what it needs.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 check
*Student:* Review your goal tracker and take a quiz on food groups, labels and activity.

*How to teach:* Review the goal tracker, then give the Unit 1 quiz orally or written.

- **Q:** What did your goal tracker show? What would you do differently?
  **A:** Answer should cite the number of days the goal was met and name one specific change.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 quiz; meal plan; goal tracker review

**Answer key:** Unit 1 quiz key: 1) Five food groups: fruits, vegetables, grains, protein, dairy. 2) Half the plate should be fruits and vegetables. 3) Look at serving size first on a label. 4) Ingredients are listed from most to least. 5) Kids need 60 minutes of activity daily. 6) A drink with 0 g sugar: water. 7) One ad trick: cartoon characters/prizes/bright colors. 8) Calcium builds bones. Meal plan: proficient plan includes all 5 groups with a named food for each. Goal tracker: proficient = at least 5 of 7 days recorded with an honest reflection. 7–8 correct = proficient.
