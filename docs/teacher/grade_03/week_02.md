# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 2

## ELA 3: Fables: characters and morals

**Prepare:** Aesop's Fables ('The Ant and the Grasshopper', 'The Boy Who Cried Wolf'); a two-column trait chart (trait | evidence); ai/ay/ee/ea word cards.

**Watch for:**
- Naming a feeling instead of a trait ('the grasshopper is happy') — a trait describes how someone usually acts (lazy, careless, hardworking).
- Capitalizing common nouns ('the Boy') or not capitalizing names — proper nouns name a specific person, place or thing.

### Day 1 (Monday): Character traits
*Student:* Read 'The Ant and the Grasshopper' and fill a trait chart for each character with text evidence.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Ant and the Grasshopper.' Model one trait with evidence ('The ant is hardworking because she stores food all summer'). The child completes the chart for both characters.

- **Q:** What trait describes the grasshopper, and what is the evidence?
  **A:** Careless or lazy — he sings and plays all summer instead of storing food, so he has nothing to eat in winter.
- **Q:** What is the moral?
  **A:** Prepare today for what you will need tomorrow; work before play.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Vowel teams
*Student:* Sort and spell ai/ay and ee/ea words, then read 'The Boy Who Cried Wolf' aloud for fluency.

*How to teach:* Sort words by vowel team (rain, play, feet, meat); note ai/ee usually come in the middle and ay at the end. Then the child reads 'The Boy Who Cried Wolf' aloud three times, aiming for smoother phrasing each time.

- **Q:** Which spelling of long a usually ends a word, ai or ay?
  **A:** ay (day, play).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Nouns
*Student:* Identify common and proper nouns in 'The Boy Who Cried Wolf' and write 5 sentences using both.

*How to teach:* Teach common vs. proper nouns. The child finds nouns in 'The Boy Who Cried Wolf' (boy, sheep, villagers, wolf) and writes 5 sentences that use both kinds.

- **Q:** Is 'village' common or proper? Is 'Monday'?
  **A:** Village is common; Monday is proper.
- **Q:** Why did no one come when the wolf really came?
  **A:** The boy had lied about a wolf twice before, so the villagers no longer believed him.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Narrative leads
*Student:* Revise the first paragraph of your personal narrative with a strong lead (action, dialogue or sound).

*How to teach:* Show three kinds of leads: action ('I sprinted…'), dialogue ('"Watch out!" Mom yelled.') and sound ('CRASH!'). The child writes all three for their narrative and picks the best.

- **Q:** Rewrite 'This is a story about my bike' as an action lead.
  **A:** Example: I pushed off hard, and my bike wobbled down the driveway.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining how one character's actions taught the moral, then partner-discuss which fable lesson matters most.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph: topic sentence naming the fable and moral, two pieces of evidence about a character's actions, and a closing. Then discuss which fable lesson matters most.

- **Q:** How did the boy's actions teach the moral of 'The Boy Who Cried Wolf'?
  **A:** He lied for fun, so when he told the truth no one believed him and the wolf ate the sheep — liars are not believed even when they tell the truth.

**Assessment:** Character paragraph with evidence; noun practice sheet

**Answer key:** Character paragraph rubric: 4 = names fable and moral, 2+ accurate actions from the text, explains how actions lead to the lesson; 3 (proficient) = moral and at least 1 accurate action explained; 2 = retells without connecting to the moral; 1 = inaccurate. Noun sheet: proficient if 9/10 nouns correctly labeled common/proper and proper nouns capitalized (e.g., boy–common, Aesop–proper, village–common, Greece–proper).

## Math 3: Rounding to the nearest 10 and 100

**Prepare:** Make number line strips (0–100 by tens; 0–1,000 by hundreds) and copy a grocery flyer or receipt. Write the 10-problem check from the answer key.

**Watch for:**
- Rounding 450 down to 400 or 35 down to 30 — a number exactly halfway rounds up by convention.
- Changing the wrong digit (rounding 347 to the nearest hundred as 350) — circle the place you are rounding to and look only at the digit just to its right.
- Forgetting to regroup when rounding up from 9 (995 → 1,000; 96 → 100) — show it on the number line.

### Day 1 (Monday): Nearest ten
*Student:* Locate 2-digit and 3-digit numbers between two tens on a vertical number line and round 15 numbers to the nearest 10.

*How to teach:* Draw a vertical number line with the two tens around the number (e.g., 40 at the bottom, 50 at the top, 45 marked halfway). Ask 'Is 47 above or below the halfway mark?' so the child sees it rounds up to 50. The child rounds 15 numbers, marking each on a line first.

- **Q:** Round 63 and 285 to the nearest ten.
  **A:** 63 → 60 (3 ones is less than 5); 285 → 290 (exactly halfway rounds up).
- **Q:** Which two tens is 718 between, and what does it round to?
  **A:** 710 and 720; it rounds to 720.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Nearest hundred
*Student:* Use a 0–1,000 number line to round 15 numbers to the nearest hundred, marking the halfway point each time.

*How to teach:* Repeat yesterday's routine on a 0–1,000 line, marking the halfway point (e.g., 350 between 300 and 400). The child rounds 15 numbers to the nearest hundred, saying the halfway number aloud each time. Check that they look at the tens digit to decide.

- **Q:** Round 432 and 678 to the nearest hundred.
  **A:** 432 → 400 (below 450); 678 → 700 (above 650).
- **Q:** What is the halfway point between 800 and 900?
  **A:** 850.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tricky cases
*Student:* Round numbers like 450, 995 and 305 and explain in writing why 450 rounds to 500.

*How to teach:* Work through 450, 995 and 305 together on number lines. The child writes a sentence explaining why 450 rounds to 500 (it is exactly halfway and the rule is to round up). Have them invent two other tricky numbers and round them.

- **Q:** Round 995 to the nearest ten and to the nearest hundred.
  **A:** Nearest ten: 1,000. Nearest hundred: 1,000.
- **Q:** Round 305 to the nearest hundred and to the nearest ten.
  **A:** Nearest hundred: 300 (0 tens). Nearest ten: 310 (5 ones rounds up).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rounding in the world
*Student:* Round prices from a grocery flyer to the nearest 10 cents and nearest dollar and estimate a shopping total.

*How to teach:* Show a flyer price like $3.47 and ask which dimes and dollars it is between. The child rounds 6 prices to the nearest 10 cents and nearest dollar, then adds the rounded dollars to estimate a total. Compare the estimate to the exact total.

- **Q:** Round $2.68 to the nearest dollar and the nearest 10 cents.
  **A:** $3; $2.70.
- **Q:** Estimate $1.89 + $4.12 + $2.95 to the nearest dollar.
  **A:** $2 + $4 + $3 = about $9 (exact $8.96).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a 10-problem rounding check and write a rule for rounding in your own words.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-problem check, then have the child write a rounding rule in their own words. A good rule mentions finding the two benchmarks, checking the digit to the right (or the halfway point), and rounding up at 5 or more.

- **Q:** In your own words, how do you round to the nearest hundred?
  **A:** Find the two hundreds the number is between and look at the tens digit — 0–4 rounds down to the lower hundred, 5–9 rounds up to the higher hundred.

**Assessment:** 10-problem rounding check plus written rounding rule

**Answer key:** Nearest 10: 1) 47 → 50 2) 82 → 80 3) 355 → 360 4) 921 → 920 5) 996 → 1,000. Nearest 100: 6) 238 → 200 7) 650 → 700 8) 449 → 400 9) 871 → 900 10) 52 → 100. Rule rubric: 3 = mentions benchmarks, the digit to the right, and the halfway/5 rule; 2 = rule correct but misses the halfway case; 1 = incomplete or incorrect.

## Science 3: Investigating force and motion

**Prepare:** Toy car, a board and books for a ramp, a tape measure, and three floor surfaces (carpet, tile, towel). Draw a data table with columns for 3 trials.

**Watch for:**
- Changing more than one thing at a time — in a fair test only one variable changes.
- Thinking one trial is enough — repeat trials to make results reliable.

### Day 1 (Monday): Plan a test
*Student:* Plan a fair test: does ramp height change how far a toy car travels? Identify what stays the same.

*How to teach:* Ask: Does ramp height change how far a car travels? The child writes the question, a prediction, the one thing they will change (height) and the things that stay the same (car, release point, floor, no pushing).

- **Q:** What must stay the same in this test?
  **A:** The car, the surface, the starting spot on the ramp, and releasing without pushing — only the ramp height changes.
- **Q:** Why is it called a 'fair test'?
  **A:** Only one variable changes, so we know that variable caused the result.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ramp investigation
*Student:* Run 3 trials at each of 3 ramp heights and record distances in a data table.

*How to teach:* Run 3 trials at each of 3 ramp heights (1, 2 and 3 books), measuring from the bottom of the ramp to where the car stops. The child records each distance in the table.

- **Q:** Predict: will the car go farther from a higher ramp?
  **A:** Yes — a higher ramp gives the car more speed, so it usually travels farther.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Friction test
*Student:* Roll the car down the same ramp onto carpet, tile and towel and measure distances.

*How to teach:* Keep the ramp the same height and change the surface at the bottom: carpet, tile, towel. Measure and record distances, 3 trials each.

- **Q:** On which surface will the car go farthest, and why?
  **A:** Tile — it is smoothest, so there is the least friction slowing the car.
- **Q:** What is friction?
  **A:** A force between surfaces rubbing together that slows motion.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Analyze
*Student:* Graph results and write a claim about force, friction and motion supported by data.

*How to teach:* Help the child find the middle (or typical) result for each test and make a bar graph. The child writes a claim supported by data, e.g., 'Higher ramps make the car go farther, and rough surfaces stop it sooner.'

- **Q:** What does a rough surface do to a moving car?
  **A:** It creates more friction, which slows the car and makes it stop sooner.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain how you kept the test fair and what you would improve.

*How to teach:* The child writes how they kept the test fair and one thing to improve. Discuss sources of error (pushing the car, measuring to different points).

- **Q:** Name one way to improve the investigation.
  **A:** Do more trials, release the car the same way every time, or measure more carefully to the back wheels.

**Assessment:** Lab report with data table and claim

**Answer key:** Lab report: 4 = question, prediction, controlled variables named, complete data table with 3 trials, graph, claim supported by numbers, and an improvement; 3 (proficient) = most parts with a data-based claim (higher ramp → farther; more friction → shorter distance); 2 = data but no claim; 1 = incomplete.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Landforms and bodies of water

**Prepare:** A physical map of California (showing elevation colors), flour, salt and water for salt dough (2 cups flour, 1 cup salt, about 3/4 cup water), cardboard base. Look up which landforms and bodies of water are near your town.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing a bay with a lake. A bay is part of an ocean or lake partly surrounded by land; a lake is a body of water surrounded entirely by land.
- Reading physical-map colors as political borders. On a physical map, colors show elevation (green low, brown high).

### Day 1 (Monday): Landform vocabulary
*Student:* Define and sketch 10 landforms and water bodies.

*How to teach:* Define and sketch 10: mountain, hill, valley, plain, plateau, desert, island, peninsula, ocean, lake, river, bay. The child makes a picture dictionary.

- **Q:** What is the difference between an island and a peninsula?
  **A:** An island is land completely surrounded by water; a peninsula is land surrounded by water on three sides.
- **Q:** What is a valley?
  **A:** Low land between hills or mountains, often with a river.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): California regions
*Student:* Locate the coast, Central Valley, Sierra Nevada and desert regions on a physical map.

*How to teach:* On the physical map, find the coast, Central Valley, Sierra Nevada and desert regions.

- **Q:** Which California region is a large flat area used for farming between mountain ranges?
  **A:** The Central Valley.
- **Q:** Which mountain range runs along California's eastern side?
  **A:** The Sierra Nevada.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Local features
*Student:* Identify the landforms and water near your town using a local physical map.

*How to teach:* On a local physical map, the child names nearby landforms and water.

- **Q:** What landforms and water are near your town?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; e.g., 'the Santa Monica Mountains and the Pacific Ocean' or 'the American River and the Sierra foothills.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Salt dough map
*Student:* Build a salt dough relief map of your region showing main landforms.

*How to teach:* Mix salt dough and shape a relief map of your region on cardboard: raise mountains, flatten valleys, carve rivers. Paint when dry.

- **Q:** On your relief map, why are the mountains raised?
  **A:** To show they have higher elevation than the valleys and plains.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Label a blank map of your region and describe its features.

*How to teach:* The child labels a blank map of the region and writes 3–4 sentences describing it.

- **Q:** What is the highest mountain in the lower 48 states, and where is it?
  **A:** Mount Whitney, in California's Sierra Nevada.

**Assessment:** Labeled regional map

**Answer key:** Labeled regional map key: map shows the four California regions — Coast (including the Coast Ranges and Pacific shoreline), Central Valley (Sacramento and San Joaquin valleys), Sierra Nevada (mountains in the east; Mount Whitney is the highest peak in the lower 48 states), and Desert (Mojave and Colorado deserts in the southeast; Death Valley has the lowest point in North America) — plus your town and its nearby landforms and water. Description is proficient if it names 3 correct local features. Rubric: 4 = all regions and local features correctly labeled with a description; 3 = one error; 2 = several errors; 1 = incomplete.

## Art & Music 3: Contour and observational drawing

**Prepare:** Sketchbook, a soft pencil (2B if possible) and eraser, 3 pieces of fruit with interesting shapes (banana, apple, pear), a sneaker, a desk lamp to light the still life from one side, and a small mirror (optional for hand drawing). Keep week 1 drawings for Friday.

**Watch for:**
- Drawing what you think an object looks like (a symbol) instead of what you see. Look at the object more than the paper and draw the actual curves and bumps.
- Thinking a blind contour drawing should look 'right.' It is an exercise to train the eye and hand; wobbly results are expected.

### Day 1 (Monday): Blind contour
*Student:* Draw your hand three times without looking at the paper, moving the pencil slowly with your eyes.

*How to teach:* Explain contour line: a line that follows the edges of an object. For blind contour, the child looks only at their hand (not the paper) and moves the pencil slowly as their eyes trace every edge and wrinkle. Do 3 drawings of about 2 minutes each.

- **Q:** What is a contour line?
  **A:** A line that follows the outside edge (and important inside edges) of an object.
- **Q:** Why draw without looking at the paper?
  **A:** It trains you to really look at the object and connect what your eyes see to your hand.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Still life
*Student:* Arrange 3 pieces of fruit and draw their outer and inner contour lines from observation.

*How to teach:* Arrange 3 fruits overlapping slightly, lit from one side. The child draws the outer contours first, then inner contour lines (where the fruits overlap, the stem, a crease), looking up often.

- **Q:** When one fruit is in front of another, how do you draw it?
  **A:** Draw the front fruit's whole outline and only the visible part of the one behind it (overlapping).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Add detail
*Student:* Return to the still life and add texture lines, shadows and a table edge to make it more realistic.

*How to teach:* Return to the still life. Add texture lines (speckles, banana ridges), shading on the side away from the light, cast shadows on the table, and a table edge line behind the fruit.

- **Q:** Where do you put the shadows?
  **A:** On the side of each fruit away from the light, and a cast shadow on the table on that same side.
- **Q:** Why add a table edge line?
  **A:** It shows the fruit sitting on a surface and makes the space look more real.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Shoe drawing
*Student:* Draw a sneaker from observation, including laces, stitching and tread patterns.

*How to teach:* Place a sneaker on the table at an angle. Draw its overall contour first, then laces, stitching, logo shapes and the tread pattern on the sole.

- **Q:** Name 3 details you saw that you might normally skip.
  **A:** Answers vary, e.g., stitching lines, the holes for the laces, the zigzag tread, the curve of the heel.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Lay out week 1 and week 2 drawings side by side and write 2 ways your observation improved.

*How to teach:* Lay week 1 and week 2 drawings side by side. The child writes 2 specific ways their observation improved.

- **Q:** What is one way your drawing improved this week?
  **A:** Answer should be specific, e.g., 'I drew the real shape of the banana instead of a curved line' or 'I added shadows on the side away from the lamp.'

**Assessment:** Observational drawings with written comparison

**Answer key:** Observational drawings rubric: 4 = blind contours completed slowly with detail; still life shows accurate shapes, overlapping, shading on the side away from the light and a cast shadow; shoe shows laces, stitching and tread; written comparison names 2 specific improvements; 3 = most elements with one missing; 2 = drawings are mostly symbols/outlines, comparison vague; 1 = drawings incomplete.

## PE 3: Fitness components and heart rate

**Prepare:** Gather a timer or phone stopwatch and make a fitness/heart-rate log (columns: date, activity, heart rate before, heart rate after). Plan 6 circuit stations with simple equipment (jump rope or jumping jacks, wall push-ups, curl-ups, sit-and-reach, plank hold, jog laps).

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up muscular strength and muscular endurance. Strength is how much force a muscle can make once (a hard push); endurance is how many times or how long it can keep working (many curl-ups).
- Counting the pulse with the thumb. The thumb has its own pulse; use the index and middle fingers on the side of the neck or the thumb side of the wrist.

### Day 1 (Monday): Components
*Student:* Learn the 5 components of fitness and act out an exercise for each.

*How to teach:* Teach the 5 components of health-related fitness: cardiorespiratory (heart-lung) endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility and body composition. For each, act out an exercise together (jog in place, wall push-up, 10 curl-ups, toe-touch reach, and for body composition talk about eating well plus being active). Have the child match each card to its exercise.

- **Q:** Name the 5 components of health-related fitness.
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility and body composition.
- **Q:** Which component does a sit-and-reach stretch work on?
  **A:** Flexibility.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pulse
*Student:* Find your pulse and count heart rate at rest for 15 seconds × 4.

*How to teach:* Show the child how to find the pulse with two fingers on the side of the neck (carotid) or inside the wrist below the thumb (radial). Sit quietly 2 minutes, count beats for 15 seconds while you time, then multiply by 4. Record resting heart rate in the log.

- **Q:** You counted 22 beats in 15 seconds. What is your heart rate per minute?
  **A:** 22 × 4 = 88 beats per minute.
- **Q:** Which fingers do you use to find your pulse, and why not the thumb?
  **A:** The index and middle fingers; the thumb has its own pulse and can confuse the count.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Heart rate change
*Student:* Measure heart rate before and after 2 minutes of jumping jacks; record in a log.

*How to teach:* Take a 15-second resting count, then do 2 minutes of jumping jacks and immediately count again. Record both numbers and the difference in the log. Ask the child to describe what else changed (breathing faster, feeling warm, sweating).

- **Q:** Your heart rate was 84 before and 140 after jumping jacks. How much did it go up?
  **A:** 140 − 84 = 56 beats per minute higher.
- **Q:** What else changes in your body when you exercise hard?
  **A:** You breathe faster and deeper, feel warmer, and may sweat.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Circuit
*Student:* Complete a 6-station fitness circuit (one per component).

*How to teach:* Set up 6 stations (e.g., jog laps = cardio, wall push-ups = strength, curl-ups = endurance, sit-and-reach = flexibility, plank = endurance, jumping jacks = cardio) and work 45–60 seconds at each with a short rest. At each station the child says which fitness component it builds.

- **Q:** Which component does jogging laps build?
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance (heart and lungs).
- **Q:** Which component do push-ups build?
  **A:** Muscular strength (and muscular endurance when you do many).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Explain why heart rate rises during exercise.

*How to teach:* Review the log together. Ask the child to explain in their own words why the heart rate rose during exercise, then check the log for completeness.

- **Q:** Why does your heart rate rise when you exercise?
  **A:** Working muscles need more oxygen and energy, so the heart pumps faster to send more oxygen-rich blood to them (and you breathe faster to get more oxygen).
- **Q:** What happens to your heart rate after you stop and rest?
  **A:** It slowly goes back down toward your resting heart rate.

**Assessment:** Heart rate log

**Answer key:** Heart rate log check: proficient log shows a resting heart rate correctly calculated (15-second count × 4, typically about 70–110 bpm for this age), a post-exercise rate that is higher, the difference subtracted correctly, and a sentence explaining that the heart beats faster to deliver more oxygen to working muscles. 4 = all parts correct; 3 = one math slip; 2 = numbers recorded but no explanation; 1 = log incomplete.

## Health 3: Breakfast, snacks and water

**Prepare:** Measuring cup, a water bottle, 2–3 snack choices, a can of regular soda (or its label), white sugar, a teaspoon and a clear cup. Know the numbers: a 12-oz can of regular soda has about 39 g of sugar, which is about 10 teaspoons (1 tsp ≈ 4 g). Children about 8 years old need roughly 5–7 cups of water a day, more when active or hot.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking juice and sports drinks count as 'just water.' They often contain a lot of sugar; plain water or milk are the best everyday drinks.
- Believing skipping breakfast is fine if you aren't hungry. Breakfast refuels the brain and body after a night without food and helps focus and energy at school.
- Thinking 'healthy snack' means 'no taste.' Help the child find snacks they enjoy that combine 2 food groups, like apple with peanut butter or cheese with crackers.

### Day 1 (Monday): Breakfast power
*Student:* Discuss how breakfast fuels the brain and plan 3 balanced breakfasts with at least 3 food groups each.

*How to teach:* Talk about how the body has been 'fasting' all night and breakfast is fuel for the brain. The child plans 3 breakfasts, labeling the food groups in each, and you check that each has at least 3 groups.

- **Q:** Why is breakfast important?
  **A:** It refuels the body and brain after a night without food, giving energy and helping you pay attention and learn.
- **Q:** Is 'oatmeal with banana and a glass of milk' a breakfast with 3 food groups? Which?
  **A:** Yes: grains (oatmeal), fruit (banana) and dairy (milk).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Snack decisions
*Student:* Use the decide-act-check steps to choose between snack pairs (chips vs. apple with peanut butter).

*How to teach:* Teach the decide-act-check steps: Decide (think of choices and what happens with each), Act (choose), Check (was it a healthy choice? would I choose it again?). Walk through chips vs. apple with peanut butter aloud, then the child does 3 more pairs.

- **Q:** Chips or apple with peanut butter — which is healthier and why?
  **A:** Apple with peanut butter: it has fruit and protein plus fiber, while chips are mostly salt, fat and refined starch with few nutrients.
- **Q:** What are the three steps of decide-act-check?
  **A:** Decide: think about the choices and results. Act: make the choice. Check: look back at whether it was healthy and what you'd do next time.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Water
*Student:* Measure how many cups of water you drink in a day and compare to the recommended amount.

*How to teach:* Have the child fill a water bottle and record each cup they drink today with tally marks. At the end of the day, compare to about 5–7 cups and plan one time of day to add water.

- **Q:** About how many cups of water should a third grader drink each day?
  **A:** Roughly 5–7 cups (more when it is hot or when exercising).
- **Q:** Name two jobs water does in the body.
  **A:** It cools the body through sweat, carries nutrients, helps digestion, and keeps joints and organs working.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sugary drinks
*Student:* Measure the teaspoons of sugar in a can of soda using sugar and a teaspoon and compare to water.

*How to teach:* Read the soda label: about 39 g sugar. Divide by 4 g per teaspoon to get about 10 teaspoons, and have the child spoon 10 teaspoons of sugar into a clear cup. Compare with water, which has 0.

- **Q:** A soda has 39 grams of sugar. About how many teaspoons is that if 1 teaspoon is 4 grams?
  **A:** About 10 teaspoons (39 ÷ 4 ≈ 9¾).
- **Q:** What is the healthiest drink to choose when you're thirsty?
  **A:** Water (plain milk is also a good choice at meals).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Make a 'healthy snack menu' card for the kitchen with 6 choices.

*How to teach:* The child designs a 'healthy snack menu' card with 6 snacks and posts it in the kitchen. Ask them to explain which food groups each snack includes.

- **Q:** Give one snack that combines two food groups.
  **A:** Examples: apple + peanut butter (fruit + protein), yogurt + berries (dairy + fruit), cheese + whole-grain crackers (dairy + grains), carrots + hummus (vegetable + protein).
- **Q:** Why is a snack with two groups better than one?
  **A:** It gives more nutrients and keeps you full longer.

**Assessment:** Healthy snack menu

**Answer key:** Healthy snack menu rubric: 4 = card lists 6 snacks, each includes at least one fruit, vegetable, whole grain, dairy or protein food (most combine 2 groups), no more than one 'sometimes' treat, and it is neat and easy to read; 3 = 6 snacks, mostly healthy, 1 weak choice; 2 = fewer than 6 or several sugary/salty items; 1 = mostly chips, candy or soda.
