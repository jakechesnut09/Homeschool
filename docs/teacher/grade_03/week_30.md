# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 30

## ELA 3: Research questions and sources

**Prepare:** Library books on California natural wonders (Yosemite, redwoods, Death Valley, Monterey Bay), a kid-safe search engine (e.g., Kiddle) or National Park Service pages, a dictionary.

**Watch for:**
- Asking questions that are too broad ('Tell me about Yosemite') — narrow them ('How did glaciers shape Yosemite Valley?').
- Using an index like a table of contents — the index is alphabetical at the back and lists exact pages.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose a topic
*Student:* Choose a California natural wonder (Yosemite, redwoods, Death Valley, Monterey Bay) and write 5 research questions.

*How to teach:* Show photos of the four wonders and let the child choose. Model one specific question, then the child writes 5 research questions.

- **Q:** Give a strong research question about Death Valley.
  **A:** Why is Death Valley so hot and dry?

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Search tools
*Student:* Use keyword searches and an index to find sources; record titles in a source list.

*How to teach:* Model choosing keywords (not whole sentences) and using a book's index. The child finds 3–4 sources and records title, author or website, and date on a source list.

- **Q:** What keywords would you search to learn how tall redwoods grow?
  **A:** redwood height tallest tree.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dictionary
*Student:* Use a dictionary and glossary to define 6 domain words; spell inter- and trans- words.

*How to teach:* The child uses a dictionary and a book glossary to define 6 domain words (granite, glacier, canyon, habitat, erosion, kelp). Then spell inter- and trans- words.

- **Q:** What does 'transport' mean?
  **A:** To carry from one place to another (trans = across).
- **Q:** What is a glacier?
  **A:** A huge, slow-moving mass of ice.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Read sources
*Student:* Read 2 sources and highlight answers to your questions.

*How to teach:* The child reads 2 sources and highlights or flags answers to the research questions, noting which questions still need answers. Model flagging one answer and writing the question number next to it.

- **Q:** What is the tallest kind of tree on Earth, and where does it grow?
  **A:** The coast redwood, which grows along the northern California coast (and southern Oregon).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Turn in question list and source list for review.

*How to teach:* Review the question list and source list together and give feedback. Give one compliment and one suggestion for narrowing or adding a question.

- **Q:** What information belongs on a source list?
  **A:** The title, the author or website name, and the date.

**Assessment:** Research question and source list

**Answer key:** Proficient: 5 specific, open-ended questions about the chosen wonder; at least 2 appropriate, reliable sources (library book, National Park Service, encyclopedia) listed with title and author or website.

## Math 3: Measurement data and line plots

**Prepare:** A ruler marked in 1/2 and 1/4 inches, 15 leaves or pencils, a line-plot template. Write the Unit 8 test in the answer key.

**Watch for:**
- Starting at the end of the ruler instead of 0 — line the object up with the 0 mark.
- An unevenly spaced line plot — mark 0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1 … with equal spaces.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ruler fractions
*Student:* Practice measuring lines to the nearest half and quarter inch.

*How to teach:* Measure drawn lines to the nearest half and quarter inch; name the marks 1/4, 2/4 (= 1/2) and 3/4. Model lining up the end of the line at 0 and counting quarter marks after the last whole inch.

- **Q:** A line ends at the third quarter mark after 2 inches. How long is it?
  **A:** 2 3/4 inches.
- **Q:** What is 2/4 inch also called?
  **A:** 1/2 inch.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Collect data
*Student:* Measure 15 leaves or pencils to the nearest quarter inch and record.

*How to teach:* The child measures 15 leaves or pencils to the nearest 1/4 inch and lists the measurements. Have the child record each measurement in a list so the data can be plotted tomorrow.

- **Q:** A leaf ends between 3 1/4 and 3 1/2 inches but closer to 3 1/2. What do you record?
  **A:** 3 1/2 inches.
- **Q:** Why do we line up the object at 0?
  **A:** So we measure the length from the very start.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Line plot
*Student:* Create a line plot with the data, labeling the scale in fourths.

*How to teach:* Draw a number line scaled in fourths that covers the range of the data; place one X for each measurement. Model the first two Xs, stacking repeated values neatly above the same mark.

- **Q:** What does each X on a line plot stand for?
  **A:** One measurement.
- **Q:** If four leaves are 4 1/4 inches, how many Xs go above 4 1/4?
  **A:** 4.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Analyze
*Student:* Answer questions about the line plot: most common length, longest, shortest.

*How to teach:* Answer questions about the line plot. Have the child count Xs to answer each question and point to the line plot as evidence.

- **Q:** Data: 3, 3 1/4, 3 1/4, 3 1/2, 4. What is the most common length?
  **A:** 3 1/4 inches.
- **Q:** What is the longest minus the shortest?
  **A:** 4 − 3 = 1 inch.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 8 assessment
*Student:* Complete the Unit 8 test on graphs and line plots.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** What is a line plot used for?
  **A:** To show how often each measurement occurs.

**Assessment:** Unit 8 test

**Answer key:** Unit 8 test: 1) ★ = 10; 3 stars → 30 2) 2 1/2 stars when ★ = 10 → 25 3) Bar graph: soccer 35, baseball 20 — how many more chose soccer? → 15 4) Measure a 2 1/4-inch line → 2 1/4 in 5) Line plot data 1, 1 1/4, 1 1/4, 1 1/2, 1 1/2, 1 1/2: most common → 1 1/2 in 6) How many measurements → 6 7) Longest − shortest → 1/2 inch. Proficient: 6+/7.

## Science 3: Weather and climate review

**Prepare:** The weather log (4 weeks of data), data for a city with a different climate (same weeks), vocabulary flash cards, a map for the weather report. Write the Unit 8 test.

**Watch for:**
- Drawing conclusions from one day of data — look for patterns across weeks.
- Mixing weather vocabulary (calling precipitation 'clouds').

### Day 1 (Monday): Log analysis
*Student:* Graph 4 weeks of your own temperature data and find the warmest and coolest days.

*How to teach:* The child graphs 4 weeks of temperature data and circles the warmest and coolest days.

- **Q:** How do you find the warmest day on a line graph?
  **A:** Find the highest point on the line.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Compare
*Student:* Compare your local data to the same weeks in a city with a different climate.

*How to teach:* Compare the child's data with the same weeks in a city with a different climate.

- **Q:** Why might temperatures differ between two cities on the same day?
  **A:** Different climates — latitude, elevation, distance from the ocean.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Vocabulary game
*Student:* Play a weather vocabulary matching game with flash cards (precipitation, climate, cirrus, etc.).

*How to teach:* Play a matching game with vocabulary cards.

- **Q:** Match: cirrus.
  **A:** Thin, wispy, high ice-crystal clouds.
- **Q:** Match: climate.
  **A:** The usual weather pattern of a place over many years.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Weather report
*Student:* Present a 2-minute TV-style weather report for your city using your data and a map.

*How to teach:* The child presents a 2-minute TV-style weather report using their data and a map.

- **Q:** What should a weather report include?
  **A:** Today's temperature, precipitation, wind, clouds and a forecast.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 8 assessment
*Student:* Complete the unit test on weather data, seasons and climates.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** What is the difference between weather and climate?
  **A:** Weather is day-to-day; climate is the long-term pattern.

**Assessment:** Unit 8 test; weather report

**Answer key:** Unit 8 test: 1) Rain gauge measures → precipitation 2) Wind vane points → where the wind comes from 3) Cumulus look like → puffy white clouds 4) Weather or climate: 'snow today' → weather 5) CA rainy season → winter 6) Why coast is milder → ocean 7) Hot dry city → Cairo or Death Valley 8) Read a bar graph's highest month. Proficient 6+/8. Weather report: clear, uses data, includes a forecast.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): American heroes: founders and freedom seekers

**Prepare:** Short biographies of Benjamin Franklin, Thomas Jefferson, Frederick Douglass and Harriet Tubman. An adapted excerpt from Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass (chapters 6–7, learning to read). Franklin sayings from Poor Richard's Almanack: 'Early to bed and early to rise, makes a man healthy, wealthy, and wise'; 'Well done is better than well said'; 'Lost time is never found again.' Parent preview: slavery is discussed; keep it honest and age-appropriate.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Franklin was a President. He was a printer, inventor, scientist and diplomat who helped write and sign the Declaration and Constitution but was never President.
- Thinking the Underground Railroad was an actual train underground. It was a secret network of people and safe houses helping enslaved people escape.

### Day 1 (Monday): Benjamin Franklin
*Student:* Learn about Franklin's role in independence and read 3 sayings from Poor Richard's Almanack.

*How to teach:* Learn about Franklin's role (helped write and signed the Declaration, got France's help in the war) and read 3 sayings; the child explains each.

- **Q:** What does 'Well done is better than well said' mean?
  **A:** Doing something is more important than talking about doing it.
- **Q:** Name two of Franklin's jobs.
  **A:** Any two: printer, writer, inventor, scientist, diplomat, postmaster.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Thomas Jefferson
*Student:* Learn about Jefferson writing the Declaration of Independence.

*How to teach:* Learn how Jefferson wrote the Declaration in 1776.

- **Q:** Why was signing the Declaration risky?
  **A:** It was treason against Britain; signers could have been hanged if the colonies lost the war.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Frederick Douglass
*Student:* Read an adapted excerpt from Douglass's Narrative about learning to read and discuss the risk he took.

*How to teach:* Read the adapted Douglass excerpt about learning to read: Mrs. Auld began teaching him the alphabet; Mr. Auld forbade it; Douglass kept learning from white boys in the street, trading bread for lessons.

- **Q:** Why was learning to read risky for Frederick Douglass?
  **A:** Enslavers did not allow enslaved people to read, and he could be punished; Mr. Auld said reading would make him unfit to be a slave.
- **Q:** How did Douglass keep learning?
  **A:** He traded bread to poor white boys in the neighborhood for reading lessons and practiced secretly.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Harriet Tubman
*Student:* Learn about Harriet Tubman's escape and Underground Railroad journeys.

*How to teach:* Learn about Harriet Tubman's escape (1849, from Maryland to Philadelphia) and her journeys back.

- **Q:** What did Harriet Tubman do after escaping?
  **A:** She returned to the South about 13 times and guided about 70 people to freedom on the Underground Railroad.
- **Q:** What was the Underground Railroad?
  **A:** A secret network of people and safe houses that helped enslaved people escape to freedom.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph about the risk one hero took and why it mattered.

*How to teach:* The child writes the hero paragraph.

- **Q:** Why does taking a risk for others make someone a hero?
  **A:** They put their own safety on the line to help others or stand up for what's right.

**Assessment:** Hero paragraph

**Answer key:** Hero paragraph rubric: 4 = names the hero, the specific risk taken (e.g., Douglass secretly learning to read when it was forbidden; Tubman returning to the South about 13 times to guide about 70 people to freedom; Franklin and Jefferson signing the Declaration, an act of treason against Britain), why it mattered, and a concluding sentence; 3 = risk and importance but vague; 2 = facts without risk; 1 = inaccurate.

## Art & Music 3: Revising music

**Prepare:** The song draft, Song Maker file, a family listener, a 'glow and grow' sheet, and simple percussion (shaker, drum, pot).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking revising means starting over. Revising means improving parts of the song based on feedback.
- Accepting all feedback without thinking. Composers decide which suggestions make the song better.

### Day 1 (Monday): Feedback
*Student:* Play your song for a family member and collect 2 'glows' and 1 'grow.'

*How to teach:* Play or sing the song for a family member. They give 2 'glows' (what works) and 1 'grow' (a suggestion). Write them down.

- **Q:** What is a 'grow'?
  **A:** A specific, kind suggestion for something to improve.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Revise
*Student:* Revise rhythm or melody based on the feedback and document changes.

*How to teach:* Revise one rhythm or melody part based on the feedback. Write a 'change note' (what changed and why).

- **Q:** Example of a change note?
  **A:** E.g., 'I changed measure 3 from all quarter notes to ti-ti ta ta so the words fit better.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Practice
*Student:* Practice performing the revised song with steady tempo.

*How to teach:* Practice the revised song with a steady tempo, using a drum or metronome app.

- **Q:** Why practice with a metronome?
  **A:** It keeps the tempo steady so you don't rush or slow down.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Accompaniment
*Student:* Add a percussion ostinato accompaniment.

*How to teach:* Add a 1- or 2-measure percussion ostinato that repeats under the whole song.

- **Q:** What makes a good ostinato accompaniment?
  **A:** A short, simple, repeating pattern that fits the beat and doesn't cover the melody.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Compare the original and revised versions and explain which is better and why.

*How to teach:* Compare the original and revised versions (recordings or notation) and explain which is better and why.

- **Q:** Why might the revised version be better?
  **A:** Answers vary but should name the specific change and its effect, e.g., 'The words fit the rhythm now, so it is easier to sing.'

**Assessment:** Revised composition with change notes

**Answer key:** Revised composition rubric: 4 = feedback recorded (2 glows, 1 grow), at least one clear revision documented in change notes, steady-tempo performance with a fitting ostinato, and a reasoned comparison; 3 = revision made, notes brief; 2 = little revision; 1 = no revision.

## PE 3: Net and wall games

**Prepare:** Balloon or beach ball, a rope tied between two chairs at about the child's head height as a net, a playground ball, chalk to draw a four-square court (4 squares, each about 5–6 feet), and a wall.

**Watch for:**
- Hitting the balloon with a fist. Use two-hand volleys with fingers spread and slightly bent, pushing up.
- Catching or holding the ball in four square. Hit it with an open palm; no catching or carrying.

### Day 1 (Monday): Balloon volleyball
*Student:* Rally a balloon over a rope net using two-hand volleys; count team hits.

*How to teach:* Teach the two-hand volley: hands above forehead in a triangle shape, fingers spread, push up. Rally a balloon over the rope net, counting team hits.

- **Q:** Where should your hands be for a volley?
  **A:** Above your forehead, fingers spread like holding a big ball, pushing up and out.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Four square
*Student:* Learn four square rules and play 15 minutes, using an open-palm underhand hit.

*How to teach:* Teach four square rules: the ball bounces once in your square, then you hit it with an open palm (underhand) to another square; you are out if it bounces twice, you miss, or you hit it out of bounds. The server is in the highest square (4 or 'king').

- **Q:** When are you out in four square?
  **A:** If the ball bounces twice in your square, you hit it out of bounds or on a line (by your rules), or you don't hit it after it bounces in your square.
- **Q:** How do you hit the ball in four square?
  **A:** With an open palm (or both hands), underhand, after one bounce — no catching.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Wall ball
*Student:* Rally a playground ball against a wall with a partner, alternating hits.

*How to teach:* Partners take turns hitting a playground ball against a wall after one bounce, alternating hits. Count the longest rally.

- **Q:** Why is moving your feet important in a rally?
  **A:** It gets you in position behind the ball so you can make a controlled hit.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rally game
*Student:* Play a 2v2 balloon or beach ball game over a net, aiming for open space.

*How to teach:* Play 2v2 over the net with a balloon or beach ball, aiming for open spaces on the other side; up to 3 team hits per side.

- **Q:** Where should you aim to win the point?
  **A:** Into open space where the other team isn't standing.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Complete a volley skill check: 10 controlled hits to a partner.

*How to teach:* Skill check: 10 two-hand volleys to a partner, counting controlled ones (reach the partner and are catchable).

- **Q:** Out of 10 volleys, 8 reached your partner. What percent is that?
  **A:** 8 out of 10 = 80%.

**Assessment:** Skill check

**Answer key:** Volley skill check: count controlled two-hand volleys (correct hand shape, ball goes up and reaches partner) out of 10. 4 = 9–10; 3 = 7–8; 2 = 4–6; 1 = 0–3.

## Health 3: Sleep, sun and hygiene

**Prepare:** Sleep log, sunscreen (SPF 30+), UV beads or dark construction paper. Know: AASM recommends 9–12 hours of sleep for ages 6–12.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking sunscreen is only needed at the beach or on hot days. UV rays reach you on cloudy days and in winter.
- Thinking sleep is wasted time. During sleep the brain stores memories and the body grows and repairs.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sleep
*Student:* Learn that 8–12-year-olds need 9–12 hours of sleep and why the brain needs it.

*How to teach:* Explain sleep needs (9–12 hours) and why: memory, growth, mood, fighting germs. The child lists 3 good bedtime habits.

- **Q:** How many hours of sleep do kids your age need?
  **A:** 9 to 12 hours.
- **Q:** Name two good bedtime habits.
  **A:** No screens an hour before bed, same bedtime every night, a quiet dark room, reading, a calm routine.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sleep log
*Student:* Track bedtime, wake time and hours slept for 5 nights.

*How to teach:* The child records bedtime and wake time each night and calculates hours slept.

- **Q:** You went to bed at 8:30 p.m. and woke at 6:30 a.m. How long did you sleep?
  **A:** 10 hours.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sun protection
*Student:* Learn sun safety (SPF 30+, hats, shade) and test sunscreen on UV beads or dark paper in the sun.

*How to teach:* Teach sun safety: SPF 30+ sunscreen, reapply every 2 hours, hats, shade from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. Test sunscreen on UV beads (or cover half of dark paper with a sunscreen-coated plastic bag) in sunlight.

- **Q:** What did sunscreen do to the UV beads (or paper)?
  **A:** The beads under sunscreen changed color less, showing sunscreen blocks UV rays.
- **Q:** When is the sun strongest?
  **A:** About 10 a.m. to 4 p.m.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Hygiene routine
*Student:* Create a morning and evening hygiene checklist (bathing, hair, nails, clean clothes).

*How to teach:* The child creates a morning and evening hygiene checklist.

- **Q:** Why is daily bathing and wearing clean clothes important?
  **A:** It removes sweat, dirt and germs, prevents odor and skin problems, and helps you feel good.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Analyze your sleep log and set a bedtime goal.

*How to teach:* Analyze the 5-night log: find the average and set a bedtime goal.

- **Q:** If you slept 9, 10, 8, 10 and 8 hours, what's the average?
  **A:** 9 hours (45 ÷ 5).

**Assessment:** Sleep log analysis and hygiene checklist

**Answer key:** Sleep log analysis and hygiene checklist key: analysis correctly computes hours slept each night (e.g., bedtime 9:00 p.m., wake 7:00 a.m. = 10 hours), finds the average, compares to 9–12 hours and sets a specific bedtime goal. Hygiene checklist proficient: morning (brush teeth, wash face, deodorant if needed, brush hair, clean clothes) and evening (bathe/shower, brush and floss, trim/clean nails weekly, lay out clean clothes). Rubric: 4 = all correct and complete; 3 = one math error or missing item; 2 = several errors; 1 = incomplete.
