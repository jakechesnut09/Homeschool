# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 3

## ELA 3: Folktales from around the world

**Prepare:** Borrow an Anansi folktale (e.g., 'Anansi and the Moss-Covered Rock' by Eric Kimmel) and a 'Stone Soup' picture book (Marcia Brown or Jon J Muth), or find read-alouds on Storyline Online. Make irregular-plural cards.

**Watch for:**
- Adding -s to every plural (mouses, childs) — memorize irregular plurals and add -es after s, x, ch, sh (boxes, benches).
- Thinking a trickster is always the hero — discuss whether the trickster's choices were good and what happens to him.

### Day 1 (Monday): Anansi tales
*Student:* Read an Anansi the Spider folktale and identify the trickster's problem, choices and lesson.

*How to teach:* Read the Anansi tale. Ask: What does Anansi want? What tricks does he try? What happens in the end? The child fills a problem–choices–lesson chart.

- **Q:** What kind of character is Anansi in these tales?
  **A:** A clever trickster spider from West African (Ashanti) stories who often tries to get something by tricking others.
- **Q:** In 'Anansi and the Moss-Covered Rock,' how is Anansi finally tricked?
  **A:** Little Bush Deer, who watched him trick the others, leads him to the rock and gets him to say the magic words himself, so he faints and she takes back the food he stole — the trickster gets tricked. (Adjust to the version you read.)

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Stone Soup
*Student:* Read a version of 'Stone Soup' and chart how the villagers change from start to end; spell r-controlled words (ar, or, er, ir, ur).

*How to teach:* Read 'Stone Soup' and chart how villagers act at the beginning (hiding food, unfriendly) and end (sharing, celebrating). Then sort and spell ar, or, er, ir, ur words.

- **Q:** How do the villagers change in 'Stone Soup'?
  **A:** At first they hide their food and won't share; by the end each adds something to the soup and they share a feast together.
- **Q:** Which three spellings can make the same sound in her, bird and turn?
  **A:** er, ir, ur.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Plural nouns
*Student:* Practice regular (-s, -es) and irregular plurals (mouse/mice, child/children) with a sorting activity and sentences.

*How to teach:* Sort plural cards into -s, -es and irregular piles. The child writes sentences using 6 plurals.

- **Q:** What are the plurals of mouse, child, box and foot?
  **A:** mice, children, boxes, feet.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revise for detail
*Student:* Add sensory details and feelings to the middle of your personal narrative, using a revision checklist.

*How to teach:* Use a checklist: Did I tell what I saw, heard, felt? Did I show my feelings? The child adds at least 3 sensory details to the middle of the narrative.

- **Q:** Make this more detailed: 'I was scared.'
  **A:** Example: My hands shook and my heart pounded so loudly I could hear it.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Compare the lessons of Anansi and Stone Soup in a Venn diagram and discuss with a family member.

*How to teach:* The child completes a Venn diagram comparing Anansi and Stone Soup (characters, problem, lesson) and explains it to a family member. Check that each side of the Venn diagram has at least two accurate details and the middle names the shared features.

- **Q:** What is one thing the two folktales share?
  **A:** Both are folktales passed down orally, both include a trick, and both teach a lesson.
- **Q:** How are their lessons different?
  **A:** Stone Soup teaches that sharing creates plenty; Anansi tales usually warn against greed or show that tricksters get tricked.

**Assessment:** Venn diagram comparison; plural nouns check

**Answer key:** Venn diagram: proficient (3) includes at least 2 accurate differences per side and 2 similarities, including each story's lesson. Plurals check (10): cats, boxes, wishes, benches, foxes, mice, children, feet, teeth, geese — proficient 8+/10.

## Math 3: Addition within 1,000

**Prepare:** Base-ten blocks and a place value mat; write 8 word problems for Thursday (store, library, miles) and the quiz in the answer key.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting the regrouped 1 (367 + 248 = 505 instead of 615) — model trading 10 units for a rod with blocks and write the small 1 above the tens.
- Adding digits without lining up places (405 + 37 with 37 under 40) — use grid paper or a place value chart so ones sit under ones.

### Day 1 (Monday): Open number line addition
*Student:* Solve 8 sums such as 367 + 248 by jumping hundreds, tens and ones on an open number line.

*How to teach:* Model 367 + 248 on an open number line: start at 367, jump +200 to 567, +40 to 607, +8 to 615. The child solves 8 sums, drawing each jump. Check that the jumps add up to the second addend.

- **Q:** Solve 256 + 138 on an open number line.
  **A:** 256 + 100 = 356, + 30 = 386, + 8 = 394.
- **Q:** Solve 367 + 248.
  **A:** 615.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Partial sums
*Student:* Add 3-digit numbers by adding hundreds, tens and ones separately, then combining partial sums.

*How to teach:* Show partial sums: for 475 + 368 write 400 + 300 = 700, 70 + 60 = 130, 5 + 8 = 13, then 700 + 130 + 13 = 843. The child solves 8 problems this way, saying the value of each part (seventy, not seven).

- **Q:** Use partial sums for 529 + 283.
  **A:** 500 + 200 = 700, 20 + 80 = 100, 9 + 3 = 12; 700 + 100 + 12 = 812.
- **Q:** Why do we write 70 + 60 and not 7 + 6?
  **A:** Because those digits are in the tens place, so they are worth 70 and 60.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Standard algorithm
*Student:* Model regrouping with base-ten blocks, then record the standard algorithm for 10 problems.

*How to teach:* Build 158 + 276 with blocks: combine units (14, trade 10 for a rod), combine rods (13, trade 10 for a flat), combine flats. Record each trade in the standard algorithm as it happens. The child does 10 problems, using blocks for the first 3.

- **Q:** Solve 158 + 276 with the standard algorithm.
  **A:** 434 (8 + 6 = 14, write 4 carry 1; 1 + 5 + 7 = 13, write 3 carry 1; 1 + 1 + 2 = 4).
- **Q:** Solve 609 + 294.
  **A:** 903.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Estimate and check
*Student:* Estimate each sum by rounding, solve, and judge if the answer is reasonable for 8 word problems.

*How to teach:* Before solving, round each addend to estimate. The child solves 8 word problems and writes 'reasonable' or 'check again' by comparing to the estimate.

- **Q:** A library has 386 fiction and 427 nonfiction books. Estimate, then find the total.
  **A:** Estimate 400 + 400 = 800; exact 813 — reasonable.
- **Q:** Sam says 312 + 289 = 501. Is that reasonable?
  **A:** No. 300 + 300 = 600, so 501 is too low; the correct sum is 601.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a mixed addition quiz using any strategy and explain one solution aloud.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz; the child may use any strategy. Then have them explain one problem aloud, naming the strategy and each step.

- **Q:** Explain how you could solve 495 + 305 mentally.
  **A:** 495 + 5 = 500, then 500 + 300 = 800 (move 5 over to make a friendly number).

**Assessment:** Addition quiz and oral explanation of one strategy

**Answer key:** 1) 243 + 152 = 395 2) 367 + 248 = 615 3) 158 + 276 = 434 4) 509 + 391 = 900 5) 625 + 185 = 810 6) 444 + 389 = 833 7) 299 + 450 = 749 8) 275 students Monday and 318 Tuesday → 593 students. Oral explanation: 3 = names strategy and explains place value in each step; 2 = correct steps, vague on place value; 1 = cannot explain. Proficient: 7+/8.

## Science 3: Patterns of motion

**Prepare:** String, metal washers, tape, a timer, a ball and a meter stick or tape measure. Find a doorframe or table edge to hang the pendulum from.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking heavier washers change a pendulum's swing rate — mainly the length changes it; test to see.
- Thinking a ball should bounce back to the same height — some energy changes to sound and heat each bounce.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pendulum build
*Student:* Build a pendulum from string and washers and count swings in 15 seconds.

*How to teach:* Tape a string with a washer to the edge of a table so it can swing freely. Pull it back a little and release (don't push); count complete back-and-forth swings in 15 seconds. Repeat 3 times and record.

- **Q:** What is a pendulum?
  **A:** A weight hanging from a fixed point that swings back and forth.
- **Q:** Why count swings 3 times?
  **A:** To make sure the result is reliable.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Change length
*Student:* Change the string length and record the pattern in swing count; predict the next result.

*How to teach:* Shorten and then lengthen the string, counting swings in 15 seconds for each. The child looks for the pattern and predicts the result for the next length before testing.

- **Q:** What happens to the number of swings when the string gets longer?
  **A:** There are fewer swings in 15 seconds — a longer pendulum swings more slowly.
- **Q:** Predict for a very short string.
  **A:** Many quick swings.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Bouncing ball
*Student:* Drop a ball and measure bounce heights; describe the pattern of decreasing bounces.

*How to teach:* Drop a ball from 1 meter next to a wall marked with tape and record the height of each bounce. The child describes the pattern.

- **Q:** What pattern do the bounces show?
  **A:** Each bounce is lower than the one before until the ball stops.
- **Q:** Why does each bounce get lower?
  **A:** Some energy changes into sound and heat every time the ball hits the ground.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Playground patterns
*Student:* Observe a swing or seesaw and describe repeating motion patterns.

*How to teach:* Watch a swing or seesaw (or a video) and describe the repeating motion. The child draws arrows to show the back-and-forth or up-and-down pattern.

- **Q:** How is a playground swing like a pendulum?
  **A:** It hangs from a fixed point and swings back and forth in a repeating pattern.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Use your pendulum data to predict swings for a new length, then test the prediction.

*How to teach:* Using their data, the child predicts the number of swings for a new string length, then tests it. They explain whether the prediction matched and why.

- **Q:** If a 20 cm pendulum swings 16 times in 15 seconds and a 40 cm one swings 11 times, predict a 30 cm pendulum.
  **A:** Between 11 and 16 swings (about 13) — it is between the two lengths.

**Assessment:** Prediction test and explanation

**Answer key:** Prediction test: proficient (3) = prediction falls between or beyond the data in the correct direction (longer = fewer swings), test performed fairly (same release height), and explanation compares result to prediction; 2 = prediction made but not explained; 1 = prediction ignores the pattern.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Climate and natural resources

**Prepare:** Look up your town's average monthly temperatures and rainfall (weather.gov climate data or a weather website) and your water agency's 'where your water comes from' page. Resource picture cards.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing weather and climate. Weather is today's conditions; climate is the usual pattern over many years.
- Thinking water comes from the faucet or the store. Most California towns get water from rivers, reservoirs, aqueducts or groundwater wells.

### Day 1 (Monday): Climate
*Student:* Describe the climate of your region using temperature and rainfall data.

*How to teach:* Look at the temperature and rainfall data. The child finds the warmest and wettest months and describes the climate (e.g., Mediterranean: dry summers, mild wet winters, common in coastal California).

- **Q:** What is the difference between weather and climate?
  **A:** Weather is the conditions on one day; climate is the usual weather pattern of a place over many years.
- **Q:** In much of California, which season gets the most rain?
  **A:** Winter (summers are usually dry).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Resources
*Student:* List local natural resources (water, soil, timber, minerals, fish).

*How to teach:* Define natural resource: something from nature that people use. List local ones.

- **Q:** Name three natural resources found in California.
  **A:** Any three: water, fertile soil, timber/forests, fish, gold and other minerals, oil, sunshine/wind.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Water source
*Student:* Research where your town's drinking water comes from.

*How to teach:* Read your water agency's page together and trace the path of your water on a map.

- **Q:** Where does your town's drinking water come from?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; e.g., Sierra snowmelt carried by aqueduct, a local river or reservoir, the Colorado River, or groundwater wells.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Resource map
*Student:* Make a resource map with symbols.

*How to teach:* The child makes a resource map with a key of symbols.

- **Q:** Why does a resource map need a key?
  **A:** So readers know what each symbol (like a tree for timber) means.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write how one resource is important to your community.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph about one resource's importance.

- **Q:** Why is water such an important resource in California?
  **A:** California has dry summers and droughts, and water is needed for drinking, farming and industry.

**Assessment:** Resource map and paragraph

**Answer key:** Resource map and paragraph key: resource map uses a key with symbols for at least 4 local natural resources (e.g., water, farmland/soil, forests/timber, minerals, fish, oil) placed in the right areas. Paragraph rubric: 4 = names one resource, explains 2 ways the community uses it (jobs, food, drinking water, building) and why protecting it matters; 3 = one use explained; 2 = names the resource only; 1 = incomplete.

## Art & Music 3: Geometric and organic shapes

**Prepare:** Colored paper and scraps, scissors, glue stick, white paper painted ahead of time with bright tempera (for cut-outs). Pull up images of Henri Matisse's 'The Snail' (1953, Tate) and a Kandinsky work such as 'Several Circles' (1926, Guggenheim) or 'Composition VIII'. Choose a lively orchestral piece (e.g., the finale of Rossini's 'William Tell Overture').

**Watch for:**
- Thinking organic shapes are only from nature. Organic shapes are free-form and irregular; they can be made by people too, like a puddle shape cut from paper.
- Thinking abstract art means 'random.' Artists like Kandinsky carefully chose shapes and colors to show feelings and music.

### Day 1 (Monday): Shape sort
*Student:* Cut 20 shapes from scrap paper and sort them into geometric and organic groups.

*How to teach:* Cut 20 shapes from scrap paper — some with a ruler or traced (circle, square, triangle, rectangle, hexagon) and some freehand curvy blobs, leaves, puddles. Sort into two groups and label them geometric and organic.

- **Q:** What is the difference between geometric and organic shapes?
  **A:** Geometric shapes are regular, with measurable straight edges or perfect curves (circle, square, triangle); organic shapes are free-form and irregular, like leaves, clouds or puddles.
- **Q:** Is a cloud shape geometric or organic?
  **A:** Organic.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Matisse
*Student:* Look at Henri Matisse's paper cut-outs (e.g., 'The Snail') and speculate how he made them.

*How to teach:* Show 'The Snail' and tell the child Matisse made it late in life when illness made painting hard: assistants painted paper with gouache, and he cut shapes with scissors ('drawing with scissors') and directed where they were pinned. Ask how they think he made it and where they see the spiral.

- **Q:** How did Matisse make 'The Snail'?
  **A:** He cut shapes out of paper painted with gouache and arranged them in a spiral on a large background, then they were glued down.
- **Q:** What kind of shapes are in 'The Snail'?
  **A:** Mostly large, colorful geometric-like rectangles with rough, cut edges arranged in a spiral — showing a snail's shell through color and arrangement, not detail.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cut-paper collage
*Student:* Create a Matisse-inspired collage of organic shapes cut freehand from painted paper.

*How to teach:* The child cuts organic shapes freehand (no drawing first) from painted paper and arranges them on a background before gluing. Encourage overlapping and contrasting colors.

- **Q:** Why arrange the shapes before gluing?
  **A:** To try different compositions and choose the best balance of shapes and colors.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Kandinsky
*Student:* Listen to a lively orchestral piece and make a geometric composition of circles, lines and triangles inspired by Kandinsky.

*How to teach:* Show a Kandinsky painting and share that he connected colors and shapes to music. Play the orchestral piece; the child makes a composition of circles, lines and triangles (traced or cut) that matches the music's energy.

- **Q:** Why did Kandinsky paint shapes and colors instead of real objects?
  **A:** He wanted art to express feelings like music does, using shapes and colors on their own (abstract art).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Give a 1-minute artist talk explaining the shapes you chose and why.

*How to teach:* The child gives a 1-minute artist talk about either artwork: what shapes they chose (geometric or organic), which artist inspired them, and why.

- **Q:** Name the artist who inspired your collage and one thing you learned from them.
  **A:** Matisse (cut-paper organic shapes, bright colors) or Kandinsky (geometric shapes that show music and feelings).

**Assessment:** Collage and artist talk

**Answer key:** Collage and artist talk rubric: 4 = Matisse collage uses freehand organic shapes with overlapping and contrasting color, Kandinsky composition uses geometric shapes, and the 1-minute talk correctly names shape types, the artist and reasons for choices; 3 = both artworks complete, talk names shapes and artist; 2 = one artwork incomplete or shape types confused; 1 = little work and no talk.

## PE 3: Baseline fitness assessment

**Prepare:** Gather a tape measure or yardstick, a box about 12 inches high for sit-and-reach (or tape a yardstick to the floor), a mat, a stopwatch, and mark a half-mile route (or a 20-meter lane with cones for the PACER; free PACER audio is online). Print a fitness log with baseline columns. Warm up before every test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the tests are a competition with others. These scores are a starting point to compare against yourself later in the year.
- Doing push-ups or curl-ups as fast as possible. FitnessGram-style tests use a steady cadence (about one every 3 seconds) with correct form; reps with bad form do not count.
- Writing a goal like 'get better.' A SMART goal is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.

### Day 1 (Monday): Strength
*Student:* Record curl-ups and 90-degree push-ups.

*How to teach:* Warm up, then teach curl-up form: knees bent, feet flat, arms straight with fingertips sliding along the mat about 3 inches, at a slow cadence (one every 3 seconds) until form breaks. Then 90-degree push-ups: straight body, lower until elbows bend to 90 degrees, at the same cadence. Record both counts.

- **Q:** What does a 90-degree push-up mean?
  **A:** You lower your body until your elbows are bent at a right angle (upper arm parallel to the floor), keeping your body straight.
- **Q:** Which fitness component do curl-ups test?
  **A:** Abdominal muscular strength and endurance.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Flexibility
*Student:* Measure sit-and-reach and shoulder stretch.

*How to teach:* After warming up, do the back-saver sit-and-reach: one leg straight with the foot flat against the box, the other knee bent; reach forward slowly 4 times and hold the 4th for 1 second; measure inches, then switch legs. For the shoulder stretch, reach one hand over the shoulder and the other up the back — record yes if fingertips touch, for each side.

- **Q:** Why do we warm up before the flexibility test?
  **A:** Warm muscles stretch farther and are less likely to be strained.
- **Q:** Should you bounce to reach farther?
  **A:** No — reach slowly and smoothly; bouncing can pull a muscle and does not count.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Endurance
*Student:* Complete a timed half-mile walk/run or PACER and record.

*How to teach:* Warm up, then time the half-mile walk/run (or run the PACER, counting laps until the child misses two beeps). Remind them to pace themselves rather than sprinting at the start. Record the time or laps and cool down with walking and stretches.

- **Q:** Why shouldn't you sprint at the start of the half-mile?
  **A:** You will tire out quickly; a steady pace lets you finish with your best time.
- **Q:** Which fitness component does the half-mile or PACER test?
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory (aerobic) endurance.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Goal
*Student:* Set a SMART fitness goal for the semester.

*How to teach:* Explain SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. Look at the baseline scores and help the child choose one area and write a goal, e.g., 'I will do 15 curl-ups by the March retest by doing curl-ups 3 times a week.'

- **Q:** What does SMART stand for?
  **A:** Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.
- **Q:** Is 'I want to be stronger' a SMART goal? Fix it.
  **A:** No — it is not measurable or time-bound. Example fix: 'I will do 10 push-ups by March by practicing push-ups 3 days a week.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Begin a weekly fitness log to track progress.

*How to teach:* Set up the weekly fitness log (date, activity, minutes, how it felt). Have the child fill in this week's entries and copy their goal at the top. Check that all baseline scores are recorded.

- **Q:** How will the log help you reach your goal?
  **A:** It shows how often you practice and lets you see progress toward your goal over time.

**Assessment:** Baseline fitness scores and written goal

**Answer key:** Baseline check: the log should show (1) curl-up count, (2) push-up count, (3) sit-and-reach in inches for right and left, (4) shoulder stretch yes/no for each side, (5) half-mile time or PACER laps, and (6) a written SMART goal. Proficient goal example: 'I will improve my PACER from 12 to 18 laps by the spring retest by jogging 15 minutes three times a week.' Score: 4 = all scores recorded and goal has all 5 SMART parts; 3 = goal missing 1 part; 2 = scores missing or goal vague; 1 = no goal.

## Health 3: Reading food labels

**Prepare:** Three cereal boxes and two yogurt containers with Nutrition Facts labels. Preview the labels so you know serving size, sugar and fiber for each. Plan a short grocery trip for Day 4. Prepare 5 questions about one new label for Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Comparing whole-package numbers instead of per-serving numbers. All label numbers are for ONE serving; check the serving size first.
- Thinking the first ingredient is the least important. Ingredients are listed from most to least by weight, so the first ingredient is what the food has the most of.
- Assuming 'low fat' or a healthy-looking box means healthy. Always check the sugar, sodium and fiber numbers.

### Day 1 (Monday): Label parts
*Student:* Find serving size, calories, sugar, sodium and fiber on a Nutrition Facts label.

*How to teach:* Show a Nutrition Facts label and point to each part: serving size, calories, total sugars, sodium, dietary fiber. The child highlights each one on two different labels.

- **Q:** Where on a label do you look first, and why?
  **A:** Serving size, because all the numbers on the label are for one serving.
- **Q:** Which two numbers on a label should usually be low, and which should be higher?
  **A:** Sugar and sodium should be low; fiber should be higher.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Compare cereals
*Student:* Compare sugar and fiber in 3 cereals and rank them from healthiest to least healthy.

*How to teach:* Make a table of the 3 cereals' sugar and fiber per serving. The child ranks them from healthiest (low sugar, high fiber) to least healthy and explains the ranking.

- **Q:** Cereal A: 3 g sugar, 4 g fiber. Cereal B: 14 g sugar, 1 g fiber. Which is healthier?
  **A:** Cereal A, because it has much less sugar and more fiber.
- **Q:** Why is fiber good?
  **A:** It helps digestion and helps you feel full longer.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ingredients
*Student:* Read ingredient lists and learn that ingredients are listed from most to least.

*How to teach:* Read an ingredient list aloud and explain that ingredients go from most to least. Have the child find words that mean sugar (sugar, corn syrup, honey, cane juice, words ending in -ose) in 2 lists.

- **Q:** If 'sugar' is the first ingredient, what does that tell you?
  **A:** The food has more sugar than any other ingredient.
- **Q:** Name two other names for sugar on an ingredient list.
  **A:** Any two: corn syrup, high-fructose corn syrup, honey, cane sugar, dextrose, sucrose, fructose, molasses.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Store hunt
*Student:* At the grocery store, compare 2 brands of the same food and choose the healthier one with reasons.

*How to teach:* At the store, the child picks one food (bread, yogurt or crackers) and compares two brands' labels. They choose the healthier one and give 2 reasons based on the numbers.

- **Q:** Yogurt 1 has 18 g sugar; Yogurt 2 has 9 g. Same serving size. Which is the better choice?
  **A:** Yogurt 2, with half the sugar.
- **Q:** Why must the serving sizes be the same to compare fairly?
  **A:** If the servings differ, the numbers aren't measuring the same amount of food.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Answer 5 questions about a new food label.

*How to teach:* Give a new label and ask the 5 questions in the assessment. Let the child point to the line on the label as they answer.

- **Q:** A label says 2 servings per container and 150 calories per serving. How many calories in the whole container?
  **A:** 300 calories (2 × 150).
- **Q:** Does a big number for sodium mean the food is salty or sweet?
  **A:** Salty; sodium is in salt.

**Assessment:** Food label questions

**Answer key:** Food label quiz (5 questions on a label you choose): answers come from that label. Model key for a typical cereal label: 1) the serving size (e.g., 1 cup); 2) calories per serving (e.g., 140); 3) grams of sugar per serving (e.g., 12 g); 4) grams of fiber (e.g., 3 g); 5) the first ingredient = the ingredient it contains most of (e.g., whole grain oats). Scoring: 5/5 proficient, 4/5 approaching, 3 or fewer reteach how to locate each line.
