# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 26

## ELA 3: The Wonderful Wizard of Oz, chapters 7–12

**Prepare:** The Wonderful Wizard of Oz chapters 7–12 with Denslow illustrations (Gutenberg's illustrated edition or a library copy). Write the possessives quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Lion is a coward because he says so — his actions show bravery.
- Putting the apostrophe in the wrong place for plurals (witche's) — plural possessive: add the apostrophe after the s (the witches' castle).

### Day 1 (Monday): Chapters 7–9
*Student:* Read chapters 7–9 and describe the Cowardly Lion's brave actions.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 7–9. The child lists each brave thing the Lion does, even though he says he is afraid.

- **Q:** What brave thing does the Lion do in chapter 7?
  **A:** He leaps across a deep ditch carrying each friend on his back, and he stands up to the fierce Kalidahs.
- **Q:** How is the Lion rescued from the poppy field?
  **A:** The Queen of the Field Mice's subjects pull him out on a wooden truck the Tin Woodman builds.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Illustrations
*Student:* Study 3 Denslow illustrations and explain what they show about mood and character.

*How to teach:* Look closely at 3 Denslow illustrations. For each, ask what mood it shows and what it tells about a character that the words don't. The child writes a caption for each.

- **Q:** What can illustrations add to a story?
  **A:** Details of how characters look and feel and the mood of a scene.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Possessives
*Student:* Write singular and plural possessives (Dorothy's shoes, the witches' castle); spell soft c and g words.

*How to teach:* Teach singular possessives ('s) and plural possessives (s'). The child writes 8 possessives about the book. Then spell soft c (city) and soft g (giant) words.

- **Q:** Write the possessive: the shoes of Dorothy.
  **A:** Dorothy's shoes.
- **Q:** Write the possessive: the castle of the witches.
  **A:** the witches' castle.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Chapters 10–12
*Student:* Read chapters 10–12 and chart each character's wish and the Wizard's demand.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 10–12. The child charts each character's wish and what the Wizard demands.

- **Q:** What does Oz demand before granting their wishes?
  **A:** That they kill the Wicked Witch of the West.
- **Q:** How is the Wicked Witch of the West destroyed?
  **A:** Dorothy angrily throws a bucket of water on her, and she melts away.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a possessives quiz and write a character trait paragraph.

*How to teach:* Give the possessives quiz and have the child write a paragraph about one character's trait with 2 examples from the text. Have the child reread the chapter to find examples before writing.

- **Q:** Why does everything look green in the Emerald City?
  **A:** The Guardian of the Gates makes everyone wear green spectacles.

**Assessment:** Possessives quiz; character trait paragraph

**Answer key:** Possessives: Dorothy's shoes, the Lion's mane, the witches' castle, the Munchkins' houses, Toto's bark, the children's books, the Scarecrow's brains, the mice's queen — proficient 7+/8. Trait paragraph: 3 = trait + 2 text examples; 2 = trait + 1 example; 1 = no evidence.

## Math 3: Time to the minute and elapsed time

**Prepare:** Analog clock with movable hands (or a paper-plate clock), open number line paper. Write the time quiz in the answer key.

**Watch for:**
- Reading the hour hand wrong near the next hour (saying 3:55 is 4:55) — the hour hand points to the hour it has most recently passed.
- Treating an hour like 100 minutes (2:50 to 3:10 = 60 minutes) — jump to the next hour on a number line: 10 + 10 = 20 minutes.

### Day 1 (Monday): Nearest minute
*Student:* Read and set an analog clock to the nearest minute for 15 times.

*How to teach:* Count by 5s around the clock then add ones (7:23 is 4 fives + 3 past 7). The child reads and sets 15 times.

- **Q:** The hour hand is just past 4; the minute hand is 2 marks past the 7. What time is it?
  **A:** 4:37.
- **Q:** Show 9:52. Where is the hour hand?
  **A:** Almost at the 10 (between 9 and 10).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Elapsed on a number line
*Student:* Use an open number line to find elapsed time between start and end times.

*How to teach:* Use an open number line: 2:45 → 3:00 is 15 min, 3:00 → 3:20 is 20 min; total 35 min. The child solves 8 problems.

- **Q:** How long from 2:45 to 3:20?
  **A:** 35 minutes.
- **Q:** How long from 10:15 to 11:05?
  **A:** 50 minutes.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Start and end unknown
*Student:* Solve problems where the start time or end time is unknown.

*How to teach:* For an unknown end time, jump forward; for an unknown start time, jump backward. Model one of each on the number line, then the child solves 6 problems, checking each by counting forward from the start time.

- **Q:** Practice started at 4:20 and lasted 45 minutes. When did it end?
  **A:** 5:05.
- **Q:** A show ended at 7:10 after 50 minutes. When did it start?
  **A:** 6:20.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Daily schedule
*Student:* Record your day's schedule and calculate the length of 5 activities.

*How to teach:* The child records the start and end times of 5 activities in their day and finds the length of each. Have the child draw a number line for any activity that crosses the hour.

- **Q:** Lunch was 12:05 to 12:40. How long?
  **A:** 35 minutes.
- **Q:** Reading was 1:50 to 2:25. How long?
  **A:** 35 minutes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a time and elapsed-time quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the time quiz. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** How many minutes are in an hour?
  **A:** 60.

**Assessment:** Time quiz

**Answer key:** 1) Read a clock showing 3:47 2) Read 11:08 3) Draw hands for 6:34 (hour hand a bit past halfway between 6 and 7) 4) 8:15 to 8:50 → 35 min 5) 1:40 to 2:15 → 35 min 6) Start 9:25, lasts 40 min → ends 10:05 7) Ends 4:00 after 25 min → started 3:35 8) 10:50 to 11:30 → 40 min. Proficient: 7+/8.

## Science 3: Collecting weather data

**Prepare:** The homemade instruments, the weather log, the NOAA SciJinks cloud page, and the National Weather Service forecast for your town (weather.gov).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all clouds bring rain — puffy white cumulus often mean fair weather; dark, thick clouds bring rain.
- Recording data at different times each day — measure at the same time daily so data can be compared.

### Day 1 (Monday): Clouds
*Student:* Learn cumulus, stratus and cirrus clouds using SciJinks, then identify today's clouds and sketch them in the log.

*How to teach:* Learn the three main cloud types on SciJinks. Go outside, identify today's clouds and sketch them in the log.

- **Q:** Describe cumulus, stratus and cirrus clouds.
  **A:** Cumulus: puffy, white, like cotton balls. Stratus: flat gray layers covering the sky. Cirrus: thin, wispy, high clouds made of ice crystals.
- **Q:** Which cloud type often brings drizzle or gray days?
  **A:** Stratus.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Daily data
*Student:* Record temperature, rainfall, wind direction and cloud type at the same time each day.

*How to teach:* Record temperature, rainfall, wind direction and cloud type at the same time. Empty the rain gauge after reading.

- **Q:** Why record at the same time each day?
  **A:** So the data is a fair comparison from day to day.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Forecasts
*Student:* Compare your observations to the NOAA/National Weather Service forecast for your town and note matches.

*How to teach:* Look up the National Weather Service forecast and compare it to the child's observations; mark matches and differences.

- **Q:** Why might your thermometer read differently from the forecast?
  **A:** Location — sun, shade or nearby buildings — and forecasts are predictions for a whole area.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Data table
*Student:* Organize the week's measurements into a data table with units and calculate the week's high and low.

*How to teach:* Organize the week's data into a table with units and find the highest and lowest temperatures.

- **Q:** Temperatures: 62, 65, 70, 68, 59 °F. High and low?
  **A:** High 70 °F, low 59 °F.
- **Q:** What is the difference between them?
  **A:** 11 degrees.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Describe the week's weather pattern in 3 sentences using numbers from your table.

*How to teach:* The child writes 3 sentences describing the week's pattern using numbers.

- **Q:** Write a model sentence.
  **A:** The temperature ranged from 59 °F to 70 °F, and it rained 0.5 inches on Tuesday.

**Assessment:** Weather log and pattern description

**Answer key:** Weather log: proficient if 5 days of data are recorded at a consistent time with units. Pattern description: 3 = three sentences with specific numbers and a trend; 2 = general description; 1 = no data used.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Local and state government

**Prepare:** Your city website (mayor/council/city manager) and the California State Capitol or Governor's website. A recorded city council meeting (most cities post videos); preview a 15-minute segment.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the governor runs cities. Cities have their own governments (mayor, council); the governor leads the state.
- Thinking services are free. Taxes pay for police, fire, parks and libraries.

### Day 1 (Monday): City government
*Student:* Look up your mayor or city manager and council members on the city website and list their jobs.

*How to teach:* Look up the mayor or city manager and council members and list their jobs.

- **Q:** Who leads your city?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; the mayor (or city manager) named on the city website.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Local services
*Student:* List services local government provides (police, fire, parks, libraries) and who pays for them.

*How to teach:* List local services and who pays (taxes).

- **Q:** Who pays for local services like parks and fire departments?
  **A:** The people, through taxes (like property and sales taxes).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): State government
*Student:* Learn about the governor, the legislature and the State Capitol in Sacramento.

*How to teach:* Learn about the governor, the legislature and the Capitol in Sacramento.

- **Q:** What is California's capital city?
  **A:** Sacramento.
- **Q:** What are the two houses of California's legislature?
  **A:** The State Senate (40 members) and the State Assembly (80 members).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Council meeting
*Student:* Watch 15 minutes of a recorded city council meeting and note one decision made.

*How to teach:* Watch 15 minutes of a council meeting; note one decision.

- **Q:** What decision did the council make?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; answer describes a decision from the meeting watched.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Make a comparison chart of local, state and national government.

*How to teach:* Make the comparison chart.

- **Q:** Who leads the state of California?
  **A:** The Governor.

**Assessment:** Government comparison chart

**Answer key:** Government comparison chart key: Local — leader: mayor or city manager; lawmakers: city council; services: police, fire, parks, libraries, streets, trash. State — leader: Governor; lawmakers: California State Legislature (Senate with 40 members and Assembly with 80) meeting at the State Capitol in Sacramento; services: state highways, state parks, education rules, DMV. National — leader: President; lawmakers: Congress in Washington, D.C.; services: military, mail, money, national parks. Proficient: all three levels with correct leader, lawmakers, location and 2 services.

## Art & Music 3: Assemblage sculpture

**Prepare:** Images of Louise Nevelson's wall assemblages (e.g., 'Sky Cathedral,' MoMA). Recycled materials: boxes, tubes, lids, bottle caps, egg cartons; masking tape, strong glue or hot glue (adult only); one color of tempera or spray paint (adult applies spray outdoors).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking sculpture must be carved from stone or wood. Assemblage builds a sculpture by joining found objects.
- Building tall without a wide base. Put the heaviest, widest pieces at the bottom so the sculpture balances.

### Day 1 (Monday): Nevelson
*Student:* Look at Louise Nevelson's assemblage sculptures and discuss how painting them one color unifies them.

*How to teach:* Look at Nevelson's assemblages: found wooden objects arranged in boxes and painted all one color (often black). Ask how one color changes what we notice.

- **Q:** Why did Nevelson paint her sculptures all one color?
  **A:** It unifies the different objects so we see them as one artwork and focus on shapes, shadows and patterns instead of what each object was.
- **Q:** What is an assemblage?
  **A:** An artwork made by putting together found or recycled objects.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plan
*Student:* Collect recycled materials and sketch a plan for a free-standing sculpture.

*How to teach:* Collect materials and sketch a plan from at least 2 views (front and side) for a free-standing sculpture.

- **Q:** What does free-standing mean?
  **A:** It stands up on its own and can be seen from all sides.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Build
*Student:* Build the sculpture, testing balance as you add pieces.

*How to teach:* Build the sculpture, testing balance after each piece. Tape first, then glue.

- **Q:** How do you make a tall sculpture stable?
  **A:** Use a wide, heavy base and place lighter pieces higher up; balance weight on both sides.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Paint
*Student:* Paint the whole sculpture one color to unify it.

*How to teach:* Paint the whole sculpture one color, covering every surface.

- **Q:** What does painting it one color do to the bottle caps and boxes?
  **A:** They blend together, so you notice the shapes, patterns, light and shadow.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Present the sculpture and view it from all sides with family.

*How to teach:* Turn the sculpture slowly as family views it from all sides. The child describes their favorite view and how they balanced it.

- **Q:** Why should a sculpture be viewed from all sides?
  **A:** It is 3D, so it looks different from each angle.

**Assessment:** Assemblage sculpture

**Answer key:** Assemblage sculpture rubric: 4 = free-standing sculpture of 8+ recycled objects, stable without support, painted completely in one color, interesting from all sides; child explains unity and balance; 3 = stands with minor wobble or a few unpainted spots; 2 = needs support or few objects; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 3: Line dances

**Prepare:** Preview recordings of the 'Cha-Cha Slide' and an Electric Slide song (radio edits are fine; check content). Clear space with room to step 4 steps in each direction. Timer and heart-rate log.

**Watch for:**
- Crossing in front in the grapevine. The trailing foot crosses behind the leading foot.
- Turning the wrong way in a line dance. In the Electric Slide, the quarter turn goes to the left so the group faces a new wall each time.

### Day 1 (Monday): Grapevine
*Student:* Learn the grapevine step right and left in 8 counts.

*How to teach:* Teach the grapevine to the right: step right to the side (1), left foot crosses behind (2), step right to the side (3), touch left beside right (4); then reverse to the left (5–8). Practice slowly, then to music.

- **Q:** In a grapevine to the right, where does the left foot cross?
  **A:** Behind the right foot.
- **Q:** How many counts is a grapevine right and left?
  **A:** 8 counts (4 right, 4 left).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cha-Cha Slide
*Student:* Learn and dance the Cha-Cha Slide.

*How to teach:* Play the Cha-Cha Slide; the song calls out each move, so the child listens and responds (slides, hops, stomps, cha-cha). Practice the moves without music first, then with it.

- **Q:** Why is the Cha-Cha Slide easy to learn?
  **A:** The song tells you which move to do next, so you just listen and follow the calls.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Electric Slide
*Student:* Learn and dance the Electric Slide.

*How to teach:* Teach the Electric Slide pattern: grapevine right, grapevine left, walk back 3 and touch, rock forward and back, then a quarter turn to the left and repeat facing the new wall.

- **Q:** After one full pattern, which way do you face?
  **A:** A new wall, a quarter turn to the left.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Heart rate
*Student:* Dance 3 songs in a row, then measure heart rate and compare to rest.

*How to teach:* Take resting heart rate. Dance 3 songs in a row, then immediately take a 15-second pulse × 4 and compare.

- **Q:** Resting 84 bpm; after dancing you count 33 in 15 seconds. What is your heart rate and how much did it rise?
  **A:** 33 × 4 = 132 bpm; 132 − 84 = 48 bpm higher.
- **Q:** Is line dancing exercise? How do you know?
  **A:** Yes — your heart rate and breathing go up, which works your heart and lungs.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Perform a full line dance without cues.

*How to teach:* The child performs the Electric Slide (or Cha-Cha Slide) start to finish without you calling the steps.

- **Q:** What is a line dance?
  **A:** A dance where people stand in lines and do the same steps at the same time, often turning to face new walls.

**Assessment:** Line dance performance

**Answer key:** Line dance performance rubric: 4 = performs a full line dance with correct steps in order, on the beat, with correct turns and no cues; 3 = one or two small errors, recovers quickly; 2 = needs some cues; 1 = needs cues throughout.

## Health 3: Alcohol

**Prepare:** A simple brain diagram (cerebrum, cerebellum, brain stem). Clear space and an adult spotter for the spinning activity (soft surface, stop if the child feels sick). Parent note: if alcohol use is part of family life, explain that adults can choose to drink responsibly but it is harmful to kids.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking alcohol is a stimulant that makes people more active. Alcohol is a depressant; it slows the brain and body.
- Thinking a little alcohol is fine for kids. Growing brains are especially sensitive; it's illegal and harmful under 21.

### Day 1 (Monday): Effects
*Student:* Learn how alcohol slows the brain, affecting judgment, balance and reaction time.

*How to teach:* Explain that alcohol is a depressant drug that slows messages in the brain, affecting judgment, balance, speech and reaction time. Point to brain areas.

- **Q:** Does alcohol speed up or slow down the brain?
  **A:** It slows it down; alcohol is a depressant.
- **Q:** Name two things alcohol affects.
  **A:** Judgment, balance, coordination, speech, memory, reaction time.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Coordination
*Student:* Try walking a line while spinning in place (with an adult) to model impaired balance, then discuss.

*How to teach:* With an adult spotter, the child spins slowly 5 times then tries to walk a straight line. Discuss how alcohol similarly affects balance.

- **Q:** Why was it hard to walk the line?
  **A:** Spinning confused the balance system; alcohol also impairs balance and coordination.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Growing bodies
*Student:* Discuss why alcohol is especially harmful to growing brains and why the legal age is 21.

*How to teach:* Explain the brain keeps developing until the mid-20s and alcohol can harm it. The legal age is 21 to protect young people.

- **Q:** Why is alcohol especially harmful for kids?
  **A:** Their brains and bodies are still growing, and alcohol can damage development and affect learning and memory.
- **Q:** What is the legal drinking age in the U.S.?
  **A:** 21.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Influences
*Student:* Identify alcohol messages in ads and media.

*How to teach:* Look at ads or TV scenes showing alcohol. Discuss what message they send (fun, friends, relaxing) and what they leave out.

- **Q:** What do alcohol ads usually leave out?
  **A:** The harmful effects: accidents, poor decisions, illness and addiction.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Answer 6 questions about alcohol's effects.

*How to teach:* Give the 6-question alcohol quiz.

- **Q:** Why is it dangerous to drink and drive?
  **A:** Alcohol slows reaction time and impairs judgment and coordination, causing crashes.

**Assessment:** Alcohol questions

**Answer key:** Alcohol quiz key: 1) Alcohol is a depressant; it slows the brain. 2) It harms judgment (decision making). 3) It harms balance and coordination. 4) It slows reaction time, which is why drinking and driving is dangerous. 5) The legal age to buy or drink alcohol in the U.S. is 21. 6) It is especially harmful to kids because their brains are still growing. Proficient: 5–6 correct.
