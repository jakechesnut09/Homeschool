# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 16

## ELA 3: Myths: Persephone and Icarus

**Prepare:** Borrow Persephone and Icarus retellings (e.g., D'Aulaires' Book of Greek Myths). Find two different artworks of Icarus online (e.g., Bruegel's 'Landscape with the Fall of Icarus' and Herbert Draper's 'The Lament for Icarus' — check that images are suitable).

**Watch for:**
- Taking idioms literally — 'flying too close to the sun' means being overconfident or taking a dangerous risk.
- Mixing up their/there/they're — their = belongs to them; there = a place; they're = they are.

### Day 1 (Monday): Persephone
*Student:* Read the Persephone myth and explain how it explained the seasons.

*How to teach:* Read the Persephone myth. Ask the child to explain step by step how the story explains the seasons, using a simple circle diagram of the year.

- **Q:** How does the myth explain winter?
  **A:** When Persephone is in the Underworld with Hades, her mother Demeter, goddess of the harvest, grieves and plants stop growing.
- **Q:** Why must Persephone return to the Underworld each year?
  **A:** She ate pomegranate seeds in the Underworld, so she must spend part of each year there.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Illustrations
*Student:* Compare two illustrations of Icarus and explain how each shows mood.

*How to teach:* Show the two Icarus artworks. Ask what the child notices about colors, light, faces and where Icarus is placed, and how each makes them feel. The child writes 2 sentences comparing the moods.

- **Q:** What details show mood in a picture?
  **A:** Colors, light and dark, facial expressions and where figures are placed (Bruegel shows Icarus tiny in the corner while life goes on — calm and ironic; Draper shows sorrowful faces — sad).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Icarus
*Student:* Read the Icarus myth and discuss nonliteral phrases like 'flying too close to the sun.'

*How to teach:* Read the Icarus myth. Ask what Daedalus warned and why Icarus ignored him. Discuss what 'flying too close to the sun' means when people say it today.

- **Q:** What happened to Icarus?
  **A:** He flew too close to the sun, the wax holding his feathers melted, and he fell into the sea.
- **Q:** What does 'flying too close to the sun' mean today?
  **A:** Being overconfident or taking a dangerous risk.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Capitalize titles
*Student:* Write the titles of all myths and books read so far with correct capitalization; spell homophones (their/there/they're).

*How to teach:* Teach title capitalization: capitalize the first, last and important words; leave short words (a, the, and, of) lowercase unless first. The child writes every title read this year correctly. Then practice their/there/they're.

- **Q:** Capitalize: charlotte's web
  **A:** Charlotte's Web.
- **Q:** Choose: (There/Their/They're) going to the fair.
  **A:** They're.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Answer questions about both myths and listen to a family member's retelling, asking 3 questions.

*How to teach:* Ask the myth questions in the key. Then a family member retells either myth; the child listens and asks 3 thoughtful questions.

- **Q:** Who warned Icarus not to fly too high?
  **A:** His father, Daedalus.

**Assessment:** Title capitalization check; myth questions

**Answer key:** Titles (5): Charlotte's Web; The Wonderful Wizard of Oz; The Golden Touch; The Tortoise and the Hare; The Lion and the Mouse — proficient 4+/5. Myth questions: 1) What does Persephone's story explain → the seasons 2) Who is Persephone's mother → Demeter 3) Why must she return → she ate pomegranate seeds 4) What warning did Icarus ignore → don't fly too high (or too low) 5) Lesson → listen to wise advice; don't be overconfident. Proficient 4+/5.

## Math 3: Area and multiplication

**Prepare:** Square tiles, ruler, grid paper. Write the 8 area problems in the answer key for Friday.

**Watch for:**
- Adding side lengths instead of multiplying (3 + 5 = 8 for area) — tile the rectangle to show 3 rows of 5 = 15.
- Forgetting square units — area is labeled in square units (sq ft), not feet.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tile and count
*Student:* Tile rectangles and show that rows × columns gives the same total as counting.

*How to teach:* Tile a 4 × 6 rectangle and count every tile, then point out 4 rows of 6 = 4 × 6 = 24. The child tiles 4 rectangles and writes both methods.

- **Q:** A rectangle has 4 rows of 6 tiles. What is its area?
  **A:** 24 square units.
- **Q:** Why does multiplying work for area?
  **A:** The tiles form an array: rows × tiles in each row = total tiles.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Multiply sides
*Student:* Measure side lengths of rectangles drawn on grid paper and multiply to find area.

*How to teach:* Measure side lengths of grid-paper rectangles with a ruler and multiply. The child finds 8 areas with units.

- **Q:** A rectangle is 7 cm by 3 cm. What is its area?
  **A:** 21 square centimeters.
- **Q:** A square has 9-inch sides. What is its area?
  **A:** 81 square inches.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Unknown side
*Student:* Solve problems like 'A rug has area 24 sq ft and width 4 ft; how long is it?'

*How to teach:* Use an unknown-factor equation: 4 × ? = 24. Draw the rectangle with one side marked '?'.

- **Q:** A rug has area 24 sq ft and width 4 ft. How long is it?
  **A:** 6 feet.
- **Q:** A garden has area 45 sq m and length 9 m. What is its width?
  **A:** 5 meters.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Distributive area
*Student:* Split a 7 × 8 rectangle into 7 × 5 and 7 × 3 and add the areas.

*How to teach:* Draw a 7 × 8 rectangle and split it into 7 × 5 and 7 × 3; find 35 + 21 = 56. Connect this to the distributive property.

- **Q:** Find 7 × 8 by splitting the rectangle.
  **A:** 7 × 5 + 7 × 3 = 35 + 21 = 56 square units.
- **Q:** Split 6 × 9 into two rectangles.
  **A:** 6 × 5 + 6 × 4 = 30 + 24 = 54.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete 8 area problems using multiplication and the distributive property.

*How to teach:* Give the 8 area problems. Read directions aloud if needed but give no hints. Afterward, score it with the answer key and have the child correct each missed item, explaining the fix to you.

- **Q:** What is the area of an 8 by 4 rectangle?
  **A:** 32 square units.

**Assessment:** Area problems worksheet

**Answer key:** 1) 5 × 6 → 30 sq units 2) 8 cm × 3 cm → 24 sq cm 3) 9 ft × 9 ft → 81 sq ft 4) Area 36, one side 4 → other side 9 5) Area 56, one side 7 → 8 6) 7 × 8 = 7 × 5 + 7 × 3 → 35 + 21 = 56 7) 6 × 7 = 6 × 5 + 6 × 2 → 30 + 12 = 42 8) A 10 m by 4 m patio → 40 sq m. Proficient: 7+/8 with correct units.

## Science 3: Environment and traits

**Prepare:** Two similar potted plants (or bean seedlings), a sunny window and a dark closet, a ruler. Pictures of flamingos and hydrangeas.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all traits come only from parents — the environment (food, light, soil) also affects traits.
- Thinking environmental changes are passed to offspring — a flamingo's pink color comes from food, not genes alone.

### Day 1 (Monday): Plant experiment setup
*Student:* Place one plant in sunlight and one in a dark closet; predict differences.

*How to teach:* Measure two similar plants, then put one in the sun and one in a dark closet, watering both equally. The child predicts differences.

- **Q:** What should stay the same between the two plants?
  **A:** Type of plant, pot, soil and amount of water — only light changes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Examples
*Student:* Read about flamingos' color from diet and hydrangea color from soil.

*How to teach:* Read about flamingos and hydrangeas.

- **Q:** Why are flamingos pink?
  **A:** They eat algae and tiny shrimp with pink pigments; without them they turn pale.
- **Q:** How does soil change hydrangea color?
  **A:** In acidic soil many hydrangeas bloom blue; in less acidic (alkaline) soil they bloom pink.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Measure plants
*Student:* Measure and observe plants and record height and color.

*How to teach:* Measure and observe both plants, recording height and color.

- **Q:** How does a plant kept in the dark look after a week?
  **A:** Pale or yellow, tall and spindly, with weak stems.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Human examples
*Student:* Discuss how exercise and diet can influence traits.

*How to teach:* Discuss how exercise and diet affect traits in people (muscle strength, height with good nutrition).

- **Q:** Can exercise change a person's traits?
  **A:** Yes — it can make muscles stronger, though it does not change inherited traits like eye color.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write evidence that environment influences traits from the plant experiment.

*How to teach:* The child writes evidence from the plant experiment that environment affects traits.

- **Q:** What is the evidence?
  **A:** The plant in the dark became pale and spindly while the plant in the sun stayed green and healthy.

**Assessment:** Plant experiment report

**Answer key:** Plant experiment report: 4 = question, prediction, controlled variables, data (heights and colors), conclusion that light (environment) affects traits; 3 (proficient) = data and correct conclusion; 2 = observations only; 1 = incomplete.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Why our community was founded

**Prepare:** Your local historical society website, a historic map of your town (Sanborn map at the Library of Congress or a historic USGS topo map from TopoView) and a current map. Research your town's founding year and reason.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking towns appear randomly. Most began where there was water, a harbor, a railroad stop, good farmland or a mine.

### Day 1 (Monday): Founding
*Student:* Research when and why your town was founded (railroad, farming, port, mining) using the historical society website.

*How to teach:* Research when and why your town was founded.

- **Q:** Why was your town founded where it is?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; e.g., a railroad stop, near a river for water, a harbor for ships, farmland or a mine.
- **Q:** Why were many towns founded along railroads?
  **A:** Railroads brought people and supplies and let farmers and businesses ship goods.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Founders
*Student:* Read about 2 founding individuals or families and their contributions.

*How to teach:* Read about 2 founders and their contributions.

- **Q:** What did one founder contribute?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; answer names a person/family and a specific contribution (built a store, mill, church, laid out streets).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Old maps
*Student:* Compare a historic map of your town (Sanborn fire insurance map or USGS topo) with a current map.

*How to teach:* Compare a historic and current map; list 3 changes.

- **Q:** Name one change between the old and new maps.
  **A:** Answers vary by community; e.g., more streets, a freeway, a creek channeled, farms turned into neighborhoods.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Timeline
*Student:* Begin a community timeline with founding events and dates.

*How to teach:* Start a community timeline with founding events.

- **Q:** What goes on a timeline?
  **A:** Events placed in order with their dates.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining why your community began where it did.

*How to teach:* The child writes the founding paragraph.

- **Q:** What geographic feature helped your town grow?
  **A:** Answers vary by community; e.g., a river, harbor, flat valley land or a mountain pass.

**Assessment:** Founding paragraph

**Answer key:** Founding paragraph rubric: 4 = gives the founding year, the main reason (e.g., railroad, farming, port, mining), how geography helped (river, harbor, flat land), and names a founder or founding group, citing the historical society; 3 = reason and year without geography; 2 = vague; 1 = inaccurate.

## Art & Music 3: Singing expressively

**Prepare:** Song sheet for 'This Land Is Your Land' (Woody Guthrie; use a lyric sheet you own or a children's songbook — don't need to print the whole text) and a lullaby and a march. Two different recorded versions of the same song (e.g., a choir version and a solo folk version).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking crescendo means 'loud.' Crescendo means gradually getting louder; decrescendo (diminuendo) means gradually getting softer.
- Singing every song the same way. Expression (dynamics, tempo, phrasing) should match the words and purpose of the song.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dynamics
*Student:* Sing 'This Land Is Your Land' with forte, piano, crescendo and decrescendo.

*How to teach:* Teach forte, piano, crescendo (<, getting louder) and decrescendo (>, getting softer). Sing the song with you signaling each with your hands.

- **Q:** What does a crescendo sign look like and mean?
  **A:** An opening wedge (<); gradually get louder.
- **Q:** What is the opposite of crescendo?
  **A:** Decrescendo (or diminuendo) — gradually getting softer.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tempo
*Student:* Sing a lullaby at a slow tempo and a march at a fast one and discuss why.

*How to teach:* Sing a lullaby slowly and softly and a march quickly and strongly. Discuss why each tempo fits its purpose.

- **Q:** Why is a lullaby slow and soft?
  **A:** It is meant to calm a baby to sleep.
- **Q:** Why is a march fast and steady?
  **A:** It is for walking or marching together in step.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compare performances
*Student:* Compare two recorded versions of the same song and describe the differences in expression.

*How to teach:* Listen to two recordings of the same song. Make a T-chart comparing tempo, dynamics, instruments and voices.

- **Q:** Name one difference you heard between the two versions.
  **A:** Answers vary, e.g., 'The choir version was louder and had piano; the folk version was slower with just a guitar.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Expression plan
*Student:* Mark dynamics on a song sheet to match the meaning of its words.

*How to teach:* Mark dynamics (p, f, <, >) on the song sheet to match the meaning of the words; e.g., softer for quiet images, louder for big ideas.

- **Q:** Where would you put a crescendo, and why?
  **A:** Answers vary but should connect to the words, e.g., building up toward a big, important line.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Perform the song with your marked expression for family.

*How to teach:* Perform the song for family using the marked expression.

- **Q:** What makes a performance expressive?
  **A:** Using dynamics, tempo and phrasing that match the meaning and feeling of the song.

**Assessment:** Expressive performance

**Answer key:** Expressive performance rubric: 4 = sings with accurate words and pitch and clearly follows at least 3 marked expressions (p, f, crescendo, decrescendo) that fit the words; 3 = follows 2 markings; 2 = mostly one dynamic level; 1 = does not complete performance.

## PE 3: Dribbling with changes

**Prepare:** Ball and 6–8 cones set in a zigzag (cones alternating about 4 feet left and right). Agree on 'red light/green light' signals.

**Watch for:**
- Crossing over high. A crossover bounce should be low (below the knee) and quick so a defender can't steal it.
- Stopping the dribble to tag in dribble tag. Players must keep dribbling the whole time; if you pick up the ball you must restart.

### Day 1 (Monday): Crossover
*Student:* Learn a low crossover dribble from hand to hand and practice 3 sets of 20.

*How to teach:* Teach the crossover: dribble low with the right hand, push the ball in a V-shape across the body to the left hand, bounce below the knee. Practice 3 sets of 20.

- **Q:** Where should the ball bounce in a crossover?
  **A:** Low, in front of your body (a V shape), below the knees.
- **Q:** Why keep the crossover low?
  **A:** A low, quick bounce is harder for a defender to steal.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Zigzag
*Student:* Dribble a zigzag cone pattern, switching hands at each cone.

*How to teach:* Dribble through the zigzag cones, switching to the outside hand (the hand farther from the cone) at each one.

- **Q:** If a cone is on your left, which hand should you dribble with?
  **A:** Your right hand — the hand away from the cone or defender, protecting the ball.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Speed
*Student:* Change dribbling speed when a family member calls 'red light' or 'green light.'

*How to teach:* Play red light/green light: green = speed dribble, yellow = slow, red = stop and control the ball dribbling in place (or hold it). Practice quick changes.

- **Q:** How do you dribble fast?
  **A:** Push the ball farther ahead and a bit higher, and run.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Dribble tag
*Student:* Play dribble tag, keeping your dribble while tagging and dodging.

*How to teach:* Play dribble tag in a small area: everyone dribbles; the tagger tries to tag others while dribbling. Lost balls must be recovered before playing on.

- **Q:** In dribble tag, how do you protect your ball from a tagger?
  **A:** Keep your body between the tagger and the ball and dribble with the hand away from them.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Complete a dribble skills check: crossover, zigzag and speed change.

*How to teach:* Skills check: 10 crossovers in place, one zigzag pass through the cones switching hands, and a speed change on call.

- **Q:** Which hand do you use after a crossover from right to left?
  **A:** The left hand.

**Assessment:** Dribble skills check

**Answer key:** Dribble skills check (1 point each, 6 total): crossover low and controlled (1) for 10 in a row (1); zigzag with hand switches (1) and no loss of control (1); speed change on 'red/green' (1) with control maintained (1). 4 = 6; 3 = 5; 2 = 3–4; 1 = 0–2.

## Health 3: Bullying and upstanders

**Prepare:** Read the KidsHealth (kidshealth.org/kids) article on bullying ahead of time. Write scenario cards: 2 physical, 2 verbal, 2 social, 2 cyberbullying, plus 2 ordinary conflicts. Parent note: ask gently whether the child has seen or experienced bullying, and follow up if so.

**Watch for:**
- Calling every disagreement bullying. Bullying is repeated, on purpose, and involves a power imbalance; a one-time argument between equals is conflict.
- Thinking telling an adult is 'tattling.' Tattling is to get someone in trouble; reporting is to keep someone safe.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is bullying
*Student:* Read the KidsHealth article on bullying and distinguish bullying (repeated, power imbalance) from ordinary conflict.

*How to teach:* Read the KidsHealth article together. Make a T-chart: bullying (repeated, on purpose, power imbalance) vs. conflict (disagreement between equals).

- **Q:** What three things make something bullying?
  **A:** It is on purpose, it happens repeatedly, and there is a power imbalance (one person has more power).
- **Q:** Two friends argue once about a game rule. Is that bullying?
  **A:** No, that's a conflict between equals, not repeated or a power imbalance.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Types
*Student:* Sort scenario cards into physical, verbal, social and cyberbullying.

*How to teach:* The child sorts scenario cards into physical, verbal, social and cyberbullying.

- **Q:** Spreading rumors and leaving someone out on purpose is what type of bullying?
  **A:** Social (relational) bullying.
- **Q:** Sending mean messages in a game chat over and over is what type?
  **A:** Cyberbullying.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Upstander
*Student:* Practice 4 upstander responses (speak up, stay with the target, walk away together, get help).

*How to teach:* Practice the 4 upstander moves with role-play: speak up ('Stop, that's not okay'), stay with the target, walk away together, get help.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a bystander and an upstander?
  **A:** A bystander sees bullying and does nothing; an upstander takes safe action to help.
- **Q:** Name a safe upstander action that doesn't involve confronting the bully.
  **A:** Walk away with the target, be kind to them later, or tell a trusted adult.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Reporting
*Student:* Practice telling a trusted adult the who, what, where and when of a bullying incident.

*How to teach:* The child practices reporting to you: who, what, where, when.

- **Q:** What's the difference between tattling and reporting?
  **A:** Tattling tries to get someone in trouble; reporting is to help someone who is hurt or in danger.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write and sign an upstander pledge.

*How to teach:* The child writes and signs an upstander pledge and posts it.

- **Q:** What will you do if you see someone bullied online?
  **A:** Don't join in or share it, save or screenshot it, and tell a trusted adult.

**Assessment:** Upstander pledge

**Answer key:** Upstander pledge rubric: 4 = pledge names at least 3 specific actions (speak up, stay with the target, walk away together, get help from an adult, include the target later), is in the child's own words and signed; 3 = 2 actions; 2 = general 'I'll be nice'; 1 = incomplete.
