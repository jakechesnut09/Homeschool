# Teacher's Guide — 3rd Grade, Week 7

## ELA 3: Charlotte's Web, chapters 11–15

**Prepare:** Charlotte's Web chapters 11–15, a dictionary, irregular verb cards (spin/spun, write/wrote, catch/caught, see/saw, bring/brought).

**Watch for:**
- Adding -ed to irregular verbs (spinned, writed, catched) — practice the irregular forms aloud in sentences.
- Guessing word meanings without context — reread the sentence before and after and substitute your guess.

### Day 1 (Monday): Chapters 11–12
*Student:* Read chapters 11–12 and discuss why the web words ('Some Pig') work; list strong words Charlotte chooses.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 11–12. Discuss why 'Some Pig' works and list strong words Charlotte chooses.

- **Q:** What words appear in the web first, and who sees them?
  **A:** 'SOME PIG' — Lurvy sees them first.
- **Q:** Why does Charlotte call a meeting in chapter 12?
  **A:** To ask the animals for new words to write; she needs more ideas.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Irregular verbs
*Student:* Practice irregular past tense verbs (spin/spun, write/wrote, catch/caught) using sentences about the book; spell -ly and -ness words.

*How to teach:* Practice irregular past forms using book sentences ('Charlotte spun a web'). Spell -ly and -ness words.

- **Q:** Past tense of write and catch?
  **A:** wrote, caught.
- **Q:** What does 'kindness' mean?
  **A:** The quality of being kind.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Chapters 13–14
*Student:* Read chapters 13–14 and figure out the meaning of 5 tricky words from context.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 13–14. The child finds 5 tricky words (e.g., 'versatile', 'radiant') and uses context to define them.

- **Q:** What word does Charlotte write next, and how does Templeton help?
  **A:** 'Terrific'; Templeton brings back words from magazine and newspaper scraps at the dump.
- **Q:** What does Dr. Dorian tell Mrs. Arable?
  **A:** That Fern is fine, and the web itself is a kind of miracle.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Draft story
*Student:* Draft the beginning and middle of your animal story with at least 2 lines of dialogue.

*How to teach:* The child drafts the beginning and middle of the animal story with at least 2 lines of dialogue. Remind the child to start a new line for each new speaker and to use quotation marks.

- **Q:** Fix punctuation: Wilbur said I am scared
  **A:** Wilbur said, "I am scared."

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take an irregular verbs quiz and answer comprehension questions on chapters 11–15.

*How to teach:* Give the irregular verb quiz and comprehension questions on chapters 11–15. Have the child find the page that proves any missed comprehension answer.

- **Q:** What word does Charlotte write after 'Terrific'?
  **A:** Radiant.

**Assessment:** Irregular verbs quiz; comprehension questions

**Answer key:** Irregular verbs: spin–spun, write–wrote, catch–caught, see–saw, bring–brought, go–went, eat–ate, run–ran, think–thought, find–found (proficient 8+/10). Comprehension: 1) First web words → Some Pig 2) Who fetches words? → Templeton 3) Second word → Terrific 4) What did Dr. Dorian say → Fern is fine; the web is a kind of miracle 5) Word after Terrific → Radiant. Proficient 4+/5.

## Math 3: Arrays and the commutative property

**Prepare:** Square tiles (or paper squares), grid paper, a muffin tin and an egg carton for the array hunt. Write the quiz in the answer key.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up rows and columns — rows go across (like rows of seats), columns go up and down; label them on the first array.
- Thinking 3 × 6 and 6 × 3 give different answers — rotate the same tile array to show the total stays 18.

### Day 1 (Monday): Build arrays
*Student:* Build arrays with tiles for 10 facts and label rows × columns.

*How to teach:* Build a 3 × 5 array of tiles and say '3 rows, 5 in each row, 3 × 5 = 15.' The child builds 10 arrays and labels rows × columns.

- **Q:** Draw an array for 4 × 6. How many in all?
  **A:** 4 rows of 6 dots = 24.
- **Q:** An array has 2 rows of 8. Write the equation.
  **A:** 2 × 8 = 16.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Turn it around
*Student:* Rotate arrays to show 3 × 6 = 6 × 3 and record turnaround facts.

*How to teach:* Draw a 3 × 6 array on paper, then turn the paper a quarter turn — now it is 6 rows of 3 and still 18. Introduce the word 'commutative.' The child records 8 turnaround pairs.

- **Q:** If 7 × 4 = 28, what is 4 × 7?
  **A:** 28 — the commutative property says changing the order of factors does not change the product.
- **Q:** Write the turnaround fact for 9 × 2 = 18.
  **A:** 2 × 9 = 18.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Arrays around us
*Student:* Hunt for arrays at home (muffin tin, egg carton) and write two equations for each.

*How to teach:* Hunt the house for arrays (muffin tin, egg carton, ice cube tray, buttons on a remote). For each one, write both equations.

- **Q:** A muffin tin has 3 rows of 4 cups. Write two equations.
  **A:** 3 × 4 = 12 and 4 × 3 = 12.
- **Q:** An ice cube tray has 2 rows of 7. How many cubes?
  **A:** 14.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Array problems
*Student:* Solve array word problems (chairs in rows, garden beds) using grid paper drawings.

*How to teach:* Draw array problems on grid paper by shading rows. The child solves problems about chairs in rows and garden beds.

- **Q:** Chairs are in 5 rows with 6 chairs each. How many chairs?
  **A:** 5 × 6 = 30 chairs.
- **Q:** A garden has 4 rows of 9 carrots. How many carrots?
  **A:** 36.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete an arrays quiz and explain the commutative property using a drawing.

*How to teach:* Give the arrays quiz, then the child draws one array and explains the commutative property in writing. Check that the drawing shows the same array turned, not two different arrays.

- **Q:** Explain why 2 × 5 = 5 × 2 with a drawing.
  **A:** An array of 2 rows of 5, turned sideways, is 5 rows of 2; it is the same 10 dots, so the products are equal.

**Assessment:** Arrays quiz with written explanation

**Answer key:** 1) 3 × 4 = 12 2) 2 × 7 = 14 3) 5 × 5 = 25 4) 4 × 8 = 32 5) If 6 × 3 = 18, then 3 × 6 = 18 6) Draw 2 × 9 (2 rows of 9) = 18 7) 6 rows of 4 seats = 24 8) 3 rows of 10 = 30. Explanation: 3 = drawing shows the rotation and states order doesn't change the product; 2 = correct but no drawing or vague; 1 = incorrect. Proficient: 7+/8 and a 3 on the explanation.

## Science 3: Magnet design problem

**Prepare:** Magnets, paper clips, cardboard, string, tape. Write the Unit 2 quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Skipping criteria and constraints — criteria say what success looks like; constraints are limits (materials, time).
- Building the first idea without comparing options.

### Day 1 (Monday): Define a problem
*Student:* Define a problem (keeping a cabinet door closed or finding lost pins) with criteria and constraints.

*How to teach:* Discuss problems magnets could solve. The child chooses one (a cabinet door that won't stay shut, finding dropped pins) and writes the problem, criteria (what it must do) and constraints (limits).

- **Q:** What is the difference between criteria and constraints?
  **A:** Criteria describe what the design must do to succeed; constraints are limits like materials, cost or time.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Brainstorm
*Student:* Sketch 3 possible magnet solutions and compare them against the criteria.

*How to teach:* The child sketches 3 different magnet solutions and rates each against the criteria in a table, then picks the best.

- **Q:** Why brainstorm more than one solution?
  **A:** Comparing several ideas helps you find the best one.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Build
*Student:* Build the best solution prototype.

*How to teach:* The child builds a prototype of the chosen solution using magnets, cardboard and tape.

- **Q:** What is a prototype?
  **A:** A first model of a design used for testing.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Test
*Student:* Test and evaluate the prototype; record what worked.

*How to teach:* Test the prototype several times and record results. The child notes what worked and what to improve.

- **Q:** If a magnetic latch won't hold the door, what could you change?
  **A:** Use a stronger magnet, add more magnets, or move the magnet closer to the steel part.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 assessment
*Student:* Present the design and complete the unit quiz on magnets and static electricity.

*How to teach:* The child presents the design to the family and takes the Unit 2 quiz.

- **Q:** Why won't a magnet latch work on a wooden door alone?
  **A:** Wood is not magnetic; you need a piece of steel or iron on the door for the magnet to attract.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 quiz; design presentation

**Answer key:** Unit 2 quiz: 1) Which are attracted: nail, penny, paper clip, foil → nail and paper clip 2) N and N → repel 3) N and S → attract 4) Magnetic force through paper? → yes 5) Force as distance increases → weaker 6) Why balloon sticks to wall → static charge attracts 7) Two charged balloons → repel. Proficient 6+/7. Design presentation: 3 = problem, criteria/constraints, test results and an improvement explained.

## Continuity and Change (Local History): Adapting to the environment

**Prepare:** A few acorns (oak trees or a store), Calisphere photographs of grinding stones (mortars and pestles), tule houses, redwood plank houses and baskets. Natural materials (sticks, grass, bark) for a model shelter.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking acorns can be eaten raw. They contain bitter tannins that must be leached out with water.
- Thinking all California Indians lived in tipis. Homes were built from local materials: tule reeds near marshes and lakes, bark or redwood planks in the north.

### Day 1 (Monday): Food
*Student:* Learn how acorns were gathered, leached and ground; look at photos of grinding stones.

*How to teach:* Show the acorn process: gather, dry, crack and shell, grind into flour in a mortar with a pestle, leach with water to remove bitter tannins, then cook into mush or bread. Look at grinding stone photos.

- **Q:** Why did people leach acorn flour with water?
  **A:** To wash out the bitter tannins so it was safe and good to eat.
- **Q:** What tools were used to grind acorns?
  **A:** A stone mortar (or bedrock mortar hole) and a pestle.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Shelter
*Student:* Compare tule, redwood bark and plank houses by region.

*How to teach:* Compare photos of tule houses (marsh areas), redwood bark houses and plank houses (northwest). Discuss why materials differed.

- **Q:** Why did people near marshes build houses from tule?
  **A:** Tule reeds grew plentifully in marshes and lakes, so they were the handy local material.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Clothing and tools
*Student:* Study Calisphere photographs of tools and basketry.

*How to teach:* Study Calisphere photos of tools and baskets: what material, what use.

- **Q:** What were California Indian baskets used for?
  **A:** Gathering, storing and carrying food, cooking (with hot stones), cradles, ceremonies and trade.
- **Q:** What were tools made from?
  **A:** Local materials such as stone (obsidian, chert), bone, wood, shell and plant fibers.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Model
*Student:* Build a model shelter from natural materials.

*How to teach:* The child builds a model shelter from natural materials.

- **Q:** What material did you choose for your model and why?
  **A:** Answer matches a real regional house type and explains the material came from the local environment.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain how geography shaped daily life.

*How to teach:* The child explains how geography shaped food, shelter and clothing.

- **Q:** How did geography shape daily life for first peoples?
  **A:** People used what their environment provided: acorns and game inland, fish and shellfish on the coast, tule near marshes, redwood in the north.

**Assessment:** Shelter model and explanation

**Answer key:** Shelter model and explanation rubric: 4 = model clearly resembles a regional house type (e.g., tule dome, cone of redwood bark, plank house) and the explanation names the region, the local material used and why it suited the climate; 3 = model and explanation with one gap; 2 = model without explanation of geography; 1 = incomplete.

## Art & Music 3: Half notes and meter

**Prepare:** Rhythm cards with half notes (an open note with a stem, 'ta-a,' 2 beats) and half rests (a small 'hat' sitting on a line, 2 beats). Recordings: a march (e.g., Sousa's 'The Stars and Stripes Forever'), a waltz (Johann Strauss II's 'The Blue Danube'), and a pop song in 4/4 you have previewed. Paper with 6 rhythms to bar-line.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all music is counted in 4. Some music groups beats in 3 (waltzes) or 2 (many marches).
- Putting bar lines after a set number of notes instead of beats. Count beats: in 4/4, every 4 beats gets a bar line; ti-ti counts as 1 beat.

### Day 1 (Monday): Half note
*Student:* Learn half notes and half rests and clap rhythms that combine them with quarters.

*How to teach:* Teach the half note ('ta-a,' 2 beats) and half rest (2 beats of silence). Clap rhythms combining them with quarters, sliding hands apart on the held beat.

- **Q:** How many beats does a half note get?
  **A:** 2 beats.
- **Q:** ta ta ta-a: how many beats?
  **A:** 1 + 1 + 2 = 4 beats.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Conducting
*Student:* Conduct 2-beat, 3-beat and 4-beat patterns to a march, a waltz and a pop song.

*How to teach:* Teach conducting patterns: 2-beat (down-up), 3-beat (down-out-up — a triangle), 4-beat (down-in-out-up). Conduct the march in 2 (or 4), the waltz in 3, the pop song in 4.

- **Q:** What is the 3-beat conducting pattern?
  **A:** Down, out (to the side), up — a triangle shape.
- **Q:** Which piece did you conduct in 3?
  **A:** The waltz ('The Blue Danube').

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Measures
*Student:* Draw bar lines into 6 written rhythms in 4/4 and 3/4 time.

*How to teach:* Explain measures and the time signature (top number = beats per measure). Draw bar lines into 6 rhythms: 3 in 4/4, 3 in 3/4.

- **Q:** In 3/4 time, how many beats are in each measure?
  **A:** 3 beats.
- **Q:** Put bar lines in this 4/4 rhythm: ta ta ti-ti ta | ta-a ta ta.
  **A:** ta ta ti-ti ta | ta-a ta ta | (4 beats each).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Waltz
*Student:* Listen to Strauss's 'The Blue Danube' and sway on beat 1 of each measure.

*How to teach:* Play 'The Blue Danube.' The child sways or steps on beat 1 of each measure while counting 1-2-3.

- **Q:** Which beat is strongest in a waltz?
  **A:** Beat 1 (counted 1-2-3, 1-2-3).
- **Q:** Who wrote 'The Blue Danube'?
  **A:** Johann Strauss II, an Austrian composer.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Identify the meter of 4 recordings and complete a bar-line worksheet.

*How to teach:* Play 4 recordings; the child identifies whether each feels in 2/4, 3, or 4 by conducting, then completes the bar-line worksheet.

- **Q:** How can you tell a piece is in 3?
  **A:** The strong beat comes every 3 beats (ONE-two-three), and it feels like a swaying waltz.

**Assessment:** Meter identification worksheet

**Answer key:** Meter worksheet key (sample): Recordings — march: 2 (or 4), waltz: 3, pop song: 4, and the fourth piece as you choose (e.g., 'Happy Birthday': 3). Bar lines: each 4/4 line has bar lines every 4 beats; each 3/4 line every 3 beats (ti-ti = 1 beat, half note = 2). Proficient = 3 of 4 meters correct and at least 5 of 6 rhythms barred correctly.

## PE 3: Rolling and tumbling

**Prepare:** Use a tumbling mat, old mattress or soft grass; make a gentle incline by putting a cushion or folded blanket under one end of the mat. Clear the area. Parent spots every forward roll. Skip forward rolls if the child has any neck or back problems.

**Watch for:**
- Rolling on the top of the head. Tuck chin to chest so the back of the head and shoulders touch the mat, never the top of the head.
- Opening the body mid-roll. Stay in a tight ball (hold shins) so the rounded back rolls smoothly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Log roll
*Student:* Practice log rolls down a mat with arms overhead and body straight, 5 each direction.

*How to teach:* Lie the child on the mat with arms stretched overhead and body straight like a pencil. They roll sideways the length of the mat 5 times each direction, keeping legs together and arms up.

- **Q:** What shape is your body in a log roll?
  **A:** Long and straight like a pencil, arms overhead, legs together.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Egg roll
*Student:* Practice tucked side 'egg rolls' and rocking chair rocks to learn a rounded back.

*How to teach:* Teach the egg roll: kneel in a tight tuck with forearms and head tucked, roll sideways onto the back and back to knees. Then rocking chair: sit in a tuck holding shins, chin to chest, rock back and forth on a rounded back 10 times.

- **Q:** Why do we practice rocking chairs before a forward roll?
  **A:** To learn to keep a rounded back and tucked chin so we roll smoothly and safely.
- **Q:** Where should your chin be during a roll?
  **A:** Tucked to your chest.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Forward roll
*Student:* Practice forward rolls down a gentle incline with an adult spotting the neck and hips.

*How to teach:* On the incline mat: squat at the high end, hands on the mat shoulder-width, chin to chest, push with the legs and roll over the back of the head and shoulders, finishing in a squat. Spot with one hand on the back of the neck/head and one under the hips/thighs. Repeat 5–8 times.

- **Q:** What part of your body touches the mat first in a forward roll?
  **A:** The hands, then the back of the head and shoulders (never the top of the head).
- **Q:** Why use a gentle incline?
  **A:** Gravity helps you roll over easily, making it safer while you learn.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Routine
*Student:* Combine a balance, a roll and a jump with soft landing into a 3-part routine.

*How to teach:* The child plans a 3-part routine: a balance (held 3–5 seconds), a roll (log, egg or forward), and a jump with a soft landing. Practice the transitions and finish with a still end position.

- **Q:** How should you land the jump at the end of your routine?
  **A:** On both feet with bent knees, arms up or forward, holding still.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Perform the routine and explain the safety cue 'chin to chest.'

*How to teach:* The child performs the routine. Then ask them to explain 'chin to chest' and show it. Score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Why do we say 'chin to chest'?
  **A:** It rounds the back and keeps the top of the head and neck off the mat so the neck is protected during the roll.

**Assessment:** Tumbling routine with safe technique

**Answer key:** Rubric for tumbling routine: 4 = balance held still, roll performed with chin tucked and rounded back, jump landed softly on two feet, smooth transitions, explains 'chin to chest' protects the neck; 3 = all parts safe with one wobble or pause; 2 = roll unsafe (head not tucked) or a part missing; 1 = cannot complete safely.

## Health 3: Skeletal and muscular systems

**Prepare:** Clean a chicken drumstick bone (boil and scrub off meat), a jar and vinegar; start the soak on Day 2 and check on Day 5 (after about 3 days). A skeleton/muscle diagram to label. A book to lift.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking bones are dead, dry and unchanging. Living bones are growing tissue with blood vessels; they repair themselves and need calcium and exercise.
- Thinking muscles push. Muscles can only pull; that's why they work in pairs (biceps pull the arm up, triceps pull it straight).

### Day 1 (Monday): Bones
*Student:* Learn major bones (skull, ribs, spine, femur) and why bones need calcium.

*How to teach:* Point to the skull, ribs, spine and femur on a diagram and on the child's body, and say what each protects or does. Explain bones store calcium and need it to stay hard.

- **Q:** What does the skull protect? What do the ribs protect?
  **A:** The skull protects the brain; the ribs protect the heart and lungs.
- **Q:** What is the longest bone in your body?
  **A:** The femur (thigh bone).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Vinegar bone
*Student:* Soak a cleaned chicken bone in vinegar to see what happens when calcium is removed; observe after 3 days.

*How to teach:* Place the clean bone in a jar of vinegar and have the child predict what will happen. Record a 'before' drawing and how it feels (hard, stiff).

- **Q:** Predict: what will vinegar do to the bone?
  **A:** The vinegar (an acid) dissolves the calcium, so the bone will become soft and bendy after a few days.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Joints
*Student:* Find hinge and ball-and-socket joints in your body and test their movements.

*How to teach:* Have the child bend their elbow and knee (hinge joints: back and forth) and swing their arm and leg in circles (ball-and-socket at shoulder and hip). They list where each joint type is.

- **Q:** Where are ball-and-socket joints? What movement can they do?
  **A:** Shoulder and hip; they can move in circles and many directions.
- **Q:** What kind of joint is your elbow?
  **A:** A hinge joint; it moves back and forth like a door.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Muscles
*Student:* Feel biceps and triceps work in pairs while lifting a book.

*How to teach:* Have the child lift a book by bending the elbow while you feel the biceps tighten, then lower it and feel the triceps. Explain muscles pull in pairs.

- **Q:** When you bend your arm, which muscle tightens? When you straighten it?
  **A:** Bending: the biceps contracts. Straightening: the triceps contracts.
- **Q:** Why do muscles work in pairs?
  **A:** Muscles can only pull, not push, so one pulls a bone one way and its partner pulls it back.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Label a skeleton and muscle diagram and list 2 ways to keep bones and muscles strong.

*How to teach:* Remove the bone from the vinegar and let the child bend it. Then they label the skeleton and muscle diagram and list 2 ways to keep bones and muscles strong.

- **Q:** Why did the bone become bendy?
  **A:** The vinegar removed the calcium, which is what makes bones hard.
- **Q:** Name two ways to keep your bones strong.
  **A:** Eat calcium-rich foods (milk, yogurt, cheese, leafy greens) and do weight-bearing exercise like running and jumping.

**Assessment:** Labeled diagram

**Answer key:** Labeled diagram key: skeleton labels skull (protects brain), ribs (protect heart and lungs), spine/backbone (supports body, protects spinal cord), femur (thigh bone, longest bone), plus any other correct bones; muscles: biceps (front of upper arm), triceps (back of upper arm), and one more (e.g., heart, quadriceps). Two ways to keep bones and muscles strong: eat calcium foods (milk, yogurt, greens), weight-bearing exercise (running, jumping), get enough protein, wear protective gear. Proficient: 6 correct labels and 2 strategies.
