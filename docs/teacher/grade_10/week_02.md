# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 2

## English 10: Unreliable narrators: Poe's 'The Cask of Amontillado'

**Prepare:** Print 'The Cask of Amontillado' (Project Gutenberg) and get highlighters. Read it once yourself. Parent preview: the narrator walls up a man alive; no graphic gore.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing verbal irony (saying the opposite of what one means) with dramatic irony (the audience knows something a character doesn't). Many moments in the story are both — have the student label who knows what.
- Dropping quotes into a paragraph without introducing them ('plop quotes'). Teach the lead-in: speaker + context + quote.

### Day 1 (Monday): Read Poe
*Student:* Read 'The Cask of Amontillado' aloud and highlight every instance of verbal irony in Montresor's speech to Fortunato.

*How to teach:* Read the story aloud together (about 25 minutes); the student takes Montresor's lines. Pause after each of Montresor's lines to Fortunato and ask, 'Does he mean that?' The student highlights verbal irony.

- **Q:** What does Montresor say is his motive at the start, and what is missing?
  **A:** He says Fortunato caused him 'a thousand injuries' and then an insult, but he never says what the insult was, so readers can't judge his claim.
- **Q:** What does 'I must not only punish, but punish with impunity' mean?
  **A:** He wants revenge without being caught or punished himself.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Irony chart
*Student:* Build a three-column chart (quote / what it says / what it really means) for six ironic moments, labeling each verbal or dramatic.

*How to teach:* Model one row of the chart ('I drink to your long life' / a toast / he plans to kill him). The student completes six rows, labeling each verbal or dramatic.

- **Q:** Explain the irony when Montresor produces a trowel to prove he is a mason.
  **A:** Fortunato means the secret society of Freemasons; Montresor means a literal bricklayer — he is about to wall Fortunato in (verbal and dramatic irony).
- **Q:** Why is the Montresor family motto 'Nemo me impune lacessit' ironic?
  **A:** It means 'No one attacks me with impunity' — it announces revenge, but Fortunato doesn't understand the warning.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Word study: Latin roots
*Student:* Study roots (ven-, mort-, cred-, jur-) from the story's vocabulary (impunity, immolation, retribution) and build a 12-word root web.

*How to teach:* Introduce each root with its meaning (ven- come, mort- death, cred- believe, jur- swear/law) and the story words impunity (without punishment), immolation (sacrifice), retribution (payback). The student builds a 12-word web and uses three words in sentences.

- **Q:** Give two words from the root cred- and their meanings.
  **A:** Credible (believable), incredulous (unwilling to believe), credit (belief/trust).
- **Q:** What does 'retribution' mean, and how does it fit the story?
  **A:** Punishment given in return for a wrong; Montresor seeks retribution for the insult.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Analytical paragraph
*Student:* Write a TIQA paragraph (topic, intro to quote, quote, analysis) answering: Is Montresor a reliable narrator? Cite two pieces of evidence.

*How to teach:* Teach TIQA with a model paragraph. The student writes a paragraph answering whether Montresor is reliable, using two introduced quotes and analysis that is longer than the quotes. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Give one piece of evidence that Montresor may be unreliable.
  **A:** His heart 'grew sick—on account of the dampness of the catacombs' — he blames the damp for what may be guilt; or he never explains the insult; or he tells the story fifty years later.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz & revise
*Student:* Take a short quiz on irony and point of view, then revise the paragraph using teacher feedback on quote integration.

*How to teach:* Give the five-item irony and point-of-view quiz from the assessment key (about 15 minutes) and go over it together. Then return the TIQA paragraph with one comment on each quote; the student rewrites each quote with a lead-in that names the speaker and situation (e.g., As Montresor leads Fortunato deeper, he insists, '...').

- **Q:** How long ago did the murder take place, according to the ending?
  **A:** Half a century — 'For the half of a century no mortal has disturbed them.'

**Assessment:** Analytical paragraph rubric (claim, evidence, analysis) and irony quiz.

**Answer key:** Irony quiz (answers): 1) Fortunato's name means 'fortunate' — situational irony, since he is doomed. 2) 'I shall not die of a cough.' / 'True—true' — dramatic/verbal irony; Montresor knows he will die another way. 3) 'I drink to your long life' — verbal irony. 4) Montresor shows a trowel when Fortunato asks if he is a mason — verbal/dramatic irony (a stonemason's tool). 5) Point of view → first person, Montresor, a possibly unreliable narrator. Paragraph rubric: 4 = clear claim on reliability; two accurate quotes, each introduced (e.g., his unexplained 'thousand injuries'; 'My heart grew sick—on account of the dampness of the catacombs'); analysis explains how the quote proves the claim; 3 = good claim, thin analysis; 2 = evidence summarized not quoted; 1 = no claim.

## Geometry: Compass-and-straightedge constructions

**Prepare:** Compass (one that locks is easiest), straightedge, patty paper or tracing paper, and GeoGebra (geogebra.org/geometry) open on a device. Practice the four constructions yourself first so you can demonstrate them.

**Watch for:**
- Changing the compass width in the middle of a construction. Explain that every step relies on 'same radius means same distance.'
- Using the ruler's markings to measure. In a construction the straightedge only draws lines; lengths are copied with the compass.

### Day 1 (Monday): Copy segment & angle
*Student:* Construct a copy of a segment and an angle with compass and straightedge, writing the steps.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate copying a segment (set compass to AB, swing from a new point) and copying an angle (arc on original, same arc on new ray, then copy the chord width). The student does each twice and writes numbered steps.

- **Q:** When copying an angle, why do you set the compass to the distance between where the arc crosses the two sides?
  **A:** That distance is the third side of a triangle; copying it makes the two triangles congruent by SSS, so the angles are congruent.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Bisectors
*Student:* Construct a perpendicular bisector and an angle bisector, then verify by folding patty paper.

*How to teach:* Model the perpendicular bisector and angle bisector, then have the student fold patty paper (segment endpoint onto endpoint; one side of the angle onto the other) to see the fold lands on the constructed line.

- **Q:** Why must the compass opening be more than half the segment for a perpendicular bisector?
  **A:** Otherwise the arcs from the two endpoints do not intersect.
- **Q:** Every point on a perpendicular bisector has what property?
  **A:** It is equidistant from the two endpoints of the segment.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Perpendiculars & parallels
*Student:* Construct a perpendicular through a point and a parallel line through a point not on a line.

*How to teach:* Show the perpendicular through a point (on or off the line) by marking two points on the line equidistant from P and bisecting. For a parallel line, draw a transversal through P and copy the angle. The student does both and labels the congruent angles.

- **Q:** Which angle relationship justifies the parallel-line construction?
  **A:** Congruent corresponding angles: if corresponding angles are congruent, the lines are parallel.

### Day 4 (Thursday): GeoGebra constructions
*Student:* Recreate all four constructions in GeoGebra and drag points to confirm they hold.

*How to teach:* Have the student use GeoGebra's circle-with-center-through-point and intersect tools (not the built-in bisector button) to rebuild each construction, then drag the original points. A correct construction stays correct when dragged.

- **Q:** Why does a correct GeoGebra construction keep working when you drag the points?
  **A:** Because it is built from relationships (circles of equal radius, intersections), not from fixed measurements.

### Day 5 (Friday): Construction check
*Student:* Perform three constructions from memory and explain why each works using circle radii.

*How to teach:* Run the performance check in the assessment key: the student performs three constructions from memory and explains each aloud. Score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Explain why the angle-bisector construction works.
  **A:** The arc from the vertex gives two equal segments on the sides, the two equal arcs give two more equal segments, and the shared ray is common; the two triangles are congruent by SSS, so the two angles at the vertex are congruent.

**Assessment:** Construction performance check.

**Answer key:** Performance check (3 constructions from memory, observe each): Perpendicular bisector — arcs from both endpoints with the same radius larger than half the segment; line through the two intersections. Angle bisector — arc from vertex hits both sides; equal arcs from those two points meet; ray from vertex through that point. Parallel through a point — draw a transversal through P and copy the angle at P (corresponding angles). Rubric per construction: 3 = accurate with no erased re-dos and a correct 'why' (equal radii make congruent segments/triangles); 2 = accurate but explanation vague; 1 = steps out of order or radius changed; 0 = not attempted.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: What happens when things burn?

**Prepare:** Gather tea lights, three glass jars of different sizes, a metal spoon, pickling lime (calcium hydroxide) to make limewater (stir 1 tsp into 1 cup water, let settle, pour off the clear liquid), a straw, fine steel wool (0000), a 9-volt battery, a baking tray and the kitchen scale. Have water and the fire extinguisher nearby. Preview a video of steel wool burning so you know what to expect (it sparks).

**Watch for:**
- Students believe matter is destroyed when something burns. Emphasize that atoms are rearranged into invisible gases (CO₂ and H₂O), and show that steel wool actually gains mass because it combines with oxygen.
- Students think the candle flame goes out under a jar because the jar 'runs out of air'. Clarify that it runs low on oxygen specifically; nitrogen, which is most of the air, remains.
- Students confuse the wick with the fuel. The wick only carries melted wax upward; the wax vapor is what burns.

### Day 1 (Monday): Candle under a jar
*Student:* Burn a tea light under jars of different sizes and time how long each burns; graph volume vs. time.

*How to teach:* Light identical tea lights and cover each with a jar of a different size at the same moment; time how long each burns. Measure each jar's volume by filling it with water and pouring into a measuring cup, then graph jar volume (x) vs. burn time (y). Ask the student to explain the trend.

- **Q:** Predict what happens to burn time if the jar volume doubles.
  **A:** Burn time roughly doubles, because the jar holds about twice as much oxygen for the flame to use.
- **Q:** What reactant from the air does the candle need?
  **A:** Oxygen (O₂).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Products of combustion
*Student:* Hold a cool spoon over the flame to collect water and test exhaled air vs. candle air with limewater for CO₂.

*How to teach:* Hold a cool metal spoon briefly above (not in) the flame and observe droplets; then collect candle exhaust in an inverted jar and shake with limewater. Compare with limewater the student has blown into through a straw. Discuss what both cloudy results show.

- **Q:** What forms on the cool spoon and where did it come from?
  **A:** Water droplets; hydrogen atoms in the wax combined with oxygen to make water vapor, which condensed on the cool spoon. (Black soot may also appear from incomplete combustion.)
- **Q:** Why does limewater turn cloudy?
  **A:** Carbon dioxide reacts with calcium hydroxide to form insoluble calcium carbonate: CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Steel wool burns
*Student:* Weigh steel wool, ignite it with a 9-V battery over a baking tray, re-weigh, and explain the mass gain.

*How to teach:* Fluff a pad of steel wool, weigh it on a foil-lined tray, then touch the 9-V battery terminals to it (adult present, goggles, over the tray, nothing flammable nearby). After it cools, reweigh it, including any fallen bits. The student explains why mass increased.

- **Q:** The steel wool weighed 5.20 g before and 5.45 g after burning. Explain.
  **A:** Mass increased by 0.25 g because iron atoms combined with oxygen atoms from the air to form iron oxide; the added mass is the oxygen.
- **Q:** Write a balanced equation for iron reacting with oxygen to form iron(III) oxide.
  **A:** 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃

### Day 4 (Thursday): Model it
*Student:* Draw particle models of methane combustion and write the word and symbol equation.

*How to teach:* Model drawing methane (one C with four H) and oxygen molecules as circles, then rearranging the same atoms into products. The student draws before/after particle diagrams, writes the word equation and the balanced symbol equation, and counts atoms of each element on both sides.

- **Q:** Write the balanced equation for methane combustion.
  **A:** CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
- **Q:** Count the oxygen atoms on each side of that equation.
  **A:** Left: 2 × 2 = 4. Right: 2 (in CO₂) + 2 (in 2H₂O) = 4. Balanced.

### Day 5 (Friday): CER
*Student:* Write a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph explaining where the candle's mass goes.

*How to teach:* Review the claim-evidence-reasoning structure with a model paragraph on a different topic. The student writes the CER paragraph explaining where the candle's mass goes, citing this week's data. Score with the rubric and have the student revise one weak part.

- **Q:** What is the difference between evidence and reasoning in a CER?
  **A:** Evidence is the data or observations; reasoning is the scientific principle that explains why the evidence supports the claim (here, conservation of atoms in a chemical reaction).

**Assessment:** CER paragraph scored with rubric.

**Answer key:** CER rubric (4 points each for claim, evidence, reasoning): 4 = Claim states that the candle's mass goes into the air as carbon dioxide and water vapor (gases). Evidence cites at least two observations: water condensing on the cool spoon, limewater turning cloudy with candle gases, the candle getting shorter/lighter, and the flame going out sooner under smaller jars (oxygen is a reactant). Reasoning explains that atoms are conserved — carbon and hydrogen atoms from wax combine with oxygen from air to form CO₂ and H₂O, so mass is not destroyed but leaves as gas; the steel wool gaining mass shows that combustion adds oxygen atoms. 3 = correct claim with one piece of evidence and partial reasoning. 2 = claim with evidence but no atomic reasoning. 1 = claim only or incorrect claim (e.g., 'mass is destroyed').

## Modern World History: Judeo-Christian ethics and the spread of constitutional ideas

**Prepare:** Print excerpts: Exodus 20 (Ten Commandments), a prophetic passage such as 2 Samuel 12 (Nathan confronts King David), Matthew 5–7 (Sermon on the Mount), Japan's 1947 Constitution (Articles 9, 14, 20, 21) and Germany's Basic Law Article 1. Note to parent: these religious texts are studied as historical sources of ideas, per CA standard 10.1.1.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking ancient ideas transferred directly into the Constitution. Ideas passed through centuries of medieval and Enlightenment thought.
- Assuming every constitution modeled on the U.S. is identical. Japan's Article 9 renouncing war has no U.S. equivalent.

### Day 1 (Monday): Judaism & law
*Student:* Read excerpts on the covenant and Ten Commandments and note ideas of law above rulers.

*How to teach:* Read Exodus 20 and the story of Nathan rebuking King David. Discuss how a covenant creates law that binds rulers as well as subjects; the student writes two sentences connecting this to 'rule of law.'

- **Q:** What idea about rulers appears when the prophet Nathan condemns King David?
  **A:** Even a king is subject to a higher moral law and can be held accountable.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Christian ethics
*Student:* Read the Sermon on the Mount excerpt and identify ideas of individual worth and conscience.

*How to teach:* Read selections from the Sermon on the Mount (the Beatitudes, the Golden Rule, 'love your enemies'). The student identifies ideas of individual worth and conscience and explains how they could support ideas of equality.

- **Q:** What is the Golden Rule as stated in Matthew 7:12?
  **A:** Do to others what you would have them do to you.
- **Q:** How could the idea that every person has worth to God influence political thought?
  **A:** It supports the belief that all individuals deserve dignity and equal treatment, which later fed ideas of natural rights.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Venn diagram
*Student:* Compare Greco-Roman and Judeo-Christian views of law, reason and duty in a Venn diagram.

*How to teach:* Draw a Venn diagram: Greco-Roman (reason, citizenship, natural law, civic duty) and Judeo-Christian (moral law from God, individual worth, conscience); the overlap includes law above rulers and duty to community. The student fills it with at least four items per section.

- **Q:** Name one idea both traditions share.
  **A:** Law should be above any individual ruler (rule of law), or that there is a higher law (natural/divine) that human law should follow.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Constitutional influence
*Student:* Compare articles of Japan's 1947 constitution with the U.S. Bill of Rights.

*How to teach:* Compare Japan's 1947 constitution (written under U.S. occupation) with the U.S. Bill of Rights in a two-column chart. The student finds at least three parallels and one difference. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Which article of Japan's constitution parallels the First Amendment?
  **A:** Article 21 (freedom of assembly, association, speech, press) and Article 20 (freedom of religion).
- **Q:** What is unique about Article 9?
  **A:** Japan renounces war as a sovereign right and the threat or use of force to settle international disputes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 assessment
*Student:* Write a 1-page essay on how ancient ideas shaped the U.S. Constitution and its global spread.

*How to teach:* The student outlines, then writes a one-page essay on how ancient ideas shaped the U.S. Constitution and its global spread. Score with the rubric. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What does Germany's Basic Law Article 1 say, and why after WWII?
  **A:** 'Human dignity shall be inviolable' — written in 1949 in reaction to Nazi crimes against human dignity.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 essay.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 essay rubric (4-point): Thesis — explains how at least two ancient traditions shaped the U.S. Constitution and spread globally; Evidence — specific ideas (Greek citizen participation, Roman republic and checks, rule of law in the Twelve Tables, the Hebrew idea that law binds even kings, Christian ideas of individual worth and conscience) and a global example (Japan 1947 Articles 14 and 21; Germany's Basic Law Article 1 'Human dignity shall be inviolable'); Analysis — explains the link rather than listing; Writing — organized, cited.

## Spanish III: Identidad bicultural

**Prepare:** Obtain 'Mi nombre' (the 'My Name' vignette from Sandra Cisneros's 'La casa en Mango Street', Spanish translation by Elena Poniatowska) and choose a short Radio Ambulante episode excerpt about identity (Radio Ambulante provides transcripts). Make a ser/estar chart.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking ser = permanent and estar = temporary always: use estar for location (even permanent: Madrid está en España) and ser for events' location (La fiesta es en mi casa).
- Using ser for emotions (*Soy orgullosa de...): emotions use estar or sentirse — Estoy orgullosa / Me siento orgullosa de mi herencia.

### Day 1 (Monday): Lectura
*Student:* Read 'Mi nombre' and identify five details the narrator gives about her name and heritage.

*How to teach:* Read 'Mi nombre' aloud together; your child lists five details about the narrator's name and heritage, quoting the text.

- **Q:** What does Esperanza say her name means in English and in Spanish?
  **A:** In English it means hope; in Spanish it means too many letters — sadness, waiting.
- **Q:** Whom was she named after?
  **A:** Her great-grandmother, who was born in the Chinese year of the horse and spent her life looking out a window.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ser vs. estar
*Student:* Practice ser vs. estar in 20 sentences describing identity and emotions.

*How to teach:* Review ser (identity, origin, characteristics, time, events) vs. estar (location, condition, emotion, progressive). Your child completes 20 sentences.

- **Q:** Choose: 'Mi familia ___ de México, pero ahora ___ en California.'
  **A:** es (origin); está (location)
- **Q:** Choose: 'Yo ___ nervioso hoy porque el examen ___ a las nueve.'
  **A:** estoy; es

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Escuchar
*Student:* Listen to a short Radio Ambulante excerpt about Latino identity and answer comprehension questions.

*How to teach:* Play the Radio Ambulante excerpt twice with the transcript available the second time. Your child answers questions on the speaker's main points.

- **Q:** What listening strategy helps with authentic radio?
  **A:** First listen for gist, then reread or relisten for details; use the transcript only to confirm.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mi nombre
*Student:* Write a paragraph 'Mi nombre' about the meaning or story of your own name.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a paragraph about the meaning or story of their own name, modeled on Cisneros.

- **Q:** Write a sentence like Cisneros's: 'In English my name means...'
  **A:** Example: En inglés mi nombre significa 'regalo de Dios'; en español suena más suave.

### Day 5 (Friday): Comparación
*Student:* Discuss in Spanish: How is bicultural identity in California similar or different from your own?

*How to teach:* Discuss in Spanish: ¿Cómo es la identidad bicultural en California? Your child compares it to their own experience.

- **Q:** Translate: 'I feel proud of my heritage.'
  **A:** Me siento orgulloso/a de mi herencia.

**Assessment:** Paragraph rubric.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric ('Mi nombre'): 4 = explains the name's meaning or origin, who chose it, and how the writer feels about it, 8+ sentences, accurate ser/estar and past tenses, vivid detail; 3 = complete with a few errors; 2 = short or many errors; 1 = fragments. Day-1 detail key (from the vignette): in English her name means hope; in Spanish it means too many letters, sadness, waiting; she was named after her great-grandmother, a 'wild horse of a woman' born in the Chinese year of the horse who looked out the window her whole life; at school people say her name as if the syllables were made of tin; she wants a new name like 'Zeze the X'.

## Music & Media Arts: Rhythm notation and meter

**Prepare:** Metronome app, musictheory.net rhythm lessons, Chrome Music Lab Rhythm, a printed rhythm ladder (whole → half → quarter → eighth → sixteenth), and an 8-measure body-percussion score (stomp/pat/clap/snap rows). Phone for video.

**Watch for:**
- Students hold notes the wrong length after dotted notes. A dot adds half the note's value: dotted half = 3 beats in 4/4.
- Students rush rests. Rests are counted silence; say the counts aloud.
- Students think the top number of a time signature names the note type. Top = beats per measure; bottom = which note gets one beat (4 = quarter).

### Day 1 (Monday): Note values
*Student:* Learn note and rest values and clap a rhythm ladder.

*How to teach:* The student learns note and rest values and claps a rhythm ladder while counting ('1-e-&-a').

- **Q:** In 4/4, how many eighth notes equal one half note?
  **A:** Four.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Time signatures
*Student:* Count and clap rhythms in 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 with a metronome at 80 bpm.

*How to teach:* Count and clap rhythms in 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 with the metronome at 80 bpm.

- **Q:** What does 3/4 mean?
  **A:** Three beats per measure; the quarter note gets one beat.
- **Q:** How many beats does a dotted half note get in 3/4?
  **A:** Three — a full measure.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dictation
*Student:* Notate four 2-measure rhythms you hear (Chrome Music Lab Rhythm).

*How to teach:* The student notates four 2-measure rhythms you clap or play in Chrome Music Lab.

- **Q:** Tip for dictation?
  **A:** Count the beats aloud while listening, first mark where each beat falls, then fill in subdivisions.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Body percussion
*Student:* Perform an 8-measure body-percussion piece reading notation.

*How to teach:* The student performs an 8-measure body-percussion piece reading notation.

- **Q:** What should you do if you make a mistake while performing?
  **A:** Keep the beat going and rejoin on the next measure.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Rhythm reading assessment recorded on video.

*How to teach:* Record the rhythm reading assessment on video and score with the rubric.

- **Q:** How many sixteenth notes fit in one quarter note?
  **A:** Four.

**Assessment:** Recorded rhythm performance.

**Answer key:** Recorded rhythm performance rubric: 4 = steady tempo with metronome at 80 bpm, all note and rest values correct, counts spoken or clear, 8 measures without stopping; 3 = 1–2 errors; 2 = several errors or tempo drift; 1 = cannot complete. Theory checks: whole = 4 beats, half = 2, quarter = 1, eighth = ½, sixteenth = ¼ (in 4/4); 3/4 has three quarter-note beats per measure.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Heart rate and FITT

**Prepare:** Get a stopwatch or heart-rate app. Have the student take resting heart rate on three mornings before getting out of bed. Print an RPE (Borg 6–20 or 0–10) scale. Plan a safe interval-run route.

**Watch for:**
- Students count pulse for 10 seconds and forget to multiply. Count 15 seconds and multiply by 4 (or 30 × 2).
- Students take their pulse with their thumb. The thumb has its own pulse; use index and middle fingers on the wrist (radial) or neck (carotid, gently).
- Students think harder is always better. FITT intensity should match the goal; easy days support recovery.

### Day 1 (Monday): Heart rate
*Student:* Measure resting heart rate three mornings and calculate max and target zones (Karvonen).

*How to teach:* The student averages three morning resting HRs and calculates max and target zones using Karvonen.

- **Q:** Age 15, resting HR 65 bpm: what is the 60–80% target zone?
  **A:** Max = 205; HRR = 140; 60% → 84 + 65 = 149; 80% → 112 + 65 = 177 bpm.
- **Q:** How do you find your pulse?
  **A:** Two fingers on the radial artery (thumb side of the wrist), count 15 seconds and multiply by 4.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Interval run
*Student:* Do a 20-minute interval run, taking heart rate after each interval.

*How to teach:* The student does a 20-minute interval run (e.g., 2 min jog / 1 min fast × 6, plus warm-up and cool-down), taking HR after each interval.

- **Q:** What happened to your heart rate during recovery jogs?
  **A:** It dropped (recovery), though not all the way to resting; faster recovery indicates better fitness.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): FITT
*Student:* Learn frequency, intensity, time and type and apply them to sample plans.

*How to teach:* Teach FITT; the student applies it to three sample plans and identifies which FITT variable changes in each.

- **Q:** A runner increases from 20 to 30 minutes per run. Which FITT variable changed?
  **A:** Time.
- **Q:** Give a FITT plan for building aerobic fitness.
  **A:** F: 4 days/week; I: 60–80% HRR or RPE 13–15; T: 30 minutes; Type: running or cycling.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Circuit
*Student:* Complete a 30-minute bodyweight circuit and record RPE.

*How to teach:* Lead a 30-minute bodyweight circuit (squats, push-ups, lunges, planks, jumping jacks, mountain climbers; 40 s work/20 s rest) and record RPE after each round.

- **Q:** What RPE (6–20 scale) corresponds to moderate intensity?
  **A:** About 12–14 ('somewhat hard').

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on heart-rate zones and FITT.

*How to teach:* Give the heart-rate/FITT quiz and review the HR log.

- **Q:** Why does resting heart rate often drop as fitness improves?
  **A:** The heart muscle becomes stronger and pumps more blood per beat (higher stroke volume), so fewer beats are needed.

**Assessment:** Quiz and HR log.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Estimated max HR — 220 − age (205 for a 15-year-old). 2) Karvonen target HR = (HRR × intensity) + resting HR, where HRR = max HR − resting HR. 3) Example: age 15, RHR 65 → HRR 140; 60% = 149 bpm; 80% = 177 bpm. 4) FITT = Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type. 5) Teens need 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily, with vigorous, muscle- and bone-strengthening activity on at least 3 days each. 6) RPE — perceived exertion; moderate ≈ 12–14 on Borg 6–20 (5–6 on 0–10). 7) Lower resting heart rate generally indicates better cardiovascular fitness. HR log full credit: three resting readings and average, plus post-interval readings.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Vehicle controls and pre-drive routine

**Prepare:** Park the family car somewhere safe and level with the engine off and parking brake set for each in-car session; you hold the keys. Find the owner's manual (or download it). Print a pre-drive checklist (walk-around + cockpit).

**Watch for:**
- Students adjust side mirrors to see the side of their own car. Using the BGE (blindspot/glare elimination) method, push mirrors outward until the car's side just disappears, which shrinks blind spots.
- Students ignore dashboard lights that are yellow/amber. Amber means check soon; red means stop safely and address now (e.g., oil pressure, brake, temperature).
- Students think mirrors eliminate blind spots. A quick over-the-shoulder head check is still required before lane changes.

### Day 1 (Monday): Controls tour
*Student:* Sitting in a parked car with a parent, locate and explain every control: lights, wipers, hazards, defroster, parking brake and gear selector.

*How to teach:* Sit in the parked car with the student in the driver's seat. Point to and name each control; the student operates it (lights, high beams, wipers and washer, hazards, defroster front and rear, horn, parking brake, gear selector P-R-N-D-L) and explains when it is used.

- **Q:** When should you turn on your hazard lights?
  **A:** When your vehicle is stopped or disabled on or near the road, or to warn drivers behind of a sudden hazard ahead; not while driving normally in rain.
- **Q:** What gear must the car be in to start it (automatic)?
  **A:** Park (or Neutral), with your foot on the brake.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dashboard
*Student:* Use the owner's manual to identify 10 dashboard warning lights and what action each requires.

*How to teach:* Using the owner's manual, the student identifies 10 dashboard warning lights, their color and the required action, and makes a chart.

- **Q:** What should you do if the red oil-pressure light comes on while driving?
  **A:** Pull over safely as soon as possible and turn off the engine; low oil pressure can quickly destroy the engine.
- **Q:** What does a solid amber check-engine light mean?
  **A:** A problem has been detected by the engine computer; have it checked soon. A flashing check-engine light means a serious misfire — reduce speed and get service promptly.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pre-drive check
*Student:* Do a walk-around check (tires, lights, fluids under the car, windows) and a cockpit check (seat, mirrors, belt) and time yourself.

*How to teach:* The student performs the walk-around and cockpit check while you time it, then repeats to improve.

- **Q:** Why check behind the car before getting in?
  **A:** Small children, pets or objects may be hidden in the blind area behind the car and could be struck when backing.
- **Q:** How should the head restraint be positioned?
  **A:** The center of the restraint about level with the back of the head (ears to top of head), close to the head, to prevent whiplash.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mirrors & blind spots
*Student:* Adjust mirrors using the BGE method and have a family member walk around the car to map blind spots.

*How to teach:* Teach BGE mirror adjustment: driver's mirror — lean your head against the left window and set the mirror so the car's side just disappears; passenger mirror — lean to the center and do the same. A family member walks slowly around the car while the student calls out when the person disappears from all mirrors.

- **Q:** Where are the car's blind spots?
  **A:** Over each rear shoulder, beside and slightly behind the car (and directly behind the car, low down).
- **Q:** How do you check a blind spot?
  **A:** A quick glance over the shoulder in the direction you want to move.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Demonstrate the full pre-drive routine for a parent with a checklist.

*How to teach:* The student demonstrates the full pre-drive routine from approaching the car to buckling up, using the checklist. Score it.

- **Q:** What are the steps after you are seated, before starting?
  **A:** Lock doors, adjust seat, head restraint and mirrors, fasten seat belt, check passengers are belted, foot on brake.

**Assessment:** Pre-drive checklist.

**Answer key:** Pre-drive checklist key: Walk-around — tires (inflation, damage), lights and lenses clean/intact, no fluid puddles under the car, windows and mirrors clean, nothing behind or around the car (children, pets, objects). Cockpit — doors locked, seat adjusted (feet reach pedals with bent knees, wrists rest on top of wheel, at least 10 inches between breastbone and steering wheel), head restraint level with the middle/top of the head, mirrors adjusted, seat belt on and passengers belted, parking brake on, foot on brake before starting. Proficient = all items performed in order without prompts in under 3 minutes; developing = 1–2 prompts; beginning = 3+ prompts.

## Computer Science Principles: Binary numbers and overflow

**Prepare:** Make five flip-cards labeled 16, 8, 4, 2, 1 (one side shows the number, the back is blank = 0). Open the Code.org Binary Odometer widget. Have a browser console ready (press F12 → Console) or Python at trinket.io for the 0.1 + 0.2 demo.

**Watch for:**
- Reading binary left-to-right as ordinary digits ('101 is one hundred one'). Always label place values (…8, 4, 2, 1) above the bits before converting.
- Thinking overflow means the computer 'crashes'. Overflow means the value needs more bits than are available, so it wraps around or gives a wrong result.
- Believing computers do decimal arithmetic perfectly. Many decimal fractions (like 0.1) have no exact binary representation, which causes round-off error.

### Day 1 (Monday): Binary place value
*Student:* Use five flip-cards (16-8-4-2-1) to build numbers 0–31 and convert 10 decimal numbers to binary.

*How to teach:* Line up the flip-cards 16-8-4-2-1. Show that each card is double the one to its right. Build 13 together (8 + 4 + 1 → 01101), then let the student build 0–31 and convert 10 decimal numbers, writing the binary under each card.

- **Q:** Convert 19 to binary using the 16-8-4-2-1 cards.
  **A:** 19 = 16 + 2 + 1 → 10011.
- **Q:** What is the largest number five cards can show?
  **A:** 11111 = 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 31.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Conversion practice
*Student:* Use the Code.org Binary Odometer widget, then convert 15 numbers between binary and decimal by hand.

*How to teach:* Let the student play with the Binary Odometer, watching how each place rolls over. Then do 15 hand conversions both directions; for binary → decimal, write place values above each bit and add the ones that are 1.

- **Q:** Convert 101101₂ to decimal.
  **A:** 32 + 8 + 4 + 1 = 45.
- **Q:** Convert 50 to binary.
  **A:** 50 = 32 + 16 + 2 → 110010.
- **Q:** Convert 1111111₂ (seven ones) to decimal.
  **A:** 127 (that's 2^7 − 1).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Overflow
*Student:* Model a 4-bit odometer rolling over from 1111 and research the Gangnam Style YouTube counter and Y2K overflow examples.

*How to teach:* Count up on a 4-bit odometer from 1100 to 1111, then add 1: it rolls to 0000. Explain this is an overflow error. Have the student read about the YouTube view counter (Gangnam Style passed the 32-bit signed limit of 2,147,483,647 views in 2014, so YouTube upgraded to 64-bit) and Y2K (years stored as 2 digits rolled from 99 to 00).

- **Q:** In a 4-bit system, what is 1111 + 0001?
  **A:** The true answer 10000 needs 5 bits, so the 4-bit result is 0000 — an overflow error.
- **Q:** Why did Gangnam Style cause YouTube to upgrade its counter?
  **A:** Its views were about to exceed 2,147,483,647, the largest value of a 32-bit signed integer, which would have overflowed; YouTube switched to a 64-bit integer.
- **Q:** How was Y2K an overflow-type problem?
  **A:** Years were stored with only two digits, so after 99 the value would roll to 00 and computers could read 2000 as 1900.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Round-off
*Student:* In a Python or JavaScript console run 0.1 + 0.2 and explain why the result is not exactly 0.3.

*How to teach:* Have the student type 0.1 + 0.2 into a JavaScript console or Python. The result is 0.30000000000000004. Explain that computers store numbers in a fixed number of bits, and 0.1 in binary is a repeating fraction (like 1/3 in decimal), so it must be rounded.

- **Q:** Why isn't 0.1 + 0.2 exactly 0.3 on a computer?
  **A:** 0.1 and 0.2 are repeating fractions in binary, so they're rounded to fit a fixed number of bits; the tiny rounding errors add up — a round-off error.
- **Q:** Give a decimal-world analogy for round-off error.
  **A:** 1/3 = 0.333… must be cut off, so 0.333 + 0.333 + 0.333 = 0.999, not 1.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Complete an AP-style practice set of 8 binary/overflow questions and correct any errors.

*How to teach:* Give the 8-question practice set in the assessment key (about 20 minutes, no widget). Correct together; for each miss, have the student redo the conversion with labeled place values.

- **Q:** How many values can a 6-bit number represent, and what is the largest?
  **A:** 64 values, 0 through 63.

**Assessment:** Binary conversion practice set.

**Answer key:** Practice set answers: 1) 10110₂ = 22. 2) 37 = 100101₂. 3) 11111111₂ = 255. 4) 13 = 1101₂. 5) Largest value in 4 bits (unsigned) = 15. 6) Adding 1 to 1111 in a 4-bit system gives 0000 (overflow error). 7) 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.30000000000000004 because 0.1 and 0.2 cannot be represented exactly in binary — a round-off error. 8) Which is greater, 1010₂ or 1100₂? 1100₂ (12 > 10). Additional hand-conversions from the week: 25 = 11001₂, 100 = 1100100₂, 1001₂ = 9, 110110₂ = 54. Score 1 point each; 7–8 = mastery.
