# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 30

## English 10: Orwell and language

**Prepare:** Orwell's 'Politics and the English Language' (1946) and excerpts of Animal Farm (chapters 6, 8 and 10, where the commandments change). A jargon-filled paragraph (corporate or bureaucratic memo) for revision.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Orwell objects to all long words. He objects to vague, inflated language that hides meaning.
- Confusing euphemism with polite speech. Political euphemism disguises something unpleasant (e.g., 'collateral damage' for civilian deaths).

### Day 1 (Monday): Orwell essay
*Student:* Read 'Politics and the English Language' and list Orwell's six rules.

*How to teach:* Read the essay together. The student lists the six rules and paraphrases each. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What are Orwell's six rules?
  **A:** 1) Never use a metaphor, simile or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print. 2) Never use a long word where a short one will do. 3) If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out. 4) Never use the passive where you can use the active. 5) Never use a foreign phrase, scientific word or jargon if there's an everyday English equivalent. 6) Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Revise like Orwell
*Student:* Apply Orwell's rules to revise a jargon-filled paragraph.

*How to teach:* Model revising one sentence ('It is incumbent upon all personnel to facilitate the optimization of workflow' → 'Everyone should work efficiently'). The student revises the jargon paragraph using the rules.

- **Q:** Revise using Orwell's rules: 'Mistakes were made in the implementation of the policy.'
  **A:** 'We made mistakes when we put the policy in place.' (active voice, plain words, names who acted).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Animal Farm excerpt
*Student:* Read the chapter where the commandments change and analyze propaganda techniques.

*How to teach:* Read the excerpts where the commandments change. The student analyzes propaganda techniques (rewriting history, euphemism, fear, repetition). Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How does the commandment 'All animals are equal' change by the end?
  **A:** It becomes 'All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others.'
- **Q:** Who spreads the pigs' propaganda, and how?
  **A:** Squealer, who uses confusing statistics, threats ('Jones will come back'), and rewrites the commandments.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Propaganda hunt
*Student:* Find three examples of euphemism or doublespeak in ads or news and explain them.

*How to teach:* Show examples of euphemism ('pre-owned' for used, 'collateral damage' for civilian deaths). The student finds three examples in ads or news and explains what each hides or softens. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does the euphemism 'downsizing' hide?
  **A:** That workers are being fired or laid off.

### Day 5 (Friday): Short essay
*Student:* Write a 500-word response linking Orwell's essay to Fahrenheit 451.

*How to teach:* The student plans a thesis linking Orwell's essay to Fahrenheit 451 and writes a 500-word response. Grade with the rubric. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Give one connection between Orwell's essay and Beatty.
  **A:** Beatty uses smooth, reasonable-sounding language ('peace of mind,' making everyone 'equal') to disguise censorship — exactly the language Orwell warns about.

**Assessment:** Response essay.

**Answer key:** Response essay rubric: 4 = thesis connecting Orwell's argument (vague, euphemistic language lets people avoid thinking and makes 'lies sound truthful and murder respectable') to Fahrenheit 451 (Beatty's 'everyone made equal,' firemen as 'custodians of our peace of mind,' parlor-wall chatter); two quotes from each text; clear analysis; 3 = sound but less precise; 2 = summary; 1 = off-topic.

## Geometry: Modeling with density and design

**Prepare:** Kitchen scale (grams), measuring cup, household objects (a rock, a metal spoon, a wood block), calculator. Look up a few CA county populations and areas (census.gov or Wikipedia). Prepare the Unit 8 test.

**Watch for:**
- Inverting the density ratio. Density = mass/volume (or people/area): 'per' means divide by the unit after 'per'.
- Mixing units (mL vs cm³ — they are equal; L vs cm³ — 1 L = 1000 cm³).

### Day 1 (Monday): Density
*Student:* Compute population density of CA counties and BTU-per-cubic-foot problems.

*How to teach:* Explain density as 'amount per unit.' Compute population density of a few CA counties and a BTU-per-cubic-foot heating problem (e.g., a room needs about 5 BTU per ft³ — use the given value from the problem).

- **Q:** Los Angeles County has about 9.7 million people in about 4,060 square miles. Population density?
  **A:** ≈ 2,390 people per square mile.
- **Q:** A room 12 × 15 × 8 ft needs 5 BTU per cubic foot. How many BTU?
  **A:** 1440 ft³ × 5 = 7,200 BTU.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Material density lab
*Student:* Measure mass and volume of household objects to compute density.

*How to teach:* Weigh each object, find volume by water displacement in the measuring cup (1 mL = 1 cm³), and compute density. Compare to known values (water 1 g/cm³, aluminum 2.7).

- **Q:** A rock has mass 150 g and raises the water level 60 mL. What is its density?
  **A:** 150/60 = 2.5 g/cm³.
- **Q:** Will an object with density 0.8 g/cm³ float in water?
  **A:** Yes — it is less dense than water (1 g/cm³).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Design challenge
*Student:* Design a cylindrical can holding 355 mL with minimum surface area.

*How to teach:* Set up V = πr²h = 355 cm³, write h = 355/(πr²), and SA = 2πr² + 2πrh = 2πr² + 710/r. The student builds a table or uses Desmos to find the minimum.

- **Q:** For a 355 mL can, what radius minimizes surface area?
  **A:** About 3.84 cm (height ≈ 7.67 cm, where h = 2r), SA ≈ 278 cm².
- **Q:** Why don't real soda cans use this shape?
  **A:** Real cans (r ≈ 3.3 cm, h ≈ 12.2 cm) are taller for grip, stacking, thicker top/bottom metal, and fitting machines and cupholders.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Presentation
*Student:* Write up the design with drawings and calculations.

*How to teach:* The student writes up the design with a labeled drawing, the table/graph, and the final dimensions and comparison.

- **Q:** What should a design write-up include?
  **A:** The problem, equations, method (table/graph), final dimensions with units, a drawing, and a comparison to a real can.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 8 test
*Student:* Unit 8 test on area, volume and modeling.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test from the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Why is a cylinder with h = 2r the most efficient?
  **A:** For a fixed volume, that shape gives the smallest surface area (it is closest to a sphere-like proportion for a cylinder).

**Assessment:** Design project and Unit 8 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 8 test with answers: 1) 5,000 people in 20 km² → 250 people/km². 2) Object with mass 270 g and volume 100 cm³ → 2.7 g/cm³. 3) Volume of a cylinder r = 5, h = 12 → 300π ≈ 942.5. 4) Sphere r = 6 → 288π ≈ 904.8. 5) Composite area: 10 × 6 rectangle with a semicircle of diameter 6 on one end → 60 + 4.5π ≈ 74.1. 6) Cross section of a cylinder sliced parallel to the base → circle. 7) A 355 mL can with r = 3.84 cm → h ≈ 7.67 cm. Design project rubric: 4 = volume equation set to 355 cm³, SA expressed in r, minimum found (table, graph or h = 2r) at r ≈ 3.84 cm, h ≈ 7.67 cm, SA ≈ 278 cm², labeled drawing and comparison to a real can; 3 = correct minimum with minor gaps; 2 = only trial values without conclusion; 1 = incomplete.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Le Chatelier's principle

**Prepare:** Make red cabbage indicator: chop 2 cups of red cabbage, cover with boiling water, steep 15 minutes, strain and refrigerate. Gather vinegar, baking soda, clear cups, two sodas (one cold, one warm), and ice/warm water baths. Print a description of the Haber process.

**Watch for:**
- Students think Le Chatelier means the system returns to its original state. It shifts to partially counteract the stress and settles at a new equilibrium.
- Students think adding a catalyst shifts equilibrium. A catalyst speeds both directions equally; it gets to equilibrium faster without changing the yield.
- Students treat temperature like concentration without considering ΔH. For an exothermic reaction, heat is a 'product', so raising temperature shifts left.

### Day 1 (Monday): Le Chatelier
*Student:* Predict shifts for changes in concentration, temperature and pressure.

*How to teach:* Teach Le Chatelier's principle with stresses: add/remove reactant or product, change temperature, change pressure. The student predicts shifts for 10 cases using N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ + heat and explains each.

- **Q:** For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ + heat, what happens if the temperature is raised?
  **A:** The equilibrium shifts left (toward N₂ and H₂), absorbing the added heat; less NH₃.
- **Q:** What happens if N₂ is added?
  **A:** Shifts right, using up some added N₂ and producing more NH₃.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cabbage indicator
*Student:* Make red cabbage indicator and shift color by adding acid and base.

*How to teach:* Put cabbage indicator in three cups: add vinegar to one and baking-soda solution to another; keep one neutral. Then add base to the acid cup drop by drop and watch the color shift back. Explain that indicator molecules exist in an equilibrium between acid and base forms.

- **Q:** What color is cabbage indicator in vinegar and in baking-soda solution?
  **A:** Pink/red in vinegar (acid); blue-green in baking-soda solution (base); purple near neutral.
- **Q:** Why does adding base to the pink cup change its color?
  **A:** Base removes H⁺ ions, shifting the indicator's equilibrium toward its base-colored form.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Soda temperature
*Student:* Compare fizz of cold and warm soda and explain with equilibrium.

*How to teach:* Open a cold soda and a warm soda (in a sink!) and compare fizz. The student explains with equilibrium: CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq) + heat.

- **Q:** Which soda fizzed more and why?
  **A:** The warm soda; gas solubility decreases at higher temperature, so the equilibrium shifts toward gaseous CO₂.
- **Q:** Why does warm lake water hold less oxygen for fish?
  **A:** Gases are less soluble in warmer water, so less O₂ stays dissolved.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Haber process
*Student:* Analyze the Haber process and propose conditions to maximize ammonia yield.

*How to teach:* Present the Haber process and real conditions (~200 atm, ~400–450 °C, iron catalyst, NH₃ removed by cooling). The student proposes conditions to maximize yield and explains the trade-offs.

- **Q:** Why not run the Haber process at room temperature if low temperature favors ammonia?
  **A:** The reaction would be far too slow; a moderate temperature with a catalyst gives an acceptable rate with a reasonable yield.
- **Q:** Why does high pressure increase the yield?
  **A:** The product side has fewer gas molecules (2 vs. 4), so increasing pressure shifts toward ammonia.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on Le Chatelier.

*How to teach:* Give the Le Chatelier quiz and collect the design proposal.

- **Q:** For CO(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ CH₄(g) + H₂O(g), which way does decreasing pressure shift it?
  **A:** Left, toward the side with more gas molecules (4 vs. 2).

**Assessment:** Design proposal and quiz.

**Answer key:** Design proposal rubric: 4 = For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (ΔH = −92 kJ): high pressure (shifts toward fewer gas moles, 2 vs. 4); removing NH₃ by condensing it; adding excess N₂/H₂; a compromise moderate temperature (~400–450 °C) because low temperature favors yield but is too slow; iron catalyst for rate; trade-offs of cost and safety of high pressure. Quiz key: 1) Adding H₂ shifts right. 2) Removing NH₃ shifts right. 3) Raising temperature of an exothermic reaction shifts left. 4) Increasing pressure for N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ shifts right. 5) Increasing pressure for H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI: no shift (2 gas moles each side). 6) Catalyst: no shift. 7) Cold soda holds more CO₂ because CO₂ dissolving is exothermic / gas solubility is higher at low temperature. 8) Adding acid to cabbage indicator turns it pink-red (shifts the indicator's equilibrium to its acid form).

## Spanish III: Cláusulas con si

**Prepare:** Chart the imperfect subjunctive: take the ellos preterite, drop -ron, add -ra, -ras, -ra, -'ramos, -rais, -ran (hablaran → hablara; tuvieron → tuviera; fueron → fuera). Choose a song with si clauses and print the lyrics (use only the portion you need). Write the Unit 8 test (technology, conditional, media, imperfect subjunctive, si clauses).

**Watch for:**
- Using the conditional in the si clause (*Si tendría dinero): Si tuviera dinero, viajaría.
- Missing the accent on the nosotros form: habláramos, tuviéramos, fuéramos.
- Mixing types: si + present → present/future/command (real); si + imperfect subjunctive → conditional (hypothetical).

### Day 1 (Monday): Imperfecto de subjuntivo
*Student:* Learn the imperfect subjunctive from preterite ellos form.

*How to teach:* Teach formation from the ellos preterite; your child conjugates 20 verbs.

- **Q:** Give the imperfect subjunctive yo form of hablar, tener, hacer, and ir.
  **A:** hablara, tuviera, hiciera, fuera
- **Q:** Give the nosotros form of estar.
  **A:** estuviéramos

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Si clauses
*Student:* Write 10 sentences 'Si yo fuera…, …'.

*How to teach:* Teach si + imperfect subjunctive + conditional; your child writes 10 'Si yo fuera…' sentences.

- **Q:** Translate: 'If I were rich, I would buy a house for my parents.'
  **A:** Si yo fuera rico/a, compraría una casa para mis padres.
- **Q:** Translate: 'If I had a dog, I would walk it every day.'
  **A:** Si tuviera un perro, lo pasearía todos los días.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Canción
*Student:* Analyze lyrics of a song with si clauses.

*How to teach:* Listen to a song with si clauses; your child highlights each si clause in the lyrics and explains whether it is real or hypothetical.

- **Q:** How do you tell a real si clause from a hypothetical one?
  **A:** Real: si + present indicative (Si puedo, voy). Hypothetical/contrary to fact: si + imperfect subjunctive with the conditional (Si pudiera, iría).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Ensayo corto
*Student:* Write 'Si pudiera cambiar el mundo…' (200 words).

*How to teach:* Your child writes 'Si pudiera cambiar el mundo…' (200 words).

- **Q:** Write an opening sentence.
  **A:** Example: Si pudiera cambiar el mundo, eliminaría el hambre y daría educación a todos los niños.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Unit 8 test.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test.

- **Q:** Correct: 'Si yo tendría alas, volaré.'
  **A:** Si yo tuviera alas, volaría.

**Assessment:** Unit 8 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 8 test sample key: 1) tener/yo — tuviera 2) ser/ir/ellos — fueran 3) poder/nosotros — pudiéramos 4) decir/tú — dijeras 5) Si yo fuera presidente, cambiaría las leyes 6) Si tuviéramos tiempo, iríamos al cine 7) Si llueve mañana, me quedaré en casa 8) ¿Qué harías si ganaras la lotería? 9) media vocabulary: el titular, el noticiero, la noticia falsa. Essay rubric (200 words, 'Si pudiera cambiar el mundo…'): 4 = 6+ correct si clauses, clear ideas, varied vocabulary; 3 = minor errors; 2 = few si clauses or many errors; 1 = incomplete.

*How to teach:* Storyboard 12+ shots mapped to song sections.

*How to teach:* Shoot all footage in one session with extra takes.

*Student:* Edit cuts to land on the beat by marking beats on the timeline; vary pacing between verse and chorus.

*How to teach:* Edit to the beat by marking beats on the timeline.

### Day 5 (Friday): Draft screening

## PE 10 (Course 2): Volleyball skills

**Prepare:** Volleyball (or a softer trainer ball), a wall, a net or rope tied about 7 ft 4 in (girls' height) to 7 ft 11 in (men's) — a lower rope is fine for practice — and target zones taped or marked.

**Watch for:**
- Students swing their arms for a forearm pass. Keep a flat, still platform and move the legs to aim.
- Students set with palms. Use finger pads in a triangle/window shape above the forehead.
- Students hit the ball twice in a row. A player can't contact the ball twice consecutively (except after a block).

### Day 1 (Monday): Forearm pass
*Student:* Pass against a wall 100 times using a flat platform, then pass to a partner.

*How to teach:* Pass against a wall 100 times using a flat platform, then pass to a partner.

- **Q:** How do you aim a forearm pass?
  **A:** Angle the platform toward the target and move your feet to face it; don't swing the arms.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Set
*Student:* Practice overhead setting to a target 10 feet high, 50 reps.

*How to teach:* Practice overhead setting to a target 10 feet high, 50 reps.

- **Q:** Where should you contact the ball when setting?
  **A:** Just above the forehead, with finger pads, then extend arms and legs.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Serve
*Student:* Practice underhand and overhand serves, scoring hits into target zones.

*How to teach:* Practice underhand and overhand serves into target zones.

- **Q:** What makes an overhand float serve 'float'?
  **A:** Hitting the center of the ball with a firm, flat hand and no spin.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Modified game
*Student:* Play 2-on-2 or 3-on-3 volleyball with a rope net, requiring three touches.

*How to teach:* Play 2-on-2 or 3-on-3 with a rope net, requiring three touches.

- **Q:** What's the maximum number of team contacts before the ball must cross the net?
  **A:** Three (a block doesn't count as one).

### Day 5 (Friday): Skill test
*Student:* Skill test: 10 passes, 10 sets, 10 serves to target.

*How to teach:* Give the skill test.

- **Q:** What's the ideal three-touch sequence?
  **A:** Pass (bump), set, attack (hit).

**Assessment:** Skill test.

**Answer key:** Skill test: 10 forearm passes to a target (7+ proficient), 10 sets to a 10-foot height and target (7+), 10 serves into target zones (6+). Technique: ready position, platform angle toward target, set from forehead with extension of arms and legs, serve with consistent toss and firm contact.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Nutrition for health and performance

**Prepare:** Gather three packaged-food labels, open MyPlate.gov (MyPlate Plan for the student's age, sex and activity), and plan a grocery budget for the meal plan. Parental note: Day 4 covers eating disorders; avoid weight-focused comments and watch for warning signs.

**Watch for:**
- Students compare nutrients without checking serving size. Compare per serving and note servings per container.
- Students think all fat or all carbohydrates are bad. Active teens need carbohydrates for energy, protein for repair, and healthy fats; focus on whole foods and limiting added sugar and sodium.
- Students think eating disorders only affect thin girls. They affect all genders, sizes and backgrounds; they are serious illnesses, not choices.

### Day 1 (Monday): Food labels
*Student:* Read three food labels and compare serving size, added sugars, sodium and fiber.

*How to teach:* The student compares three labels for serving size, calories, added sugars, sodium and fiber, using %DV (5% low, 20% high).

- **Q:** A cereal has 12 g added sugars per serving (24% DV) and the bowl you eat is 2 servings. How much added sugar is that?
  **A:** 24 g, about 48% of the daily value.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Food log
*Student:* Log meals for three days and compare to MyPlate recommendations for an active teen.

*How to teach:* The student logs three days of meals and compares to MyPlate recommendations.

- **Q:** Which food groups do teens most often under-eat?
  **A:** Vegetables, fruits and dairy (calcium) are commonly low.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fueling performance
*Student:* Plan pre- and post-activity snacks and hydration for a sports day.

*How to teach:* Plan pre- and post-activity snacks and hydration for a sports day.

- **Q:** What's a good pre-activity snack 1–2 hours before exercise?
  **A:** Easily digested carbohydrates with a little protein, such as a banana with peanut butter or yogurt with fruit.
- **Q:** How can you check hydration?
  **A:** Pale yellow urine; drink water before, during and after activity.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Disordered eating
*Student:* Learn warning signs of eating disorders and where to get help.

*How to teach:* The student learns warning signs of eating disorders and where to get help.

- **Q:** Name three warning signs of an eating disorder.
  **A:** Preoccupation with weight or food, skipping meals or rigid rules, secret eating, compensatory exercise or vomiting, rapid weight change, withdrawal from social eating.
- **Q:** Where can someone get help?
  **A:** A parent or trusted adult, doctor, school counselor, or the ANAD helpline or similar national organizations.

### Day 5 (Friday): Meal plan
*Student:* Create a one-day balanced meal plan within a budget and cook one meal.

*How to teach:* The student creates a one-day balanced meal plan within a budget and cooks one meal.

- **Q:** Why include protein after exercise?
  **A:** It provides amino acids to repair and build muscle.

**Assessment:** Meal plan.

**Answer key:** Meal plan rubric: 4 = One-day plan covers all MyPlate groups in appropriate amounts for an active teen (roughly 2,200–3,200 calories depending on age, sex and activity), includes breakfast, lunch, dinner and snacks, limits added sugar (<10% of calories) and sodium (<2,300 mg), includes hydration; cost calculated within the set budget; one meal cooked with a photo and reflection. 3 = balanced but missing cost or a group. 2 = unbalanced or incomplete. 1 = minimal. Note key facts: percent Daily Value of 5% or less is low, 20% or more is high.

## Computer Science Principles: Emerging technologies and computing innovations

**Prepare:** Help the student choose an innovation and find credible sources (major news outlets, government reports, academic or Pew Research). Prepare the Unit 7 test.

**Watch for:**
- Treating all effects as intended. Many important effects are unintended (e.g. ride-sharing increasing traffic congestion).
- Using opinion blogs as evidence. Check author, publisher, date and evidence for credibility.
- Thinking an innovation is purely good or purely bad. Most have both beneficial and harmful effects for different groups.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose an innovation
*Student:* Pick a computing innovation (generative AI, self-driving cars, facial recognition, ride-sharing apps) and write three research questions.

*How to teach:* The student chooses an innovation and writes three research questions (What data does it use? Who benefits? Who is harmed?).

- **Q:** What makes something a 'computing innovation'?
  **A:** An innovation that includes a program as an integral part of its function (e.g. a self-driving car, a social media app, facial recognition).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Research
*Student:* Find at least three credible sources and record evidence on data used, benefits, harms and who is affected.

*How to teach:* Find three credible sources and record evidence in a notes table with citations.

- **Q:** Name two checks for whether a source is credible.
  **A:** Who wrote/published it and their expertise, whether it cites evidence, and how recent it is (plus checking other sources agree).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Effects analysis
*Student:* Build a table of intended vs. unintended and beneficial vs. harmful effects on social, economic and cultural life.

*How to teach:* Build a table of effects: intended vs. unintended and beneficial vs. harmful across social, economic and cultural life.

- **Q:** Give an unintended effect of ride-sharing apps.
  **A:** Studies have found they added to traffic congestion in some cities and reduced transit ridership — not what the companies intended.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Write
*Student:* Write a 600-word impact essay with citations taking a position on how the innovation should be used or regulated.

*How to teach:* Write the 600-word essay with citations taking a position on use or regulation.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a beneficial-intended and a harmful-unintended effect?
  **A:** Beneficial-intended is a planned positive outcome (e.g. faster rides); harmful-unintended is a negative outcome nobody planned (e.g. lost income for taxi drivers).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 test

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test in the assessment key.

- **Q:** Why might generative AI raise both economic benefits and harms?
  **A:** It can make work faster and cheaper (benefit) but may displace some jobs, spread misinformation, or use copyrighted data without permission (harms).

**Assessment:** Unit 7 assessment and impact essay.

**Answer key:** Unit 7 test (12 items): 1) PII definition. 2) Third-party cookies track across sites. 3) CCPA right to delete. 4) Caesar 'BAD' shift 1 → 'CBE'. 5) Frequency analysis cracks substitution ciphers. 6) Public key shared/private key secret. 7) Phishing = tricking users into revealing info. 8) Ransomware encrypts files for payment. 9) MFA requires multiple factors. 10) DDoS floods a server. 11) Example beneficial and harmful effect of an innovation. 12) Unintended consequence = an outcome not planned by the creators. Mastery 10+/12. Impact essay rubric (4 levels, ~600 words): 4 = clear position on use/regulation, at least three credible cited sources, discusses data the innovation uses, benefits and harms to specific groups, intended and unintended effects, addresses a counterargument; 3 = all elements, less depth; 2 = few sources or one-sided; 1 = incomplete.
