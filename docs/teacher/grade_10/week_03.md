# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 3

## English 10: Time and twist: 'An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge' and 'The Necklace'

**Prepare:** Print 'An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge' (Bierce) and 'The Necklace' (Maupassant) from Gutenberg. Find the 1961 short film by Robert Enrico (often shown as a Twilight Zone episode; check library streaming). Parent preview: Owl Creek depicts an execution by hanging; the film shows the hanging at the end.

**Watch for:**
- Missing that Part III of Owl Creek is imagined. Point to the clues: unnaturally sharp senses, strange stars, the endless road, the pain in his neck.
- Reading 'The Necklace' as simply 'don't lie.' Push to the deeper theme of class aspiration and appearances.

### Day 1 (Monday): Read Bierce
*Student:* Read 'An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge' and mark where time slows, jumps back, and resumes; sketch a timeline of real vs. imagined time.

*How to teach:* Explain the three-part structure before reading. The student marks where time slows (the ticking watch), jumps back (Part II flashback), and the long escape, and sketches a real-time vs. imagined-time timeline.

- **Q:** How did Peyton Farquhar end up on the bridge?
  **A:** A Federal scout disguised as a Confederate soldier told him about the bridge, and he was caught trying to burn it.
- **Q:** What actually happens to Farquhar at the end?
  **A:** He is hanged; his body swings beneath the bridge. The escape was imagined in the moment before death.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Film comparison
*Student:* Watch the 1961 short film of Owl Creek Bridge (library/streaming) and list three choices the director made differently than Bierce (RL.9-10.7 preview).

*How to teach:* Watch the film (about 25 minutes). The student lists three director choices different from Bierce. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Name one major difference between the 1961 film and the story.
  **A:** The film leaves out Part II's backstory about the scout (and uses almost no dialogue, adding a song during the escape).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Read Maupassant
*Student:* Read 'The Necklace' and annotate details of 19th-century French class and status that drive Mathilde's choices.

*How to teach:* Briefly explain class in 19th-century France (clerks vs. wealthy society). The student reads 'The Necklace' and annotates details about status that drive Mathilde. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Why does Mathilde borrow the necklace?
  **A:** She feels ashamed to go to the ball without jewels and wants to look rich among wealthy guests.
- **Q:** What is the twist at the end?
  **A:** Madame Forestier reveals the necklace was paste (fake), worth at most 500 francs, while the Loisels spent ten years paying off 36,000 francs for a real diamond replacement.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compare endings
*Student:* Write a 250-word comparison of the twist endings: how does each author plant clues so the surprise feels earned?

*How to teach:* First have the student list three clues for each twist in a two-column chart. Then they write the 250-word comparison with a topic sentence that names both authors' techniques, one quote per story, and a closing sentence on which twist is better prepared. Check that the response analyzes structure, not just plot.

- **Q:** What clue in 'The Necklace' hints the necklace might not be valuable?
  **A:** Madame Forestier lends it easily, and when it is returned she doesn't even open the case.

### Day 5 (Friday): Socratic discussion
*Student:* Lead a 15-minute discussion with family on 'Which twist is more meaningful, and why?' using three prepared questions and evidence.

*How to teach:* The student writes three open questions the night before (questions with more than one defensible answer) and marks evidence for each. During the 15-minute discussion they lead, cite the text at least twice and invite others in; you tick the checklist in the assessment key and debrief for two minutes afterward.

- **Q:** What does each twist reveal about its main character?
  **A:** Farquhar's romantic dreams of heroism and home; Mathilde's vanity and how appearances cost her years of hardship.

**Assessment:** Comparison response scored on evidence and analysis of structure; discussion participation checklist.

**Answer key:** Comparison response rubric: 4 = explains specific planted clues in both stories (Owl Creek: time slowing, ticking watch, hyper-sharp senses, unfamiliar stars, swollen neck; The Necklace: Madame Forestier's easy offer, her not opening the case on return, Mathilde's focus on appearances) and how structure (Bierce's nonlinear sections; Maupassant's chronological ten years) makes the twist earned; 3 = clues identified with partial analysis; 2 = summary of endings; 1 = off-topic. Discussion checklist: prepared 3 open questions; cited text at least twice; responded to others; invited another speaker.

## Geometry: Midpoint, distance, and inscribed polygons

**Prepare:** Graph paper, compass, calculator. Review the Pythagorean theorem with the student before Day 2. Prepare the Unit 1 test items listed in the assessment key.

**Watch for:**
- Subtracting coordinates in mixed order in the distance formula. Remind the student squaring removes sign, but both differences must be in the same order (x2 − x1 and y2 − y1).
- For partitioning, using the ratio 2:3 as the fraction 2/3. The point is 2/5 of the way, because the whole has 2 + 3 = 5 parts.

### Day 1 (Monday): Midpoint formula
*Student:* Derive and apply the midpoint formula, including finding an endpoint given a midpoint.

*How to teach:* Show that the midpoint averages the coordinates, using a segment on graph paper. Practice finding midpoints, then the reverse problem: given a midpoint and one endpoint, find the other by 'stepping' the same change again.

- **Q:** Find the midpoint of (2, −3) and (8, 5).
  **A:** ((2 + 8)/2, (−3 + 5)/2) = (5, 1).
- **Q:** M(4, 1) is the midpoint of AB and A = (1, −2). Find B.
  **A:** From A to M is +3 in x and +3 in y, so B = (7, 4).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Distance formula
*Student:* Derive the distance formula from the Pythagorean theorem and compute perimeters of polygons on the grid.

*How to teach:* Draw a segment on the grid, build a right triangle under it, and use a² + b² = c² to derive d = √((x2 − x1)² + (y2 − y1)²). Then have the student find the perimeter of a polygon given by vertices.

- **Q:** Find the distance from (1, 2) to (7, 10).
  **A:** √(6² + 8²) = √100 = 10.
- **Q:** Find the perimeter of the triangle with vertices (0, 0), (3, 0), (0, 4).
  **A:** Sides 3, 4 and 5, so the perimeter is 12.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Partitioning
*Student:* Find the point that divides a segment in ratios like 2:3 using the coordinate method.

*How to teach:* Explain that a 2:3 ratio splits the segment into 5 equal parts, so the point is 2/5 of the way from A. Compute A + (2/5)(B − A) in each coordinate, and check by graphing.

- **Q:** Find the point dividing A(0, 0) to B(10, 5) in a 2:3 ratio from A.
  **A:** 2/5 of the change (10, 5) is (4, 2), so the point is (4, 2).
- **Q:** Find the point 1/3 of the way from (−3, 6) to (9, 0).
  **A:** Change is (12, −6); 1/3 is (4, −2); the point is (1, 4).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Inscribed polygons
*Student:* Construct an equilateral triangle, square and regular hexagon inscribed in a circle.

*How to teach:* Construct the hexagon by marking the radius six times around the circle; connect every other vertex for the equilateral triangle. For the square, draw a diameter, construct its perpendicular bisector to get a second diameter, and connect the four endpoints.

- **Q:** Why can you step the radius around a circle exactly six times?
  **A:** Each chord equal to the radius forms an equilateral triangle with the center, giving a 60° central angle; 360° ÷ 60° = 6.
- **Q:** How do you inscribe a square in a circle?
  **A:** Draw two perpendicular diameters and connect their four endpoints.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 test
*Student:* Unit 1 test: definitions, constructions, midpoint and distance.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 test from the assessment key (about 50 minutes, compass allowed). Grade together and log missed skills for review. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** What does the midpoint formula have in common with an average?
  **A:** Each coordinate of the midpoint is the average (mean) of the two endpoints' coordinates.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test (sample items with answers): 1) Midpoint of (−4, 7) and (6, −1) → (1, 3). 2) M(2, 5) is the midpoint of AB with A(−1, 8); find B → (5, 2). 3) Distance from (−2, 1) to (4, 9) → √(36 + 64) = 10. 4) Point 1/4 of the way from A(0, 0) to B(8, 12) → (2, 3). 5) Complement of 27° → 63°. 6) Construction: perpendicular bisector of a given segment (graded with the Week 2 rubric). 7) Explain why the inscribed hexagon construction uses the radius as the side length → a regular hexagon is six equilateral triangles whose sides equal the radius. Mastery: 80% or better.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Energy from fuels

**Prepare:** Gather an empty soda can, a paperclip bent into a stand, a cork or clay base, a ring stand or stack of books with a pencil to hang the can, a thermometer, a 100 mL measuring cup, cheese puffs, cashews (check for nut allergies — use another snack if needed), matches, a lighter, a scale reading to 0.01 g if possible, and nutrition labels. Prepare a fire-safe surface. Look up the California Energy Commission's 'Total System Electric Generation' page for Day 4.

**Watch for:**
- Students mix up the food Calorie (capital C, = 1 kilocalorie) and the calorie. Show that a 100 Calorie snack contains 100,000 calories, or about 418 kJ.
- Students think heat and temperature are the same. Heat (q, in joules) is energy transferred; temperature measures average particle kinetic energy. The same heat raises a small mass of water more than a large one.
- Students expect their calorimeter result to match the label. Most of the heat escapes to the air and can, so home calorimeters typically capture well under half of the label value; this is the error to analyze, not a mistake.

### Day 1 (Monday): Energy units
*Student:* Convert between joules, calories and food Calories and read nutrition labels for energy content.

*How to teach:* Teach the conversions 1 cal = 4.184 J and 1 food Calorie = 1 kcal = 4184 J. Model one label conversion, then have the student convert three snack labels to kJ per serving and kJ per gram. Check that the student multiplies, not divides, when going from Calories to joules.

- **Q:** A granola bar has 190 Calories. How many kilojoules is that?
  **A:** 190 × 4.184 = 795 kJ (about 795,000 J).
- **Q:** How many calories (small c) are in one food Calorie?
  **A:** 1000 calories.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Food calorimeter
*Student:* Build a soda-can calorimeter and burn a cheese puff and a cashew to heat 100 mL of water.

*How to teach:* Build the calorimeter: hang the can with 100 mL (100 g) of water above a food sample stuck on a paperclip stand. Record the water's starting temperature and the food's mass, ignite the food, let it burn out, stir and record the highest temperature, then reweigh the remains. Repeat with the cashew.

- **Q:** Why do we measure the food mass both before and after burning?
  **A:** To find the mass that actually burned; energy per gram uses only the mass consumed.
- **Q:** Why use water as the substance being heated?
  **A:** Its specific heat (4.184 J/g·°C) is known and it is easy to measure, so the energy absorbed can be calculated from its temperature change.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Calculate q
*Student:* Use q = mcΔT to compute energy released per gram and compare to label values; explain error.

*How to teach:* Write q = mcΔT on the board and model it with the student's cheese-puff data. The student calculates energy per gram for both foods, converts the label value to J/g, computes percent error, and lists reasons the measured value is low.

- **Q:** 100.0 g of water warms from 21.0 °C to 39.0 °C. How much heat did it absorb?
  **A:** q = 100.0 × 4.184 × 18.0 = 7530 J (7.53 kJ).
- **Q:** A cashew lost 0.60 g while releasing 7530 J to the water. What is the measured energy per gram?
  **A:** 7530 ÷ 0.60 = 12,550 J/g ≈ 13 kJ/g (about 3.0 Cal/g); the label value is about 23 kJ/g, so much heat was lost.
- **Q:** Give one way to reduce the error.
  **A:** Shield the flame and can from drafts (e.g., a foil chimney), put the can closer to the flame, or use a lid to reduce heat lost to the air.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fuels of California
*Student:* Research CA's energy mix (CA Energy Commission) and compare energy per gram of gasoline, natural gas and hydrogen.

*How to teach:* Have the student find California's current electricity sources on the California Energy Commission site and record the top sources (natural gas, solar, wind, hydro, nuclear, etc.). Then compare energy per gram of fuels in a table and discuss why hydrogen is energy-dense by mass but hard to store.

- **Q:** Rank gasoline, natural gas (methane) and hydrogen by energy released per gram.
  **A:** Hydrogen (~120–142 kJ/g) > natural gas (~50–55 kJ/g) > gasoline (~46 kJ/g).
- **Q:** What product does hydrogen combustion make, and why does that matter for climate?
  **A:** Only water (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O), so burning it produces no CO₂; however, how the hydrogen is produced can still create emissions.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on energy calculations.

*How to teach:* Give the five-question energy quiz with a calculator and formula sheet (q = mcΔT, c of water = 4.184 J/g·°C, 1 Cal = 4.184 kJ). Grade together and file corrections in the lab notebook.

- **Q:** How much energy is needed to warm 250 g of water by 4.0 °C?
  **A:** q = 250 × 4.184 × 4.0 = 4184 J ≈ 4.2 kJ.

**Assessment:** Calorimetry lab report.

**Answer key:** Lab report rubric (4 levels): 4 = Data table with mass of food before/after, water mass, initial/final temperatures; q = mcΔT calculated correctly for both foods (e.g., 100 g × 4.184 J/g·°C × 18.0 °C = 7530 J); energy per gram = q ÷ mass burned; comparison to label (label kJ/g = Calories per serving × 4.184 ÷ grams per serving); percent error; at least two specific error sources (heat lost to air and can, incomplete burning, soot) with a suggested improvement (shield, lid, closer can). 3 = correct calculations with general error analysis. 2 = calculation errors or missing comparison. 1 = incomplete data. Friday quiz key: 1) 500 Cal = 2092 kJ. 2) 200 g water warmed 20 °C → 45 °C: q = 200 × 4.184 × 25 = 20,900 J (20.9 kJ). 3) 1.20 g of food heats 50.0 g water by 30.0 °C: q = 6276 J; 6276 ÷ 1.20 = 5230 J/g (5.23 kJ/g, about 1.25 Cal/g). 4) 4184 J added to 100 g water: ΔT = 4184 ÷ (100 × 4.184) = 10.0 °C. 5) Which has more energy per gram, gasoline or hydrogen? Hydrogen (~120–142 kJ/g vs. ~46 kJ/g).

## Modern World History: The Enlightenment and the English Revolution

**Prepare:** Excerpts of Magna Carta (1215; clauses 12, 39, 40) and the English Bill of Rights (1689) from the Avalon Project or British Library; Locke's Second Treatise (chapters on the state of nature and dissolution of government); Hobbes's Leviathan ch. 13; Montesquieu's Spirit of Laws (Book XI ch. 6); Rousseau's Social Contract (Book I).

**Watch for:**
- Treating Hobbes and Locke as agreeing because both use a social contract. Hobbes wants an absolute sovereign; Locke wants limited government and a right to revolution.
- Thinking Magna Carta created democracy. It protected barons' rights and limited the king, a first step toward rule of law.

### Day 1 (Monday): Scientific Revolution to Enlightenment
*Student:* Read an overview and create a timeline from Newton to Rousseau.

*How to teach:* Read a short overview of the Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment. The student builds a timeline: Copernicus (1543), Galileo, Newton's Principia (1687), Hobbes (1651), Locke (1689–90), Montesquieu (1748), Voltaire, Rousseau (1762), and explains how applying reason to nature led to applying reason to government.

- **Q:** How did the Scientific Revolution lead to the Enlightenment?
  **A:** Thinkers saw that reason and observation could discover natural laws, so they applied reason to society and government, looking for natural rights and better institutions.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Magna Carta & Bill of Rights
*Student:* Analyze key clauses of the Magna Carta and English Bill of Rights and list the rights they protect.

*How to teach:* Read key clauses together. The student lists rights protected by each document and who they protected. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What does Magna Carta clause 39 guarantee?
  **A:** No free man may be imprisoned, stripped of rights or property, or punished except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land.
- **Q:** Name two limits the English Bill of Rights (1689) placed on the monarch.
  **A:** The king could not suspend laws, levy taxes, or keep a standing army in peacetime without Parliament's consent; elections must be free; no excessive bail or cruel and unusual punishment.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Locke & Hobbes
*Student:* Compare Hobbes's Leviathan and Locke's Second Treatise views of human nature and government.

*How to teach:* Read Hobbes chapter 13 and Locke's state-of-nature passages. The student charts each thinker's view of human nature, purpose of government, and what happens if government fails. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** How does Hobbes describe life without government?
  **A:** 'Solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short' — a war of all against all.
- **Q:** According to Locke, what may people do if government violates their natural rights?
  **A:** They may alter or overthrow it and form a new government.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Montesquieu & Rousseau
*Student:* Read excerpts from The Spirit of Laws and The Social Contract and summarize each idea.

*How to teach:* Read excerpts of Montesquieu and Rousseau. The student summarizes each main idea in one sentence and connects it to a U.S. institution. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What is Montesquieu's main idea and its U.S. reflection?
  **A:** Separating legislative, executive and judicial powers prevents tyranny — reflected in the three branches and checks and balances.
- **Q:** What does Rousseau mean by the 'general will'?
  **A:** Legitimate government follows the collective will of the people for the common good, not any individual's private interest.

### Day 5 (Friday): Philosophers' dinner party
*Student:* Write a dialogue among four philosophers debating 'What makes government legitimate?'

*How to teach:* The student writes a one- to two-page dialogue among Hobbes, Locke, Montesquieu and Rousseau at a dinner party debating 'What makes government legitimate?' Score with the rubric for accuracy. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Which philosopher would most strongly reject a right to revolution?
  **A:** Hobbes — he argued subjects should obey the sovereign to avoid chaos.

**Assessment:** Dialogue assessed for accuracy of ideas.

**Answer key:** Dialogue rubric (accuracy of ideas): 4 = each philosopher's view stated accurately in his own terms — Hobbes (life in the state of nature 'solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short'; people give power to an absolute sovereign for security), Locke (natural rights of life, liberty and property; government by consent; right to overthrow a government that violates rights), Montesquieu (separation of legislative, executive and judicial powers to prevent tyranny), Rousseau ('Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains'; legitimacy comes from the general will of the people); philosophers respond to one another; 3 = mostly accurate; 2 = one or more views confused; 1 = superficial.

## Music & Media Arts: Scales, keys and intervals

**Prepare:** Keyboard app, musictheory.net (Scales, Key Signatures, Intervals), a circle-of-fifths printout, and song references for intervals.

**Watch for:**
- Students think there is no half step between E–F and B–C. Those white-key pairs are half steps (no black key between).
- Students read key signatures by counting accidentals only. Sharp key: the note a half step above the last sharp. Flat key: the second-to-last flat (F major has one flat, B♭).
- Students name intervals only by letter distance. Quality matters too: C–E is a major 3rd (4 half steps); C–E♭ is a minor 3rd (3 half steps).

### Day 1 (Monday): Whole & half steps
*Student:* Build major scales using W-W-H-W-W-W-H on a keyboard.

*How to teach:* The student builds C, G, D and F major scales on the keyboard using W-W-H-W-W-W-H.

- **Q:** Build the D major scale.
  **A:** D E F♯ G A B C♯ D.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Key signatures
*Student:* Learn the circle of fifths and identify sharp key signatures.

*How to teach:* Teach the circle of fifths for sharp keys and the order of sharps.

- **Q:** How many sharps are in E major, and which?
  **A:** Four: F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Flat keys
*Student:* Identify flat key signatures.

*How to teach:* The student identifies flat key signatures.

- **Q:** A key signature has B♭, E♭ and A♭. What major key is it?
  **A:** E♭ major (second-to-last flat).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Intervals
*Student:* Identify and sing intervals using song references (e.g., 'Here Comes the Bride' = P4).

*How to teach:* Identify and sing intervals using song references.

- **Q:** What interval begins 'Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star' (on 'twinkle, twinkle')?
  **A:** A perfect 5th.
- **Q:** How many half steps are in a perfect 4th?
  **A:** Five.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on scales and intervals.

*How to teach:* Give the scales and intervals quiz.

- **Q:** What's the relative minor of C major?
  **A:** A minor (no sharps or flats).

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Major scale pattern — W-W-H-W-W-W-H. 2) G major scale — G A B C D E F♯ G. 3) D major — D E F♯ G A B C♯ D. 4) F major — F G A B♭ C D E F. 5) Order of sharps — F C G D A E B. 6) Order of flats — B E A D G C F. 7) Key with 3 sharps — A major. 8) Key with 4 flats — A♭ major. 9) C to G — perfect 5th. 10) C to F — perfect 4th. 11) C to E — major 3rd. 12) C to A — major 6th. 13) 'Here Comes the Bride' opening — P4; 'Twinkle Twinkle' first leap — P5; 'Happy Birthday' first step — M2; 'Somewhere Over the Rainbow' first leap — octave.

## PE 10 (Course 2): SMART goals and a personal plan draft

**Prepare:** Print the baseline FITNESSGRAM log and a 4-week plan template. Have the fitness log ready. Find a 20-minute yoga/stretching video for Day 4.

**Watch for:**
- Students write goals they can't measure ('get fit'). SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.
- Students increase everything at once. Progressive overload means small, gradual increases (about 10% per week in volume).
- Students count only formal workouts toward 60 minutes. Walking, biking to errands and active chores count.

### Day 1 (Monday): SMART goals
*Student:* Write three SMART goals based on baseline results.

*How to teach:* Model one SMART goal; the student writes three from baseline results.

- **Q:** Rewrite 'get stronger' as a SMART goal.
  **A:** 'Increase 90° push-ups from 12 to 18 by December 15 by doing push-up workouts 3 days per week.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plan
*Student:* Draft a 4-week plan with cardio, strength and flexibility days.

*How to teach:* The student drafts a 4-week plan with cardio, strength and flexibility days, applying FITT and progression.

- **Q:** Why not do intense strength training on the same muscles two days in a row?
  **A:** Muscles need about 48 hours to recover and adapt.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Workout 1
*Student:* Do the first planned workout (40 minutes) and log it.

*How to teach:* The student completes the first 40-minute planned workout and logs it (warm-up, main set, cool-down, RPE).

- **Q:** What should a cool-down include?
  **A:** Light activity to gradually lower heart rate, then static stretching.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Flexibility
*Student:* Complete a 20-minute static stretching and yoga routine.

*How to teach:* Lead a 20-minute static stretching and yoga routine (hold stretches 15–30 seconds, no bouncing).

- **Q:** How long should you hold a static stretch?
  **A:** About 15–30 seconds (up to 60), without bouncing, to the point of mild tension.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Review the week's activity log against the 60-minute daily guideline.

*How to teach:* The student totals the week's activity minutes against the 60-minutes-per-day guideline and adjusts the plan.

- **Q:** What three types of activity should teens include weekly?
  **A:** Aerobic activity daily, muscle-strengthening on at least 3 days, and bone-strengthening on at least 3 days.

**Assessment:** Plan and activity log.

**Answer key:** Plan rubric: 4 = Three SMART goals tied to baseline needs (e.g., 'Increase PACER from 32 to 40 laps by Week 18 by running 3×/week'); a 4-week plan that includes cardio (3–5 days), strength (2–3 non-consecutive days), flexibility (most days), rest/recovery, warm-up and cool-down, and progression each week; activity log with daily minutes. 3 = plan complete, progression unclear. 2 = missing a component. 1 = incomplete.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Occupant protection

**Prepare:** Gather a toy car (or cart), a board for a ramp, a raw egg (in a zip bag to contain mess), a rubber band, and a book as a barrier. Read the handbook sections on seat belts, child restraints and airbags.

**Watch for:**
- Students think a lap belt alone or holding on is enough. Only a properly worn lap-and-shoulder belt (lap belt low on hips, shoulder belt across the chest, never behind the back) spreads crash forces.
- Students think airbags replace seat belts. Airbags are supplemental; without a belt a person can be thrown into the deploying bag or out of the vehicle.
- Students think short trips are safe without a belt. Most crashes happen close to home at lower speeds.

### Day 1 (Monday): Crash physics
*Student:* Run an egg 'passenger' crash demo on a ramp with and without a rubber-band seat belt and explain inertia.

*How to teach:* Set the egg 'passenger' in the toy car and roll it down the ramp into a book, first without and then with a rubber band 'belt'. Repeat at a higher ramp. The student explains using Newton's first law (inertia).

- **Q:** Why does the unbelted egg fly forward when the car stops?
  **A:** Inertia — the egg keeps moving at the car's speed until something stops it (the 'dashboard', windshield or ground).
- **Q:** How does a seat belt reduce injury?
  **A:** It stops the body with the car, spreading force over the strong bones of the hips and chest and lengthening the stopping time.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Belt & airbag laws
*Student:* Read handbook rules on seat belts and child restraints (children under 2 rear-facing; under 8 in a car seat or booster in the back seat).

*How to teach:* The student reads handbook rules and makes a chart of seat-belt and child-restraint law by age and size.

- **Q:** At what age/size can a child ride in a car without a booster seat in California?
  **A:** Age 8, or 4 feet 9 inches tall, if the seat belt fits properly.
- **Q:** Who is responsible if a passenger under 16 is not buckled?
  **A:** The driver can be cited.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Airbag safety
*Student:* Explain safe seating distance from airbags and why children ride in back.

*How to teach:* Explain airbag deployment speed (up to about 200 mph). The student measures his or her seated distance from the steering wheel and explains why children under 13 should ride in back.

- **Q:** How far should the driver sit from the airbag?
  **A:** At least 10 inches between the breastbone and the center of the steering wheel.
- **Q:** Why should a rear-facing infant seat never be placed in front of an active airbag?
  **A:** The deploying airbag strikes the back of the seat near the baby's head with enormous force and can kill the child.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Persuade
*Student:* Create a 30-second PSA script encouraging peers to buckle up every ride.

*How to teach:* The student writes and rehearses a 30-second PSA script with a hook, one statistic, and a clear call to action, then performs it for you.

- **Q:** What makes a PSA persuasive to teens?
  **A:** A relatable hook, an accurate, specific fact, and a simple action to take (e.g., 'Click it before you start it').

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 test
*Student:* Unit 1 test on licensing, controls and occupant protection.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 test. Grade together.

- **Q:** Where should the lap belt sit?
  **A:** Low and snug across the hips/upper thighs, not across the stomach.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test key (sample): 1) Permit age — 15½. 2) Provisional passenger rule — no passengers under 20 for the first 12 months unless accompanied by a licensed driver 25+, parent/guardian or instructor. 3) Night rule — 11 p.m.–5 a.m. 4) Supervised hours — 50 (10 night). 5) Purpose of the parking brake — hold the car when parked (and as an emergency backup). 6) Red dashboard light meaning — stop safely and address immediately. 7) Mirror method — BGE, outward until the car's side disappears. 8) Children under 2 — rear-facing seat (unless 40 lb or 40 in tall). 9) Children under 8 — car seat or booster in the back seat. 10) Who must wear seat belts — driver and all passengers. 11) Airbag distance — at least 10 inches between breastbone and steering wheel. 12) Explain inertia in a crash — the body keeps moving at the car's speed until something stops it; the belt stops it safely. Score 1 point each; 11–12 mastery.

## Computer Science Principles: Representing text and images

**Prepare:** Print or open an ASCII table (decimal and binary for A–Z, a–z, space). Open the Code.org Pixelation widget. Have grid paper and colored pencils. Prepare a short secret message in ASCII binary for the student to decode (e.g. 'HI' = 01001000 01001001).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking uppercase and lowercase letters share a code. In ASCII 'A' = 65 and 'a' = 97 — they differ by 32.
- Confusing resolution with color depth. Resolution = how many pixels; color depth = bits per pixel. Both increase file size.
- Believing hex is a different kind of data. Hex is just a shorter way to write binary: each hex digit = 4 bits, so #FF0000 is 24 bits.

### Day 1 (Monday): Encoding text
*Student:* Encode your name in ASCII binary and decode a partner's (or parent's) secret message.

*How to teach:* Show the ASCII table. Encode 'Hi' together: H = 72 = 01001000, i = 105 = 01101001. Have the student encode their name, then decode your prepared message. Check by converting each byte back to decimal and looking it up.

- **Q:** Encode 'CAB' in ASCII decimal and binary.
  **A:** C = 67 = 01000011, A = 65 = 01000001, B = 66 = 01000010.
- **Q:** What letter is 01001010?
  **A:** 64 + 8 + 2 = 74 = 'J'.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Unicode and emoji
*Student:* Look up the Unicode code points for three emoji and explain why ASCII alone was not enough for the world's languages.

*How to teach:* Explain that ASCII only has 128 characters — enough for English but not Chinese, Arabic, Hindi or emoji. Unicode assigns a code point (written U+XXXX) to over 140,000 characters. Have the student look up three emoji (e.g. 😀 U+1F600, ❤ U+2764, 👍 U+1F44D) and explain the need for Unicode.

- **Q:** Why wasn't ASCII enough for the world's languages?
  **A:** ASCII's 7 bits allow only 128 characters — just English letters, digits and symbols. Languages like Chinese have thousands of characters, so a larger system (Unicode) was needed.
- **Q:** What is the Unicode code point for the 'grinning face' emoji 😀?
  **A:** U+1F600.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Black-and-white images
*Student:* Use the Code.org Pixelation widget to draw a 10×10 black-and-white image and write its bits.

*How to teach:* In the Pixelation widget, set 10×10 and 1 bit per pixel. Model: 0 = white, 1 = black (or as the widget defines). Have the student draw a simple shape, then write out the 100 bits row by row on grid paper.

- **Q:** How many bits does a 10×10 black-and-white image need (ignoring the header)?
  **A:** 100 bits (1 bit per pixel × 100 pixels).
- **Q:** How many bits would an 8×8 black-and-white image need?
  **A:** 64 bits.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Color images
*Student:* Create a color image using hex RGB values and calculate the file size in bits and bytes at 24 bits per pixel.

*How to teach:* Explain RGB: each pixel mixes red, green and blue, each 0–255 (8 bits), so 24 bits per pixel; hex writes each channel as two digits (FF = 255). Have the student create a color image with hex values and compute file size.

- **Q:** What color is #FFFF00?
  **A:** Yellow (full red + full green, no blue).
- **Q:** Find the file size of a 20×20 image at 24 bits per pixel.
  **A:** 400 pixels × 24 = 9,600 bits = 1,200 bytes.
- **Q:** Convert hex 80 to decimal.
  **A:** 8 × 16 = 128.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on text and image representation; explain how changing bits-per-pixel affects color depth and size.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key (about 20 minutes). Then have the student write 3–4 sentences on how bits-per-pixel affects color depth and file size.

- **Q:** If you go from 8 bits per pixel to 24 bits per pixel, how many more colors are possible and what happens to file size?
  **A:** From 256 colors to 16,777,216; the file becomes 3 times larger (same number of pixels).

**Assessment:** Pixelation project and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) ASCII 'A' = 65 = 01000001. 2) Decode 01000011 01000001 01010100 → 'CAT' (67, 65, 84). 3) Standard ASCII uses 7 bits (128 characters), usually stored in 8-bit bytes. 4) Unicode was needed because 128/256 characters can't cover the world's scripts and emoji. 5) A 10×10 black-and-white image needs 100 bits. 6) #00FF00 is pure green. 7) A 100×100 image at 24 bits per pixel = 240,000 bits = 30,000 bytes. 8) Changing from 1 to 24 bits per pixel increases colors from 2 to about 16.7 million (2^24 = 16,777,216) and multiplies file size by 24. Explanation item: proficient answers state that more bits per pixel = more possible colors (2^n) but a proportionally larger file. Pixelation project rubric: 4 = correct image, header/size and bits written correctly; 3 = minor bit errors; 2 = image without correct bits; 1 = incomplete.
