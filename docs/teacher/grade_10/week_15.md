# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 15

## English 10: Things Fall Apart Part 1 (cont.): Tradition and change

**Prepare:** Things Fall Apart; glossary at the back of most editions.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Okonkwo's exile is punishment for murder. It's for an accidental killing — a 'female' crime — so he gets seven years.
- Assuming Igbo justice is arbitrary. The egwugwu trial shows a structured process.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ch. 9–10
*Student:* Read chapters 9–10 on Ezinma and the egwugwu trial; note how justice works in Umuofia.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 9–10. The student notes how justice works. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What happens in the egwugwu trial?
  **A:** The masked egwugwu hear a dispute between Uzowulu and his wife's family; they rule that Uzowulu must go to his in-laws with a pot of wine and beg his wife to return.
- **Q:** What is found in chapter 9?
  **A:** Ezinma's iyi-uwa, which the medicine man digs up, believed to end her ogbanje cycle.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ch. 11–13
*Student:* Read chapters 11–13 and explain how Okonkwo's exile happens.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 11–13. The student explains how the exile happens. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Why is Okonkwo exiled?
  **A:** At Ezeudu's funeral his gun explodes and kills Ezeudu's sixteen-year-old son; it is an accidental ('female') killing, so he must flee to his motherland for seven years.
- **Q:** Who carries Ezinma to the cave in chapter 11?
  **A:** Chielo, the priestess of Agbala; Ekwefi follows.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Vocabulary in context
*Student:* Define 10 Igbo words from context clues and the glossary, then use five in sentences about the novel.

*How to teach:* Show how to use context clues before the glossary. The student defines ten Igbo words (e.g., chi, obi, egwugwu, ogbanje) and writes five sentences about the novel using them correctly; quiz orally.

- **Q:** What is a chi?
  **A:** A person's personal god or spirit, connected to their destiny.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Analysis paragraph
*Student:* Write a paragraph analyzing Okonkwo's tragic flaw with three cited examples.

*How to teach:* Model a topic sentence that names Okonkwo's flaw. The student writes a paragraph with three cited examples, each followed by analysis of how it shows the flaw; check that analysis is at least as long as the quotes.

- **Q:** Give one example of Okonkwo's rashness before the exile.
  **A:** He beats his wife Ojiugo during the sacred Week of Peace, or shoots at Ekwefi with his gun.

### Day 5 (Friday): Discussion
*Student:* Discuss with family: Which Igbo traditions does the novel present as strengths, and which as weaknesses?

*How to teach:* Lead a 15-minute family discussion: which traditions does Achebe present as strengths and which as weaknesses? Ask the student to support each claim with a specific scene, and push for nuance — Achebe shows both.

- **Q:** Give one strength and one weakness Achebe shows.
  **A:** Strengths: democratic councils, justice, art, proverbs, community. Weaknesses: throwing away twins, the osu outcasts, harshness toward the vulnerable.

**Assessment:** Analysis paragraph and vocabulary check.

**Answer key:** Analysis paragraph rubric: 4 = names the flaw (fear of weakness leading to rashness, anger and violence), three accurate cited examples (beating Ojiugo during the Week of Peace; killing Ikemefuna; shooting at Ekwefi; later killing the messenger), analysis linking each to the flaw; 3 = two strong examples; 2 = summary; 1 = no claim. Vocabulary check (answers): chi — personal god/spirit; obi — head of household's hut; egwugwu — masked ancestral spirits who judge; ogbanje — a child who dies and returns to be reborn; iyi-uwa — stone linking an ogbanje to the spirit world; ilo — village playground/meeting ground; ndichie — elders; efulefu — worthless man; agbala — woman, or a man with no titles; osu — outcast dedicated to a god.

## Geometry: Dilations

**Prepare:** Ruler, graph paper, GeoGebra, colored pencils.

**Watch for:**
- Measuring the scale factor from the original vertex to the image vertex instead of from the center. All distances in a dilation are measured from the center.
- Applying (kx, ky) when the center is not the origin. Subtract the center, scale, then add the center back.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dilation by construction
*Student:* Dilate a triangle from a center with scale factors 2 and 1/2 using a ruler.

*How to teach:* Choose a center O outside a triangle. The student measures OA, multiplies by 2 (and ½), and marks A' on ray OA; repeat for B and C. Compare side lengths and angles of image and pre-image.

- **Q:** If OA = 3 cm and the scale factor is 2, how far is A' from O?
  **A:** 6 cm, on ray OA.
- **Q:** Does a dilation preserve angle measure? Side length?
  **A:** Angle measure yes; side lengths are multiplied by k.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Properties
*Student:* Verify that a dilation maps a line not through the center to a parallel line and scales lengths by k.

*How to teach:* In GeoGebra dilate a line not through the center and a line through the center. The student observes the first is parallel and the second maps to itself, and lengths scale by k.

- **Q:** What happens to a line through the center of dilation?
  **A:** It maps onto itself.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Coordinate dilations
*Student:* Dilate figures about the origin and other points with the rule (x, y) → (kx, ky).

*How to teach:* Practice the origin rule (kx, ky), then dilations about another center (subtract center, scale, add back).

- **Q:** Dilate (−6, 3) by k = 1/3 about the origin.
  **A:** (−2, 1).
- **Q:** Dilate (5, 2) by k = 3 about (2, 0).
  **A:** (5 − 2, 2 − 0) = (3, 2) → (9, 6) → add back → (11, 6).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Similarity transformations
*Student:* Find a sequence of rigid motions plus dilation mapping one figure to another.

*How to teach:* Define similarity as a dilation followed by rigid motions. Given pairs of similar figures, the student finds the scale factor from corresponding sides and then the motions.

- **Q:** Two similar triangles have corresponding sides 4 and 10. What is the scale factor from small to large?
  **A:** 10/4 = 2.5.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on dilations.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** If k is between 0 and 1, is the dilation an enlargement or reduction?
  **A:** A reduction.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Dilate (4, −2) by k = ½ about the origin → (2, −1). 2) Dilate (3, 4) by k = 2 about (1, 1) → (5, 7). 3) A segment of length 6 is dilated by k = 1.5 → 9. 4) A dilation maps line y = 2x + 3 (not through the center at origin) by k = 2 → y = 2x + 6, a parallel line. 5) A'(6, 9) is the image of A(2, 3) about the origin; k → 3. 6) Describe a similarity transformation mapping △ABC with A(0, 0), B(2, 0), C(0, 1) to A'(1, 1), B'(5, 1), C'(1, 3) → dilate by 2 about the origin, then translate ⟨1, 1⟩.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Thermal energy and particle motion

**Prepare:** Gather clear cups, food coloring, hot (not boiling) and ice-cold water, a timer, a pot, ice, a stove, a thermometer that reads to at least 100 °C (candy or digital probe), and a clock. Open PhET 'States of Matter'. Supervise all stove work.

**Watch for:**
- Students think temperature rises steadily while ice melts. During a phase change, added energy goes into separating particles (potential energy), so temperature stays constant.
- Students say 'cold' flows into objects. Heat (energy) always flows from hotter to colder; cold is just less thermal energy.
- Students think particles stop moving in solids. Particles in solids vibrate in place; only at absolute zero would motion be minimal.

### Day 1 (Monday): Food coloring diffusion
*Student:* Drop food coloring into hot and cold water and record diffusion times.

*How to teach:* Fill two clear cups, one with hot water and one with cold, let them settle, then add one drop of food coloring to each simultaneously. Time how long until the color is evenly spread. The student explains using particle motion.

- **Q:** Why did the color spread faster in hot water?
  **A:** Water molecules in hot water have greater average kinetic energy, so they collide with dye particles more often and harder, spreading them faster.
- **Q:** What is temperature, at the particle level?
  **A:** A measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): States of matter sim
*Student:* Use PhET States of Matter to model particle motion during heating.

*How to teach:* In PhET States of Matter, the student heats and cools water, argon and oxygen, observing particle motion in each phase and recording what changes at phase transitions.

- **Q:** What happens to particle motion when a solid is heated to its melting point?
  **A:** Particles vibrate faster until they break free of fixed positions and slide past each other as a liquid.
- **Q:** In the simulation, what happens to particle spacing during boiling?
  **A:** It increases greatly as particles escape into the gas phase.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Heating curve
*Student:* Heat ice water on the stove, record temperature every minute, and graph the heating curve.

*How to teach:* Fill a pot with ice and a little water; record the temperature every minute while heating on medium until it has boiled for 5 minutes (stir gently; adult at the stove). The student graphs temperature vs. time and labels phases.

- **Q:** What did the temperature do while ice was still present?
  **A:** Stayed near 0 °C until all the ice melted.
- **Q:** Why might your water boil below 100 °C?
  **A:** At higher altitude, air pressure is lower, so water boils at a lower temperature.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Explain plateaus
*Student:* Explain phase-change plateaus in terms of potential energy in bonds.

*How to teach:* Use the graph to explain plateaus: during melting and boiling, energy goes into overcoming attractions (raising potential energy) rather than raising kinetic energy. Compare the length of the melting and boiling plateaus.

- **Q:** Why is the boiling plateau longer than the melting plateau?
  **A:** Vaporization requires much more energy (2260 J/g) than melting (334 J/g) because molecules must separate completely.
- **Q:** Does steam at 100 °C or water at 100 °C cause a worse burn? Why?
  **A:** Steam — it releases its large heat of vaporization as it condenses on skin, in addition to the heat from cooling.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on thermal energy concepts.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and grade together. Collect the heating-curve graph.

- **Q:** When you hold an ice cube, which way does heat flow?
  **A:** From your hand into the ice.

**Assessment:** Heating curve graph and quiz.

**Answer key:** Heating curve graph: full credit for time on x-axis, temperature on y-axis, plotted points, plateau near 0 °C while ice melts, rising section, and plateau near 100 °C (slightly lower at high altitude) while boiling, each section labeled with the phase(s) present. Quiz key: 1) Temperature measures average kinetic energy of particles. 2) Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. 3) Food coloring spreads faster in hot water because particles move faster. 4) Why the temperature is flat at 0 °C — energy breaks attractions between molecules (increasing potential energy), not speeding them up. 5) Thermal equilibrium — two objects in contact reach the same temperature and net heat flow stops. 6) A hot spoon in cool water: heat flows from spoon to water. 7) Which has more thermal energy: a cup of boiling water or a bathtub of warm water? The bathtub (far more particles). 8) Name the phase change at the 100 °C plateau — boiling/vaporization. 9) In which state do particles have the most freedom? Gas. 10) Absolute zero is 0 K (−273.15 °C).

## Modern World History: The Armenian Genocide and human costs

**Prepare:** Readings on the Armenian Genocide (1915) from a reputable source (e.g., Armenian National Institute, USHMM, Facing History). Ambassador Henry Morgenthau's account. Parent preview: descriptions of mass killings and death marches.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the deportations were wartime relocation. They were a deliberate campaign of mass murder.
- Assuming 'genocide' means any mass death. It requires intent to destroy a group.

### Day 1 (Monday): Context
*Student:* Read about Armenians in the Ottoman Empire and the 1915 deportations.

*How to teach:* Read background on Armenians as a Christian minority in the Ottoman Empire and the 1915 events (arrests of April 24, deportations into the Syrian desert). The student builds a short timeline and notes the wartime context the government used as cover.

- **Q:** When did the Armenian Genocide begin?
  **A:** April 24, 1915, with the arrest of Armenian leaders in Constantinople.
- **Q:** About how many Armenians were killed?
  **A:** About 1 million to 1.5 million.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Morgenthau
*Student:* Analyze Ambassador Morgenthau's account and survivor testimony.

*How to teach:* Source Ambassador Henry Morgenthau (U.S. ambassador to the Ottoman Empire, 1913–1916, writing to the State Department and later in his memoir). The student reads his account and one survivor testimony and lists what each reveals about intent and method.

- **Q:** What did Morgenthau conclude?
  **A:** That the deportations were a death warrant for a whole race — a deliberate campaign.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Defining genocide
*Student:* Read Raphael Lemkin's definition and apply it to 1915 events.

*How to teach:* Read Raphael Lemkin's definition and the UN Genocide Convention (1948). The student makes a chart matching each element of the legal definition to evidence from 1915. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Who coined 'genocide'?
  **A:** Raphael Lemkin, in 1944.
- **Q:** What is the UN definition?
  **A:** Acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or part, a national, ethnic, racial or religious group.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Human costs
*Student:* Graph military and civilian casualties by country and discuss the 1918 influenza.

*How to teach:* Give the student casualty data for major WWI nations. They make a bar graph of military deaths by country and discuss the 1918 influenza, which killed an estimated 50 million people worldwide — more than the war.

- **Q:** About how many soldiers died in WWI?
  **A:** About 9–10 million.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflection
*Student:* Write a reflection on why remembering genocide matters (CA has a large Armenian American community).

*How to teach:* The student writes a one-page reflection on why remembering genocide matters, using Morgenthau or survivor testimony and Lemkin's definition as evidence. Discuss California's large Armenian American community (Fresno, Glendale) and April 24 commemorations.

- **Q:** Why does California have a large Armenian community?
  **A:** Many survivors' descendants settled in Fresno and Los Angeles.

**Assessment:** Reflection and source analysis.

**Answer key:** Reflection rubric (4-point): explains why remembering genocide matters; uses evidence (Morgenthau's account; Lemkin's definition); connects to the Armenian American community in California; thoughtful. Source analysis: Morgenthau — U.S. ambassador to the Ottoman Empire 1913–16, eyewitness reports; purpose to inform U.S. government and public.

## Music & Media Arts: American music: spirituals, blues, jazz and rock

**Prepare:** Library of Congress and Smithsonian recordings: a spiritual ('Swing Low, Sweet Chariot' or 'Go Down, Moses'), Robert Johnson ('Cross Road Blues'), Louis Armstrong ('West End Blues', 1928), Duke Ellington ('It Don't Mean a Thing (If It Ain't Got That Swing)', 1932). *How to teach:* Listen to spirituals and Robert Johnson; identify call and response.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Jazz
*Student:* Listen to Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington; describe swing.

*How to teach:* Listen to Armstrong and Ellington; describe swing.

- **Q:** What did Louis Armstrong contribute to jazz?

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Improvisation
*Student:* Improvise over a 12-bar blues using the blues scale.

*How to teach:* Improvise over a 12-bar blues using the blues scale.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rock & beyond
*Student:* Trace rock, soul, hip-hop origins on a family tree.

*How to teach:* Trace rock, soul and hip-hop origins on a family tree.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a 400-word response on how one genre reflects its era.

*How to teach:* The student writes a 400-word response on how one genre reflects its era.

**Assessment:** Written response.

**Answer key:** Written response rubric (400 words): 4 = thesis connecting a genre to its era; at least two specific musical features (call and response, blue notes, 12-bar form, swing eighths, improvisation, backbeat) and two historical contexts (slavery and emancipation; Great Migration; Jim Crow; Harlem Renaissance; postwar youth culture; civil rights) with named examples; clear organization; accurate facts. 3 = solid but less specific. 2 = general. 1 = inaccurate or incomplete.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Alcohol, drugs and driving

**Prepare:** Read the handbook sections on alcohol, drugs and driving. Find an impairment-goggles video (e.g., a DUI simulation by a police or safety organization). Parental note: some videos include crash footage; preview first.

**Watch for:**
- Students think coffee, cold showers or food sober you up. Only time lowers BAC (roughly one standard drink per hour).
- Students think cannabis makes people drive more carefully. Cannabis impairs reaction time, coordination and lane control, and driving under its influence is a DUI.
- Students think a 'little' alcohol is legal for teens. Drivers under 21 may not drive with a BAC of 0.01% or higher (zero tolerance), and it's illegal for them to have alcohol in the car unless with a parent for a legitimate reason.

### Day 1 (Monday): Effects
*Student:* Learn how alcohol, cannabis and medications affect judgment, vision and reaction time.

*How to teach:* The student researches effects of alcohol, cannabis and certain medications on judgment, vision, coordination and reaction time, and makes a comparison chart.

- **Q:** What does alcohol affect first: judgment or coordination?
  **A:** Judgment — impairment of decision-making begins before the person feels or looks drunk.
- **Q:** Can an over-the-counter allergy medicine impair driving?
  **A:** Yes; many antihistamines cause drowsiness — read warnings like 'may cause drowsiness; use caution operating a vehicle'.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): BAC laws
*Student:* Learn BAC limits (0.08 adults, 0.01 under 21) and implied consent.

*How to teach:* The student learns BAC limits and implied consent.

- **Q:** What is the legal BAC limit for drivers under 21?
  **A:** 0.01% (zero tolerance).
- **Q:** What is implied consent?
  **A:** By driving in California you agree to submit to a chemical test (breath/blood) if lawfully arrested for DUI; refusing brings penalties such as license suspension.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Penalties
*Student:* Research DUI penalties for minors in California, including license suspension.

*How to teach:* The student researches DUI penalties for minors (license suspension, fines, possible jail/juvenile court, insurance increases, DUI programs).

- **Q:** What happens to a driver under 21 who is caught with a BAC of 0.01% or more?
  **A:** One-year license suspension (plus possible other penalties).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Decision-making
*Student:* Role-play refusing a ride with an impaired driver.

*How to teach:* Role-play refusing a ride from an impaired driver and calling a parent. Agree on a family 'no-questions-asked' pickup plan.

- **Q:** Your ride home has been drinking. What could you say and do?
  **A:** 'I'm not comfortable — I'm going to call my parent.' Then call a parent, a sober adult, or a rideshare, and don't get in the car.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* DUI law quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the DUI law quiz. Review.

- **Q:** How long does it take the body to remove the alcohol in one standard drink, roughly?
  **A:** About one hour; nothing speeds it up.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** DUI law quiz key: 1) Adult BAC limit — 0.08%. 2) Under 21 — 0.01% (zero tolerance); 0.05%+ can be charged as DUI for under 21. 3) Commercial drivers — 0.04%. 4) Implied consent — by driving you agree to a chemical test if lawfully arrested for DUI; refusal brings license suspension. 5) Under 21 with BAC 0.01%+ — license suspended for 1 year. 6) Under 21 refusing a preliminary alcohol screening test — license suspension. 7) Minors carrying alcohol in a vehicle — illegal unless accompanied by a parent/adult for a legitimate reason (container must be full and sealed). 8) Only thing that lowers BAC — time. 9) Prescription and OTC drugs can impair — read labels. 10) DUI applies to alcohol, cannabis and any drug that impairs.

## Computer Science Principles: Lists

**Prepare:** Have the AP reference sheet list section (APPEND, INSERT, REMOVE, LENGTH, 1-based indexing) and App Lab list commands (appendItem, insertItem, removeItem, 0-based). Prepare 10 tracing problems.

**Watch for:**
- Index confusion: AP pseudocode lists start at index 1; JavaScript/App Lab lists start at index 0. Always ask 'which language?' first.
- Forgetting that INSERT and REMOVE shift other elements' indices.
- Accessing an index past the end (e.g. list[length] in JavaScript), giving undefined or an error.

### Day 1 (Monday): Why lists?
*Student:* Rewrite a program with five separate variables using one list and explain the benefit.

*How to teach:* Show a program with five variables score1…score5 and computing the average. Rewrite with one list. Discuss: easier to add a sixth score, can loop through, less repetitive code.

- **Q:** Why is a list better than score1, score2, … score5?
  **A:** One name holds any number of values, so adding more data doesn't require new variables, and the program can process them all with a loop.
- **Q:** What is an element? An index?
  **A:** An element is one value in a list; the index is its position number.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): List operations
*Student:* Practice appendItem, insertItem, removeItem and indexing in App Lab.

*How to teach:* Practice App Lab commands: var pets = ["cat", "dog"]; appendItem(pets, "fish"); insertItem(pets, 0, "bird"); removeItem(pets, 2); console.log(pets). Predict before running.

- **Q:** Starting with ["cat", "dog"], after appendItem(pets,"fish"), insertItem(pets,0,"bird") and removeItem(pets,2), what is pets?
  **A:** After append: [cat, dog, fish]; after insert at 0: [bird, cat, dog, fish]; remove index 2 (dog): [bird, cat, fish].
- **Q:** What does pets.length return for ["bird","cat","fish"]?
  **A:** 3.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Index tracing
*Student:* Trace 10 AP reference-sheet pseudocode problems that use lists with 1-based indexing and compare to App Lab's 0-based indexing.

*How to teach:* Trace 10 AP pseudocode list problems on paper, keeping a picture of the list after each line; then compare the same operations in App Lab with 0-based indices.

- **Q:** AP pseudocode: nums ← [4, 6, 8]. What is nums[1]? In App Lab, what is nums[1]?
  **A:** AP: 4 (1-based). App Lab: 6 (0-based).
- **Q:** AP: L ← [1, 2, 3]; REMOVE(L, 2). Result?
  **A:** [1, 3].

### Day 4 (Thursday): List app
*Student:* Build a reminder or playlist app that adds and displays items from a list.

*How to teach:* Build a reminder or playlist app: text input + Add button appends to a list; a label or text area shows the list (e.g. list.join("\n")).

- **Q:** Why must the list be declared globally in this app?
  **A:** So items added on each click are kept between events instead of the list resetting.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
