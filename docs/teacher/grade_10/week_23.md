# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 23

## English 10: Comparing mediums: war in film and text

**Prepare:** The trailer or a 3-minute clip of 'They Shall Not Grow Old' (Peter Jackson, 2018; preview first — it shows real wartime footage including dead soldiers), 'Dulce et Decorum Est,' and the Library of Congress digital collections. Slide software.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking documentary film is objective and poetry is subjective. Both involve choices: what to show, cut, colorize, or emphasize.
- Using images without crediting them. Every image needs a caption with title, creator/date if known, and source.

### Day 1 (Monday): Film vocabulary
*Student:* Learn shot types and editing terms and identify them in a 3-minute WWI documentary clip.

*How to teach:* Teach five terms with quick sketches: long shot, close-up, pan, cut, montage (and note colorization in Jackson's film). Watch the clip twice; the second time the student logs each shot type they see.

- **Q:** What effect does a close-up of a soldier's face create?
  **A:** It builds emotional connection and focuses attention on individual experience.
- **Q:** What is a montage?
  **A:** A sequence of short shots edited together to compress time or build an idea or mood.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Compare
*Student:* Compare how the clip and 'Dulce et Decorum Est' emphasize different details of the trenches.

*How to teach:* Have the student list details of the trenches in the clip and in Owen's poem in two columns. Then discuss what each medium emphasizes and why. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What can Owen's poem do that the film clip cannot?
  **A:** Use figurative language (the 'green sea,' 'like old beggars under sacks') and give a speaker's inner thoughts and bitter judgment ('the old Lie').
- **Q:** What can the film do that the poem cannot?
  **A:** Show real faces, movement and setting directly, often with sound, making the history visually concrete.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Plan presentation
*Student:* Plan a 5-slide presentation pairing one poem with archival images (Library of Congress).

*How to teach:* The student picks one WWI poem and chooses five key lines. For each, they sketch a slide plan: the line, an archival image idea from the Library of Congress, and why they belong together.

- **Q:** What makes a strong poem–image pairing?
  **A:** An image that directly echoes or deliberately contrasts a specific line, so the pairing creates new meaning.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Build slides
*Student:* Build the slides with captions citing image sources.

*How to teach:* The student builds the five slides, searching loc.gov for images (keywords like 'trench,' 'gas mask,' 'recruitment poster'). Each slide needs the poem line, the image and a caption citing the source.

- **Q:** What information belongs in an image caption citation?
  **A:** Title or description, creator and date if known, the collection (e.g., Library of Congress), and the URL.

### Day 5 (Friday): Present
*Student:* Present for 4 minutes, explaining how image and text work together.

*How to teach:* The student presents for about 4 minutes, explaining how image and text work together. Score with the rubric and give one glow and one grow. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Name one delivery skill to focus on during the presentation.
  **A:** Making eye contact rather than reading slides, or pausing after each image so the audience can look.

**Assessment:** Multimedia presentation rubric.

**Answer key:** Multimedia presentation rubric (SL.9-10.5): 4 = five slides that pair specific poem lines with relevant archival images; captions cite each source; 4-minute talk explains how each image and line reinforce or complicate each other; clear voice and eye contact; 3 = solid pairing, explanation less specific; 2 = images loosely related or uncited; 1 = incomplete.

## Geometry: Circle basics, arcs and similarity

**Prepare:** Compass, string, several circular objects (cans, plates), ruler, calculator.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing arc measure (degrees) with arc length (distance). Arc measure equals the central angle; arc length depends on the radius.
- Naming a major arc with two letters. Major arcs and semicircles need three letters (e.g., arc ACB).

### Day 1 (Monday): All circles similar
*Student:* Show any circle maps to any other by a translation and dilation.

*How to teach:* Take two circles with different centers and radii. Show the translation that moves center to center, then the dilation with k = r₂/r₁. The student writes the argument.

- **Q:** Circle A has center (1, 2), radius 3; circle B has center (−4, 0), radius 6. Describe a similarity mapping A to B.
  **A:** Translate by ⟨−5, −2⟩, then dilate about (−4, 0) by scale factor 2.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Parts of a circle
*Student:* Label chords, secants, tangents, central angles and arcs on diagrams.

*How to teach:* Label diagrams with chord, diameter, radius, secant, tangent, point of tangency, central angle, minor/major arc, semicircle. Quiz with flash diagrams.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a chord and a secant?
  **A:** A chord is a segment with both endpoints on the circle; a secant is a line that intersects the circle at two points.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Arc measure
*Student:* Find measures of arcs and central angles using arc addition.

*How to teach:* Teach: minor arc = central angle; major arc = 360° − minor; arc addition. The student solves problems including algebraic central angles.

- **Q:** Central angles of a circle are 2x, 3x, and 4x (filling the whole circle). Find each.
  **A:** 9x = 360 → x = 40; angles 80°, 120°, 160°.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Chords
*Student:* Prove congruent chords have congruent arcs and that a radius perpendicular to a chord bisects it.

*How to teach:* Prove congruent chords have congruent central angles (SSS with radii) and that a radius perpendicular to a chord bisects it (HL). Solve chord-distance problems.

- **Q:** A circle has radius 10, and a chord is 6 units from the center. How long is the chord?
  **A:** Half-chord = √(100 − 36) = 8, so chord = 16.
- **Q:** A chord of length 24 is 5 units from the center. Find the radius.
  **A:** √(12² + 5²) = 13.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on circle vocabulary and chords.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** A diameter is perpendicular to a chord. What does it do to the chord and its arc?
  **A:** It bisects the chord and its arc.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Identify chord, secant, tangent, radius on a diagram (visual check). 2) Central angle 70° → minor arc 70°, major arc 290°. 3) Arcs AB = 80°, BC = 45° adjacent → arc ABC = 125°. 4) Radius 10, chord 6 units from center → chord length 16. 5) Two chords in the same circle are congruent; one's arc is 100° → the other's arc is 100°. 6) Why are all circles similar? → any circle can be translated to share a center with another and dilated by k = r₂/r₁.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Bond energy and energy in reactions

**Prepare:** Gather baking soda, vinegar, 3% hydrogen peroxide, yeast, two cups, and a thermometer. Prepare a bond-energy table (kJ/mol): C–H 413, O=O 498, C=O (in CO₂) 799, O–H 463, H–H 436, N≡N 945, N–H 391. Build the Unit 5 test from the key.

**Watch for:**
- Students think breaking bonds releases energy (like breaking a stick). Breaking bonds always requires energy; forming bonds releases energy.
- Students reverse the sign: ΔH = bonds broken − bonds formed; a negative ΔH is exothermic.
- Students think exothermic reactions need no activation energy. Even exothermic reactions (like burning wood) need an initial energy input to start.

### Day 1 (Monday): Hot & cold
*Student:* Measure temperature change of baking soda + vinegar (endo) and yeast + peroxide (exo).

*How to teach:* Mix baking soda and vinegar in a cup with a thermometer and record the temperature change; then mix yeast into peroxide and record. The student classifies each as endo- or exothermic.

- **Q:** The baking soda reaction dropped from 21 °C to 17 °C. Endothermic or exothermic?
  **A:** Endothermic — it absorbed energy from the surroundings.
- **Q:** The peroxide reaction rose from 21 °C to 35 °C. What does that tell you about bonds?
  **A:** Exothermic — more energy was released forming bonds in H₂O and O₂ than was absorbed breaking bonds in H₂O₂.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Energy diagrams
*Student:* Draw energy diagrams for each reaction with activation energy.

*How to teach:* Model an energy diagram: reactant level, hump (activation energy), product level, and ΔH arrow. The student draws one for each Day 1 reaction and labels them.

- **Q:** On an exothermic energy diagram, are products higher or lower than reactants?
  **A:** Lower — energy is released.
- **Q:** What does a catalyst do to the diagram?
  **A:** It lowers the activation energy hump; ΔH is unchanged.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Bond energy calc
*Student:* Calculate ΔH for methane combustion using bond energies.

*How to teach:* Model ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed) for methane combustion step by step. The student then does H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl and 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

- **Q:** Calculate ΔH for CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
  **A:** Broken: 4(413) + 2(498) = 2648. Formed: 2(799) + 4(463) = 3450. ΔH = 2648 − 3450 = −802 kJ/mol.
- **Q:** Calculate ΔH for 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.
  **A:** Broken: 2(436) + 498 = 1370. Formed: 4(463) = 1852. ΔH = −482 kJ.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Model
*Student:* Build a model explaining why breaking bonds absorbs energy and forming bonds releases it.

*How to teach:* Use two magnets as a model: pulling them apart takes energy (breaking), letting them snap together releases energy (forming). The student builds a written or physical model explaining why combustion is exothermic overall.

- **Q:** Why is burning methane exothermic overall if breaking its bonds absorbs energy?
  **A:** The bonds formed in CO₂ and H₂O are stronger in total, releasing more energy (3450 kJ) than was absorbed breaking the reactant bonds (2648 kJ).
- **Q:** What does the magnet model represent?
  **A:** Atoms attracting; separating them requires energy input and bringing them together releases energy.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 test
*Student:* Unit 5 test on reactions and stoichiometry.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test (50 minutes) with periodic table, bond-energy table and activity series. Grade together.

- **Q:** Is photosynthesis endothermic or exothermic?
  **A:** Endothermic — it absorbs light energy to make glucose.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test key (sample): 1) Balance Fe₂O₃ + CO → Fe + CO₂: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. 2) Classify AgNO₃ + KCl → AgCl + KNO₃: double replacement. 3) Grams CO₂ from 12.0 g C burned (C + O₂ → CO₂): 44.0 g. 4) 5.0 mol H₂ and 2.0 mol O₂ (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O): O₂ limits, 4.0 mol H₂O. 5) Percent yield, 18.0 g theoretical, 15.3 g actual: 85.0%. 6) ΔH for CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O from bond energies: −802 kJ/mol. 7) ΔH for H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (H–H 436, Cl–Cl 242, H–Cl 431): (436 + 242) − 2(431) = −184 kJ (exothermic). 8) Endothermic or exothermic: baking soda + vinegar — endothermic (temperature drops). 9) Label an energy diagram: reactants, products, activation energy, ΔH. 10) Explain why forming bonds releases energy — atoms reach a lower, more stable energy state.

## Modern World History: Home fronts, the atomic bomb and human costs

**Prepare:** Executive Order 9066 (February 19, 1942), Truman's statement on Hiroshima (August 6, 1945), the Franck Report (June 1945) excerpt, a Hiroshima survivor account (e.g., from John Hersey's Hiroshima), WWII casualty data by country. Parent preview: survivor accounts describe severe burns and death.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking only soldiers died in WWII. Most deaths were civilians.
- Presenting the atomic bomb decision as having only one side.

### Day 1 (Monday): Home fronts
*Student:* Compare home fronts in the U.S., Britain, USSR and Japan, including Japanese American incarceration (EO 9066).

*How to teach:* Compare home fronts in the U.S., Britain, USSR and Japan (rationing, women's work, propaganda). Read Executive Order 9066 and a Japanese American incarceration account; the student explains the constitutional issues.

- **Q:** What did EO 9066 lead to?
  **A:** The forced removal and incarceration of about 120,000 Japanese Americans, most of them U.S. citizens.
- **Q:** How did the U.S. later respond?
  **A:** The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 apologized and paid $20,000 to each surviving incarceree.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Atomic decision
*Student:* Analyze Truman's statement and the Franck Report.

*How to teach:* Source Truman's statement (August 6, 1945) and the Franck Report (June 1945, scientists' recommendation). The student charts arguments for using the bomb and alternatives proposed. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What did the Franck Report recommend?
  **A:** A demonstration of the bomb on an uninhabited area before using it on a city.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hiroshima voices
*Student:* Read a Hiroshima survivor account.

*How to teach:* Read a Hiroshima survivor account (e.g., from John Hersey's Hiroshima) aloud together; preview first. The student writes what the source adds that official statements leave out. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** About how many died in Hiroshima by the end of 1945?
  **A:** About 140,000.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Casualties
*Student:* Graph military and civilian deaths by country and discuss China's and the USSR's losses.

*How to teach:* Give casualty data by country. The student graphs military and civilian deaths and explains why China and the USSR suffered so heavily. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Which country suffered the most deaths?
  **A:** The Soviet Union (about 24–27 million).

### Day 5 (Friday): Seminar
*Student:* Seminar: Was the atomic bombing justified? Use four documents.

*How to teach:* Hold a 20-minute seminar: Was the atomic bombing justified? The student uses four documents and considers alternatives. Score with the seminar rubric. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** When were the bombs dropped?
  **A:** August 6 (Hiroshima) and August 9 (Nagasaki), 1945.

**Assessment:** Seminar rubric.

**Answer key:** Seminar rubric (4-point): clear position; uses four documents accurately (Truman's statement, the Franck Report, a survivor account, casualty data or an invasion estimate); considers alternatives (demonstration, blockade, waiting for Soviet entry, modifying surrender terms); responds to others.

## Music & Media Arts: Sound design and recording

**Prepare:** Install Audacity (free). Gather household objects for foley (gloves for bird wings, celery for bone snaps, cornstarch in a pouch for footsteps in snow, rice on a tray for rain). Phone microphone and headphones.

**Watch for:**
- Students record too close or too loud. Keep peaks below about −6 dB to avoid clipping; distance changes tone.
- Students think foley must use the real object. Foley uses whatever sounds convincing on screen.
- Students apply noise reduction too strongly. Over-processing creates 'underwater' artifacts; use light settings.

### Day 1 (Monday): Audacity
*Student:* Learn Audacity: record, cut, fade.

*How to teach:* Learn Audacity: record, select, cut, fade in/out, export.

- **Q:** How do you remove a section in Audacity?
  **A:** Select it on the waveform and press Delete (or Edit > Cut).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Foley
*Student:* Create foley sounds (footsteps, rain) with household objects.

*How to teach:* Create foley sounds with household objects and record them.

- **Q:** What is foley?
  **A:** Everyday sound effects recorded in sync with picture, named after Jack Foley.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Soundscape
*Student:* Build a 1-minute soundscape of a place.

*How to teach:* Build a 1-minute soundscape of a place.

- **Q:** What's the difference between ambience and spot effects?
  **A:** Ambience is the background environment (wind, traffic); spot effects are specific sounds (a door slam).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mic technique
*Student:* Test mic placement and room acoustics.

*How to teach:* Test mic placement and room acoustics (closet with clothes vs. kitchen).

- **Q:** Why does a closet full of clothes make a good vocal booth?
  **A:** Soft materials absorb reflections, reducing echo.

### Day 5 (Friday): Share
*Student:* Share the soundscape.

*How to teach:* Share the soundscape.

- **Q:** How does noise reduction work in Audacity?
  **A:** You select a section of pure background noise to 'Get Noise Profile', then apply the effect to the whole track to subtract that noise.

**Assessment:** Soundscape.

**Answer key:** Soundscape rubric: 1 minute; at least 6 layered sounds (ambience, foley, details); fades in/out; levels balanced; panning to create space; clear sense of place; no clipping; uses noise reduction appropriately. Proficient = place clearly recognizable with clean, balanced audio.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Environmental emergencies and certification

**Prepare:** Gather a rope and a plastic bottle with a little water (for the throw). Find the Red Cross or AHA blended First Aid/CPR/AED course options and costs, or schedule an in-person session. Prepare the Unit 6 written test and the skills checklist.

**Watch for:**
- Students think drowning looks like splashing and yelling. Drowning is often quiet: the person is vertical, head low, mouth at water level, unable to wave or call.
- Students treat heat stroke like heat exhaustion. Heat stroke (confusion, very high body temperature, may stop sweating) is life-threatening: call 911 and cool aggressively (cold-water immersion).
- Students rub frostbitten skin or rewarm a hypothermic person quickly with hot water. Handle gently; don't rub; rewarm gradually.

### Day 1 (Monday): Heat & cold
*Student:* Compare heat exhaustion vs. heat stroke and frostbite vs. hypothermia; make a quick-reference card for hiking and sports.

*How to teach:* Compare heat exhaustion vs. heat stroke and frostbite vs. hypothermia; the student makes a quick-reference card.

- **Q:** Name two signs that separate heat stroke from heat exhaustion.
  **A:** Confusion or altered mental state, and very high body temperature (about 104 °F+) with hot skin; sweating may have stopped.
- **Q:** What's the first aid for heat stroke?
  **A:** Call 911 and cool the person immediately, ideally by cold-water immersion or ice packs and wet cloths with fanning.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Water safety
*Student:* Learn drowning signs (quiet, vertical, no waving) and reach-throw-don't-go rescue; practice a throw with a rope and floating bottle.

*How to teach:* The student learns drowning signs and practices reach-throw-don't-go, throwing the rope and bottle to a target 15 feet away.

- **Q:** What does drowning usually look like?
  **A:** Quiet: vertical in the water, head back, mouth at the surface, unable to wave or call for help.
- **Q:** Why 'don't go' into the water?
  **A:** A panicking swimmer can pull the rescuer under; untrained rescuers often drown themselves.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mock emergencies
*Student:* Run three mock scenarios with family (unresponsive adult, bleeding cyclist, choking child), rotating rescuer and patient roles.

*How to teach:* Run three mock scenarios with family, rotating rescuer and patient roles; debrief each.

- **Q:** In the bleeding-cyclist scenario, what comes before treating the wound?
  **A:** Check the scene (traffic), put on gloves, call 911.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Certification
*Student:* Complete an online/blended Red Cross or AHA First Aid/CPR/AED course module or schedule an in-person skills session.

*How to teach:* The student completes an online/blended module or schedules an in-person skills session for a recognized certification.

- **Q:** Why does a certification require an in-person skills check?
  **A:** Instructors must verify correct depth, rate and technique that can't be judged online.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit test
*Student:* Unit 6 test: written exam plus timed CPR/AED skills demonstration.

*How to teach:* Give the written Unit 6 test and a timed CPR/AED skills demonstration.

- **Q:** How quickly should AED pads be applied after it arrives?
  **A:** As soon as possible, with minimal interruption to compressions.

**Assessment:** Unit 6 written and skills test.

**Answer key:** Unit 6 test key (written, sample): 1) Check–Call–Care. 2) Adult compressions — 2 inches, 100–120/min, 30:2. 3) Infant compressions — 1.5 inches. 4) AED steps — on, pads, analyze, shock if advised, resume CPR. 5) Infant choking — 5 back blows, 5 chest thrusts. 6) Tourniquet placement — 2–3 inches above wound, not on a joint. 7) Burn — cool water 10+ min. 8) BE-FAST. 9) Heat exhaustion — heavy sweating, cool clammy skin, dizziness, nausea: move to cool place, fluids, loosen clothing. Heat stroke — confusion, hot skin, body temp ~104 °F or higher: call 911, cool rapidly. 10) Hypothermia — shivering (may stop), confusion, core temperature below 95 °F: get to warmth, remove wet clothes, warm blankets, warm sweet drinks if alert. 11) Frostbite — numb, white/gray waxy skin: warm gently, don't rub. 12) Water rescue — Reach or Throw, Don't Go. Skills test: timed 2-minute CPR with AED (pads placed within 90 seconds of arrival) scored with the Week 20 checklist.

## Computer Science Principles: Collecting and cleaning data

**Prepare:** Set up a Google account for Google Forms and Sheets. Recruit at least 15 respondents (family, co-op, friends). Preview Sheets 'Data → Data cleanup → Remove duplicates' and Find/Replace.

**Watch for:**
- Survey questions with open text answers ('How much do you sleep?') produce messy data ('8', 'eight', '7-8 hrs'). Use consistent formats like numbers or multiple choice.
- Deleting data that doesn't fit the hypothesis. Cleaning fixes errors and inconsistencies; it doesn't remove inconvenient real answers.
- Collecting more personal info than needed. Ask only what the question requires; avoid names and addresses (privacy).

### Day 1 (Monday): Data questions
*Student:* Write a question to investigate (e.g., teen sleep vs. screen time) and design a 6-question survey with consistent answer formats.

*How to teach:* The student writes a question to investigate, then a 6-question survey using number fields or multiple choice so answers are consistent. Review together for bias or confusing wording.

- **Q:** Rewrite 'How much screen time do you get?' so answers are consistent.
  **A:** 'On a typical school day, how many hours of recreational screen time do you have? (enter a whole number)' or a multiple-choice range like 0–1, 2–3, 4–5, 6+.
- **Q:** Why avoid collecting names in this survey?
  **A:** They aren't needed for the analysis and create privacy risk.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Collect
*Student:* Collect survey responses from at least 15 people using Google Forms and export them to a spreadsheet.

*How to teach:* Build the survey in Google Forms, send it, collect 15+ responses, and link responses to a spreadsheet.

- **Q:** What is metadata in a survey dataset?
  **A:** Data about the data, such as the timestamp of each response or the column descriptions.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Clean
*Student:* Clean the data by standardizing entries and removing duplicates.

*How to teach:* Clean: standardize entries (e.g. 'yes', 'Yes', 'Y' → 'Yes'), convert text to numbers, remove duplicates, flag impossible values. Have the student document every change.

- **Q:** Why is cleaning necessary before analysis?
  **A:** Inconsistent or duplicate entries would be counted wrongly (e.g. 'Yes' and 'yes' as different categories) and produce misleading results.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Storage tradeoffs
*Student:* Compare storing data in a spreadsheet vs. a database vs. cloud storage.

*How to teach:* Compare storage options: spreadsheet (easy, small data), database (structured, fast queries, many users), cloud storage (access anywhere, depends on provider and connection).

- **Q:** When is a database better than a spreadsheet?
  **A:** When data is large, many users must access or update it at once, or you need fast, complex queries.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
