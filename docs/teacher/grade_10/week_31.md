# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 31

## English 10: Dystopian writing and Unit 7 assessment

**Prepare:** Fahrenheit 451, notes; prepare the Unit 7 test with a cold-read dystopian excerpt (e.g., the opening of 'Harrison Bergeron' by Kurt Vonnegut, available on CommonLit).

**Watch for:**
- Writing an original dystopia that only copies Bradbury. A transformation keeps the premise but changes the banned thing, the setting, or the point of view.
- Peer feedback that is only praise. Require two specific 'grows' tied to the rubric.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose task
*Student:* Choose to write either a theme analysis of Fahrenheit 451 or an original dystopian short story that transforms Bradbury's premise.

*How to teach:* Explain both options. The student chooses and plans with a short outline. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does it mean to 'transform' Bradbury's premise?
  **A:** Keep the core idea (a society destroying knowledge) but change something important — e.g., music or art is banned, or the story is told by a censor.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Draft
*Student:* Draft 900–1,200 words.

*How to teach:* The student drafts 900–1,200 words in one or two sittings. For the analysis option, check thesis and evidence; for the story option, check that the world and its rules are clear by page one.

- **Q:** What should a dystopian story's opening establish?
  **A:** The world's rules and atmosphere, and a hint of what's wrong.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Peer review
*Student:* Exchange with a peer/parent using a rubric and give two glows and two grows.

*How to teach:* Exchange with a parent or peer using the rubric: two glows and two grows. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What makes feedback useful?
  **A:** Being specific, tied to the rubric, and suggesting how to fix the issue.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Final
*Student:* Revise and submit final draft.

*How to teach:* The student revises based on the two 'grows' first, then proofreads. Submit the final draft and score with the writing rubric. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How should you prioritize revisions?
  **A:** Fix big issues (thesis, structure, clarity) before small ones (word choice, commas).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit test
*Student:* Unit 7 test with a cold-read dystopian excerpt.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test with a cold-read dystopian excerpt (e.g., opening of 'Harrison Bergeron'). Grade with the assessment key. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What makes 'Harrison Bergeron' dystopian?
  **A:** The government forces everyone to be equal by handicapping anyone above average.

**Assessment:** Writing rubric and unit test.

**Answer key:** Writing rubric — Theme analysis option: thesis, three quoted pieces of evidence, analysis, organization, conventions (4-point each). Story option: a dystopian world clearly established, a conflict that comments on society, narrative technique (dialogue, pacing, description), conventions. Unit 7 test (sample answers with 'Harrison Bergeron' excerpt): 1) Dystopian feature → enforced equality via handicaps. 2) Who enforces it → the Handicapper General, Diana Moon Glampers. 3) How it connects to Beatty's 'everyone made equal' → both societies flatten individuality to avoid discomfort. 4) Define allusion, symbol, dystopia.

## Geometry: Sample spaces and set operations

**Prepare:** Two dice, coins, a standard 52-card deck. Remind yourself: a deck has 4 suits × 13 ranks; 12 face cards.

**Watch for:**
- Counting a sum like 7 as one outcome instead of 6 ordered pairs. Use the 6 × 6 table.
- Adding probabilities of overlapping events without subtracting the overlap.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sample spaces
*Student:* List sample spaces with tree diagrams and tables for dice and coins.

*How to teach:* Make a tree for 3 coin flips and a 6 × 6 table for two dice. The student counts outcomes for several events.

- **Q:** How many outcomes for rolling two dice? How many give a sum of 7?
  **A:** 36; 6.
- **Q:** P(at least one head in 2 coin flips)?
  **A:** 3/4.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Set notation
*Student:* Describe events using union, intersection and complement in Venn diagrams.

*How to teach:* Draw Venn diagrams for events from one die roll (A = even, B = greater than 3). Name union, intersection and complement.

- **Q:** Roll a die: A = even {2, 4, 6}, B = greater than 3 {4, 5, 6}. Find A ∩ B and A ∪ B.
  **A:** A ∩ B = {4, 6}; A ∪ B = {2, 4, 5, 6}.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Addition rule
*Student:* Apply P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) – P(A and B).

*How to teach:* Derive the addition rule from the Venn diagram (overlap counted twice). The student solves card and die problems.

- **Q:** P(heart or king) from a standard deck?
  **A:** 13/52 + 4/52 − 1/52 = 16/52 = 4/13.
- **Q:** P(even or greater than 3) on one die?
  **A:** 3/6 + 3/6 − 2/6 = 4/6 = 2/3.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Simulation
*Student:* Roll two dice 100 times and compare experimental vs. theoretical probability of sums.

*How to teach:* Roll two dice 100 times and tally sums. Compare experimental frequencies to the theoretical (e.g., P(7) = 1/6 ≈ 16.7%).

- **Q:** Why might the experimental results differ from theoretical probability?
  **A:** Random variation; with more trials the experimental frequencies tend to get closer (Law of Large Numbers).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on sample spaces and the addition rule.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Are 'rolling an even number' and 'rolling a 5' mutually exclusive?
  **A:** Yes — they cannot both happen, so P(even or 5) = 3/6 + 1/6 = 2/3.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Sample space for flipping 3 coins → 8 outcomes (HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT). 2) P(sum of 7 with two dice) → 6/36 = 1/6. 3) P(sum of 8) → 5/36. 4) P(heart or king) → 16/52 = 4/13. 5) P(not a face card) → 40/52 = 10/13. 6) In a Venn diagram, A ∩ B means → outcomes in both A and B; A ∪ B → in A or B or both; Aᶜ → not in A.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Engineering an equilibrium system

**Prepare:** Print overviews of the Contact process for sulfuric acid (2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, ΔH ≈ −198 kJ, vanadium(V) oxide catalyst, ~400–450 °C, near 1–2 atm with excess air) or methanol synthesis (CO + 2H₂ ⇌ CH₃OH, ΔH ≈ −91 kJ, copper/zinc oxide catalyst, ~250 °C, 50–100 atm). Build the Unit 7 test from the key.

**Watch for:**
- Students choose the highest-yield conditions regardless of cost or speed. Industrial chemists optimize rate, yield, cost and safety together.
- Students think pressure always helps. Pressure only shifts equilibria involving unequal moles of gas, and high pressure is expensive and hazardous.
- Students forget that recycling unreacted gases is a key way to improve overall yield.

### Day 1 (Monday): Case study
*Student:* Study industrial production of sulfuric acid or methanol and its equilibrium.

*How to teach:* The student reads the case study and summarizes the equation, ΔH, catalyst, conditions and uses (sulfuric acid: fertilizers, batteries; methanol: fuels, plastics).

- **Q:** For 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ (exothermic), why is a catalyst used?
  **A:** To speed the reaction at a moderate temperature where yield is still good.
- **Q:** What is sulfuric acid mostly used for?
  **A:** Making phosphate fertilizers (also batteries and chemical processing).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Design
*Student:* Propose changes in conditions to increase yield and explain trade-offs (cost, safety, rate).

*How to teach:* The student proposes changes to temperature, pressure, concentrations and catalyst and explains each effect on rate and yield in a table, plus trade-offs.

- **Q:** For methanol synthesis (CO + 2H₂ ⇌ CH₃OH, exothermic), what pressure change improves yield?
  **A:** Higher pressure — 3 gas moles become 1.
- **Q:** What is a trade-off of very high pressure?
  **A:** Stronger, costlier equipment and greater safety risk.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Peer critique
*Student:* Present your design to a parent and answer questions.

*How to teach:* The student presents the design to you; ask probing questions (Why that temperature? What happens to unreacted gas? Is it safe?). The student records questions he or she could not answer.

- **Q:** How can a plant increase overall yield without changing equilibrium?
  **A:** Separate the product and recycle the unreacted reactants back through the reactor.
- **Q:** Does the catalyst change the equilibrium yield?
  **A:** No, only how fast equilibrium is reached.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revise
*Student:* Revise based on feedback.

*How to teach:* The student revises the proposal to address feedback, marking changes.

- **Q:** Why might an engineer accept a lower yield per pass?
  **A:** Higher temperature gives a much faster rate, so more product is made per hour overall, especially with recycling.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 test
*Student:* Unit 7 test on rates and equilibrium.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test. Grade together.

- **Q:** For H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g), does increasing pressure change the yield?
  **A:** No — there are 2 moles of gas on each side.

**Assessment:** Design proposal and Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Design proposal rubric: 4 = Correct equation and ΔH; predicted effect of temperature, pressure, concentration and catalyst on both rate and yield; chosen operating conditions with justification; at least two trade-offs (cost, safety, energy, rate); revision based on feedback. Unit 7 test key (sample): 1) Four factors affecting rate: temperature, concentration, surface area, catalyst. 2) Why temperature speeds reactions — more frequent, more energetic collisions; more exceed Ea. 3) Catalyst lowers activation energy; does not change K or yield. 4) K for 2NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄: [N₂O₄] ÷ [NO₂]². 5) For 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ (exothermic): increasing pressure shifts right; raising temperature shifts left; adding O₂ shifts right. 6) Equilibrium means equal rates, not equal amounts. 7) Why industry recycles unreacted gases — to convert more feedstock over repeated passes.

**Answer key:** Comparison outline rubric (4-point): thesis comparing two nations' paths; three points with evidence (e.g., South Africa's TRC; Mexico's PRI era ending 2000; Chile's Pinochet dictatorship 1973–1990 and return to democracy); counterpoint; clear structure.

## Spanish III: Fiestas del mundo hispano

**Prepare:** Find short videos (Smithsonian, National Geographic en español, tourism boards) on Día de los Muertos, Las Fallas (Valencia, Spain), and Inti Raymi (Cusco, Peru). Choose a pan de muerto or tamales recipe in Spanish and buy ingredients.

**Watch for:**
- Calling Día de los Muertos 'Mexican Halloween': it is a family celebration on November 1–2 honoring and welcoming the spirits of loved ones, rooted in Indigenous and Catholic traditions.
- Thinking Inti Raymi is in December: it is held June 24 in Cusco, near the Southern Hemisphere winter solstice, honoring Inti, the Inca sun god.

### Day 1 (Monday): Día de Muertos
*Student:* Read about Día de los Muertos and the meaning of ofrenda items.

*How to teach:* Read about Día de los Muertos; your child labels an ofrenda diagram explaining each item in Spanish.

- **Q:** Why are marigolds (cempasúchil) placed on the ofrenda?
  **A:** Their bright color and strong scent are believed to guide the spirits to the altar.
- **Q:** Translate: 'We put photos of our grandparents on the altar.'
  **A:** Ponemos fotos de nuestros abuelos en el altar (la ofrenda).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Fallas & Inti Raymi
*Student:* Watch videos on Las Fallas and Inti Raymi and take notes.

*How to teach:* Watch videos on Las Fallas and Inti Raymi; your child takes notes (where, when, what happens, meaning).

- **Q:** What happens at the end of Las Fallas?
  **A:** On the night of March 19 (la Cremà) the giant figures are burned.
- **Q:** What does Inti Raymi celebrate?
  **A:** Inti, the Inca sun god, at the winter solstice and the start of the new year in the Andes.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Comparación
*Student:* Compare a Hispanic celebration to one your family celebrates.

*How to teach:* Your child compares one Hispanic celebration with a family celebration using comparison phrases (al igual que, a diferencia de, mientras que).

- **Q:** Write a comparison sentence.
  **A:** Example: Al igual que el Día de Acción de Gracias, el Día de Muertos reúne a la familia, pero a diferencia de él, se recuerda a los muertos.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Receta
*Student:* Follow a Spanish recipe (pan de muerto or tamales) and narrate the process.

*How to teach:* Follow a Spanish recipe together; your child narrates the steps with commands or se (Se mezcla la harina...).

- **Q:** Translate the step: 'Mix the flour with the eggs.'
  **A:** Mezcla la harina con los huevos (Se mezcla la harina con los huevos).

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the festivals quiz.

- **Q:** When and where is Inti Raymi celebrated?
  **A:** June 24 in Cusco, Peru.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz sample key: 1) Día de los Muertos — Nov. 1–2, Mexico; ofrendas honor deceased relatives 2) el cempasúchil (marigold) — its color and scent guide the spirits 3) las velas — light the way 4) el agua — quenches the spirits' thirst after the journey 5) el pan de muerto — offering of food 6) Las Fallas — Valencia, March, giant figures (ninots/fallas) burned on the night of March 19 (San José) 7) Inti Raymi — June 24, Cusco, Festival of the Sun 8) la quinceañera — a girl's 15th birthday celebration marking the passage to young womanhood.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Volleyball strategy

**Prepare:** Court diagram, volleyball, net/rope. Players: as many as available.

**Watch for:**
- Students rotate the wrong way. Rotate clockwise when your team wins the serve back (side-out).
- Students think rotation positions stay fixed during the rally. Players must be in rotational order at the serve, then may switch.
- Students stay silent. Calling 'mine' early prevents collisions and dropped balls.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rotation
*Student:* Learn the 6-player rotation and positions using a court diagram.

*How to teach:* Teach the 6-player rotation and positions on a court diagram.

- **Q:** Which position serves?
  **A:** Position 1, right back.
- **Q:** Which direction do players rotate?
  **A:** Clockwise.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pass-set-hit
*Student:* Practice the pass-set-hit sequence, adding an attack approach.

*How to teach:* Practice pass-set-hit adding an attack approach (left-right-left for right-handers).

- **Q:** What's the footwork for a right-handed attack approach?
  **A:** Left, right, left (last two quick), then jump off both feet.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Communication
*Student:* Practice calling 'mine' and 'out' in a seam-ball drill.

*How to teach:* Practice calling 'mine' and 'out' in a seam-ball drill.

- **Q:** Why call the ball early?
  **A:** It tells teammates who's taking it, preventing collisions and balls dropping between players.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Games
*Student:* Play games tracking successful three-touch sequences.

*How to teach:* Play games tracking successful three-touch sequences.

- **Q:** How is volleyball scored?
  **A:** Rally scoring: a point on every rally; sets to 25, win by 2.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Reflect on how communication affected team success.

*How to teach:* The student reflects on how communication affected success.

- **Q:** Give an example of communication improving play.
  **A:** Calling 'out' on a long serve so the passer lets it go, winning the point.

**Assessment:** Observation.

**Answer key:** Observation rubric: rotation correct (Y/N), uses pass-set-hit sequence (count successful sequences), communicates ('mine', 'out', 'free'), covers hitter. Rules key: positions 1 (right back, server), 2 (right front), 3 (middle front), 4 (left front), 5 (left back), 6 (middle back); rotate clockwise on side-out; rally scoring to 25, win by 2 (deciding set to 15).

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Consumer health and health care access

**Prepare:** Gather five supplement or skin-care ads, two health products to compare (e.g., two sunscreens or toothpastes) with prices and sizes, and the CRAAP test checklist. Know California's confidential services for minors to explain on Day 3.

**Watch for:**
- Students assume supplements are FDA-approved for safety and effectiveness. Supplements do not need FDA approval before sale; claims like 'supports immunity' are not proof.
- Students believe a website with a professional look is reliable. Use the CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose).
- Students compare price per package instead of cost per use. Divide price by number of uses or ounces.

### Day 1 (Monday): Advertising claims
*Student:* Analyze five supplement or skin-care ads for health claims and evidence.

*How to teach:* The student analyzes five ads for health claims and evidence and identifies vague language.

- **Q:** What does the FDA disclaimer 'This statement has not been evaluated by the FDA' mean?
  **A:** The claim has not been reviewed or proven; the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent disease.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Reliable information
*Student:* Evaluate three health websites using the CRAAP test.

*How to teach:* The student evaluates three health websites with the CRAAP test.

- **Q:** Which is more reliable: a .gov health agency page or a supplement company's blog? Why?
  **A:** The .gov page — expert authority, evidence-based, and no profit motive in selling the product.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Health care access
*Student:* Learn how to make an appointment, use insurance cards, and what confidential services minors can access in California.

*How to teach:* Explain how to make an appointment, read an insurance card (member ID, group number, copay, phone number), and which services California minors can consent to confidentially (e.g., outpatient mental health counseling at 12+, sexual and reproductive health care, drug and alcohol counseling at 12+).

- **Q:** What information on an insurance card do you need when making an appointment?
  **A:** The member ID number, group number, insurer name and phone number.
- **Q:** Can a 15-year-old in California get confidential reproductive health care?
  **A:** Yes — minors can consent to many sexual and reproductive health services, and providers must keep them confidential.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Product comparison
*Student:* Compare cost per use of two health products (sunscreen, toothpaste) and their active ingredients.

*How to teach:* The student compares cost per use and active ingredients of two products.

- **Q:** Sunscreen A costs $9 for 3 oz; B costs $14 for 6 oz. Which is cheaper per ounce?
  **A:** A: $3.00/oz; B: $2.33/oz — B is cheaper per ounce.

### Day 5 (Friday): Consumer project
*Student:* Write a consumer guide on choosing a safe product or app.

*How to teach:* The student writes a consumer guide on choosing a safe product or app.

- **Q:** What should you check in a health app's privacy policy?
  **A:** What data it collects, whether it shares or sells data, and whether you can delete your data.

**Assessment:** Consumer guide.

**Answer key:** Consumer guide rubric: 4 = Chooses a product type or health app, explains active ingredients or features to look for (e.g., sunscreen: broad spectrum, SPF 30+, water resistance; toothpaste: fluoride, ADA Seal), how to evaluate claims and sources (CRAAP), cost-per-use comparison with math shown, privacy considerations for apps, and cites reliable sources. 3 = complete with weaker evidence. 2 = mostly opinion. 1 = minimal.

## Computer Science Principles: Python basics

**Prepare:** Create a Trinket (trinket.io) or Replit account. Prepare a short Python program with three syntax errors (missing colon, missing quote, bad indentation). Have the App Lab pet-quiz code open for comparison.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting that input() returns a string. Use int() or float() before doing math.
- Inconsistent indentation. Python uses indentation, not braces, to group code blocks.
- Using = in a condition. Python comparisons use ==.

### Day 1 (Monday): Hello, Python
*Student:* Create a Trinket account, write print statements, and fix three syntax errors in a provided program.

*How to teach:* Run print("Hello, world!"). Then fix three syntax errors in the provided program, reading the error messages for line numbers.

- **Q:** Fix: print("Hi)
  **A:** Add the closing quote: print("Hi").
- **Q:** What error do you get from 'if x > 3' with no colon?
  **A:** A SyntaxError — Python requires a colon at the end of an if line.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Variables and input
*Student:* Write a Python tip calculator using input(), float() conversion and formatted output.

*How to teach:* Write a tip calculator: bill = float(input("Bill: ")); tip = bill * 0.18; print(f"Tip: ${tip:.2f}").

- **Q:** Why use float() around input()?
  **A:** input() returns a string; float() converts it to a number so you can multiply.
- **Q:** What does f"{42.567:.2f}" display?
  **A:** 42.57.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Conditionals
*Student:* Rewrite your App Lab pet-quiz logic in Python using if/elif/else and compare the two versions side by side.

*How to teach:* Rewrite the App Lab pet-quiz logic in Python with if/elif/else; place the two versions side by side and list differences (no braces, elif, and/or keywords, colons, indentation).

- **Q:** Translate if (a > 5 && b < 3) { … } to Python.
  **A:** if a > 5 and b < 3:  (then an indented block).
- **Q:** What is Python's version of JavaScript's 'else if'?
  **A:** elif.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Loops
*Student:* Write a number-guessing game with a while loop and a times-table printer with a for loop and range().

*How to teach:* Write a number guessing game with while and random.randint(1, 100); write a times table with for i in range(1, 13).

- **Q:** What values does range(1, 5) produce?
  **A:** 1, 2, 3, 4 (the end value is excluded).
- **Q:** Write a for loop printing the 7 times table up to 7 × 12.
  **A:** for i in range(1, 13): print(7, "x", i, "=", 7 * i)

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Solve five short Python practice problems (even/odd, FizzBuzz, sum to n, grade letter, countdown) and submit them.

*How to teach:* The student solves the five problems in the assessment key; test each with three inputs.

- **Q:** What does 17 % 5 give in Python?
  **A:** 2.

**Assessment:** Python practice set.

**Answer key:** Python practice set solutions: 1) Even/odd: n = int(input()); print("even" if n % 2 == 0 else "odd"). 2) FizzBuzz: for i in range(1, 101): if i % 15 == 0: print("FizzBuzz") elif i % 3 == 0: print("Fizz") elif i % 5 == 0: print("Buzz") else: print(i) (multiple of 15 must be checked first). 3) Sum to n: total = 0; for i in range(1, n + 1): total += i (n = 10 → 55). 4) Grade letter: if s >= 90 'A' elif s >= 80 'B' elif s >= 70 'C' elif s >= 60 'D' else 'F'. 5) Countdown: for i in range(10, 0, -1): print(i) then print("Liftoff!"). Each program correct on 3 tests = 1 point; 5/5 mastery.
