# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 12

## English 10: Julius Caesar Acts 4–5: Consequences

**Prepare:** Folger Julius Caesar; film clips (1953 Mankiewicz version with Marlon Brando, and a 2012 version such as the RSC production). Prepare the unit test.

**Watch for:**
- Assuming Caesar is the tragic hero because of the title. Use Aristotle's criteria — Brutus has the flaw, reversal and recognition.
- Forgetting the ghost's significance — Caesar's spirit lives on in the revenge.

### Day 1 (Monday): Act 4
*Student:* Read the quarrel scene (4.3) and analyze how Brutus and Cassius's friendship unravels.

*How to teach:* Read 4.3. The student analyzes how the friendship unravels. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What do Brutus and Cassius argue about in 4.3?
  **A:** Brutus accuses Cassius of taking bribes ('itching palm') and refusing him gold; Cassius feels insulted. They reconcile after Brutus reveals Portia has died.
- **Q:** What appears to Brutus at the end of 4.3?
  **A:** Caesar's ghost, saying it will see him at Philippi.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Act 5
*Student:* Read Act 5 and record how each conspirator dies and what Antony says of Brutus.

*How to teach:* Read Act 5. The student records each death and Antony's tribute. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How do Cassius and Brutus die?
  **A:** Cassius, believing Titinius was captured, has Pindarus kill him; Brutus runs onto his own sword held by Strato.
- **Q:** What does Antony say about Brutus?
  **A:** 'This was the noblest Roman of them all' — the only conspirator who acted for the common good, not envy.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Medium comparison
*Student:* Compare the funeral scene in two film versions and write which interpretation is more persuasive.

*How to teach:* Compare the funeral scene in two films. The student writes which is more persuasive. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What should the comparison focus on?
  **A:** Specific director and actor choices (setting, crowd reactions, pacing, delivery) and how they shape the speech's effect.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Tragic hero
*Student:* Decide whether Caesar or Brutus is the tragic hero, completing an Aristotelian criteria chart.

*How to teach:* Review Aristotle's criteria: noble stature, tragic flaw (hamartia), reversal, recognition, and audience pity and fear. The student fills the chart for Caesar and for Brutus with a quote in each box, then writes a two-sentence verdict.

- **Q:** Make a case that Brutus is the tragic hero.
  **A:** He is noble and respected; his hamartia is idealism and misjudgment (trusting Antony, believing others act for Rome); he suffers reversal at Philippi and dies by his own hand, evoking pity.

### Day 5 (Friday): Test
*Student:* Unit test on the play: passage identification, rhetoric analysis, and short essay.

*How to teach:* Give the Julius Caesar unit test from the assessment key (about 60 minutes): passage identification, rhetoric analysis and a short essay. Grade together and have the student correct any misidentified passages.

- **Q:** Who says 'Caesar, now be still; I killed not thee with half so good a will'?
  **A:** Brutus, as he dies.

**Assessment:** Julius Caesar unit test.

**Answer key:** Unit test (sample answers): Passage IDs — 'The fault, dear Brutus...' (Cassius to Brutus, 1.2); 'Et tu, Brute?' (Caesar, 3.1); 'This was the noblest Roman of them all' (Antony about Brutus, 5.5); 'Cowards die many times...' (Caesar to Calpurnia, 2.2). Rhetoric: identify pathos in Antony's showing of the mantle; parallelism in Brutus's speech. Essay rubric (4-point): clear thesis on the tragic hero, at least three quotes, Aristotelian terms used correctly.

## Geometry: Parallelograms

**Prepare:** GeoGebra, graph paper. Prepare cut-out quadrilaterals (or GeoGebra files) for the parallelogram tests.

**Watch for:**
- Using (n − 2)·180 for each interior angle rather than the sum. Divide by n for one angle of a regular polygon.
- Thinking a quadrilateral with one pair of parallel sides and the other pair congruent must be a parallelogram (an isosceles trapezoid is a counterexample).

### Day 1 (Monday): Polygon angle sums
*Student:* Derive the interior angle sum (n – 2)·180° and find interior/exterior angles of regular polygons.

*How to teach:* Have the student split polygons into triangles from one vertex to find the pattern (n − 2)·180°. Then compute interior and exterior angles of regular polygons, noting exterior angles always sum to 360°.

- **Q:** Find the interior angle sum of a decagon and one interior angle of a regular decagon.
  **A:** (10 − 2)·180 = 1440°; 1440 ÷ 10 = 144°.
- **Q:** Each exterior angle of a regular polygon is 30°. How many sides?
  **A:** 360 ÷ 30 = 12 sides.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Parallelogram properties
*Student:* Prove opposite sides of a parallelogram are congruent using a diagonal and ASA.

*How to teach:* Draw diagonal AC in parallelogram ABCD. Use the parallel sides to find two pairs of alternate interior angles, AC as a shared side, ASA for congruent triangles, and CPCTC for opposite sides.

- **Q:** In parallelogram ABCD, AB = 2x + 3 and CD = 5x − 9. Find AB.
  **A:** 2x + 3 = 5x − 9 → x = 4, AB = 11.
- **Q:** Consecutive angles of a parallelogram are (3y)° and (2y + 30)°. Find y.
  **A:** They are supplementary: 5y + 30 = 180 → y = 30.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Diagonals
*Student:* Prove diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other.

*How to teach:* Prove that the diagonals bisect each other using the triangles formed at the intersection (ASA with alternate interior angles and opposite sides). The student practices algebra problems with diagonals.

- **Q:** Diagonals of parallelogram PQRS meet at T. PT = x + 4, TR = 2x − 1. Find PR.
  **A:** x + 4 = 2x − 1 → x = 5, PT = 9, PR = 18.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Proving parallelograms
*Student:* Use the five tests to decide if a quadrilateral is a parallelogram.

*How to teach:* List the five tests: both pairs opposite sides parallel; both pairs opposite sides congruent; both pairs opposite angles congruent; diagonals bisect each other; one pair of sides both parallel and congruent. The student decides for several given quadrilaterals and cites the test.

- **Q:** Quadrilateral has vertices (0, 0), (4, 1), (5, 4), (1, 3). Is it a parallelogram?
  **A:** Yes: slopes of opposite sides are 1/4 and 1/4, and 3 and 3, so both pairs are parallel ; also the diagonals share the midpoint (2.5, 2).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on parallelogram properties.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Must a parallelogram's diagonals be congruent?
  **A:** No — only in a rectangle.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Sum of interior angles of a hexagon → 720°. 2) Each interior angle of a regular octagon → 135°; each exterior → 45°. 3) A regular polygon has exterior angles of 24°; number of sides → 15. 4) In parallelogram ABCD, m∠A = 65°; find ∠B, ∠C, ∠D → 115°, 65°, 115°. 5) Diagonals of parallelogram bisect each other; AE = 3x − 2, EC = x + 8 → x = 5, AC = 26. 6) Is a quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite angles congruent a parallelogram? → Yes. 7) Short proof: opposite sides of a parallelogram are congruent (draw diagonal; alternate interior angles; ASA; CPCTC).

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Intermolecular forces and properties

**Prepare:** Gather pennies, an eyedropper, water, 70% or 91% rubbing alcohol, vegetable oil, paper towels, a thermometer, and a timer. Prepare a boiling-point data table: H₂O 100 °C, H₂S −60 °C, CH₄ −162 °C, ethanol 78 °C; halogens F₂ −188 °C, Cl₂ −34 °C, Br₂ 59 °C, I₂ 184 °C; noble gases He −269, Ne −246, Ar −186, Kr −153 °C. Keep alcohol away from flames.

**Watch for:**
- Students confuse intermolecular forces with covalent bonds. Boiling water separates molecules from each other (breaking hydrogen bonds); it does not break O–H bonds inside molecules.
- Students think hydrogen bonds are bonds to any hydrogen. A hydrogen bond is an attraction between H attached to N, O or F and a lone pair on another N, O or F.
- Students assume heavier molecules always boil higher. True within a family (London forces grow with size), but hydrogen bonding makes water boil far higher than heavier H₂S.

### Day 1 (Monday): Drops on a penny
*Student:* Count drops of water, alcohol and oil that fit on a penny and relate to surface tension.

*How to teach:* Using the eyedropper, the student adds drops of water one at a time onto a clean, dry penny until it spills, records the count, and repeats with alcohol and oil (cleaning and drying between). Discuss why water forms a tall dome.

- **Q:** Which liquid fit the most drops, and why?
  **A:** Water (typically 20–40+ drops), because hydrogen bonding gives it the strongest cohesion and highest surface tension.
- **Q:** What would adding a drop of dish soap to the water do?
  **A:** Fewer drops would fit; soap disrupts hydrogen bonding at the surface and lowers surface tension.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Evaporation race
*Student:* Time evaporation of water and alcohol on paper towel and measure cooling with a thermometer.

*How to teach:* Wrap a thermometer bulb in a strip of paper towel. Dip in water, record the temperature every 30 seconds for 4 minutes while fanning gently; repeat with alcohol. Also time how long equal wet spots take to evaporate.

- **Q:** Which liquid cooled the thermometer more, and why?
  **A:** Rubbing alcohol; it evaporates faster because its intermolecular forces are weaker, and evaporation removes energy from the bulb.
- **Q:** Why does sweating cool you?
  **A:** Water molecules with the most energy escape when sweat evaporates, lowering the average kinetic energy (temperature) of what remains on the skin.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Explain forces
*Student:* Rank liquids by intermolecular force strength using evidence.

*How to teach:* Introduce the three forces: London dispersion (all molecules, stronger with more electrons), dipole-dipole (polar molecules), and hydrogen bonding (H on N, O, F). The student ranks water, isopropyl alcohol and oil by force strength using two lines of evidence from this week.

- **Q:** Rank water, alcohol and oil by surface tension and support with evidence.
  **A:** Water highest (most drops on penny); alcohol and oil lower. Water's extensive hydrogen bonding creates the strongest cohesion.
- **Q:** Which force is present in all molecules?
  **A:** London dispersion forces.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Melting & boiling points
*Student:* Analyze a data table of boiling points to explain trends with molar mass and hydrogen bonding.

*How to teach:* Give the boiling-point table. The student graphs boiling point vs. molar mass for the noble gases and halogens and explains the trend, then explains why H₂O (18 g/mol) boils at 100 °C while H₂S (34 g/mol) boils at −60 °C.

- **Q:** Why does boiling point increase from F₂ to I₂?
  **A:** Larger molecules have more electrons, so London dispersion forces are stronger and more energy is needed to separate them.
- **Q:** Why does water have such a high boiling point for its size?
  **A:** Strong hydrogen bonds between water molecules must be overcome; H₂S has no hydrogen bonding.

### Day 5 (Friday): Investigation report
*Student:* Write a lab report connecting bulk properties to particle forces.

*How to teach:* The student writes the full lab report connecting bulk properties to particle forces. Review it with the rubric and have the student revise the reasoning paragraph.

- **Q:** Predict whether glycerin (many O–H groups) would evaporate faster or slower than water.
  **A:** Slower — more hydrogen bonding sites and larger molecules mean stronger attractions.

**Assessment:** Lab report (HS-PS1-3).

**Answer key:** Lab report rubric (HS-PS1-3), 4 levels: 4 = Question and hypothesis; procedure with controlled variables (same penny, same dropper, same height); data tables (drops on a penny, evaporation time, temperature drop); claim ranking water > alcohol, oil for surface tension and alcohol evaporating faster with larger cooling; reasoning that stronger intermolecular attractions (water's hydrogen bonding) hold molecules together, producing higher surface tension and slower evaporation; error sources (drop size, room air) and a next step. 3 = correct claim and data, reasoning mentions forces but not hydrogen bonding specifically. 2 = data with weak reasoning. 1 = incomplete.

## Modern World History: China, Japan and Latin America; independence movements

**Prepare:** Commissioner Lin Zexu's letter to Queen Victoria (1839), Sun Yat-sen's Three Principles of the People excerpt, readings on Meiji Japan and the Panama Canal/Roosevelt Corollary (1904). Assemble a 5–6 document DBQ set on responses to imperialism (e.g., Lin's letter, Aguinaldo, Menelik at Adwa, Naoroji, Sun Yat-sen, a Boxer poster).

**Watch for:**
- Seeing colonized peoples as passive. Responses ranged from armed resistance to reform, modernization and nationalism.
- Thinking Japan only resisted imperialism. After modernizing, Japan became an imperial power itself.

### Day 1 (Monday): Opium War
*Student:* Read Commissioner Lin's letter to Queen Victoria and analyze its arguments.

*How to teach:* Source Lin's letter (1839, an imperial official writing to Queen Victoria to stop the opium trade). The student lists his arguments and predicts Britain's response. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What is Lin Zexu's main argument?
  **A:** Opium is banned in Britain because it is harmful, so Britain should not sell it to China; he appeals to Victoria's conscience.
- **Q:** What were the results of the First Opium War (1839–1842)?
  **A:** Britain won; the Treaty of Nanjing gave Britain Hong Kong, opened five treaty ports, and required an indemnity.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): China's responses
*Student:* Chart the Taiping, Self-Strengthening, Boxer Rebellion and 1911 Revolution.

*How to teach:* The student charts the Taiping Rebellion (1850–64), Self-Strengthening Movement (1860s–90s), Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901), and 1911 Revolution: who, goal, result. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What did the Boxers want?
  **A:** To expel foreigners and Christian influence from China.
- **Q:** What were Sun Yat-sen's Three Principles?
  **A:** Nationalism, democracy (people's rights), and people's livelihood.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Japan
*Student:* Compare Japan's Meiji modernization and its own imperial expansion (Korea, Taiwan).

*How to teach:* Compare Meiji modernization with Japan's expansion (Taiwan 1895, Korea 1910). The student explains the irony. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** How did Japan avoid colonization?
  **A:** After Perry's arrival (1853), the Meiji government (1868) rapidly modernized industry, military and education on Western models.
- **Q:** Which territories did Japan take by 1910?
  **A:** Taiwan (1895) and Korea (annexed 1910).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Latin America
*Student:* Study economic imperialism, the Panama Canal and the Roosevelt Corollary.

*How to teach:* Read about economic imperialism in Latin America, the Panama Canal (opened 1914) and the Roosevelt Corollary. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What did the Roosevelt Corollary (1904) claim?
  **A:** The U.S. could act as an 'international police power' in Latin America to keep European powers out.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 DBQ
*Student:* Write a DBQ essay: How did colonized peoples respond to imperialism?

*How to teach:* Give the DBQ: How did colonized peoples respond to imperialism? Allow 10 minutes planning, 40 writing. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Name three types of response to imperialism.
  **A:** Armed resistance, modernization/reform, and nationalist movements.

**Assessment:** DBQ essay.

**Answer key:** DBQ rubric (4-point per row): Thesis — directly answers the question with a defensible claim; Evidence — uses at least 4 documents accurately plus outside knowledge; Sourcing — explains author, purpose, audience or context for at least 2 documents; Analysis — groups documents and explains how they support the claim; Writing — organized, clear. 17–20 = proficient. Strong thesis example: 'Colonized peoples responded to imperialism through armed resistance (Boxers, Ethiopia at Adwa), modernization and reform (Self-Strengthening, Meiji Japan), and nationalist movements (Sun Yat-sen, Naoroji).'

## Music & Media Arts: Songwriting: putting it together

**Prepare:** BandLab with a USB or phone microphone, the lyric draft, a chord progression, and an 'I like / I wonder / What if' feedback sheet.

**Watch for:**
- Students write bridges that sound like the chorus. A bridge contrasts: new chords (e.g., starting on vi or IV), different melodic range or rhythm.
- Students over-produce demos. A demo captures the song's structure and melody; polish comes later.
- Students defend every line in feedback. Listen first; decide later.

### Day 1 (Monday): Set lyrics
*Student:* Sing or play your lyric over the chord progression, adjusting the melody so stressed syllables fall on strong beats.

*How to teach:* Sing or play the lyric over the progression, adjusting so stressed syllables land on strong beats.

- **Q:** Which beats are strong in 4/4?
  **A:** Beats 1 and 3 (beat 1 strongest).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Bridge
*Student:* Write a 4–8 bar bridge that contrasts the chorus by changing chords (e.g., starting on vi or IV) and melodic range.

*How to teach:* Write a 4–8 bar bridge that contrasts with the chorus.

- **Q:** Give two ways to make a bridge contrast.
  **A:** Start on a different chord like vi or IV, change melodic range or rhythm, or change lyric perspective.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Demo
*Student:* Record a rough demo in BandLab with a simple guitar/keyboard part and scratch vocal.

*How to teach:* Record a rough demo in BandLab with a simple part and scratch vocal.

- **Q:** What is a scratch vocal?
  **A:** A temporary guide vocal recorded to hear the song, replaced later.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Critique
*Student:* Play the demo for a listener and use the 'I like / I wonder / What if' protocol to collect feedback.

*How to teach:* Play the demo for a listener and collect feedback with 'I like / I wonder / What if'.

- **Q:** Why use a structured protocol?
  **A:** It balances positives, questions and suggestions so feedback is useful and kind.

### Day 5 (Friday): Revise
*Student:* Revise one lyric line, one melodic phrase and the arrangement based on feedback; save as version 2.

*How to teach:* Revise a lyric line, a melodic phrase and the arrangement; save version 2.

- **Q:** Why save versions?
  **A:** To compare and go back if a change doesn't work.

**Assessment:** Demo checkpoint.

**Answer key:** Demo checkpoint: complete song form (verse–chorus–verse–chorus–bridge–chorus), lyric set with prosody, contrasting bridge, rough recording with accompaniment and scratch vocal, documented feedback, version 2 with three specific revisions (lyric line, melodic phrase, arrangement). Proficient = all present.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Pickleball fundamentals

**Prepare:** Get pickleball paddles and outdoor balls; find a court (many parks have them) or chalk a court on a driveway (20 × 44 ft, with a 7-ft non-volley zone on each side of the net; a portable net or rope at 34 inches in the middle). Read the USA Pickleball rules summary.

**Watch for:**
- Students volley while standing in the kitchen. You may not volley (hit before the bounce) while touching the non-volley zone, including the line, or by momentum after the shot.
- Students volley the return of serve or the third shot. The two-bounce rule: the serve and the return must each bounce before being hit.
- Students think either team can score on any rally. In traditional scoring only the serving team scores.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rules
*Student:* Learn pickleball rules and scoring.

*How to teach:* Teach the rules and scoring; walk the court and call sample scores.

- **Q:** What is the two-bounce rule?
  **A:** The serve must bounce before the receiver returns it, and the return must bounce before the serving team hits it; after that, volleys are allowed.
- **Q:** Why does a doubles game start at 0-0-2?
  **A:** Only one player on the first serving team serves at the start, so the game begins as if that team were on its second server.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Serve & return
*Student:* Practice the underhand serve and deep return (50 each).

*How to teach:* Practice the underhand serve and deep return, 50 each, aiming at targets.

- **Q:** Why aim the return deep?
  **A:** It keeps the serving team back at the baseline, giving you time to move to the kitchen line.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dinks
*Student:* Practice cross-court dinking rallies.

*How to teach:* Practice cross-court dink rallies from the kitchen line.

- **Q:** What is a dink?
  **A:** A soft shot from near the kitchen line that arcs over the net and lands in the opponent's kitchen.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Volleys
*Student:* Practice punch volleys at the kitchen line.

*How to teach:* Practice punch volleys at the kitchen line with a short, firm motion.

- **Q:** Can your foot touch the kitchen line when you volley?
  **A:** No — that's a fault.

### Day 5 (Friday): Games
*Student:* Play doubles games and self-assess.

*How to teach:* Play doubles games and self-assess on the skill checklist.

- **Q:** Who can score points in traditional pickleball scoring?
  **A:** Only the serving team.

**Assessment:** Skill checklist.

**Answer key:** Skill checklist: underhand serve (paddle moving upward, contact below the waist, ball lands in the diagonal service court) 7/10; deep return 7/10; cross-court dink rally of 10+; punch volley at the kitchen line with compact swing; knows scoring and calls the three-number score in doubles. Rules key: games to 11, win by 2; doubles score 'server score – receiver score – server number (1 or 2)'; first serve of the game starts at '0-0-2'; kitchen = 7 ft non-volley zone; faults include ball out, into the net, volleying in the kitchen, violating the two-bounce rule.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Sharing the road

**Prepare:** Read handbook sections on sharing the road with pedestrians, bicycles, motorcycles, large trucks, buses and light rail. Have a bicycle available to show its size. Print a truck 'no-zone' diagram. Build the Unit 3 test.

**Watch for:**
- Students think cyclists must ride on the sidewalk or far right at all times. Cyclists may take the lane when it's too narrow to share, when turning left, or avoiding hazards; drivers must give at least 3 feet when passing.
- Students assume a truck driver can see them. If you can't see the truck's mirrors, the driver can't see you.
- Students think lane splitting is illegal in California. It is legal; drivers must not block or open doors on motorcyclists, and motorcyclists must do it safely.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pedestrians & cyclists
*Student:* Learn the 3-foot passing rule for bicycles and how to share bike lanes when turning.

*How to teach:* Discuss vulnerable road users. The student learns the 3-foot passing rule and how to merge into a bike lane before turning right.

- **Q:** How much space must you give when passing a cyclist?
  **A:** At least 3 feet; if you can't, slow down and pass only when it is safe.
- **Q:** What is 'dooring' and how do you prevent it?
  **A:** Opening a car door into a cyclist's path; check mirrors and look back before opening (the 'Dutch reach' — open with the far hand).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Motorcycles
*Student:* Learn about motorcycle visibility and lane splitting in California.

*How to teach:* The student learns why motorcycles are hard to see and California's lane-splitting rules.

- **Q:** Why should you look twice for motorcycles?
  **A:** They are small, can be hidden in blind spots, and their speed and distance are hard to judge.
- **Q:** Is lane splitting legal in California?
  **A:** Yes; drivers must not block or interfere with motorcyclists, and riders must do it safely.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Trucks & buses
*Student:* Learn about truck 'no-zones' and wide turns; sit in a parked car near a truck diagram to visualize blind spots.

*How to teach:* Show the no-zone diagram. The student marks a truck's blind areas and explains wide turns.

- **Q:** Where are a large truck's no-zones?
  **A:** Directly in front, directly behind, along both sides (especially the right) and near the cab.
- **Q:** A truck ahead signals a right turn and swings left. What should you do?
  **A:** Stay back and do not pass on the right; the truck needs that space to turn.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Light rail & slow vehicles
*Student:* Learn light rail rules and slow-moving vehicle signs.

*How to teach:* The student learns light-rail rules and the slow-moving vehicle sign.

- **Q:** What does an orange triangle with a red border on a vehicle mean?
  **A:** It is a slow-moving vehicle, traveling 25 mph or less (e.g., farm equipment).
- **Q:** May you turn left across light-rail tracks in front of an approaching train?
  **A:** No — never turn in front of an approaching light-rail vehicle; obey light-rail signals.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 test
*Student:* Unit 3 test on navigating roadways and sharing the road.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 test. Grade together.

- **Q:** If you can't see a truck driver's mirrors, what does that mean?
  **A:** The driver can't see you — you are in a no-zone.

**Assessment:** Unit 3 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 3 test key (sample): 1) Signal distance before a turn — 100 ft. 2) Right turn with bike lane — merge into the bike lane within 200 ft. 3) Lane change steps — signal, mirror, head check, go. 4) Passing illegal — near hills, curves, intersections, solid line. 5) Center left-turn lane — max 200 ft. 6) Hydrant — 15 ft. 7) Uphill with curb — wheels away from curb. 8) Freeway lane change signal — 5 seconds. 9) HOV marking — white diamond. 10) Passing a cyclist — at least 3 feet; slow down if you can't. 11) Truck no-zones — directly behind, directly in front, alongside (especially the right side), and around the cab. 12) Truck making a right turn swings wide left first — never squeeze in on the right. 13) Light rail — never turn in front of an approaching light rail vehicle; obey light-rail signals. 14) Slow-moving vehicle emblem — orange triangle with red border (vehicles going 25 mph or less). 15) Motorcycles — look twice; they are hard to see and judge.

## Computer Science Principles: Variables, expressions and strings

**Prepare:** Copy the AP CSP Exam Reference Sheet (from the College Board CED) for the assignment arrow (←) and MOD. Prepare tracing problems on paper. Have App Lab open for the Mad Libs and counter apps.

**Watch for:**
- Reading a ← b as 'a equals b forever'. Assignment copies the current value once; later changes to b don't change a.
- Declaring a variable inside an event handler for a counter, which resets it each click. A counter must be a global variable declared outside the function.
- Forgetting spaces in concatenation: "Hello" + name gives 'HelloSam'. Add " " explicitly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Variables
*Student:* Trace assignment statements by hand (a ← 3; b ← a + 2; a ← b) and predict final values.

*How to teach:* Trace a ← 3; b ← a + 2; a ← b by keeping a table with a column per variable and a row per line. Do three more tracing problems together, then independently.

- **Q:** Trace: a ← 3; b ← a + 2; a ← b. Final a and b?
  **A:** a = 5, b = 5.
- **Q:** Trace: x ← 4; y ← x * 3; x ← y − x. Final x and y?
  **A:** y = 12, x = 12 − 4 = 8.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Expressions
*Student:* Write expressions with arithmetic and the MOD operator and test them in App Lab.

*How to teach:* Explain operators +, −, *, /, and MOD (remainder). Have the student test expressions in App Lab with console.log, and use MOD to check if a number is even.

- **Q:** What is 23 MOD 6?
  **A:** 5 (23 = 3 × 6 + 5).
- **Q:** How can MOD tell whether n is even?
  **A:** If n MOD 2 = 0, n is even; if it equals 1, n is odd.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Strings
*Student:* Build a 'Mad Libs' app that concatenates user input into a story.

*How to teach:* Build a Mad Libs app: text inputs for noun, verb, adjective, name; a button that reads them with getText and sets a label to the combined story. Check spaces in the output.

- **Q:** If noun is "cat" and adj is "silly", what does "The " + adj + " " + noun + " ran." produce?
  **A:** "The silly cat ran."

### Day 4 (Thursday): Counter app
*Student:* Build a clicker counter app that updates a label using a global variable.

*How to teach:* Build a clicker: declare var count = 0; outside the handler; on click, count = count + 1 and setText the label. Then ask the student to move the declaration inside to see the bug.

- **Q:** Why does the counter stay at 1 if 'var count = 0' is inside the click handler?
  **A:** The variable is recreated and reset to 0 on every click, then becomes 1; it needs to be global so its value persists between clicks.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Complete AP-style pseudocode tracing questions on variables.

*How to teach:* Give the 8-question tracing quiz in the assessment key using AP pseudocode, then check the Mad Libs and counter apps.

- **Q:** Trace: x ← 5; y ← x; x ← x + 1. What are x and y?
  **A:** x = 6, y = 5.

**Assessment:** Tracing quiz and Mad Libs app.

**Answer key:** Tracing quiz: 1) a ← 3; b ← a + 2; a ← b → a = 5, b = 5. 2) x ← 10; y ← x; x ← 20 → y = 10. 3) 17 MOD 5 = 2. 4) 20 MOD 4 = 0. 5) n ← 7; n ← n * 2 − 1 → 13. 6) s ← "Hi" + " " + "Bo" → "Hi Bo". 7) p ← 4; q ← p MOD 3; p ← q + p → p = 5, q = 1. 8) Value of 3 + 4 * 2 → 11. Score 7–8 mastery. Mad Libs app: complete if it takes at least four text inputs and displays a story built with concatenation (spaces correct); counter app: label increases by 1 per click using a global variable.
