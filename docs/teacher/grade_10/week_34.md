# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 34

## English 10: Writing poetry and dramatic monologue

**Prepare:** Poetry notebook; reread Tagore's poem. Prepare the Unit 8 test: drama and poetry terms with a cold-read poem (e.g., Claude McKay's 'America' or Langston Hughes's 'Harlem').

**Watch for:**
- Writing a 'poem' that is just chopped prose. Discuss line breaks and imagery.
- Monologue that summarizes plot. It should reveal the character's inner conflict.

### Day 1 (Monday): Imitation poem
*Student:* Write an imitation of Tagore's structure ('Where…') about your own vision of freedom.

*How to teach:* Reread Tagore's poem aloud and note the repeated 'Where...' clauses and final prayer. The student writes their own 10–15 line imitation using the same structure about their vision of freedom.

- **Q:** What makes Tagore's structure distinctive?
  **A:** Repeated 'Where...' clauses that build to a final prayer: 'Into that heaven of freedom, my Father, let my country awake.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Extended metaphor
*Student:* Write a poem built on one extended metaphor for identity.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm metaphors for identity (a river, a house, a tree). The student picks one and writes a poem extending it through at least eight lines, then reads it aloud. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What is an extended metaphor?
  **A:** A comparison sustained through several lines or a whole poem.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Monologue
*Student:* Write a 1-minute dramatic monologue for a minor character from any text this year.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm minor characters from the year's texts (Ismene, Calpurnia, Nwoye, Mrs. Linde, Mildred). The student writes a 1-minute monologue in that character's voice revealing an inner conflict. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How is a monologue different from a soliloquy?
  **A:** A monologue is a long speech to others; a soliloquy is spoken alone, revealing private thoughts.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Perform
*Student:* Rehearse and perform the monologue or a poem for family.

*How to teach:* The student rehearses the monologue or a poem three times, marking pauses and emphasis. They perform for family; give feedback on voice, expression and pacing. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What makes a performance effective?
  **A:** Clear voice, expression that matches emotion, and purposeful pauses.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 8 check
*Student:* Unit 8 test on drama and poetry terms with a cold-read poem.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test on drama and poetry terms with a cold-read poem (e.g., 'Harlem'). Grade with the assessment key. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What is a refrain?
  **A:** A repeated line or phrase in a poem.

**Assessment:** Creative portfolio pieces and unit test.

**Answer key:** Creative portfolio rubric (4-point each): imitation poem follows Tagore's 'Where...' structure with vivid images and a final turn; extended metaphor sustained throughout; monologue reveals a minor character's perspective in voice; performance with expression. Unit 8 test (sample answers with 'Harlem'): 1) 'What happens to a dream deferred?' → rhetorical question. 2) 'Does it dry up / like a raisin in the sun?' → simile. 3) Final line 'Or does it explode?' → italicized, isolated for emphasis, suggests violent frustration. Terms: soliloquy, monologue, dramatic irony, sonnet, extended metaphor, refrain.

## Geometry: Multiplication rule, counting and fair decisions

**Prepare:** Calculator with nPr/nCr (or use the formulas). Prepare the Unit 9 test from the assessment key.

**Watch for:**
- Using permutations when order doesn't matter (committees, lottery picks).
- Forgetting to update counts in multi-stage problems without replacement.

### Day 1 (Monday): Multiplication rule
*Student:* Apply P(A and B) = P(A)P(B|A) to multi-stage problems.

*How to teach:* Teach P(A and B) = P(A)·P(B|A) with tree diagrams for multi-stage problems.

- **Q:** A bag has 5 red and 3 blue marbles. P(two reds without replacement)?
  **A:** 5/8 · 4/7 = 20/56 = 5/14.
- **Q:** P(first red then blue without replacement)?
  **A:** 5/8 · 3/7 = 15/56.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Permutations
*Student:* Count arrangements with nPr (passwords, race finishes).

*How to teach:* Introduce the counting principle and nPr = n!/(n − r)! for arrangements where order matters.

- **Q:** How many ways can 8 runners finish 1st, 2nd and 3rd?
  **A:** 8 · 7 · 6 = 336.
- **Q:** How many 4-digit PINs with no repeated digits?
  **A:** 10 · 9 · 8 · 7 = 5,040.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Combinations
*Student:* Count selections with nCr and compute lottery probabilities.

*How to teach:* Show nCr = n!/(r!(n − r)!) divides out the orderings. Compute lottery odds.

- **Q:** How many ways to choose 2 people from 5 for a committee?
  **A:** 5C2 = 10.
- **Q:** Probability of winning a 6-of-49 lottery with one ticket?
  **A:** 1/13,983,816.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fair decisions
*Student:* Analyze whether games are fair and evaluate decisions like product testing.

*How to teach:* Compute expected value to decide fairness. Discuss product-testing decisions (e.g., sampling items to estimate defect rates).

- **Q:** A game costs $1; roll a die and win $5 (plus your dollar back) on a 6. Is it fair?
  **A:** Expected value = (1/6)(5) + (5/6)(−1) = 0, so it is fair.
- **Q:** Two players pick who goes first by flipping two coins: A wins if they match, B if they differ. Fair?
  **A:** Yes: P(match) = 2/4 = P(differ).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 9 test
*Student:* Unit 9 test on probability.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 9 test from the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** When do you use combinations instead of permutations?
  **A:** When order does not matter (selections, groups).

**Assessment:** Unit 9 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 9 test with answers: 1) P(two red marbles without replacement from 5 red, 3 blue) → 5/8 · 4/7 = 5/14. 2) 4-digit PIN with repetition → 10,000; without repetition → 5,040. 3) Gold/silver/bronze among 8 runners → 8P3 = 336. 4) Choose 3 toppings from 10 → 10C3 = 120. 5) Lottery choose 6 of 49 → 1/13,983,816. 6) P(A or B), P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4, P(A and B) = 0.2 → 0.7. 7) Game: roll a die; win $5 on a 6, lose $1 otherwise → expected value 0, fair. 8) P(king | face card) → 1/3.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Ocean acidification

**Prepare:** Gather cabbage indicator, a straw, eggshells (from about 4 eggs, rinsed and dried) or seashells, vinegar, water, two jars, and the kitchen scale. Bookmark NOAA's Hawaii Ocean Time-series (HOT) pH/pCO₂ graph (Station ALOHA) and readings on California oyster hatcheries and pteropods (NOAA Ocean Acidification Program). Build the Unit 8 test from the key.

**Watch for:**
- Students think the ocean is becoming acidic (below pH 7). Seawater is still basic (~pH 8.05); 'acidification' means it is shifting toward acidic — a ~0.1 drop since preindustrial times, about a 26–30% increase in H⁺.
- Students think acid directly dissolves all shells in the ocean now. The bigger problem is reduced carbonate ion availability, which makes building shells harder, especially for larvae and pteropods.
- Students think vinegar is a realistic ocean model. It is far more acidic; it models the direction of the effect, not its speed.

### Day 1 (Monday): Breathe into indicator
*Student:* Blow through a straw into cabbage indicator water and observe the pH drop from CO₂.

*How to teach:* Put purple cabbage indicator water in a cup and have the student blow gently through a straw (never suck) for 1–3 minutes, watching for a color change. Write the equations.

- **Q:** Why does the indicator turn pinker when you exhale into it?
  **A:** Exhaled CO₂ dissolves and forms carbonic acid (CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻), lowering the pH.
- **Q:** How is this like the ocean?
  **A:** The ocean absorbs about a quarter of human CO₂ emissions, which forms carbonic acid and lowers seawater pH.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Shell dissolution
*Student:* Soak eggshells in vinegar vs. water for three days, measuring mass daily.

*How to teach:* Weigh equal masses of eggshell into a jar of vinegar and a jar of water. Each day for three days, remove, pat dry, weigh, and return. The student calculates percent mass change.

- **Q:** Shells in vinegar went from 10.0 g to 6.5 g. What percent was lost?
  **A:** 3.5 ÷ 10.0 × 100 = 35%.
- **Q:** Why is there a control jar of water?
  **A:** To show that the mass loss is caused by the acid, not by soaking or handling.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ocean data
*Student:* Analyze Hawaii Ocean Time-series pH and CO₂ data from NOAA.

*How to teach:* Using the HOT/Station ALOHA graph, the student describes trends in seawater pCO₂ and pH since 1988 and calculates the approximate rate of pH change.

- **Q:** What relationship do the HOT data show between ocean CO₂ and pH?
  **A:** As dissolved CO₂ rises (tracking atmospheric CO₂), pH falls — an inverse relationship.
- **Q:** If pH fell from 8.11 to 8.05 over 30 years, what is the rate?
  **A:** 0.06 ÷ 30 = 0.002 pH units per year (0.02 per decade).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Impacts
*Student:* Research effects on CA oyster farms and pteropods.

*How to teach:* The student researches effects on California and West Coast shellfish: oyster hatchery larvae failures in the late 2000s when corrosive upwelled water reached hatcheries, monitoring and buffering now used by hatcheries, and pteropod shell dissolution observed off the West Coast. Write a summary with sources.

- **Q:** Why are oyster larvae especially vulnerable?
  **A:** They must build their first shells quickly, and low carbonate water makes that harder and more energy-costly.
- **Q:** Why do pteropods matter to the food web?
  **A:** These tiny swimming snails are food for fish such as salmon, so their decline can ripple up the food chain.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 8 test
*Student:* Unit 8 test.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test. Grade together and collect the shell lab.

- **Q:** Write the equation showing how added H⁺ reduces carbonate ions in seawater.
  **A:** H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → HCO₃⁻

**Assessment:** Shell lab and unit test.

**Answer key:** Shell lab: full credit for daily mass table for shells in vinegar vs. water, percent mass lost (vinegar shells lose much more; water shells nearly constant), bubbles noted, equation CaCO₃ + 2CH₃COOH → Ca(CH₃COO)₂ + H₂O + CO₂, and a caveat about vinegar being far more acidic than seawater. Unit 8 test key (sample): 1) Water expands ~9% when it freezes because of its open hydrogen-bonded lattice. 2) Molarity of 1.5 mol in 3.0 L — 0.50 M. 3) pH 4 vs. pH 7: 1,000× more H₃O⁺. 4) CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. 5) Added H⁺ combines with CO₃²⁻ to make HCO₃⁻, lowering carbonate available for CaCO₃ shells. 6) Surface ocean pH dropped from about 8.2 to about 8.1 (now ~8.05). 7) Breathing into indicator turns it from purple toward pink (more acidic). 8) A buffer resists changes in pH. 9) Neutralization: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. 10) Chalk dissolves faster in vinegar than water — acid reacts with carbonate.

## Modern World History: Global challenges today

**Prepare:** UN Sustainable Development Goals (sdgs.un.org), UNHCR Global Trends data, news sources on COVID-19 responses, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Op-ed examples from a newspaper.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking global problems can be solved by one country alone. Climate, migration and pandemics cross borders.
- Writing an op-ed that is all opinion. Strong op-eds use data and address counterarguments.

### Day 1 (Monday): SDGs
*Student:* Read the UN Sustainable Development Goals and choose one.

*How to teach:* Read the list of 17 SDGs together. The student chooses one, reads its targets and progress data, and summarizes why it matters. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** How many Sustainable Development Goals are there, and what is the deadline?
  **A:** 17 goals, adopted by the UN in 2015, with a target date of 2030.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Migration
*Student:* Analyze global migration data and refugee stories.

*How to teach:* Examine UNHCR data and read two refugee stories. The student explains push and pull factors. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a refugee and a migrant?
  **A:** A refugee is forced to flee persecution, war or violence and is protected under international law; a migrant may move for many reasons, including work or family.
- **Q:** Give a push factor and a pull factor.
  **A:** Push: war or persecution. Pull: safety or job opportunities.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pandemic

### Day 4 (Thursday): Human rights
*Student:* Connect the UDHR to a current human-rights issue.

- **Q:** When was the UDHR adopted, and who led its drafting?

### Day 5 (Friday): Op-ed
*Student:* Write a 600-word op-ed on a global challenge.

*How to teach:* Read a model op-ed. The student writes a 600-word op-ed on a global challenge with evidence and a call to action. Score with the rubric. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What is an op-ed?
  **A:** An opinion essay published opposite the editorial page, arguing a position with evidence.

**Assessment:** Op-ed rubric.

**Answer key:** Op-ed rubric (4-point each): Position — clear and specific on a global challenge; Evidence — at least three facts or data points with sources (e.g., UNHCR, WHO, UN); Counterargument — acknowledged and answered; Call to action — realistic; Style — about 600 words, engaging, organized. 16–20 = proficient.

## Computer Science Principles: Create Performance Task: development

**Prepare:** Set up a development log (date, what was done, problems, next steps). Arrange a peer reviewer (co-op, online CS community or local programming club) and a shared doc for feedback.

**Watch for:**
- Coding everything before testing. Develop incrementally: add one feature, test, then add the next.
- Testing only 'happy path' inputs. Include edge cases: empty input, very large numbers, no matches.
- Getting too much help from others on the code. The College Board requires the program's code and written work be the student's own; collaborators' or AI help must follow current AP rules and be acknowledged.

### Day 1 (Monday): Interface
*Student:* Build the program's screens and event handlers in App Lab (or Python) and log your progress.

*How to teach:* Build screens and event handlers; log progress.

- **Q:** What's the difference between iterative and incremental development?
  **A:** Iterative: repeatedly refining the whole program through cycles of feedback and testing. Incremental: building it in small working pieces, adding one feature at a time.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): List
*Student:* Code the list that stores your program's data and display it to verify it is populated correctly.

*How to teach:* Code the list and display it to verify it's populated correctly.

- **Q:** How can you check your list is populated correctly?
  **A:** Display or console.log the list and its length and compare to the expected data.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Procedure
*Student:* Write the student-developed procedure with a parameter that uses sequencing, selection and iteration.

*How to teach:* Write the procedure with a parameter, using sequencing, selection and iteration; call it from an event.

- **Q:** Point to the selection and iteration in your procedure.
  **A:** Answers vary; e.g. the FOR EACH loop through the list (iteration) and the IF that checks each item against the parameter (selection).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Test
*Student:* Write and run at least four test cases, including edge cases, recording expected vs. actual results.

*How to teach:* Write and run at least four test cases, including edge cases; record expected vs. actual.

- **Q:** Give an edge case for a program that filters a list by a user's choice.
  **A:** A choice that matches no items, or matches every item; also empty input.

### Day 5 (Friday): Peer review
*Student:* Share your program with a CS peer through an online community or co-op and give/receive feedback using a collaborative document.

*How to teach:* Share the program with a peer; give and receive feedback in a shared document.

- **Q:** How should you respond to peer feedback?
  **A:** Record it, decide which suggestions improve the program for users, and note what you changed and why.

**Assessment:** Development log and test cases.

**Answer key:** Development log rubric: 4 = dated entries every day describing progress, bugs and fixes; screens, list and procedure all working; at least four test cases (including two edge cases) with expected vs. actual and fixes; peer feedback recorded with a response; 3 = all parts, fewer details; 2 = missing tests or log entries; 1 = incomplete. Sample proficient test case: input = empty text box; expected = 'Please enter a value'; actual = crashed → fixed by adding a check.
