# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 25

## English 10: Letter from Birmingham Jail and the UDHR

**Prepare:** 'Letter from Birmingham Jail' (April 16, 1963; available from the King Institute at Stanford), the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (un.org), sample op-eds for fallacy practice.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking King rejects all laws. He argues for obeying just laws and openly breaking unjust ones while accepting the penalty.
- Labeling any argument they disagree with as a fallacy. A fallacy is an error in reasoning, not an opinion they dislike.

### Day 1 (Monday): Read Letter part 1
*Student:* Read the first half of the Letter and outline King's refutations of the clergymen's claims.

*How to teach:* Explain the context: King was jailed in Birmingham; eight white clergymen published a statement calling the protests unwise and untimely. The student reads the first half and outlines each criticism with King's response.

- **Q:** How does King answer the charge that he is an 'outsider'?
  **A:** He says he was invited, and that 'Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere' — we are caught in 'an inescapable network of mutuality.'
- **Q:** How does King respond to 'wait'?
  **A:** He says 'wait' has almost always meant 'never,' and 'justice too long delayed is justice denied.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Read Letter part 2
*Student:* Finish the Letter and analyze King's definition of just vs. unjust laws; connect to Antigone.

*How to teach:* The student finishes the Letter and writes King's definitions of just and unjust laws in their own words. Then they write a short paragraph connecting King's reasoning to Antigone's defense to Creon.

- **Q:** How does King define just and unjust laws?
  **A:** A just law squares with the moral law or law of God and uplifts human personality; an unjust law is out of harmony with moral law, degrades human personality, or is imposed on a minority that had no voice in making it.
- **Q:** How does King's position connect to Antigone?
  **A:** Both break a human law they consider unjust because a higher law demands it, and both accept the penalty openly.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): UDHR
*Student:* Read the 30 articles of the UDHR and choose three that King's letter supports.

*How to teach:* Read the UDHR preamble together, then the student skims all 30 articles. They choose three that King's letter supports and pair each with a line from the Letter. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Name an article King's letter supports.
  **A:** Article 1 (all humans born free and equal in dignity and rights), Article 2 (no discrimination by race), Article 7 (equal protection of the law), Article 20 (peaceful assembly), or Article 21 (taking part in government/voting).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fallacies
*Student:* Learn eight logical fallacies and identify fallacies in sample op-eds.

*How to teach:* Teach eight fallacies with examples: ad hominem, straw man, false dilemma, slippery slope, bandwagon, hasty generalization, appeal to authority, red herring. The student finds fallacies in sample op-eds. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** 'Either we ban phones in school or students will never learn anything.' Name the fallacy.
  **A:** False dilemma (either-or).
- **Q:** 'My opponent can't be trusted on taxes—he's been divorced twice.' Name the fallacy.
  **A:** Ad hominem — attacking the person instead of the argument.

### Day 5 (Friday): Rhetorical analysis
*Student:* Write a timed rhetorical analysis of one paragraph from the Letter.

*How to teach:* The student writes a 40-minute rhetorical analysis of one paragraph from the Letter. Score with the rubric. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What is the purpose of King's very long sentence listing the experiences of Black Americans?
  **A:** To make the reader feel the weight of accumulated injustice and show why 'wait' is impossible — the sentence's length itself enacts the endless waiting.

**Assessment:** Timed rhetorical analysis.

**Answer key:** Timed rhetorical analysis rubric: 4 = identifies King's purpose and audience (the eight white clergymen who called his actions 'unwise and untimely,' and a wider national audience); analyzes at least two devices in the chosen paragraph (e.g., the long periodic sentence beginning 'when you have seen vicious mobs lynch your mothers and fathers...' building pathos; allusions to Socrates or Aquinas for ethos; logical definition of just/unjust laws); explains effect on the audience; 3 = devices named with partial analysis; 2 = summary; 1 = off-topic.

## Geometry: Arc length, sectors and radians

**Prepare:** Paper plates, string, ruler, scissors, calculator. Leave answers in terms of π and as decimals.

**Watch for:**
- Using diameter instead of radius in A = πr². Circle the radius in every problem first.
- Mixing degree and radian mode on calculators when converting. Use the factor π/180 explicitly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Circumference & area
*Student:* Give informal arguments for C = 2πr and A = πr² by slicing a paper plate into sectors.

*How to teach:* Have the student cut a plate into 16 sectors and arrange them alternately into a near-parallelogram: base ≈ half the circumference (πr), height ≈ r, so area ≈ πr². Measure circumference with string to check C/d ≈ π.

- **Q:** Explain why the rearranged sectors approach a rectangle with area πr².
  **A:** The base is half the circumference (πr) and the height is the radius r, so area = πr·r = πr².

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Arc length
*Student:* Derive arc length as a fraction of circumference and solve problems.

*How to teach:* Arc length = (θ/360)·2πr. The student solves forward and backward problems.

- **Q:** Find the arc length of a 60° arc in a circle of radius 6.
  **A:** (60/360)·12π = 2π ≈ 6.28.
- **Q:** An arc of length 5π has a central angle of 90°. Find the radius.
  **A:** (1/4)·2πr = 5π → r = 10.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Radians
*Student:* Measure a radius-length string around a plate to define one radian; convert degrees and radians.

*How to teach:* Wrap a string the length of the radius around the plate's edge and mark the angle: that is 1 radian (about 57.3°). Teach conversions with π radians = 180°.

- **Q:** Convert 150° to radians.
  **A:** 150·π/180 = 5π/6.
- **Q:** Convert 3π/2 radians to degrees.
  **A:** 270°.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sector area
*Student:* Find sector and segment areas, e.g., pizza slices and sprinkler coverage.

*How to teach:* Sector area = (θ/360)·πr². Segment area = sector − triangle. Solve pizza and sprinkler problems.

- **Q:** A sprinkler sprays 15 ft and turns through 120°. What area does it water?
  **A:** (1/3)·225π = 75π ≈ 235.6 ft².
- **Q:** Find the segment area for a 90° sector of radius 6.
  **A:** Sector 9π minus triangle 18 → 9π − 18 ≈ 10.27.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on arc length and sectors.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** In radians, what is the arc length formula?
  **A:** s = rθ, with θ in radians.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Arc length, r = 6, 60° → 2π ≈ 6.28. 2) Arc length r = 10, 3π/4 radians → 7.5π ≈ 23.56. 3) Convert 45° → π/4; 150° → 5π/6; 2 radians → ≈ 114.6°. 4) Sector area r = 10, 72° → 20π ≈ 62.83. 5) 14-inch pizza cut into 8 slices → area per slice 49π/8 ≈ 19.24 in². 6) Sprinkler radius 15 ft rotating through 120° → 75π ≈ 235.6 ft². 7) Segment area: r = 6, 90° → 9π − 18 ≈ 10.27.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: The carbon cycle

**Prepare:** Print a NOAA or NASA carbon-cycle diagram with reservoirs (GtC) and fluxes (GtC/yr). Gather chalk or limestone (or seashells), vinegar, a calculator and a spreadsheet. Approximate values to use: atmosphere ~875 GtC (pre-2020 diagrams; about 900 today), surface ocean ~900, deep ocean ~37,000, vegetation ~450–650, soils ~1,500–2,400, fossil fuel reserves ~1,000+, sedimentary rocks ~60–100 million GtC; human emissions ~10 GtC/yr; 1 ppm CO₂ ≈ 2.13 GtC.

**Watch for:**
- Students think carbon is 'used up' in the cycle. Carbon atoms are conserved; they move among reservoirs.
- Students think all human CO₂ stays in the air. About half is absorbed by oceans and land; roughly 45% remains in the atmosphere.
- Students confuse reservoirs (amounts, GtC) with fluxes (rates, GtC/yr). Use units to tell them apart.

### Day 1 (Monday): Reservoirs
*Student:* Map carbon reservoirs and fluxes in gigatons using NOAA data.

*How to teach:* Using the diagram, the student makes a table of reservoirs (GtC) and fluxes (GtC/yr): photosynthesis, respiration/decay, ocean uptake/release, volcanism, fossil fuel burning. Ask which reservoir is largest and which flux humans added.

- **Q:** Which reservoir holds the most carbon?
  **A:** Sedimentary rocks (limestone and organic carbon) — tens of millions of GtC; among the fast-cycle reservoirs, the deep ocean (~37,000 GtC).
- **Q:** What process moves carbon from the atmosphere to the biosphere?
  **A:** Photosynthesis (6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Quantitative model
*Student:* Build a simple spreadsheet box model of atmospheric carbon over 50 years.

*How to teach:* Build a spreadsheet box model: Year, Atmospheric carbon, Natural in, Natural out (equal). Start with natural fluxes balanced so the line stays flat. The student explains why a balanced system is steady.

- **Q:** If natural inflows equal outflows, what happens to atmospheric carbon over time?
  **A:** It stays constant (steady state).
- **Q:** Convert 875 GtC of atmospheric carbon to ppm of CO₂.
  **A:** 875 ÷ 2.13 ≈ 411 ppm.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Human flux
*Student:* Add fossil fuel emissions to your model and compare to Keeling Curve data.

*How to teach:* Add human emissions (10 GtC/yr) with 55% taken up by sinks. The student graphs 50 years, converts to ppm, and compares the slope to the Keeling Curve's recent ~2.4 ppm/yr.

- **Q:** With 10 GtC/yr emitted and 45% remaining, how fast does atmospheric CO₂ rise in ppm/yr?
  **A:** 4.5 GtC/yr ÷ 2.13 ≈ 2.1 ppm/yr.
- **Q:** Why might the model underestimate the real rise?
  **A:** Emissions are growing rather than constant, and sinks may absorb a smaller fraction over time.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Carbon in rocks
*Student:* Drop vinegar on limestone/chalk and connect to geosphere carbon storage.

*How to teach:* Drop vinegar on chalk or limestone and observe fizzing. Write the reaction and connect to carbon stored in rocks and the slow carbon cycle (weathering, burial, volcanic release).

- **Q:** Write the equation for vinegar reacting with limestone.
  **A:** CaCO₃ + 2CH₃COOH → Ca(CH₃COO)₂ + H₂O + CO₂
- **Q:** How did carbon get into limestone originally?
  **A:** Marine organisms built shells from dissolved calcium and carbonate; the shells accumulated and became rock.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a model explanation with your spreadsheet graph.

*How to teach:* The student writes a model explanation with the spreadsheet graph, explaining reservoirs, fluxes, human perturbation and limitations. Score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Why is burning fossil fuels a problem for the carbon cycle if the carbon was originally in the atmosphere?
  **A:** It moves carbon stored over millions of years in the slow cycle into the atmosphere in decades, much faster than natural processes can remove it.

**Assessment:** Spreadsheet carbon model.

**Answer key:** Model rubric (4 levels): 4 = Spreadsheet with year column, atmospheric carbon (starting ~875 GtC), natural fluxes in and out balanced, human emissions (~10 GtC/yr) with about 55% absorbed by sinks, conversion to ppm (÷ 2.13), and a graph over 50 years showing ~2.1 ppm/yr increase (close to the observed ~2.4 ppm/yr); written explanation of assumptions, comparison to Keeling Curve data, and one limitation (e.g., constant emissions or constant sink fraction). 3 = working model but weak comparison. 2 = model errors (mixing reservoirs and fluxes). 1 = incomplete.

## Modern World History: Origins of the Cold War

**Prepare:** Churchill's 'Iron Curtain' speech (March 5, 1946), Stalin's response, Truman Doctrine speech (March 12, 1947), Marshall Plan summary, UN Charter preamble, alliance maps.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Cold War began only in 1947. Tensions grew from Yalta and 1945–46.
- Confusing containment with rollback.

### Day 1 (Monday): Yalta & UN
*Student:* Analyze Yalta agreements and read the UN Charter preamble.

*How to teach:* Read a summary of the Yalta Conference (February 1945) and the UN Charter preamble. The student lists what each of the Big Three wanted and what was agreed. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Where was the UN Charter signed?
  **A:** San Francisco, June 26, 1945.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Iron Curtain
*Student:* Read Churchill's speech and Stalin's response.

*How to teach:* Source Churchill's 'Iron Curtain' speech (March 5, 1946, Fulton, Missouri) and Stalin's response. The student compares claims. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Where did Churchill say the Iron Curtain ran?
  **A:** From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Containment
*Student:* Analyze the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan.

*How to teach:* Read the Truman Doctrine speech excerpt and a Marshall Plan summary. The student explains containment and how each policy supported it. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What was containment?
  **A:** Preventing the spread of communism.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Alliances
*Student:* Map NATO, Warsaw Pact and SEATO members.

*How to teach:* The student maps NATO, the Warsaw Pact and SEATO members with a legend. Discuss why alliances formed. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Name three founding NATO members.
  **A:** U.S., Canada, Britain, France (any three of the 12).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on Cold War origins.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key without notes (about 20 minutes). Grade it together; for each missed item the student finds the answer in their notes and writes a one-sentence correction.

- **Q:** What was the Berlin Airlift?
  **A:** U.S. and British planes supplied West Berlin during the Soviet blockade (1948–49).

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz (answers): 1) Yalta → February 1945, divided Germany into zones, promised free elections in Eastern Europe, USSR to join the war against Japan. 2) UN Charter → signed June 26, 1945 in San Francisco. 3) Iron Curtain → 'From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic.' 4) Truman Doctrine → support free peoples resisting subjugation (Greece, Turkey). 5) Marshall Plan → about $13 billion for European recovery. 6) NATO → 1949; Warsaw Pact → 1955.

**Answer key:** Proposal presentation rubric: 4 = identifies a real community need, describes the project with who/what/when/how, uses future and 3+ subjunctive structures, persuasive conclusion, 3 minutes; 3 = minor gaps or errors; 2 = vague plan or many errors; 1 = incomplete. Reading check key: Chávez and Huerta co-founded the NFWA in 1962; the Delano grape strike began in 1965 and led to a national grape boycott; nonviolent tactics (marches, fasts, boycotts); Huerta coined '¡Sí, se puede!' ## Music & Media Arts: Podcasting: production

**Prepare:** Audacity, a quiet room with soft surfaces, phone or USB mic, the interviewee, the student's original intro music from Unit 2.

**Watch for:**
- Students skip room tone. Record 30 seconds of silence in the room to patch gaps and for noise reduction.
- Students cut out every pause. Natural pauses keep speech human; remove only long gaps and filler.
- Students set music as loud as voices. Music beds sit well under speech.

### Day 1 (Monday): Record host
*Student:* Record host segments in a quiet, soft-surfaced room, re-recording any flubbed lines.

*How to teach:* Record host segments, re-recording flubbed lines.

- **Q:** Why leave a short pause before re-reading a flubbed line?
  **A:** It makes clean edit points.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Interview
*Student:* Record the interview with phone or USB mic, capturing a 30-second room-tone sample.

*How to teach:* Record the interview and capture 30 seconds of room tone.

- **Q:** What is room tone?
  **A:** The natural background sound of a room with no one speaking, used to fill gaps smoothly.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Edit
*Student:* Edit the interview down to its best 3 minutes, removing filler and long pauses.

*How to teach:* Edit the interview to its best 3 minutes.

- **Q:** What should you cut?
  **A:** Filler words, long pauses, repetition and off-topic tangents, without changing the meaning.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Music & mix
*Student:* Add your original intro music from Unit 2, crossfades between segments, and balanced levels.

*How to teach:* Add intro music, crossfades and balanced levels.

- **Q:** What is a crossfade?
  **A:** One sound fades out while another fades in, overlapping for a smooth transition.

### Day 5 (Friday): Draft
*Student:* Export a draft MP3 and collect feedback from one listener.

*How to teach:* Export a draft MP3 and collect feedback.

- **Q:** Is it ethical to edit an interview so the person seems to say something they didn't?
  **A:** No — edits must preserve the speaker's meaning.

**Assessment:** Draft episode.

**Answer key:** Draft episode checklist: host segments clean and re-recorded where flubbed; interview edited to best 3 minutes; 30-second room tone captured; intro music, crossfades and balanced levels (speech clear, music lower); exported MP3; feedback from one listener recorded.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Choreography

**Prepare:** Pick a 30-second music-video dance clip for analysis, music for the student's choreography (clean lyrics), a phone on a tripod or stack of books, and a choreography rubric.

**Watch for:**
- Students choreograph without counting. Map moves to eight-counts so the dance repeats reliably and lines up with the music's phrases.
- Students use only one level. Include high, middle and low levels and different pathways for interest.
- Students judge their performance only by feel. Video review against the beat reveals timing errors.

### Day 1 (Monday): Counts & elements
*Student:* Learn eight-counts, levels, pathways and formations by breaking down a 30-second music-video dance.

*How to teach:* Break down a 30-second dance clip into eight-counts; identify levels, pathways and formations.

- **Q:** How many beats are in four eight-counts?
  **A:** 32.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Create
*Student:* Choreograph a 32-count sequence to a song of choice, using at least two levels and one turn.

*How to teach:* The student choreographs a 32-count sequence with at least two levels and one turn.

- **Q:** What are the three levels in dance?
  **A:** High (on toes, jumping), middle (standing), low (near or on the floor).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rehearse
*Student:* Rehearse the sequence slowly, then at tempo, fixing transitions that break rhythm.

*How to teach:* Rehearse slowly, then at tempo, fixing transitions.

- **Q:** Why rehearse slowly first?
  **A:** To build correct muscle memory and smooth transitions before adding speed.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Record
*Student:* Record a full performance from the front and review timing against the beat.

*How to teach:* Record a full performance from the front and review timing.

- **Q:** How can you check timing on video?
  **A:** Clap or count along with the music while watching to see whether moves land on the beat.

### Day 5 (Friday): Self-critique
*Student:* Score your video on accuracy, rhythm, energy and creativity and set two fixes.

*How to teach:* The student scores the video and sets two fixes.

- **Q:** Write a specific fix.
  **A:** Example: 'Start the turn on count 7 instead of 8 so I finish facing front on 1.'

**Assessment:** Choreography video.

**Answer key:** Choreography video rubric (4 levels, each scored 1–4): Accuracy (performs the 32 counts as designed), Rhythm (movements land on the beat), Energy/expression, Creativity (uses at least two levels, one turn, varied pathways). Proficient total 12+/16. Self-critique must name two specific fixes with count numbers (e.g., 'arm on count 5 of the second eight is late').

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Stress, coping and communication

**Prepare:** Get a journal. Find a guided progressive muscle relaxation and a 10-minute mindfulness audio (e.g., from a reputable health system or UCLA Mindful). Prepare five blaming statements for Day 3.

**Watch for:**
- Students think all stress is bad. Short-term stress can motivate; chronic, unmanaged stress harms health.
- Students confuse assertive with aggressive. Assertive communication states your needs respectfully ('I feel… when… I need…'); aggressive blames or attacks.
- Students think coping means avoiding the problem. Healthy coping includes problem-solving, relaxation and seeking support, not avoidance or substance use.

### Day 1 (Monday): Stress log
*Student:* Track stressors and physical stress responses for three days and identify patterns.

*How to teach:* The student tracks stressors and body responses (headache, tight shoulders, racing heart) for three days and looks for patterns.

- **Q:** Name three physical signs of stress.
  **A:** Faster heart rate, muscle tension, headaches, stomachaches, trouble sleeping.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Coping strategies
*Student:* Practice box breathing, progressive muscle relaxation and a 10-minute mindfulness exercise; rate which helped most.

*How to teach:* Practice box breathing (inhale 4, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4), progressive muscle relaxation and a 10-minute mindfulness exercise; the student rates each.

- **Q:** Why does slow breathing calm the body?
  **A:** It activates the parasympathetic ('rest and digest') nervous system, slowing heart rate and lowering stress hormones.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): I-statements
*Student:* Practice assertive communication by rewriting five blaming statements as I-statements and role-playing two.

*How to teach:* The student rewrites five blaming statements as I-statements and role-plays two with you.

- **Q:** Rewrite: 'You never let me use the car!'
  **A:** 'I feel frustrated when I can't use the car for practice because I need driving hours. Can we plan a schedule?'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Conflict resolution
*Student:* Use a five-step conflict-resolution model to role-play a disagreement with a sibling or friend.

*How to teach:* Teach a five-step model: calm down, each person states the problem with I-statements, listen and paraphrase, brainstorm solutions, agree on one and follow up. Role-play a sibling disagreement.

- **Q:** Why paraphrase the other person's view?
  **A:** It shows you listened and checks understanding, which lowers defensiveness.

### Day 5 (Friday): Wellness plan
*Student:* Write a personal wellness plan with three coping strategies, two trusted adults and daily habits.

*How to teach:* The student writes the personal wellness plan. Score with the rubric.

- **Q:** What should you do if coping strategies aren't helping?
  **A:** Talk to a trusted adult, doctor or counselor; call or text 988 if in crisis.

**Assessment:** Wellness plan.

**Answer key:** Wellness plan rubric (4 levels): 4 = Names personal stressors and body signals from the log; three specific coping strategies tested this week with when/how to use each; two named trusted adults with contact info; daily habits with measurable targets (8–10 hrs sleep, 60 min activity, screen limits, time with friends); a plan for when coping isn't enough (talk to parent, counselor, 988). 3 = complete but vague. 2 = missing support people or habits. 1 = minimal.

## Computer Science Principles: Simulations and computational models

**Prepare:** Read the Code.org simulations lesson. Plan a 15-minute observation of a store line or drive-through (Day 4) with a stopwatch. Have App Lab open.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a simulation predicts exactly what will happen. Simulations estimate outcomes based on assumptions and randomness; results vary from run to run.
- Expecting exactly 500 heads in 1,000 flips. Results cluster near 50% but vary; larger samples get closer on average.
- Forgetting that every model simplifies. Always name what the model leaves out.

### Day 1 (Monday): Why simulate?
*Student:* Read the Code.org simulations lesson and list three real simulations (weather, traffic, epidemics) and what each simplifies.

*How to teach:* Read the simulations lesson and list three real simulations (weather forecasting, traffic flow, epidemic spread) with what each simplifies (e.g. traffic model treats all drivers alike).

- **Q:** Why use a simulation instead of a real experiment?
  **A:** Real experiments may be too costly, dangerous, slow or impossible (e.g. testing an epidemic); simulations let you test many scenarios quickly.
- **Q:** What does every simulation do to reality?
  **A:** Simplifies it — it's an abstraction that leaves out details and relies on assumptions.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Random numbers
*Student:* Write an App Lab program that flips a coin 1,000 times with randomNumber(0,1) and compare the result to the expected 50%.

*How to teach:* Write a loop that flips a coin 1,000 times: heads += randomNumber(0,1). Display the percentage; run several times and compare to 50%.

- **Q:** You get 512 heads out of 1,000. Is the coin unfair?
  **A:** No — 51.2% is normal random variation; results cluster near 50% but are rarely exactly 500.
- **Q:** What happens to the percentage if you flip 100,000 times?
  **A:** It tends to get closer to 50% (law of large numbers).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Build a model
*Student:* Build a checkout-line simulation where customers arrive randomly and one cashier serves them; record the average wait.

*How to teach:* Build a checkout simulation: each minute, a customer arrives with some probability (e.g. randomNumber(1,10) <= 3 for 30%); the cashier serves one customer every 3 minutes. Track the line and average wait over 120 minutes.

- **Q:** If a customer arrives each minute with probability 0.3, about how many arrive in 60 minutes?
  **A:** About 18 (0.3 × 60).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Refine the model
*Student:* Time real arrivals at a store or drive-through for 15 minutes, then change your model's arrival rate and add a second cashier to match.

*How to teach:* Observe a real line for 15 minutes, count arrivals, compute the rate per minute, then update the model. Add a second cashier and compare average waits.

- **Q:** You observed 9 arrivals in 15 minutes. What arrival probability per minute should the model use?
  **A:** 9 ÷ 15 = 0.6 per minute (60%).
- **Q:** Why refine a model with real data?
  **A:** So its assumptions match reality and its predictions are more accurate.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining what your model leaves out, how refinement changed its predictions, and one decision it could inform.

*How to teach:* The student writes the model-limitations paragraph; score with the rubric in the assessment key.

- **Q:** Name one thing the checkout model leaves out.
  **A:** Examples: customers leaving long lines, varying service times, rush-hour arrival changes, self-checkout lanes.

**Assessment:** Simulation program and model-limitations paragraph.
