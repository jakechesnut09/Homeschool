- Thinking Torvald is a cartoon villain. He reflects society's expectations of a respectable husband.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Act 1
*Student:* Read Act 1 and chart Torvald's pet names for Nora and what they reveal.

*How to teach:* Read Act 1. The student charts Torvald's pet names and what they reveal. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What do Torvald's pet names reveal?
  **A:** He sees Nora as a child or pet, not an equal.
- **Q:** What does the macaroon scene show?

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Act 2

*How to teach:* Read Act 2. Analyze the tarantella as a symbol. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

*How to teach:* The student lists every secret the audience knows that Torvald doesn't (the loan, the forgery, the macaroons, Krogstad's letter). For each, they explain the tension it creates in a scene.

## Geometry: Independence and conditional probability

**Prepare:** Deck of cards, coins. Prepare a simple medical-testing example (numbers in the guide).

**Watch for:**
- Confusing P(A|B) and P(B|A). Say it in words: 'probability of A given that B happened' and identify which group you restrict to.
- Treating draws without replacement as independent.

### Day 1 (Monday): Independent events
*Student:* Test independence with P(A and B) = P(A)·P(B) on card and coin problems.

*How to teach:* Define independence: P(A and B) = P(A)·P(B). Test with coin/die and with card events.

- **Q:** P(heads on a coin and a 6 on a die)?
  **A:** 1/2 · 1/6 = 1/12.
- **Q:** Are 'draw a red card' and 'draw a face card' independent?
  **A:** P(red and face) = 6/52 = 3/26; P(red)·P(face) = 1/2·12/52 = 6/52. Equal, so yes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Conditional probability
*Student:* Compute P(A|B) by restricting the sample space.

*How to teach:* Model P(A|B) = P(A and B)/P(B) as restricting the sample space to B. Use the card deck.

- **Q:** P(king | face card)?
  **A:** There are 12 face cards and 4 are kings: 4/12 = 1/3.
- **Q:** P(heart | red card)?
  **A:** 13/26 = 1/2.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Real-life examples
*Student:* Explain conditional probability in medical testing and weather forecasts.

*How to teach:* Work through: disease rate 1%, test catches 99% of sick people and wrongly flags 1% of healthy people. Of 10,000 people: 100 sick (99 test positive), 9,900 healthy (99 test positive). P(sick | positive) = 99/198 = 50%. Discuss weather forecasts similarly.

- **Q:** In the example, why is P(sick | positive) only 50% even though the test is 99% accurate?
  **A:** The disease is rare, so false positives from the large healthy group equal the true positives.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Dependent events
*Student:* Draw cards without replacement and compute probabilities.

*How to teach:* Draw cards without replacement and compute multi-step probabilities, adjusting counts after each draw.

- **Q:** P(drawing two hearts in a row without replacement)?
  **A:** 13/52 · 12/51 = 156/2652 = 1/17.
- **Q:** P(drawing a king then a queen without replacement)?
  **A:** 4/52 · 4/51 = 16/2652 = 4/663.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on independence and conditional probability.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** If P(A|B) = P(A), what can you conclude?
  **A:** A and B are independent.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) P(heads and rolling a 6) → 1/12. 2) Are 'draw a heart' and 'draw a king' independent? → P(heart and king) = 1/52 = (1/4)(1/13), yes. 3) P(king | face card) → 4/12 = 1/3. 4) P(two aces without replacement) → 4/52 · 3/51 = 1/221. 5) P(two aces with replacement) → 1/169. 6) A test is 99% accurate for a disease that 1% have; among 10,000 people, 99 true positives and 99 false positives → P(disease | positive) = 50%.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Water's properties and Earth's surface

**Prepare:** Gather a plastic water bottle (not glass), a marker, a ruler, the freezer, sugar cubes, chalk (calcium carbonate sidewalk chalk works), water, vinegar, a baking pan, sand, a cup with a small hole for steady flow, and books to tilt the pan.

**Watch for:**
- Students think ice is denser than water because solids usually are. Hydrogen bonds hold ice in an open hexagonal lattice, making it about 9% less dense than liquid water, so it floats and expands when freezing.
- Students think dissolving means disappearing. The solute is still present, spread out as particles or ions among water molecules.
- Students think erosion only happens in big floods. Slow, repeated processes (frost wedging, dissolution) shape landscapes over long times.

### Day 1 (Monday): Water's properties
*Student:* Review hydrogen bonding and list water's unique properties.

*How to teach:* Review hydrogen bonding. The student lists water's unique properties (high specific heat, ice less dense than liquid, high surface tension, excellent solvent, high heat of vaporization) and links each to hydrogen bonding and an Earth effect.

- **Q:** Why does ice float?
  **A:** Hydrogen bonds lock molecules into an open lattice, making ice less dense than liquid water.
- **Q:** How does water's high specific heat affect Earth's climate?
  **A:** Oceans absorb and release large amounts of heat with little temperature change, moderating coastal climates.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Freeze expansion
*Student:* Freeze a full sealed plastic bottle and measure the expansion; connect to frost wedging.

*How to teach:* Fill a plastic bottle to the brim, cap it, mark dimensions, and freeze overnight. Measure the bulge, or fill a container to a line and measure the new height after freezing. Connect to frost wedging in rock cracks.

- **Q:** If 500 mL of water freezes, about what volume of ice forms?
  **A:** About 545 mL (roughly 9% larger).
- **Q:** Explain frost wedging.
  **A:** Water seeps into cracks, freezes and expands, pushing the crack wider; repeated freeze-thaw breaks rocks apart.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dissolution
*Student:* Compare dissolving rates of sugar cubes and chalk in water and vinegar.

*How to teach:* Drop a sugar cube and a piece of chalk into water, and another of each into vinegar; time dissolving and observe bubbles.

- **Q:** Why does chalk fizz and dissolve in vinegar but not much in water?
  **A:** Chalk is calcium carbonate, which reacts with acid: CaCO₃ + 2CH₃COOH → Ca(CH₃COO)₂ + H₂O + CO₂.
- **Q:** How does this model chemical weathering?
  **A:** Rainwater containing dissolved CO₂ forms weak carbonic acid that slowly dissolves limestone, forming caves and sinkholes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Erosion table
*Student:* Build a sand stream table in a baking pan and model erosion with different water flow.

*How to teach:* Build a sand slope in a baking pan, then pour water at slow and fast rates from the same height. The student sketches channels, deltas and where sediment is deposited.

- **Q:** Where did sand get deposited and why?
  **A:** Where the water slowed (at the bottom of the slope or on inside bends), because slower water has less energy to carry sediment.
- **Q:** How did faster flow change erosion?
  **A:** It cut deeper, wider channels and carried more and larger particles.

### Day 5 (Friday): Report
*Student:* Write an investigation report on water's effect on Earth materials.

*How to teach:* The student writes the investigation report. Score with the rubric and revise.

- **Q:** Give one California example of water shaping the land.
  **A:** Examples: frost wedging of Sierra granite creating talus slopes; rivers carving canyons; waves eroding coastal cliffs; limestone caves such as those in the Sierra foothills.

**Assessment:** Investigation report (HS-ESS2-5).

**Answer key:** Investigation report rubric (HS-ESS2-5): 4 = Includes all three investigations with data (bottle volume increase ≈ 9%; chalk fizzes and dissolves faster in vinegar than water while sugar dissolves faster in water; faster flow carries more and larger sediment, cutting deeper channels); explains each with water's properties (expansion on freezing due to hydrogen-bonded lattice → frost wedging; water as a polar solvent and weak acids from dissolved CO₂ → chemical weathering of limestone; water's cohesion/mass and flow energy → erosion and deposition); connects to a California example (Sierra granite frost wedging, limestone caves, coastal and river erosion). 3 = data plus partial explanations. 2 = data only. 1 = incomplete.

## Modern World History: Nation-building research project

**Prepare:** Library databases (public library card), CIA World Factbook, World Bank Open Data, Freedom House 'Freedom in the World' scores, UNDP Human Development Index. A research report template.

**Watch for:**
- Using one source type only. Require statistics, at least two primary sources (a speech, constitution or interview) and a scholarly or news source.
- Confusing correlation with causation (e.g., 'higher literacy caused democracy'). Teach the student to say 'is associated with' unless evidence shows cause.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose country
*Student:* Choose a nation and draft a research question about freedom and development.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm nations studied this year (e.g., India, Ghana, South Africa, Mexico, South Korea, Poland). The student picks one and drafts a research question about freedom and development, then revises it to be specific and answerable.

- **Q:** Make this question more focused: 'What is Ghana like?'
  **A:** For example: 'How has Ghana's history since independence in 1957 shaped its democracy and economic growth today?'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Research
*Student:* Gather data (GDP, literacy, governance indices) and two primary sources.

*How to teach:* Show the student how to find indicators in the World Factbook, World Bank and Freedom House, recording the year of each figure. The student also finds two primary sources and sources each.

- **Q:** What does GDP per capita measure, and what does it miss?
  **A:** Average economic output per person; it doesn't show inequality, or how wealth is distributed.
- **Q:** What does a Freedom House score measure?
  **A:** Political rights and civil liberties in a country (rated Free, Partly Free or Not Free).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft
*Student:* Write a 1,000-word research report with citations.

*How to teach:* The student drafts the 1,000-word report from an outline: background history, current data, primary-source evidence, analysis, conclusion. Check that every statistic has a citation. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Why include the year with every statistic?
  **A:** Data change over time; the year lets readers judge how current and comparable the figure is.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Visual
*Student:* Create a data visualization or map.

*How to teach:* Help the student choose a visual: a line graph for change over time, a bar chart for comparisons, or a map. They build it with a title, labeled axes and a source line.

- **Q:** Which chart best shows a country's literacy rate from 1960 to 2020?
  **A:** A line graph, because it shows change over time.

### Day 5 (Friday): Present
*Student:* Present findings for 5 minutes.

*How to teach:* The student presents for 5 minutes and answers questions. Score the report and presentation with the rubric. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What should the conclusion of the presentation answer?
  **A:** The research question — a clear claim about freedom and development supported by the key evidence.

**Assessment:** Research report rubric.

**Answer key:** Research report rubric (4-point each): Research question — focused on freedom and development in one nation; Data — at least three indicators (e.g., GDP per capita, literacy rate, life expectancy, Freedom House score) with sources and years; Primary sources — two, accurately sourced; Analysis — explains how history (colonialism, revolution, Cold War) shaped current conditions and distinguishes correlation from causation; Visual — accurate, labeled; Citations — MLA; Presentation — 5 minutes, clear. 22–28 = proficient.

## Spanish III: Herencia mixta: indígena, africana y europea

**Prepare:** Find readings on mestizaje and Afro-Latino culture (e.g., Smithsonian or BBC Mundo articles), and audio of cumbia, salsa, and son jarocho. List borrowed words: Nahuatl (chocolate, tomate, aguacate, chicle, cacahuate), Quechua (papa, cóndor, llama, puma, pampa), Arabic (almohada, azúcar, aceite, arroz, ojalá, alcalde, ajedrez).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Latin American culture is only Spanish and Indigenous: millions of enslaved Africans were brought to the Americas, and African heritage is central in the Caribbean, Colombia, Venezuela, Peru, Brazil, and beyond.
- Using 'raza' or racial labels casually: discuss identity terms respectfully (afrodescendiente, indígena, mestizo) and note that people self-identify in different ways.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mestizaje
*Student:* Read about indigenous, African and European influences.

*How to teach:* Read about mestizaje; your child makes a three-column chart of Indigenous, African, and European influences.

- **Q:** Give one example of each influence in Latin American food.
  **A:** Indigenous: maíz, chocolate, papa; African: mofongo (from West African fufu), plantain dishes, okra (quimbombó); European: wheat bread, pork, olive oil.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Música
*Student:* Listen to cumbia, salsa and son jarocho and identify instruments' origins.

*How to teach:* Listen to cumbia, salsa, and son jarocho; your child identifies instruments and their origins.

- **Q:** Where does cumbia come from, and what are its roots?
  **A:** Colombia's Caribbean coast; it blends African drums, Indigenous flutes (gaitas) and maracas, and European influences.
- **Q:** Where is son jarocho from, and what instrument is typical?
  **A:** Veracruz, Mexico; the jarana (a small guitar), along with the requinto and harp, and zapateado dancing on a wooden platform (tarima).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Lenguas
*Student:* Learn words Spanish borrowed from Nahuatl, Quechua and Arabic.

*How to teach:* Your child learns borrowed words and sorts them by language of origin.

- **Q:** Which language gave Spanish 'chocolate' and 'aguacate'? Which gave 'almohada' and 'ojalá'?
  **A:** Nahuatl; Arabic.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Discusión
*Student:* Discuss sincretismo in celebrations.

*How to teach:* Discuss sincretismo in celebrations (Día de Muertos, la Virgen de Guadalupe, Santería) in Spanish.

- **Q:** What is 'sincretismo'?
  **A:** The blending of different religious or cultural traditions into a new practice, such as Indigenous ancestor honoring combined with Catholic All Souls' Day in Día de Muertos.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Write a 250-word reflective essay.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a 250-word reflective essay.

- **Q:** Translate: 'Latin American culture is a mix of many traditions.'
  **A:** La cultura latinoamericana es una mezcla de muchas tradiciones.

**Assessment:** Essay.

**Answer key:** Essay rubric (250 words, reflective): 4 = explains indigenous, African, and European influences with 3 specific examples (music, language, food, religion), personal reflection, varied past and subjunctive structures, accurate; 3 = minor gaps or errors; 2 = general with few examples; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Basketball skills and strategy

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Environmental health in California

**Prepare:** Open AirNow.gov and your local water supplier's Consumer Confidence Report (annual water quality report). Find your county's cooling center info. Prepare a home audit checklist (smoke alarms on every level and in bedrooms, CO alarms, mold, chemical storage). Build the Unit 9 test.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Water

*How to teach:* The student reads the local water quality report and explains one contaminant (e.g., nitrate, arsenic, lead) — its source, health effect and MCL.

- **Q:** What does 'MCL' mean in a water report?

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Heat & climate
*Student:* Learn about extreme heat health risks and community cooling centers.

*How to teach:* The student learns extreme heat risks and finds community cooling centers.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Home safety

*Student:* Unit 9 test on nutrition, consumer and environmental health.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 9 test. Review.

## Computer Science Principles: Python lists and functions

**Prepare:** Find the CS50x lecture for the Python week (cs50.harvard.edu/x) and choose a 20–30 minute segment on functions and lists. Ensure Trinket/Replit supports multiple files for the module.

**Watch for:**
- Python lists are 0-indexed and slicing excludes the end index: scores[1:3] gives items at index 1 and 2.
- Printing inside a function instead of returning, so the result can't be reused.
- Forgetting to import the module (import mymodule) or calling functions without the module prefix.

### Day 1 (Monday): Lists
*Student:* Create Python lists, use append, len and slicing, and traverse a list of test scores to find the max and average.

*How to teach:* Create scores = [88, 92, 75, 100]; practice append, len, and slicing; traverse to find max and average without built-ins, then compare with max() and sum()/len().

- **Q:** For scores = [88, 92, 75, 100], what is the average?
  **A:** 355 ÷ 4 = 88.75.
- **Q:** What is scores[1:3]?
  **A:** [92, 75].

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Functions
*Student:* Write functions with parameters and return values (e.g., to_fahrenheit, is_vowel) and test each with three inputs.

*How to teach:* Write to_fahrenheit(c) and is_vowel(ch) with def and return; test each with three inputs.

- **Q:** What does to_fahrenheit(37) return using c * 9/5 + 32?
  **A:** 98.6 (Python may show 98.60000000000001 due to round-off).
- **Q:** Write is_vowel.
  **A:** def is_vowel(ch): return ch.lower() in "aeiou"

### Day 3 (Wednesday): CS50 lecture
*Student:* Watch the CS50 Python-week lecture segment on functions and lists and note two ideas that match what you did in App Lab.

*How to teach:* Watch the CS50 segment; the student notes two ideas that match App Lab (e.g. functions = procedures with parameters; lists and loops = traversal).

- **Q:** Name one way Python functions are like App Lab functions.
  **A:** Both take parameters, can return values, and let you name and reuse a block of code (procedural abstraction).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mini project
*Student:* Build a Python program (gradebook, quiz game or word counter) that uses a list, a loop and at least two functions in a separate module.

*How to teach:* Build the mini project with functions in a separate module (e.g. grades.py) imported into main.py.

- **Q:** Why put functions in a separate module?
  **A:** It organizes code, lets the functions be reused in other programs, and keeps the main program short and readable.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Demonstrate the mini project and explain how your functions decompose the problem.

*How to teach:* The student demos the project and explains the decomposition; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** How does decomposition help a larger program?
  **A:** Each function handles one subproblem, so it can be written, tested and fixed independently.

**Assessment:** Python mini-project rubric.

**Answer key:** Mini-project rubric (4 levels): 4 = uses a list, a loop and at least two well-named functions with parameters/return values stored in a separate module that is imported; runs correctly on test inputs; student clearly explains how the functions decompose the problem; 3 = works, explanation weak or functions in the same file; 2 = missing list, loop or functions; 1 = doesn't run.
