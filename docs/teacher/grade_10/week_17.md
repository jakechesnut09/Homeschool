# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 17

## English 10: Literary analysis essay: Things Fall Apart

**Prepare:** Things Fall Apart, Purdue OWL MLA guide, highlighters.

**Watch for:**
- Writing a thesis that is a fact ('Okonkwo dies at the end'). A thesis is an arguable claim.
- Comma splices. Use a semicolon, a period, or a conjunction.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose prompt
*Student:* Choose a prompt (e.g., How does Achebe use Okonkwo's fall to represent the fall of Umuofia?) and write a working thesis.

*How to teach:* Review three prompt choices. The student picks one and writes a working thesis that makes an arguable claim and previews three reasons; test it by asking, 'Could someone disagree?' Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Revise this weak thesis: 'Okonkwo is a character in Things Fall Apart.'
  **A:** For example: 'Okonkwo's fear of weakness drives the violent decisions that lead to his downfall.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Evidence map
*Student:* Gather eight quotations and map them to three body paragraphs.

*How to teach:* The student gathers eight quotations with page numbers and sorts them into three body-paragraph columns, writing a one-line note on what each proves. Check that each paragraph has at least two strong quotes.

- **Q:** Why plan evidence before drafting?
  **A:** To make sure each paragraph has enough proof and the argument is balanced.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft
*Student:* Draft the 1,000-word essay with MLA in-text citations.

*How to teach:* The student drafts the 1,000-word essay from the evidence map, using MLA in-text citations (Achebe 61). Encourage drafting straight through without editing; you check that each paragraph opens with a topic sentence linked to the thesis.

- **Q:** Format an MLA in-text citation for page 61.
  **A:** (Achebe 61).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: semicolons & colons
*Student:* Mini-lesson on semicolons, colons and conjunctive adverbs; add three correct uses to the draft.

*How to teach:* Teach semicolons, colons and conjunctive adverbs. The student adds three correct uses to the draft. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Fix: 'Okonkwo was strong, however he was afraid.'
  **A:** 'Okonkwo was strong; however, he was afraid.'
- **Q:** When do you use a colon?
  **A:** After an independent clause to introduce a list, explanation or quote.

### Day 5 (Friday): Revise
*Student:* Revise using a rubric and read the essay aloud to catch errors.

*How to teach:* The student scores their own draft with the rubric, then reads it aloud to catch run-ons and missing words. Hold the draft checkpoint conference in the assessment key and agree on two revision goals.

- **Q:** Why read your essay aloud?
  **A:** You hear missing words, awkward phrasing and run-ons that the eye skips.

**Assessment:** Draft checkpoint conference.

**Answer key:** Draft checkpoint conference (checklist): arguable thesis answering the prompt; three body paragraphs each with a topic sentence; at least eight quotations, each introduced and analyzed; MLA in-text citations (Achebe page); semicolons/colons used correctly at least three times. Mark each Met/Not yet and set two revision goals. Sample strong thesis: 'Achebe parallels Okonkwo's rigid fear of weakness with Umuofia's inability to adapt, so that one man's fall mirrors the collapse of a society divided by colonialism.'

## Geometry: Proportionality theorems

**Prepare:** GeoGebra, centimeter cubes or sugar cubes (Day 5), graph paper.

**Watch for:**
- Setting up side-splitter proportions using the whole side in one ratio and a part in the other inconsistently. Use part/part = part/part.
- Assuming area scales by k (not k²). Build 2×2×2 cubes from unit cubes to see area ×4 and volume ×8.

### Day 1 (Monday): Side splitter
*Student:* Prove a line parallel to one side divides the other sides proportionally.

*How to teach:* Use AA similarity of △ADE and △ABC (DE ∥ BC) to derive AD/DB = AE/EC. The student solves several side-splitter problems.

- **Q:** DE ∥ BC, AD = 4, DB = 6, AE = 5. Find EC.
  **A:** 4/6 = 5/EC → EC = 7.5.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Angle bisector theorem
*Student:* Apply the angle bisector theorem to find segment lengths.

*How to teach:* State the theorem: an angle bisector divides the opposite side in the same ratio as the adjacent sides. Work examples and have the student check in GeoGebra.

- **Q:** In △ABC, AD bisects ∠A, AB = 6, AC = 9, BC = 10. Find BD and DC.
  **A:** BD/DC = 6/9 = 2/3; BD = 4, DC = 6.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Altitude to hypotenuse
*Student:* Show the altitude to the hypotenuse creates three similar triangles and find geometric means.

*How to teach:* Draw the altitude to the hypotenuse and cut out the three triangles to show they are similar. Derive the geometric mean relationships: altitude² = product of hypotenuse segments; each leg² = hypotenuse × adjacent segment.

- **Q:** The altitude to the hypotenuse divides it into segments of 4 and 9. Find the altitude.
  **A:** h² = 4·9 = 36 → h = 6.
- **Q:** Hypotenuse 13 is split into 4 and 9 (with 4 adjacent to leg a). Find a.
  **A:** a² = 13·4 = 52 → a = 2√13 ≈ 7.2.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Pythagorean proof
*Student:* Prove the Pythagorean theorem from similar triangles; compare to a dissection proof.

*How to teach:* Use the three similar triangles to prove a² + b² = c², then show a dissection (four copies of the triangle inside a square). The student writes the similarity proof.

- **Q:** In the similarity proof, the hypotenuse is split into x and y. What does a² + b² equal?
  **A:** cx + cy = c(x + y) = c·c = c².

### Day 5 (Friday): Scale factor effects
*Student:* Investigate how scale factor k changes perimeter, area (k²) and volume (k³) using cubes.

*How to teach:* Build cubes of side 1, 2 and 3 from unit cubes; record perimeter of one face, surface area and volume in a table. The student generalizes to k, k², k³.

- **Q:** A model car is scaled by 1/20. Its paint area compares how to the real car's?
  **A:** (1/20)² = 1/400 of the real paint area.

**Assessment:** Exit tickets and proof write-up.

**Answer key:** Exit tickets with answers: Day 1) AD = 4, DB = 6, AE = 5, DE ∥ BC → EC = 7.5. Day 2) Angle bisector from A in triangle with AB = 6, AC = 9, BC = 10 → BD = 4, DC = 6. Day 3) Altitude to hypotenuse splits it into 4 and 9 → altitude 6. Day 5) Scale factor 3 → perimeter ×3, area ×9, volume ×27. Proof write-up rubric (Pythagorean theorem via similar triangles): 4 = states the three similar triangles, writes a/c = x/a and b/c = y/b, gets a² = cx and b² = cy, adds to a² + b² = c(x + y) = c²; 3 = one step unjustified; 2 = similarity named but algebra incomplete; 1 = only the statement.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Earth's interior and convection

**Prepare:** Gather a clear heat-safe pot (or glass saucepan), water, food coloring, a few grains of uncooked rice, and the stove. For the solar heater: a cardboard box, aluminum foil, black paint or black paper, plastic wrap, clear plastic tubing or a black-painted bottle, thermometer. Print a seismic-wave diagram showing P- and S-wave shadow zones (USGS or IRIS).

**Watch for:**
- Students think the mantle is liquid lava. The mantle is solid rock that flows extremely slowly (centimeters per year) over long times; only small portions melt.
- Students think Earth's heat comes from the Sun. Internal heat comes from leftover (primordial) heat from Earth's formation and from radioactive decay of uranium, thorium and potassium-40.
- Students think plates float on a liquid layer. They ride on the slowly flowing asthenosphere and are pulled/pushed by slab pull and mantle convection.

### Day 1 (Monday): Earth's layers
*Student:* Use seismic data diagrams to infer Earth's layered structure.

*How to teach:* Show the seismic diagram. Explain that S-waves cannot travel through liquids and P-waves bend at boundaries. The student infers the layers (crust, mantle, liquid outer core, solid inner core) and labels depths.

- **Q:** What evidence shows Earth's outer core is liquid?
  **A:** S-waves do not pass through it, creating an S-wave shadow zone on the far side of Earth from an earthquake.
- **Q:** Why is the inner core solid even though it is hotter than the outer core?
  **A:** The enormous pressure at the center keeps iron-nickel in a solid state.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Convection model
*Student:* Heat water with food coloring and rice in a clear pot to observe convection cells.

*How to teach:* Fill the pot with water, add a few rice grains, and heat from one side or the center. Add a drop of food coloring at the bottom near the heat. The student sketches the circulation and labels where water rises and sinks.

- **Q:** Why does warm water rise?
  **A:** It expands and becomes less dense than surrounding cooler water, so it is pushed upward; cooler, denser water sinks to replace it.
- **Q:** How is this like the mantle?
  **A:** Hot mantle rock rises, spreads under the plates, cools and sinks, forming slow convection cells that help move plates.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Heat sources
*Student:* Explain how radioactive decay and primordial heat drive convection.

*How to teach:* Explain the two internal heat sources and connect to last unit's nuclear chemistry. The student writes a paragraph explaining how decay of U-238, Th-232 and K-40 keeps the mantle convecting.

- **Q:** Name two sources of Earth's internal heat.
  **A:** Primordial heat from Earth's formation and radioactive decay of isotopes such as uranium-238, thorium-232 and potassium-40.
- **Q:** What would happen to plate tectonics if Earth's interior cooled completely?
  **A:** Convection would stop and plate motion would eventually stop, as appears to have happened on Mars.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Plate tectonics
*Student:* Connect mantle convection to California's San Andreas Fault and Sierra Nevada formation.

*How to teach:* Use a plate map of California. Explain that the San Andreas Fault is a transform boundary where the Pacific Plate moves northwest relative to the North American Plate (~3–5 cm/yr). The Sierra Nevada granite formed from magma generated by subduction of the Farallon plate; the range was later uplifted and tilted along faults on its east side.

- **Q:** What type of plate boundary is the San Andreas Fault?
  **A:** A transform boundary (plates slide past each other).
- **Q:** How did the granite of Yosemite form?
  **A:** Magma produced by subduction of the Farallon plate cooled slowly deep underground, forming granite that was later uplifted and exposed by erosion.

### Day 5 (Friday): Energy device design
*Student:* Design and test a solar water heater from a box, foil and tubing; measure temperature gain.

*How to teach:* The student designs and builds a solar water heater, then measures the temperature of a known mass of water before and after 30 minutes in sun. Calculate energy gained with q = mcΔT.

- **Q:** 500 g of water warms from 20 °C to 32 °C. How much energy was absorbed?
  **A:** 500 × 4.184 × 12 = 25,100 J (25.1 kJ).
- **Q:** Why paint the absorber black?
  **A:** Black surfaces absorb more visible light and convert it to thermal energy.

**Assessment:** Device test data and model diagram.

**Answer key:** Device and model rubric: 4 = Labeled diagram of mantle convection (hot material rises, spreads, cools, sinks) linked to plate motion; solar heater data table with start/end temperatures, water mass and energy gained (e.g., 500 g water warming 12 °C gains 500 × 4.184 × 12 = 25,100 J); written explanation of design features (black absorbs radiation, foil reflects light onto the absorber, plastic wrap reduces convective loss) and one improvement. 3 = data and diagram correct, explanation brief. 2 = missing calculations. 1 = incomplete.

## Modern World History: The Russian Revolution and Stalin

**Prepare:** Lenin's April Theses excerpt (1917), Soviet propaganda posters (five-year plans, collectivization), Holodomor survivor testimony (preview: descriptions of starvation), doctored Soviet photographs (e.g., Yezhov removed from a photo with Stalin).

**Watch for:**
- Confusing the February and October Revolutions. February ended the tsar; October brought the Bolsheviks to power.
- Thinking Stalin's policies were economically successful at no cost. Industrial output rose, but millions died.

### Day 1 (Monday): 1917
*Student:* Chart the February and October revolutions and read Lenin's April Theses excerpt.

*How to teach:* Draw a two-column chart: February 1917 (bread riots, soldiers mutiny, Tsar abdicates, Provisional Government) and October 1917 (Bolsheviks seize power). Read the April Theses excerpt; the student explains why Lenin's slogans appealed to workers, peasants and soldiers.

- **Q:** What did Lenin promise in the April Theses?
  **A:** 'Peace, Land, Bread' and 'All power to the soviets.'
- **Q:** Why was the Provisional Government unpopular?
  **A:** It kept Russia in WWI and delayed land reform.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Civil war & NEP
*Student:* Explain War Communism, the Civil War and NEP.

*How to teach:* Explain the Civil War (1918–21) between Bolshevik Reds and anti-Bolshevik Whites, War Communism's grain seizures, and Lenin's retreat to the NEP in 1921. The student writes a cause-effect chain from War Communism to the NEP.

- **Q:** Why did Lenin introduce the NEP?
  **A:** War Communism had wrecked the economy; the NEP allowed some private trade.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Stalin
*Student:* Analyze five-year plans and collectivization with propaganda posters.

*How to teach:* Show two Soviet posters about five-year plans and collectivization. The student identifies the message and compares it with data on industrial growth and peasant resistance (slaughtering livestock, kulak deportations). Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What were the goals of the five-year plans?
  **A:** Rapid industrialization — steel, coal, machinery.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Holodomor & purges
*Student:* Read survivor testimony on the Ukrainian famine and the purges; analyze doctored photographs.

*How to teach:* Read a Holodomor survivor testimony (preview first) and a short reading on the Great Purge. Then show a doctored photo pair (Stalin with and without Yezhov); the student explains what was altered and why.

- **Q:** What was the Holodomor?
  **A:** A man-made famine in Ukraine in 1932–33 caused by collectivization and grain seizures.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on the USSR.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key without notes (about 20 minutes). Grade it together; for each missed item the student finds the answer in their notes and writes a one-sentence correction. Then check the photo analysis.

- **Q:** Why did Stalin doctor photographs?
  **A:** To erase purged officials from history.

**Assessment:** Quiz and photo analysis.

**Answer key:** Quiz (answers): 1) February 1917 → Tsar Nicholas II abdicates; Provisional Government forms. 2) April Theses → 'Peace, Land, Bread'; 'All power to the soviets.' 3) October 1917 → Bolsheviks seize power. 4) War Communism → state control of industry and grain requisitioning during the civil war (1918–21). 5) NEP (1921) → allowed some private trade. 6) Collectivization → peasants forced into collective farms; kulaks targeted. 7) Holodomor → 1932–33 man-made famine in Ukraine, millions died (about 3.5–5 million). 8) Great Purge → 1936–38, about 750,000 executed. Photo analysis: full credit for identifying the alteration, its purpose, and what it reveals about Stalin's regime.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Collisions, insurance and emergencies

**Prepare:** Read the handbook sections on collisions, financial responsibility/insurance and the point system. Review your own family's insurance card. Note: California's minimum liability limits increased to 30/60/15 for policies issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2025.

**Watch for:**
- Students think they should leave if no one seems hurt. You must stop at any collision you are involved in; leaving is hit-and-run.
- Students think insurance is optional if they drive carefully. California requires financial responsibility (usually liability insurance) for every vehicle and driver.
- Students think a blowout means slam the brakes. Grip the wheel firmly, ease off the gas, keep straight, and brake gently once under control.

### Day 1 (Monday): After a collision
*Student:* Learn steps: stop, help injured, call 911, exchange information, report SR-1 within 10 days if required.

*How to teach:* The student lists the steps after a collision and practices with a role-play.

- **Q:** When must you file an SR-1 with the DMV?
  **A:** Within 10 days if anyone was injured or killed or property damage exceeded $1,000 (regardless of fault).
- **Q:** What information should drivers exchange?
  **A:** Names, addresses, driver's license numbers, vehicle registration, and insurance information.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Insurance
*Student:* Learn minimum insurance and financial responsibility.

*How to teach:* The student reads about financial responsibility and looks at the family's insurance card.

- **Q:** What does liability insurance pay for?
  **A:** Injuries and property damage you cause to others.
- **Q:** What are California's minimum liability limits as of 2025?
  **A:** 30/60/15: $30,000 per person injured, $60,000 per accident for injuries, $15,000 property damage.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Point count
*Student:* Learn the negligent operator point system and teen penalties.

*How to teach:* The student learns the point system and provisional penalties.

- **Q:** How many points does a typical moving violation (like speeding) carry?
  **A:** One point; a DUI or hit-and-run carries two points.
- **Q:** What happens if a provisional driver gets 2 points in 12 months?
  **A:** A 30-day restriction: they may drive only with a licensed parent/guardian or adult 25+.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Emergencies
*Student:* Learn what to do for brake failure, tire blowout and stalled on railroad tracks.

*How to teach:* The student learns responses to brake failure, tire blowout and being stalled on railroad tracks.

- **Q:** Your car stalls on railroad tracks and a train is coming. What do you do?
  **A:** Get everyone out and away immediately, moving toward the train at an angle away from the tracks; then call 911 or the number on the crossing sign.
- **Q:** Your brakes fail. What do you do?
  **A:** Pump the brake pedal, shift to a lower gear, apply the parking brake gradually, and steer to a safe spot; warn others with horn and hazards.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Review.

- **Q:** You hit a parked, empty car. What must you do?
  **A:** Stop, leave a note with your name and address (and explanation), and report it to local police or CHP.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) After a collision — stop, check for injuries, call 911 if anyone is hurt, move vehicles out of traffic if possible, exchange information (name, address, license, registration, insurance), take photos. 2) SR-1 report — to DMV within 10 days if anyone was injured or killed or property damage exceeded $1,000. 3) Hitting an unattended car — leave a note with your name and address and report to police. 4) Minimum liability insurance (2025) — $30,000 injury/death to one person, $60,000 for more than one person, $15,000 property damage. 5) Negligent operator threshold for adults — 4 points in 12 months, 6 in 24, or 8 in 36. 6) Provisional drivers under 18 — 2 points in 12 months: 30-day restriction (must be accompanied by a licensed parent/guardian or adult 25+); 3 points: 6-month suspension and 1-year probation. 7) Brake failure — pump the brakes, downshift, use the parking brake gradually, steer to a safe place. 8) Tire blowout — hold the wheel firmly, ease off the gas, don't brake hard, pull off when slowed. 9) Stalled on railroad tracks — get everyone out immediately, move away at an angle toward the train, call the number on the crossing sign or 911.

## Computer Science Principles: Traversals: filter, map and reduce

**Prepare:** Prepare lists in App Lab: 10 song titles, 30 daily high temperatures (some above 90), and a list of Celsius temperatures. Browse the App Lab Data tab to pick a dataset for Day 4.

**Watch for:**
- Modifying the original list while filtering it. Filter into a new list with appendItem so indices don't shift under the loop.
- Initializing max to 0. If all values are negative, 0 is wrong; initialize max to the first element.
- Dividing by the wrong count in an average (e.g. by the index or a fixed number). Divide the sum by list.length.

### Day 1 (Monday): Traversal
*Student:* Write a FOR EACH loop and an index-based for loop that each display every item in a list of 10 song titles, then compare readability.

*How to teach:* Write the same traversal two ways: for (var i = 0; i < songs.length; i++) console.log(songs[i]); and AP's FOR EACH item IN songs. Discuss which is more readable and when you need the index.

- **Q:** When would you need an index-based loop instead of FOR EACH?
  **A:** When you need the position (e.g. to find the index of an item, compare neighbors, or change the element in place).
- **Q:** What does traversal mean?
  **A:** Accessing each element of a list one at a time, in order.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Filter
*Student:* Filter a list of 30 daily high temperatures into a new list of days above 90°F and display the count.

*How to teach:* Filter the 30 temperatures: create an empty list hot = []; loop; if temp > 90 appendItem(hot, temp); display hot.length.

- **Q:** Filter [88, 95, 91, 90, 102] for values above 90.
  **A:** [95, 91, 102]; count = 3 (90 is not above 90).
- **Q:** Why filter into a new list?
  **A:** It keeps the original data unchanged and avoids index errors from removing items mid-loop.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Map and reduce
*Student:* Convert a list of Celsius temperatures to Fahrenheit (map) and find the average (reduce).

*How to teach:* Map Celsius to Fahrenheit with F = C × 9/5 + 32 into a new list; then reduce by summing and dividing by length to find the average.

- **Q:** Map [0, 100, 25] to Fahrenheit.
  **A:** [32, 212, 77].
- **Q:** What is the average of [32, 212, 77]?
  **A:** 321 ÷ 3 = 107.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Data library
*Student:* Use App Lab's data library to load a real dataset into lists.

*How to teach:* In App Lab's Data tab, import a dataset and use getColumn("table", "column") to load a column into a list. Display the length and first five values.

- **Q:** What does getColumn return?
  **A:** A list containing every value in that column of the table.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Solve six traversal problems (count matches, find max, average, filter by length, map to uppercase, find first index) in App Lab.

*How to teach:* The student solves the six traversal problems in the assessment key in App Lab; run each on the sample data to check.

- **Q:** Write pseudocode to find the max of a list.
