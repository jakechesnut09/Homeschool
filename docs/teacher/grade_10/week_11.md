# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 11

## English 10: The funeral orations: rhetoric in action

**Prepare:** Folger 3.2 and a video of Antony's speech (e.g., Marlon Brando, 1953 film). Find three modern ads or speeches.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking repetition is just emphasis. In Antony's speech 'honourable man' becomes sarcasm — meaning reverses.
- Confusing ethos (credibility) with emotion. Pathos is emotion; ethos is trust in the speaker.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rhetorical appeals
*Student:* Learn ethos/pathos/logos and identify each in three modern ads or speeches.

*How to teach:* Define ethos, pathos, logos. The student identifies each in three modern ads or speeches. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** An ad says 'Nine out of ten dentists recommend...' Which appeal?
  **A:** Primarily ethos (expert authority), with a logos-style statistic.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Brutus's speech
*Student:* Analyze Brutus's prose oration for logos and parallel structure, marking each device.

*How to teach:* Read Brutus's speech. The student marks logos and parallel structure. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Find parallel structure in Brutus's speech.
  **A:** 'As Caesar loved me, I weep for him; as he was fortunate, I rejoice at it; as he was valiant, I honour him; but as he was ambitious, I slew him.'
- **Q:** Why does Brutus speak in prose?
  **A:** To sound plain and reasonable to the common people.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Antony's speech
*Student:* Analyze 'Friends, Romans, countrymen' tracking the repetition of 'honourable man' and how meaning shifts.

*How to teach:* Read Antony's speech. The student tracks each 'honourable man' and how the meaning shifts. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How does Antony refute the claim that Caesar was ambitious?
  **A:** He reminds them Caesar brought captives' ransoms to Rome's treasury, wept when the poor cried, and refused the crown three times.
- **Q:** What does Antony use to stir pathos?
  **A:** Caesar's body and bloody mantle, pointing to each dagger hole, and finally the will leaving money (75 drachmas) and gardens to every citizen.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Performance
*Student:* Memorize and perform 12–20 lines of Antony's speech with intentional pauses and emphasis.

*How to teach:* The student chooses 12–20 lines, marks the script with slashes for pauses and underlines for emphasis, and practices aloud daily. Watch a film version of the speech for ideas, then the student performs; score with the performance rubric, especially the ironic emphasis on 'honourable man.'

- **Q:** Why pause after 'My heart is in the coffin there with Caesar'?
  **A:** To appear overcome by grief, letting the crowd react and turn sympathetic.

### Day 5 (Friday): Comparison response
*Student:* Write a timed 40-minute response: Why does Antony's speech succeed where Brutus's fails?

*How to teach:* Set a 40-minute timer. The student spends 5 minutes planning (claim plus three pieces of evidence), 30 writing and 5 proofreading; grade with the timed response rubric and point out one strength and one goal.

- **Q:** In one sentence, why does Antony's speech succeed where Brutus's fails?
  **A:** Antony moves the crowd emotionally with concrete evidence and irony, while Brutus relies on abstract reasoning and his own reputation.

**Assessment:** Performance rubric and timed response.

**Answer key:** Performance rubric: 4 = memorized 12–20 lines accurately, deliberate pauses, emphasis that shows irony in 'honourable man,' clear volume and eye contact; 3 = minor slips; 2 = read rather than performed; 1 = incomplete. Timed response rubric: 4 = claims that Brutus relies on logos in short abstract prose and assumes the crowd agrees, while Antony uses pathos (Caesar's body, wounds, mantle, will), irony, refutation (refused crown three times; wept for the poor; brought ransoms to Rome) and calculated pauses; uses at least three quotes; 3 = good comparison, fewer quotes; 2 = summary; 1 = off-topic.

## Geometry: Triangle congruence proofs

**Prepare:** Print or draw proof templates (two columns, numbered). Make a list of reasons: Given, Reflexive Property, Vertical Angles Theorem, definitions of midpoint/bisector, alternate interior angles, SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, HL, CPCTC.

**Watch for:**
- Using CPCTC before proving the triangles congruent. CPCTC can only be used after a congruence statement.
- Forgetting the shared side (Reflexive Property) or vertical angles in a diagram. Teach the student to mark the diagram with every given, then look for 'free' parts.

### Day 1 (Monday): SSS & SAS proofs
*Student:* Write four two-column proofs using SSS and SAS.

*How to teach:* Model one SSS and one SAS proof, marking the diagram with tick marks and arcs first. The student writes the other two, saying the reason for each line aloud.

- **Q:** Given: M is the midpoint of AB, and PM ⊥ AB. Prove △PMA ≅ △PMB. Which criterion?
  **A:** SAS: AM ≅ BM (midpoint), ∠PMA ≅ ∠PMB (both right angles), PM ≅ PM (Reflexive).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): ASA, AAS, HL
*Student:* Write proofs with ASA, AAS and HL and identify when SSA fails.

*How to teach:* Introduce AAS (it follows from ASA because the third angles match) and HL for right triangles. Show again why SSA fails except in the HL case.

- **Q:** Two right triangles have congruent hypotenuses and one pair of congruent legs. Are they congruent? Why?
  **A:** Yes, by HL (Hypotenuse-Leg).
- **Q:** Two triangles share two angles and a side that is not between them. Which criterion applies?
  **A:** AAS.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): CPCTC
*Student:* Prove triangles congruent and then use CPCTC to prove segments or angles congruent.

*How to teach:* Model a proof that ends by proving a segment or angle congruent: first prove the triangles congruent, then write CPCTC. The student does three such proofs.

- **Q:** In a proof, AC and BD bisect each other at E. What triangles can be proven congruent and what follows?
  **A:** △AEB ≅ △CED by SAS (bisected segments and vertical angles), so AB ≅ CD by CPCTC (and ∠A ≅ ∠C, which makes AB ∥ CD).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Overlapping triangles
*Student:* Separate overlapping triangles by redrawing and complete two proofs.

*How to teach:* Have the student trace the overlapping figure and redraw the two triangles separately, transferring the given marks. Then complete two proofs.

- **Q:** Why redraw overlapping triangles separately?
  **A:** To see clearly which sides and angles correspond and which part (often a shared angle or side) belongs to both.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 test
*Student:* Unit 3 test: logic, line/angle proofs and triangle congruence proofs.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 test from the assessment key; allow the reasons list but not worked examples. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Can you use HL on triangles that are not right triangles?
  **A:** No — HL applies only to right triangles.

**Assessment:** Unit 3 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 3 test key: 1) Contrapositive of 'If an angle measures 30°, it is acute' → If an angle is not acute, then it does not measure 30° (true). 2) Lines parallel; alternate interior angles (4x + 6)° and (6x − 20)° → x = 13, 58°. 3) Proof: Given AB ≅ CB and D the midpoint of AC, prove △ABD ≅ △CBD → AD ≅ CD (def. midpoint), BD ≅ BD (Reflexive), SSS. 4) Proof: Given AB ∥ DC and AB ≅ DC (quadrilateral ABCD with diagonal AC), prove △ABC ≅ △CDA → ∠BAC ≅ ∠DCA (alternate interior), AC ≅ CA (Reflexive), SAS. 5) Then prove BC ≅ DA → CPCTC. 6) Which criterion: two angles and a non-included side → AAS; right triangles with congruent hypotenuses and one leg → HL. Proof rubric as in Week 9 (4-point scale).

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Covalent bonding and molecular shape

**Prepare:** Gather gumdrops (two or three colors) and toothpicks, a plastic comb, and a faucet that can make a thin steady stream. Open PhET 'Molecule Shapes' and test it. Make an electronegativity reference (H 2.2, C 2.6, N 3.0, O 3.4, Cl 3.2).

**Watch for:**
- Students draw CO₂ as bent like water. Carbon in CO₂ has no lone pairs and two double bonds, so it is linear (180°).
- Students think any molecule with polar bonds is polar. A symmetric shape (CO₂, CH₄) cancels bond dipoles, making the molecule nonpolar.
- Students forget lone pairs when predicting shape. Lone pairs repel more strongly and squeeze bond angles (109.5° → 107° in NH₃ → 104.5° in H₂O).

### Day 1 (Monday): Lewis structures
*Student:* Draw Lewis structures for H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, NH₃, O₂ and N₂.

*How to teach:* Model the steps: count valence electrons, place the least electronegative atom (not H) in the center, connect with single bonds, complete octets, and form multiple bonds if electrons run short. The student draws H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, NH₃, O₂ and N₂ and checks electron totals.

- **Q:** How many valence electrons are in CO₂, and what is its Lewis structure?
  **A:** 16 (4 + 6 + 6); O=C=O with two lone pairs on each oxygen.
- **Q:** Why does N₂ have a triple bond?
  **A:** Each N has 5 valence electrons (10 total); a triple bond plus one lone pair on each N gives both atoms an octet.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Model building
*Student:* Build gumdrop models of molecules and identify their shapes.

*How to teach:* Build gumdrop models with toothpicks, using a different color for lone pairs (or leaving a bare toothpick). The student photographs and names each shape.

- **Q:** Name the shape of NH₃ and explain why it is not flat.
  **A:** Trigonal pyramidal; the lone pair on nitrogen pushes the three N–H bonds down.
- **Q:** What shape is CH₄?
  **A:** Tetrahedral, 109.5°.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): PhET Molecule Shapes
*Student:* Use PhET to predict bond angles for molecules with lone pairs.

*How to teach:* In PhET Molecule Shapes, the student adds bonds and lone pairs to a central atom and records the electron geometry, molecular shape and bond angle for 2–6 electron domains. Discuss why lone pairs compress angles.

- **Q:** A central atom has 4 electron domains: 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs. What is the molecular shape?
  **A:** Bent (about 104.5°, like water).
- **Q:** A central atom has 3 bonding domains and no lone pairs. Shape and angle?
  **A:** Trigonal planar, 120° (e.g., BF₃).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Polarity
*Student:* Determine polarity using electronegativity and shape; test with a charged comb and a thin water stream.

*How to teach:* Explain bond polarity using electronegativity differences (O–H is polar because O pulls electrons more). Rub a comb on hair or wool and bring it near a thin water stream; the stream bends. The student classifies the six molecules as polar or nonpolar with reasons.

- **Q:** Is CH₄ polar? Explain.
  **A:** No; C–H bonds are only slightly polar and the tetrahedral shape is symmetric, so dipoles cancel.
- **Q:** Why is water polar?
  **A:** O–H bonds are polar, and the bent shape means the dipoles do not cancel; oxygen is partially negative and the hydrogens partially positive.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on Lewis structures, shapes and polarity.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz, then grade together. Collect model photos.

- **Q:** Draw the Lewis structure of HCl and state whether it is polar.
  **A:** H–Cl with three lone pairs on Cl; polar because Cl is more electronegative than H.

**Assessment:** Model photos and quiz.

**Answer key:** Model photos: full credit for correct shapes of H₂O (bent), CO₂ (linear), CH₄ (tetrahedral), NH₃ (trigonal pyramidal) with labels. Quiz key: 1) Lewis structure of H₂O: O with two single bonds to H and two lone pairs. 2) CO₂: O=C=O with two lone pairs on each O. 3) N₂: triple bond, one lone pair on each N. 4) Shape and angle of CH₄: tetrahedral, 109.5°. 5) NH₃: trigonal pyramidal, ~107°. 6) H₂O: bent, ~104.5°. 7) Is CO₂ polar? No — linear and symmetric so dipoles cancel. 8) Is NH₃ polar? Yes. 9) Bond type in O₂: double. 10) Why a water stream bends toward a charged comb: water molecules are polar and their partial charges are attracted to the charged comb.

## Modern World History: Imperialism in India and Southeast Asia

**Prepare:** Macaulay's 'Minute on Indian Education' (1835), Dadabhai Naoroji's 'drain of wealth' excerpt (Poverty and Un-British Rule in India, 1901), Emilio Aguinaldo's proclamation (1898/1899). Map of Southeast Asia.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the British government ruled India from the start. The East India Company ruled until 1858.
- Overlooking that Siam stayed independent.

### Day 1 (Monday): East India Company
*Student:* Trace the East India Company's rise and the 1857 rebellion.

*How to teach:* Draw a timeline from the East India Company's charter (1600) through the Battle of Plassey (1757) to the 1857 rebellion and the Government of India Act (1858). Explain how a trading company became a ruling power, then have the student write two causes and two results of 1857.

- **Q:** What sparked the 1857 rebellion?
  **A:** Rumors that new rifle cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat, offending Hindus and Muslims.
- **Q:** What changed after 1857?
  **A:** The British government took direct control of India (the Raj).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Raj perspectives
*Student:* Compare Macaulay's Minute and Naoroji's 'drain of wealth' argument.

*How to teach:* Source each document first: Macaulay (British official, 1835, arguing for English-language education) and Naoroji (Indian nationalist economist, 1901). The student builds a two-column chart of each author's claims and evidence, then writes which argument is more convincing and why.

- **Q:** What did Macaulay claim about Indian literature?
  **A:** That 'a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia.'
- **Q:** What was Naoroji's 'drain of wealth' argument?
  **A:** That Britain extracted India's wealth through taxes, salaries and trade, impoverishing India.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Southeast Asia
*Student:* Map French Indochina, Dutch East Indies and Siam's independence.

*How to teach:* On a blank map of Southeast Asia, the student shades French Indochina (Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia), the Dutch East Indies (Indonesia), British Burma and Malaya, the U.S. Philippines, and independent Siam (Thailand). Discuss why Siam stayed free.

- **Q:** Why did Siam remain independent?
  **A:** It served as a buffer between British and French colonies, and its kings modernized.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Philippines
*Student:* Analyze U.S. annexation debates and Aguinaldo's proclamation.

*How to teach:* Explain the Spanish-American War (1898) and the Treaty of Paris, in which the U.S. bought the Philippines for $20 million. Read Aguinaldo's proclamation and a short anti-imperialist statement (e.g., Mark Twain or the Anti-Imperialist League); the student lists arguments for and against annexation.

- **Q:** What happened after the U.S. annexed the Philippines?
  **A:** Filipinos led by Aguinaldo fought the Philippine-American War (1899–1902).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on India and Southeast Asia.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key without notes (about 20 minutes). Grade it together; for each missed item the student finds the answer in their notes and writes a one-sentence correction.

- **Q:** What colonial power ruled Indonesia?
  **A:** The Netherlands (Dutch East Indies).

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz (answers): 1) East India Company → British trading company that gained control of India after Plassey (1757). 2) Cause of the 1857 rebellion → rumors of cartridges greased with cow and pig fat, plus resentment of British rule. 3) Result → British Crown took direct control (the Raj, 1858). 4) Macaulay's goal → create a class of Indians 'English in taste, in opinions, in morals and in intellect.' 5) Naoroji's argument → Britain drained India's wealth. 6) Siam → stayed independent as a buffer. 7) Aguinaldo → declared Philippine independence (1898), fought the U.S. (1899–1902).

## Music & Media Arts: Lyrics and songwriting

**Prepare:** Lyric notebook; lyrics of a classic song (e.g., 'Yesterday' by the Beatles or 'Respect' by Aretha Franklin) printed for analysis; rhyme scheme examples.

**Watch for:**
- Students force rhymes that change meaning. Use near rhymes (slant rhymes) when perfect rhymes distort the message.
- Students write verses that repeat the chorus idea. Verses add details and story; the chorus states the main idea.
- Students ignore natural word stress. Prosody means stressed syllables land on strong beats.

### Day 1 (Monday): Lyric analysis
*Student:* Analyze lyrics of a classic song for hook and rhyme scheme.

*How to teach:* Analyze a classic song's hook and rhyme scheme.

- **Q:** Label the rhyme scheme of four lines ending 'day/night/away/light'.
  **A:** ABAB.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Brainstorm
*Student:* Brainstorm themes and write a chorus with a hook.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm themes and write a chorus with a hook.

- **Q:** What is a hook?
  **A:** A short, catchy musical or lyrical phrase that sticks in the listener's mind, usually in the chorus.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Verses
*Student:* Write two verses with imagery.

*How to teach:* Write two verses with imagery.

- **Q:** Turn 'I was sad' into an image.
  **A:** Example: 'Rain on the window, cold cup in my hand.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Prosody
*Student:* Match lyric stresses to musical rhythm.

*How to teach:* Match lyric stresses to musical rhythm (prosody).

- **Q:** Where should the stress fall in 'tomorrow'?
  **A:** On the second syllable: to-MOR-row — place it on a strong beat.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Share a lyric draft.

*How to teach:* Share a lyric draft.

- **Q:** What's a slant rhyme?
  **A:** A near rhyme with similar but not identical sounds (e.g., 'home'/'stone').

**Assessment:** Lyric draft.

**Answer key:** Lyric draft rubric: chorus with a clear, repeatable hook; two verses with concrete imagery that develop the theme; consistent rhyme scheme (e.g., ABAB or AABB) with natural phrasing; prosody check marks stressed syllables. Proficient = all present with mostly natural stresses.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Freeway driving

**Prepare:** Read the handbook sections on freeway driving and carpool lanes. Print a local freeway map with an interchange. Prepare freeway diagrams for the quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Students slow down or stop at the end of an on-ramp. Use the acceleration lane to reach freeway speed and merge into a gap; stopping is dangerous unless traffic forces it.
- Students cross double solid lines into or out of HOV lanes. Enter and exit carpool lanes only where broken lines allow.
- Students slow down on the freeway before reaching the exit lane. Signal 5 seconds before, move into the deceleration lane, and then slow down.

### Day 1 (Monday): Entering
*Student:* Learn to use the on-ramp acceleration lane to match speed and merge into a gap.

*How to teach:* Using a diagram, the student explains the on-ramp, acceleration lane and merge.

- **Q:** Why use the full acceleration lane?
  **A:** To match the speed of freeway traffic so you can merge smoothly into a gap.
- **Q:** What should you look for before merging?
  **A:** A gap in traffic, by checking mirrors and over the shoulder.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Freeway travel
*Student:* Learn lane choice, following distance and scanning on freeways.

*How to teach:* Teach lane choice, scanning 15–20 seconds ahead and maintaining 3+ seconds of following distance.

- **Q:** Which lane should slower drivers use on the freeway?
  **A:** The right lanes.
- **Q:** How far ahead should you scan on a freeway?
  **A:** About 15–20 seconds ahead (well over a quarter mile at freeway speeds).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Exiting
*Student:* Learn to plan exits early and use the deceleration lane.

*How to teach:* The student explains how to plan an exit and use the deceleration lane.

- **Q:** You miss your exit. What should you do?
  **A:** Continue to the next exit and come back; never stop, back up or cut across lanes.
- **Q:** When should you start slowing for an exit?
  **A:** After you've moved into the deceleration (exit) lane.

### Day 4 (Thursday): HOV lanes
*Student:* Learn carpool lane rules and markings.

*How to teach:* The student learns carpool lane rules and markings.

- **Q:** What symbol marks an HOV lane?
  **A:** A white diamond painted in the lane and on signs.
- **Q:** Can a solo driver use the HOV lane?
  **A:** Only if the vehicle has a valid exemption (if such programs are in effect) or outside posted HOV hours; otherwise no.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Freeway quiz with diagrams.

*How to teach:* Give the freeway quiz with diagrams. Review.

- **Q:** How many seconds should you signal before changing lanes on a freeway?
  **A:** At least 5 seconds.

**Assessment:** Freeway quiz.

**Answer key:** Freeway quiz key: 1) Entering — check traffic, signal, use the acceleration lane to match speed, merge into a gap, don't stop unless necessary. 2) Signal at least 5 seconds before freeway lane changes. 3) Following distance — at least 3 seconds. 4) Missed your exit — continue to the next exit; never back up or stop. 5) Exiting — signal ~5 seconds, move into the deceleration lane, then slow; observe the ramp speed. 6) HOV lanes — require the posted minimum number of occupants (usually 2+, sometimes 3+) during posted hours; marked with a white diamond. 7) Do not cross double solid lines (two sets of double yellow/white lines two feet apart or more) into an HOV lane. 8) Look ahead 15+ seconds on freeways. 9) Fast lane is the far-left lane; slower traffic keeps right.

## Computer Science Principles: Event-driven programming in App Lab

**Prepare:** Open Code.org App Lab and practice Design Mode yourself: drag a button and label, set an ID, then switch to Code Mode and add onEvent. Prepare five broken onEvent programs for Day 4 (examples listed in Day 4).

**Watch for:**
- Mismatched IDs: onEvent("btnHome", …) when the button's ID is "homeButton". IDs must match exactly, including capitals.
- Thinking code runs top to bottom once and ends. In event-driven programs, handlers wait and run whenever their event occurs.
- Using the wrong event type (e.g. "change" on a button). Buttons use "click"; dropdowns and text inputs often use "change" or "input".

### Day 1 (Monday): App Lab tour
*Student:* Open Code.org App Lab and use Design Mode to add buttons, labels and images with meaningful IDs.

*How to teach:* Tour App Lab: Design Mode (drag elements, Properties panel), Code Mode (blocks/text), the Run button and the debug console. Have the student add a button, a label and an image, setting IDs like startButton and titleLabel.

- **Q:** Why should element IDs be meaningful (e.g. 'startButton' not 'button1')?
  **A:** Code refers to elements by ID, so clear names make the code readable and reduce mistakes.
- **Q:** What's the difference between Design Mode and Code Mode?
  **A:** Design Mode is where you place and style UI elements; Code Mode is where you write the program that controls them.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Events
*Student:* Write onEvent handlers that change screens when buttons are clicked.

*How to teach:* Model: onEvent("startButton", "click", function() { setScreen("infoScreen"); }); Have the student write handlers that move between two screens and back.

- **Q:** Write an onEvent that shows the screen 'quizScreen' when 'quizButton' is clicked.
  **A:** onEvent("quizButton", "click", function() { setScreen("quizScreen"); });
- **Q:** What is an event handler?
  **A:** A function that runs in response to a specific event, such as a button click.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Multi-screen app
*Student:* Build a 3-screen app (home, info, quiz) using your paper prototype.

*How to teach:* Using the paper prototype, the student builds a 3-screen app (home, info, quiz), adding navigation between all screens. Check that every screen has a way back home.

- **Q:** Your app has 3 screens and each non-home screen has a Back button. How many onEvent handlers do you need at minimum for navigation if home has two buttons?
  **A:** Four: two on home (to info and quiz) and one Back on each of the other two screens.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Debugging events
*Student:* Fix five intentionally broken onEvent programs and record each bug type.

*How to teach:* Give five broken programs, e.g. 1) ID typo, 2) missing quotes around the ID, 3) wrong event type, 4) setScreen to a screen name that doesn't exist, 5) code placed outside the function so it runs immediately. The student fixes each and records the bug type.

- **Q:** A button does nothing when clicked and there's no error. What should you check first?
  **A:** That the ID in onEvent exactly matches the element's ID (spelling and capitals) and that the event type is "click".
- **Q:** Why does setText run when the app starts instead of on click if it's written below onEvent rather than inside the function?
  **A:** Only code inside the handler function waits for the event; code outside runs immediately when the program starts.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Submit a working multi-screen app and explain how events control program flow.

*How to teach:* The student demos the app using the checklist in the assessment key and explains in their own words how events control program flow.

- **Q:** How is an event-driven program different from a program that runs top to bottom?
  **A:** It sets up handlers and then waits; code runs only when events happen, in whatever order the user triggers them.

**Assessment:** Multi-screen app checklist.

**Answer key:** Multi-screen app checklist: [ ] 3 screens (home, info, quiz) built in Design Mode; [ ] every element has a meaningful ID (e.g. homeButton, not button1); [ ] each navigation button has a working onEvent with "click" and setScreen; [ ] user can reach every screen and return home; [ ] quiz screen responds to an answer button; [ ] code is commented. Explanation (proficient): events such as clicks trigger the matching handler; the program waits between events, so the user's actions determine what code runs and in what order.
