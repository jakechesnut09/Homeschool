## English 10: Fahrenheit 451 Parts 2–3

**Prepare:** Fahrenheit 451 Parts 2–3, Matthew Arnold's 'Dover Beach' (Poetry Foundation), one current article on technology, attention or censorship for the seminar.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Faber's 'three things' are just books. He says books are only one container for what society lacks.
- Reading the ending as hopeless. The phoenix and the book people suggest renewal.

### Day 1 (Monday): Part 2 & Faber
*Student:* Read Part 2 and analyze Faber's three things missing from society.

*How to teach:* Read Part 2, 'The Sieve and the Sand.' The student lists Faber's three missing things and explains each in their own words. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What three things does Faber say society is missing?
  **A:** Quality (texture) of information; leisure to digest it; and the right to act on what we learn from the interaction of the first two.
- **Q:** What does the title 'The Sieve and the Sand' refer to?
  **A:** Montag's childhood memory of trying to fill a sieve with sand — like trying to absorb the Bible quickly on the train; knowledge slips away.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dover Beach
*Student:* Read Arnold's 'Dover Beach' and explain why it makes Mrs. Phelps cry.

*How to teach:* Read 'Dover Beach' aloud. Discuss its 'Sea of Faith' and its ending, then reread the scene where Montag reads it to Mildred's friends. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Why does 'Dover Beach' make Mrs. Phelps cry?
  **A:** The poem's sadness about a world without faith, joy or peace ('ignorant armies clash by night') breaks through her numbness and makes her feel the emptiness of her own life.
- **Q:** What does the speaker of 'Dover Beach' ask of his love?
  **A:** 'Ah, love, let us be true / To one another!' — to hold onto each other in a confused, faithless world.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Part 3
*Student:* Read Part 3 and analyze the phoenix allusion and book people.

*How to teach:* Read Part 3, 'Burning Bright.' The student traces Montag's escape and analyzes Granger's phoenix story and the book people. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does Granger say about the phoenix?
  **A:** Like the phoenix, humanity burns itself up and rises again — but humans can remember their mistakes and so might stop repeating them.
- **Q:** Who are the book people?
  **A:** Exiles who each memorize a book to preserve it until society is ready to rebuild; Montag carries parts of Ecclesiastes and Revelation.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Seminar
*Student:* Seminar: Does Fahrenheit 451 describe our world? Use novel evidence plus one current article.

*How to teach:* Hold the seminar: 'Does Fahrenheit 451 describe our world?' Each participant uses novel evidence and the current article. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What novel detail most resembles modern technology?
  **A:** The seashell radios (earbuds) or the parlor walls (large screens, interactive media).

### Day 5 (Friday): Theme essay outline
*Student:* Outline a literary analysis on a theme in the novel.

*How to teach:* Model a thesis that states a theme, not a topic. The student outlines the analysis with three body points and quotes assigned to each, plus one counterpoint. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Turn 'censorship' into a theme statement.
  **A:** For example: 'When people give up thinking for comfort, censorship becomes unnecessary because they censor themselves.'

**Assessment:** Seminar rubric and outline.

**Answer key:** Seminar rubric (4-point): clear claim about whether the novel describes our world; at least three novel citations (e.g., the parlor walls, seashell radios, Beatty's speech, Faber's three things) and one from the current article; respectful responses; follow-up questions. Theme essay outline: 4 = arguable theme thesis (e.g., 'Bradbury suggests that a society that trades thought for entertainment destroys its own humanity'), three body points with assigned quotes, a counterpoint; 3 = complete with weaker evidence; 2 = topic but no thesis; 1 = incomplete.

## Geometry: Volume

**Prepare:** A stack of coins, a paper cone and cylinder with the same base and height (make from cardstock), rice, calculator.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting the 1/3 for pyramids and cones. Show the rice demo: three cones fill one cylinder.
- Using slant height instead of height in cone/pyramid volume.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cavalieri
*Student:* Stack coins straight and slanted to illustrate Cavalieri's principle.

*How to teach:* Stack coins straight, then push the stack slanted. The student explains why the volume stays the same: every cross section has the same area at each height (Cavalieri's principle).

- **Q:** Why does a slanted stack of coins have the same volume as a straight stack?
  **A:** Each layer has the same area at each height, so by Cavalieri's principle the volumes are equal.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Prisms & cylinders
*Student:* Find volumes of prisms and cylinders including oblique ones.

*How to teach:* Volume = base area × height for prisms and cylinders (right or oblique, using perpendicular height).

- **Q:** Find the volume of a cylinder with r = 4 and h = 10.
  **A:** 160π ≈ 502.7.
- **Q:** A triangular prism has a base triangle with legs 3 and 4 and length 10. Volume?
  **A:** 6 × 10 = 60.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pyramids & cones
*Student:* Fill a paper cone with rice into a same-base cylinder to show the 1/3 relationship.

*How to teach:* Fill the paper cone with rice and pour into the cylinder; it takes three cones. Generalize V = ⅓Bh.

- **Q:** Find the volume of a cone with radius 3 and height 8.
  **A:** ⅓·9π·8 = 24π ≈ 75.4.
- **Q:** Find the volume of a square pyramid with base edge 6 and height 10.
  **A:** ⅓·36·10 = 120.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Spheres
*Student:* Apply the sphere volume formula and solve composite volume problems.

*How to teach:* Introduce V = (4/3)πr³ and composite solids (cone + hemisphere, cylinder + hemispheres).

- **Q:** Find the volume of a sphere with radius 3.
  **A:** (4/3)π·27 = 36π ≈ 113.1.
- **Q:** A basketball has diameter 9.4 in. Find its volume.
  **A:** r = 4.7; (4/3)π(4.7)³ ≈ 434.9 in³.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on volume.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** If a sphere's radius doubles, what happens to its volume?
  **A:** It multiplies by 2³ = 8.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Cylinder r = 4, h = 10 → 160π ≈ 502.7. 2) Cone r = 4, h = 10 → 160π/3 ≈ 167.6. 3) Square pyramid base 6, height 10 → 120. 4) Sphere r = 3 → 36π ≈ 113.1. 5) Rectangular prism 5 × 4 × 3 → 60. 6) Oblique cylinder r = 2, height 7 → 28π ≈ 88.0 (same as right, by Cavalieri). 7) Ice cream cone r = 3, h = 10 topped with a hemisphere r = 3 → 30π + 18π = 48π ≈ 150.8.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Dynamic equilibrium

**Prepare:** Gather two identical clear cups, water, two straws of different diameters, a measuring cup or ruler, and graph paper. Open a sealed bottle of soda for Day 4 discussion.

**Watch for:**
- Students think at equilibrium the amounts of reactants and products are equal. At equilibrium the forward and reverse rates are equal; concentrations are constant but usually not equal.
- Students think reactions stop at equilibrium. Both reactions continue (dynamic); there is no net change.
- Students include solids and pure liquids in K expressions. Only gases and dissolved species appear.

### Day 1 (Monday): Reversible reactions
*Student:* Identify reversible reactions and define dynamic equilibrium.

*How to teach:* Introduce reversible reactions with examples (N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂, water evaporating and condensing in a closed bottle). The student defines dynamic equilibrium and sketches a concentration vs. time graph showing curves leveling off.

- **Q:** In a sealed water bottle, why does the water level stop dropping?
  **A:** The evaporation rate becomes equal to the condensation rate.
- **Q:** What does the double arrow ⇌ mean?
  **A:** The reaction is reversible; it proceeds in both directions.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Water transfer model
*Student:* Transfer water between two cups with different straws until levels stop changing; graph.

*How to teach:* Fill cup A with 200 mL water and leave cup B empty. Each 'turn,' the student uses the wide straw to move water A → B and the partner (or student, second hand) uses the narrow straw B → A simultaneously by pinching the straw top. Record levels every turn for 20 turns and graph.

- **Q:** Why did the levels stop changing even though water kept moving?
  **A:** The amount moved A → B each turn equaled the amount moved B → A — equal forward and reverse rates.
- **Q:** Were the final volumes equal? Why or why not?
  **A:** No; the different straw sizes mean equal transfer happens only when the cup with the narrow straw is much fuller.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Equilibrium expressions
*Student:* Write equilibrium constant expressions and interpret K values.

*How to teach:* Teach K = [products]^coefficients ÷ [reactants]^coefficients. The student writes K for five reactions and interprets large and small values.

- **Q:** Write K for 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃.
  **A:** K = [SO₃]² ÷ ([SO₂]²[O₂])
- **Q:** If K = 5 × 10⁻¹⁰, which side is favored?
  **A:** Reactants — very little product at equilibrium.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Real examples
*Student:* Explain equilibrium in a sealed soda bottle.

*How to teach:* Discuss a sealed soda: CO₂(g) above the liquid ⇌ CO₂(aq), and CO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃. Open the bottle and observe. The student explains what changes when the cap comes off.

- **Q:** Why doesn't a sealed soda keep bubbling?
  **A:** The CO₂ in the gas space and dissolved CO₂ are at equilibrium; escape and dissolving rates are equal.
- **Q:** Why does it fizz when opened?
  **A:** Pressure of CO₂ above the liquid drops, so CO₂ comes out of solution faster than it dissolves until a new equilibrium (mostly flat soda) is reached.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on equilibrium.

*How to teach:* Give the equilibrium quiz and grade together.

- **Q:** Write K for CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g).
  **A:** K = [CO₂] (solids are left out).

**Assessment:** Model graph and quiz.

**Answer key:** Model graph: full credit for water level vs. transfer number for both cups, curves that level off at unequal heights, and the explanation that equilibrium was reached when the amount moved each way per turn became equal. Quiz key: 1) Define dynamic equilibrium — forward and reverse rates equal; concentrations constant. 2) K for N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃: K = [NH₃]² ÷ ([N₂][H₂]³). 3) K for H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI: [HI]² ÷ ([H₂][I₂]). 4) K = 1 × 10⁸ means products are strongly favored. 5) K = 1 × 10⁻⁵ means reactants favored. 6) In a sealed soda, CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq) at equilibrium. 7) Why the water levels weren't equal — different straw sizes make different transfer rates; equilibrium is about equal rates. 8) Is equilibrium static? No — dynamic.

## Music & Media Arts: Film scoring

**Prepare:** Clips: Star Wars (1977) main title/Imperial March scenes and Jaws (1975) shark theme; a 2-minute film clip for sound logging; a 30-second silent clip; BandLab.

**Watch for:**
- Students think all film music is background. Some music is diegetic (characters can hear it, e.g., a radio); score is non-diegetic.
- Students score every second. Silence can be powerful.
- Students ignore sync. Musical hits should land on visual cuts or actions.

### Day 1 (Monday): Leitmotif
*Student:* Listen to themes from Star Wars and Jaws and describe how each leitmotif represents a character or threat.

*How to teach:* Listen to Star Wars and Jaws themes and describe how each leitmotif represents a character or threat.

- **Q:** How does the Jaws theme create tension?
  **A:** A low, repeated two-note half-step pattern (E–F) that speeds up as the shark approaches.
- **Q:** What is a leitmotif?
  **A:** A recurring musical theme associated with a character, place or idea (a technique Wagner made famous).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Diegetic vs. non-diegetic
*Student:* Log every sound in a 2-minute film clip as diegetic or non-diegetic and explain its purpose.

*How to teach:* Log sounds in a 2-minute clip as diegetic or non-diegetic.

- **Q:** A character turns on a car radio and a song plays. Diegetic or non-diegetic?
  **A:** Diegetic — it exists in the story world.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Two moods
*Student:* Score the same 30-second silent clip twice in BandLab—once suspenseful, once comic—changing tempo, key and instrumentation.

*How to teach:* Score a 30-second silent clip twice: suspenseful and comic.

- **Q:** Name musical choices that create suspense.
  **A:** Low pitches, minor or dissonant harmony, slow pulse building faster, tremolo strings, silence.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Score your scene
*Student:* Compose a 30–60 second underscore for your own scene with a recurring motif.

*How to teach:* Compose a 30–60 second underscore for your scene with a recurring motif.

- **Q:** What does 'underscore' mean?
  **A:** Background music beneath dialogue or action that supports mood without dominating.

### Day 5 (Friday): Sync & present
*Student:* Sync the score to picture so key hits land on cuts, then present both versions and explain choices.

*How to teach:* Sync the score to picture and present both versions.

- **Q:** What is a 'hit point'?
  **A:** A moment in the picture where the music lines up with an action or cut.

**Assessment:** Scored scene.

**Answer key:** Scored scene rubric: two contrasting versions (suspense vs. comic) with clear changes in tempo, key/mode and instrumentation; own scene's 30–60 second underscore with a recurring motif; sync points land on cuts; mix balanced under dialogue; presentation explains choices using leitmotif, diegetic/non-diegetic vocabulary.

## Computer Science Principles: Cybersecurity threats and protection

**Prepare:** Collect 10 sample emails/texts (mix of legitimate and phishing; screenshots from phishing-awareness quizzes work well). Decide which account the student will turn MFA on for (with parent permission).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking phishing is always obvious with bad spelling. Modern phishing can look professional; check sender address, link destination and urgency.
- Believing antivirus makes you fully safe. Each measure has limits; layered defenses (updates, backups, MFA, caution) are needed.
- Thinking a long password made of a common phrase is unbreakable. Length helps most when combined with unpredictability; never reuse passwords.

### Day 1 (Monday): Threat research
*Student:* Research phishing, malware, ransomware and DDoS attacks and make a chart of how each works and a real example.

*How to teach:* Research phishing, malware, ransomware and DDoS and make a chart: how it works, real example (e.g. WannaCry ransomware 2017; Mirai botnet DDoS on Dyn DNS 2016).

- **Q:** What does ransomware do?
  **A:** Encrypts the victim's files and demands payment for the key to decrypt them.
- **Q:** What is a DDoS attack?
  **A:** A distributed denial-of-service attack floods a server with traffic from many compromised devices so legitimate users can't reach it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Spot the phish
*Student:* Analyze 10 sample emails and texts, label each as legitimate or phishing, and list the clues.

*How to teach:* The student labels 10 messages as legitimate or phishing and lists clues (mismatched sender domain, urgent threats, links that point elsewhere when hovered, requests for passwords or payment).

- **Q:** Name three clues a message is phishing.
  **A:** Sender address doesn't match the real company domain, urgent or threatening language, links whose real URL (on hover) goes somewhere else, and requests for passwords or payment info.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Passwords and MFA
*Student:* Calculate the number of possible passwords for 8 vs. 16 characters and turn on multifactor authentication for one of your accounts.

*How to teach:* Calculate password possibilities using 94 printable characters: 94^8 ≈ 6.1 × 10^15; 94^16 ≈ 3.7 × 10^31. Then turn on MFA for one account.

- **Q:** How many 8-character passwords are possible with 94 characters? How many times more for 16?
  **A:** 94^8 ≈ 6.1 × 10^15; 94^16 ≈ 3.7 × 10^31, which is 94^8 ≈ 6.1 × 10^15 times more.
- **Q:** What is multifactor authentication?
  **A:** Logging in requires two or more kinds of evidence — something you know (password), have (phone/code/key), or are (fingerprint).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Security measures
*Student:* Match firewalls, antivirus, encryption, software updates and backups to the threats they address and note their limits.

*How to teach:* Match firewalls, antivirus, encryption, updates and backups to threats and note one limit of each.

- **Q:** Which measure best protects against losing files to ransomware?
  **A:** Regular backups stored separately (offline or versioned cloud) — you can restore files without paying.
- **Q:** What is one limit of a firewall?
