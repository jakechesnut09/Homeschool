# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 8

## English 10: Argument essay: Antigone and civil disobedience

**Prepare:** Excerpt of Thoreau's 'Civil Disobedience' (Gutenberg), Antigone text. Prepare the Unit 2 test (assessment key).

**Watch for:**
- Ignoring the counterclaim. A strong argument acknowledges the best opposing reason and rebuts it.
- Non-parallel lists ('to read, writing, and to think'). Match grammatical forms.

### Day 1 (Monday): Read Thoreau excerpt
*Student:* Read an excerpt of Thoreau's 'Civil Disobedience' and note parallels with Antigone's position.

*How to teach:* Read the excerpt together. The student lists parallels to Antigone. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does Thoreau say about unjust laws?
  **A:** He asks whether we should obey them, and argues people should not lend themselves to injustice; 'Under a government which imprisons any unjustly, the true place for a just man is also a prison.'
- **Q:** How is Antigone like Thoreau?
  **A:** Both break a law they believe is unjust, act openly, and accept the penalty.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Outline
*Student:* Outline an argument essay: Is Antigone right to break Creon's law? Include claim, two reasons, a counterclaim and rebuttal.

*How to teach:* Model an outline. The student outlines claim, two reasons, counterclaim and rebuttal. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What is a counterclaim for 'Antigone is right'?
  **A:** Creon must enforce laws to keep order in a city just torn by civil war; one person defying the law threatens stability.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft
*Student:* Draft the 700–900 word essay, integrating quotes from both the play and Thoreau.

*How to teach:* Review the outline together, then the student drafts the 700–900 word essay in one sitting if possible. Require at least two quotes from Antigone and one from Thoreau, each with a lead-in and analysis; you check only that the claim and counterclaim are both present.

- **Q:** How should a quote from a translated play be cited in MLA?
  **A:** With line numbers if the translation provides them (e.g., (Sophocles, lines 450–455)), or scene/page as given.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: parallel structure
*Student:* Mini-lesson on parallel structure; revise three sentences in your draft to use parallelism for emphasis.

*How to teach:* Teach parallel structure. The student revises three sentences in the draft. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Revise: 'Antigone is brave, loyal, and she has stubbornness.'
  **A:** 'Antigone is brave, loyal, and stubborn.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Final draft & unit test
*Student:* Submit the final essay and take the Unit 2 test on tragic conventions and vocabulary.

*How to teach:* The student submits the final essay; score it with the rubric in the assessment key. Then give the Unit 2 test on tragic conventions and vocabulary (about 40 minutes) and review answers together.

- **Q:** Define hubris.
  **A:** Excessive pride or self-confidence that leads to a downfall.

**Assessment:** Argument essay rubric and Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Argument essay rubric (W.9-10.1): 4 = precise claim; two reasons each supported by quotes from Antigone and Thoreau; fair counterclaim (e.g., Creon must keep order after civil war) with a rebuttal; logical transitions; formal style; MLA citations; 3 = all parts with thinner analysis; 2 = missing counterclaim or evidence; 1 = no clear claim. Unit 2 test (sample answers): tragic hero, hamartia, hubris, catharsis, chorus, ode, stichomythia, reversal; vocabulary from the play; short answer: 'How does Sophocles use the Chorus?' → to comment on action, voice community values and advise Creon (Choragos).

## Geometry: Logic and reasoning

**Prepare:** Notebook. Prepare examples of conditional statements from everyday life (ads, rules). Know these logic terms: converse (swap), inverse (negate both), contrapositive (swap and negate).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the converse of a true statement is always true. Use 'If it's a dog, then it's a mammal' — its converse is false.
- Confusing the Law of Detachment with affirming the conclusion. Knowing q is true does not let you conclude p.

### Day 1 (Monday): Inductive vs. deductive
*Student:* Make conjectures from patterns and find counterexamples.

*How to teach:* Show a pattern (sums of consecutive odd numbers: 1, 4, 9, 16) and have the student make a conjecture (inductive), then show how a counterexample disproves a false conjecture. Contrast with deductive reasoning from definitions.

- **Q:** Conjecture: 'The square of any number is greater than the number.' Give a counterexample.
  **A:** 1/2 (since (1/2)² = 1/4 < 1/2), or 1, or 0.
- **Q:** Is a conjecture from many examples proven?
  **A:** No — inductive reasoning suggests a conjecture but only a deductive proof establishes it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Conditionals
*Student:* Rewrite statements in if-then form and write converse, inverse and contrapositive with truth values.

*How to teach:* Model rewriting statements in if-then form and then generating converse, inverse and contrapositive; the student marks each true or false with a counterexample when false. Note the contrapositive always has the same truth value as the original.

- **Q:** Write the contrapositive of 'If two angles are vertical, then they are congruent.' Is it true?
  **A:** If two angles are not congruent, then they are not vertical angles. True.
- **Q:** Write the converse of 'If x = 3, then x² = 9.' Is it true?
  **A:** If x² = 9, then x = 3. False, since x could be −3.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Laws of logic
*Student:* Apply the Law of Detachment and Syllogism to draw valid conclusions.

*How to teach:* Introduce the Law of Detachment (if p→q and p, then q) and the Law of Syllogism (p→q and q→r gives p→r) with silly and real examples, and have the student decide whether conclusions are valid.

- **Q:** 'If you finish chores, you get screen time. You got screen time.' Can you conclude you finished chores?
  **A:** No — that is affirming the conclusion; screen time could have another cause.
- **Q:** 'If a shape is a square, it has four right angles. If a shape has four right angles, it is a rectangle.' What follows?
  **A:** If a shape is a square, it is a rectangle (Law of Syllogism).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Algebraic proof
*Student:* Write two-column proofs solving equations with properties of equality.

*How to teach:* Model a two-column proof that solves 2x + 5 = 17 with a reason for every step (Given, Subtraction Prop. of Equality, Division Prop.). The student writes proofs for 4–5 equations, including one with the Distributive Property.

- **Q:** Give the reasons: 5x − 4 = 21 → 5x = 25 → x = 5.
  **A:** Given; Addition Property of Equality; Division Property of Equality.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on logic and algebraic proof.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Which statement is logically equivalent to a conditional: its converse, inverse, or contrapositive?
  **A:** Its contrapositive.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Find a counterexample to 'All prime numbers are odd' → 2. 2) Write 'Right angles are congruent' in if-then form → If two angles are right angles, then they are congruent. 3) For 'If a figure is a square, then it is a rectangle': converse (If a rectangle, then a square) false; inverse (If not a square, then not a rectangle) false; contrapositive (If not a rectangle, then not a square) true. 4) 'If it rains, the game is canceled. It rains.' → The game is canceled (Detachment). 5) 'If A then B; if B then C' → If A then C (Syllogism). 6) Two-column proof: 3(x − 2) = 15 → x − 2 = 5 or 3x − 6 = 15 (Distributive), 3x = 21 (Addition Prop.), x = 7 (Division Prop.). Score 1 point per item; item 3 and 6 worth 3.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Nuclear chemistry

**Prepare:** Gather 100 pennies (or M&Ms), a shoebox with a lid, graph paper. Review the nuclear symbols (mass number on top, atomic number on bottom). Read a short overview of Diablo Canyon Power Plant (PG&E) and fusion (ITER or the 2022 National Ignition Facility result at Lawrence Livermore, California).

**Watch for:**
- Students think half-life means the sample is gone after two half-lives. After each half-life, half of what remains decays: 100% → 50% → 25% → 12.5%.
- Students think radioactive decay is a chemical reaction. Decay changes the nucleus (it can change the element); chemical reactions involve only electrons.
- Students mix up fission (splitting heavy nuclei) and fusion (joining light nuclei). Use 'fusion = fuse together'.

### Day 1 (Monday): Types of decay
*Student:* Write nuclear equations for alpha, beta and gamma decay.

*How to teach:* Teach nuclear notation and the three decay types: alpha (emits ⁴₂He, mass −4, atomic number −2), beta (a neutron becomes a proton, emits an electron, atomic number +1), gamma (high-energy photon, no change). Model two equations and have the student balance five more by checking that top and bottom numbers add up.

- **Q:** Complete: ²³⁸₉₂U → ? + ⁴₂He
  **A:** ²³⁴₉₀Th (thorium-234).
- **Q:** Complete: ¹³¹₅₃I → ? + ⁰₋₁e
  **A:** ¹³¹₅₄Xe (xenon-131).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Half-life lab
*Student:* Shake 100 pennies in a box, remove heads each round, graph results and find half-life.

*How to teach:* Put 100 pennies in the box, shake, remove all heads (they 'decayed'), and record the count. Repeat until none remain. The student graphs shakes vs. pennies remaining and finds the half-life from the graph.

- **Q:** Starting with 100 pennies, about how many remain after 3 shakes?
  **A:** About 12–13 (100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5).
- **Q:** Can you predict exactly which penny will 'decay' next?
  **A:** No; decay is random for individual nuclei, but predictable for large numbers.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Radiometric dating
*Student:* Use half-life to calculate ages of rocks (U-238) and artifacts (C-14).

*How to teach:* Model: number of half-lives = time ÷ half-life, and fraction left = (1/2)ⁿ. Work a C-14 and a U-238 example together, then the student solves six problems.

- **Q:** A wooden tool has 12.5% of its original C-14. How old is it?
  **A:** 12.5% = (1/2)³, so 3 half-lives × 5,730 yr = 17,190 years.
- **Q:** Why isn't C-14 used to date dinosaur bones?
  **A:** Dinosaurs died over 66 million years ago; after ~50,000 years almost no C-14 remains. Longer-lived isotopes in surrounding volcanic rock (e.g., U-238, K-40) are used instead.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fission vs. fusion
*Student:* Compare fission (Diablo Canyon power plant) and fusion and their energy sources (E = mc²).

*How to teach:* Compare fission and fusion in a T-chart: fuel, products, where it happens (Diablo Canyon reactor splits U-235; the Sun fuses hydrogen), waste, and energy source. Work an E = mc² example.

- **Q:** How much energy is released if 1.0 g (0.0010 kg) of mass is converted to energy?
  **A:** E = 0.0010 × (3.0 × 10⁸)² = 9.0 × 10¹³ J.
- **Q:** Give one advantage and one challenge of fission power.
  **A:** Advantage: no CO₂ emissions during operation. Challenge: long-lived radioactive waste and safety concerns.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on nuclear equations and half-life.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz with a periodic table. Grade together and collect the half-life graph.

- **Q:** Polonium-210 has a half-life of 138 days. What fraction remains after 276 days?
  **A:** One quarter (two half-lives).

**Assessment:** Half-life lab graph and quiz.

**Answer key:** Lab: full credit for a data table of remaining pennies each shake, a graph with a smooth decay curve, and a half-life of about 1 shake (half remain each round). Quiz key: 1) Alpha decay of radium-226: ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He. 2) Beta decay of thorium-234: ²³⁴₉₀Th → ²³⁴₉₁Pa + ⁰₋₁e. 3) Beta decay of carbon-14: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e. 4) Gamma radiation changes neither mass number nor atomic number. 5) 80 g of iodine-131 (half-life 8 days) after 24 days: 10 g. 6) A bone has 25% of its original C-14 (half-life 5,730 yr): age 11,460 years. 7) A rock has half its original U-238 (half-life 4.5 billion yr): about 4.5 billion years old. 8) Most penetrating radiation: gamma; least: alpha. 9) Fission splits a heavy nucleus such as U-235; fusion joins light nuclei such as hydrogen into helium. 10) Source of energy in both: a small amount of mass converted to energy (E = mc²).

## Modern World History: Capitalism and its critics; global industrialization

**Prepare:** Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations (1776) excerpts: pin factory (Book I ch. 1), 'butcher, brewer, baker' and 'invisible hand' (Book IV ch. 2); The Communist Manifesto (1848) Section I excerpt; reading on Robert Owen's New Lanark. Prepare the Unit 3 test.

**Watch for:**
- Equating Marx's communism with any government program. Marx called for workers to overthrow capitalism and abolish private ownership of the means of production.
- Thinking Smith opposed all government. He supported defense, justice and some public works.

### Day 1 (Monday): Adam Smith
*Student:* Read the 'invisible hand' and pin factory excerpts and summarize Smith's arguments.

*How to teach:* Read the pin factory and 'butcher, brewer, baker' passages. The student summarizes Smith's arguments for the division of labor and free markets in a paragraph. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What did Smith's pin factory example show?
  **A:** Division of labor: 10 workers each specializing in a step made about 48,000 pins a day, far more than they could make alone.
- **Q:** Explain 'It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner.'
  **A:** Sellers serve us out of their own self-interest, and that self-interest, through trade, benefits everyone.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Marx & Engels
*Student:* Read a Communist Manifesto excerpt and summarize its critique of capitalism.

*How to teach:* Source the Manifesto (1848, Marx and Engels, for the Communist League amid revolutions). The student summarizes its critique and predicted outcome. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What do Marx and Engels predict will happen to capitalism?
  **A:** Workers (proletariat) will overthrow the bourgeoisie in revolution, abolish private property in the means of production, and create a classless society.
- **Q:** What is the famous closing call?
  **A:** 'Workers of the world, unite!' — they have nothing to lose but their chains.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Utopians & reformers
*Student:* Research Robert Owen's New Lanark and compare responses in a chart.

*How to teach:* Read about Robert Owen's New Lanark. The student builds a chart comparing responses to industrialization: laissez-faire (Smith), utopian socialism (Owen), communism (Marx), and reform/unions. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** How did Robert Owen run New Lanark differently?
  **A:** He shortened hours, refused to employ very young children, built schools and decent housing, and showed a mill could profit while treating workers better.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Industrialization spreads
*Student:* Compare industrialization in Germany, Japan (Meiji) and the U.S.

*How to teach:* Compare Germany, Meiji Japan and the U.S. in a chart: start date, role of government, key industries. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** How did Japan's industrialization differ from Britain's?
  **A:** It was led by the Meiji government, which built railroads and factories and then sold them to private firms (zaibatsu), and it came much later (after 1868).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 test
*Student:* Unit 3 test with source-analysis section.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 test from the assessment key, including the source-analysis section. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Why did the U.S. industrialize rapidly after the Civil War?
  **A:** Abundant resources, railroads, immigrant labor, capital and inventions.

**Assessment:** Unit 3 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 3 test (sample answers): 1) Smith's 'invisible hand' → individuals pursuing self-interest in a free market unintentionally benefit society. 2) Division of labor in the pin factory → 10 workers specializing made about 48,000 pins a day. 3) Marx's view of history → 'the history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles' (bourgeoisie vs. proletariat). 4) Robert Owen → reformer who ran New Lanark mill with shorter hours, schools, and decent housing. 5) Meiji Japan → government-led industrialization after 1868. 6) Germany → rapid industrialization after unification (1871), steel and chemicals. 7) Source-analysis: identify author, purpose and bias of a given excerpt (2 points each).

## PE 10 (Course 2): Awareness and avoidance

**Prepare:** Preview videos on situational awareness and de-escalation (choose age-appropriate sources). Clear a practice space. Parental note: self-defense units focus on awareness, avoidance and escape; keep all partner drills slow and controlled.

**Watch for:**
- Students think self-defense is mostly fighting. Most personal safety is awareness, avoidance and confident communication; physical techniques are a last resort to escape.
- Students think being polite means staying in an uncomfortable situation. Clear, firm boundaries are appropriate and safe.
- Students mirror an angry person's tone. De-escalation means calm voice, space, open hands and listening.

### Day 1 (Monday): Awareness
*Student:* Learn the color codes of awareness and practice scanning in public settings.

*How to teach:* Teach the color codes and practice scanning in a store or park (exits, people, items out of place).

- **Q:** Which awareness color should you be in when walking in public?
  **A:** Yellow — relaxed but alert.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Boundaries
*Student:* Practice assertive verbal boundary setting with role-plays.

*How to teach:* Role-play assertive verbal boundaries with posture, voice and eye contact.

- **Q:** Model an assertive boundary to someone standing too close.
  **A:** Hands up, firm voice: 'Back up. You're too close.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): De-escalation
*Student:* Learn de-escalation techniques and practice scenarios.

*How to teach:* Practice de-escalation techniques in scenarios (angry customer, upset friend).

- **Q:** Name three de-escalation techniques.
  **A:** Stay calm and speak softly, give space, listen and acknowledge feelings, avoid threatening body language, offer choices.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Stance & movement
*Student:* Practice defensive stance and footwork drills for 20 minutes.

*How to teach:* Practice defensive stance and footwork drills (advance, retreat, pivot) for 20 minutes.

- **Q:** Why keep hands up and open in a defensive stance?
  **A:** It protects the face and looks non-aggressive while ready to block.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Scenario quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the scenario quiz.

- **Q:** What is the goal of self-defense?
  **A:** To get away safely and get help — not to win a fight.

**Assessment:** Scenario quiz.

**Answer key:** Scenario quiz key (sample): 1) Cooper color codes — White: unaware; Yellow: relaxed alert (default in public); Orange: specific potential threat noticed; Red: action (escape/defend). 2) Someone follows you in a parking lot — move toward people and light, keep keys/phone ready, don't go to your car if they're near it, call for help. 3) A stranger asks you to help with something in their car — decline and keep distance. 4) Verbal boundary — firm voice, eye contact, hand up: 'Stop. Back away.' 5) De-escalation — calm tone, give space, listen, acknowledge feelings, offer options, leave. 6) Defensive stance — feet shoulder-width, staggered, knees bent, hands up open in front of the face. 7) Best outcome — escape and get help, then report.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Turns, signals and lane changes

**Prepare:** Read the handbook sections on turns, signals and lane changes. Draw street diagrams with bike lanes and one-way streets. Gather toy cars.

**Watch for:**
- Students turn right from the traffic lane next to a bike lane. In California, drivers should merge into the bike lane no more than 200 feet before the turn (after checking for cyclists) and turn from there.
- Students signal at the last second. Signal at least 100 feet before turning, and 5 seconds before changing lanes on a freeway.
- Students skip the head check. Mirrors leave blind spots; always look over the shoulder before moving.

### Day 1 (Monday): Signaling
*Student:* Learn hand and turn signals; signal at least 100 feet before turning and 5 seconds before a freeway lane change.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate the three hand signals. The student practices hand and turn signals and explains when hand signals are used (bright sun, signal light out).

- **Q:** How far ahead should you signal a turn?
  **A:** At least 100 feet before turning.
- **Q:** Show the hand signal for slowing or stopping.
  **A:** Left arm extended out the window and bent down at the elbow.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Right & left turns
*Student:* Diagram right turns (merging into bike lane within 200 feet) and left turns from two-way and one-way streets.

*How to teach:* The student diagrams right turns (with and without a bike lane) and left turns from two-way and one-way streets, drawing the path with arrows.

- **Q:** Into which lane should you turn when making a left turn onto a two-way street?
  **A:** The lane closest to the center line (left-most lane for your direction).
- **Q:** When turning right where there's a bike lane, when may you enter it?
  **A:** No more than 200 feet before the turn, after checking for cyclists.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): U-turns
*Student:* List where U-turns are legal and illegal in California.

*How to teach:* The student lists where U-turns are legal and illegal in California.

- **Q:** Can you make a U-turn across a double yellow line?
  **A:** Yes, if it is otherwise legal and safe (no sign prohibits it, you can see 200 feet in each direction).
- **Q:** Is a U-turn legal in a residential district?
  **A:** Yes, if no vehicles are approaching within 200 feet, or at an intersection controlled by a sign or signal that protects you.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Lane changes
*Student:* Learn the signal–mirror–over-shoulder–go sequence and practice the head check as a passenger.

*How to teach:* Teach signal–mirror–over-the-shoulder–go. As a passenger, the student practices the head check timing.

- **Q:** Why is the head check necessary if mirrors are adjusted well?
  **A:** A vehicle, motorcycle or cyclist can still be in the blind spot, unseen in mirrors.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Diagram quiz on turns and lane changes.

*How to teach:* Give the diagram quiz. Review.

- **Q:** You are turning left from a one-way street onto another one-way street. Which lane do you start and end in?
  **A:** Start in the far-left lane and turn into the far-left lane.

**Assessment:** Diagram quiz.

**Answer key:** Diagram quiz key: 1) Right turn with bike lane — signal, check mirror and over shoulder for bikes, merge into the bike lane within 200 feet of the corner, turn into the nearest right lane. 2) Left turn from a two-way street onto a two-way street — start from the left-most lane closest to the center line, turn into the left lane of the new street. 3) Left from a one-way onto a one-way — start in the far-left lane, end in the far-left lane. 4) Right turn on red — complete stop, yield, then turn unless prohibited. 5) Hand signals — left: arm straight out; right: arm out and bent up (or right arm straight out the passenger side not used); slow/stop: arm out and bent down. 6) Signal distance — 100 feet before the turn. 7) Freeway lane change — signal 5 seconds before. 8) U-turn is illegal — on a one-way street, where a 'No U-turn' sign is posted, at or on a railroad crossing, across a divided highway's dividing section, in front of a fire station, in a business district except at intersections or openings, and where you cannot see 200 feet in each direction. 9) Lane change sequence — signal, mirrors, head check, move smoothly if clear.

## Computer Science Principles: Protocols: TCP, UDP, DNS and HTTP

**Prepare:** Learn how to open nslookup (Command Prompt or Terminal: 'nslookup example.com') and browser developer tools (F12 → Network tab). Have a speed-test site ready (e.g. fast.com or speedtest.net).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking UDP is 'broken' TCP. UDP purposely skips acknowledgments and resending to be faster — ideal when speed matters more than perfect delivery (live video, games).
- Mixing up bandwidth and latency. Bandwidth is how much data per second (pipe width); latency is delay for one piece of data to arrive (pipe length).
- Thinking DNS stores websites. DNS only translates domain names into IP addresses, like a phone book.

### Day 1 (Monday): TCP vs. UDP
*Student:* Compare TCP and UDP using a video call vs. file download example and list which each would use.

*How to teach:* Compare a video call and a file download. Ask: which one must have every bit perfect? Which one cares more about speed? Have the student list examples of apps that would use TCP vs. UDP.

- **Q:** Would a video call use TCP or UDP? Why?
  **A:** Usually UDP — it's better to skip a lost frame than wait for it to be resent, which would cause lag.
- **Q:** Would downloading a software update use TCP or UDP? Why?
  **A:** TCP — every byte must arrive correctly or the program would be corrupted, so lost packets must be resent.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): DNS
*Student:* Use nslookup to look up IP addresses for three domains and explain the DNS lookup process as a diagram.

*How to teach:* Run nslookup on three domains and record the IP addresses. Have the student draw the DNS lookup: browser → resolver → root server → top-level domain server (.com) → authoritative server → IP address returned.

- **Q:** What does DNS stand for, and what does it do?
  **A:** Domain Name System; it translates human-friendly domain names (like wikipedia.org) into IP addresses.
- **Q:** Why is DNS a hierarchy instead of one giant list on one computer?
  **A:** A distributed hierarchy scales to billions of lookups and has no single point of failure; responsibility is split among root, TLD and authoritative servers, with caching.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): HTTP and the web
*Student:* Open browser developer tools, load a web page, and record the HTTP requests, status codes and load time.

*How to teach:* Open developer tools → Network tab and reload a page. Have the student record how many requests, a few status codes (200, 301/304, 404) and total load time. Explain each file (HTML, CSS, images, scripts) is a separate HTTP request.

- **Q:** What does an HTTP status code of 404 mean? 200?
  **A:** 404 = Not Found (the server can't find the requested resource); 200 = OK (success).
- **Q:** Why does one web page create dozens of HTTP requests?
  **A:** The HTML page refers to many other files — images, stylesheets, scripts, fonts — and each is requested separately.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Bandwidth and latency
*Student:* Run an internet speed test at two times of day and explain the difference between bandwidth and latency.

*How to teach:* Run a speed test in the morning and evening and record download, upload and ping. Explain bandwidth (Mbps) vs. latency (ping in ms) using a highway: lanes = bandwidth, travel time = latency.

- **Q:** Your speed test shows 100 Mbps and 80 ms ping. Which number is bandwidth and which is latency?
  **A:** 100 Mbps is bandwidth; 80 ms ping is latency.
- **Q:** Why might the evening test be slower?
  **A:** More people are online sharing the same network (higher load/congestion).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Draw the layers of the internet (physical, IP, TCP/UDP, HTTP) for one web page request and take a short quiz.

*How to teach:* Have the student draw the protocol stack for loading one web page, then give the short quiz in the assessment key.

- **Q:** Put these in order from top (closest to the user) to bottom: IP, HTTP, physical, TCP.
  **A:** HTTP, TCP, IP, physical.

**Assessment:** Protocol stack diagram and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) TCP: reliable — numbers packets, acknowledges receipt, resends lost ones (used for web pages, email, file downloads). 2) UDP: fast, no guarantees (live video calls, online games, live streams). 3) DNS translates domain names to IP addresses. 4) HTTP is the protocol browsers and servers use to request and send web pages. 5) Status 200 = OK; 404 = Not Found. 6) Latency = time delay for data to travel; measured in milliseconds. 7) Bandwidth = max data per second (Mbps). 8) Layers top to bottom: HTTP (application) → TCP/UDP (transport) → IP (internet) → physical (wires, fiber, radio). Protocol stack diagram rubric: 4 = all four layers in correct order with one-line job of each and arrows showing the request down one side and up the other; 3 = correct order, jobs vague; 2 = one layer missing or misordered; 1 = incomplete.
