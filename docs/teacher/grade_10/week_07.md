# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 7

## English 10: Antigone: Downfall and the tragic hero

**Prepare:** Antigone text; locate a filmed production (e.g., a National Theatre recording, library DVDs, or YouTube). Parent preview: Exodos describes Antigone's, Haemon's and Eurydice's suicides (narrated, not staged).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Creon's reversal comes too late by chance. It's because he delays — show the cause-effect chain.
- Writing a theme as one word ('pride'). A theme is a complete statement about life.

### Day 1 (Monday): Read Scenes 4–5
*Student:* Read Antigone's farewell and Teiresias's warning; mark the moment Creon's reversal begins.

*How to teach:* Read Scene 4 (Antigone's farewell) and Scene 5 (Teiresias). The student marks the moment Creon begins to reverse. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does Teiresias warn Creon about?
  **A:** The gods reject Thebes' sacrifices because of the unburied body; Creon will pay with a death in his own family.
- **Q:** When does Creon change his mind?
  **A:** After Teiresias leaves and the Choragos urges him to free Antigone and bury Polyneices; he agrees to go himself.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Exodos
*Student:* Finish the play and complete a cause-effect chain from Creon's decree to the deaths.

*How to teach:* Finish the Exodos. The student builds a cause-effect chain from the decree to the deaths. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What happens in the Exodos?
  **A:** Antigone has hanged herself in the tomb; Haemon attacks Creon, then kills himself; Queen Eurydice kills herself on hearing the news; Creon is left broken.
- **Q:** What lesson does the Choragos state at the end?
  **A:** Wisdom is the key to happiness; the gods punish the proud, and proud men learn wisdom too late, in old age.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compare a staging
*Student:* Watch one scene from a filmed production and compare how actors portray Creon versus your reading.

*How to teach:* Watch one scene (Scene 2 or the Exodos works well) from a filmed production. The student writes three notes on how the actor plays Creon (voice, movement, facial expression) and compares each to how they imagined him while reading; discuss which interpretation the text supports better.

- **Q:** How might an actor show Creon's pride physically?
  **A:** Stiff posture, raised voice, refusing eye contact, turning his back on others.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Seminar
*Student:* Hold a Socratic seminar on 'Who is the tragic hero, Antigone or Creon?' using at least four cited lines.

*How to teach:* Hold the seminar using the rubric. The student must cite at least four lines. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Give an argument that Creon is the tragic hero.
  **A:** He is a high-status figure whose hubris causes a reversal; he recognizes his error too late and suffers the loss of his son and wife — evoking pity and fear.

### Day 5 (Friday): Theme statement
*Student:* Write three possible theme statements for the play and choose the strongest with a justification.

*How to teach:* Model the difference between a topic ('pride') and a theme (a complete statement about life). The student drafts three theme statements, chooses the strongest, and writes a short paragraph justifying it with two lines from the play.

- **Q:** Turn 'pride' into a complete theme statement for Antigone.
  **A:** For example: 'Leaders who let pride stop them from listening bring ruin on themselves and others.'

**Assessment:** Seminar rubric and theme statements.

**Answer key:** Seminar rubric: 4 = makes a clear claim (Antigone or Creon), cites at least four lines accurately, responds to others' evidence, asks a follow-up question; 3 = claim with 2–3 citations; 2 = opinions without text; 1 = minimal. Strong evidence for Creon as tragic hero: he falls from high status through hubris, experiences a reversal and recognition ('I alone am guilty') and survives to suffer. For Antigone: noble, principled, her stubbornness contributes to her fate. Theme statements (model): 'Pride that refuses to listen destroys those it means to protect.' 'Following conscience may require sacrifice.' Score 3 = three complete thematic sentences with justification for the chosen one.

## Geometry: Congruence through rigid motion

**Prepare:** Patty paper, straws or paper strips cut to lengths (e.g., 4, 5, 6, 7, 9 inches), brads or tape, protractor, GeoGebra. Prepare the Unit 2 test from the assessment key.

**Watch for:**
- Believing SSA or AAA proves congruence. Build counterexamples with straws: AAA gives same shape different sizes; SSA can give two different triangles.
- Writing congruence statements with vertices in the wrong order. The order must match corresponding parts: △ABC ≅ △DEF means A↔D, B↔E, C↔F.

### Day 1 (Monday): Congruence definition
*Student:* Decide whether pairs of figures are congruent by finding a rigid motion or showing none exists.

*How to teach:* Define congruent figures as figures related by a rigid motion. For each pair, the student uses patty paper to try to map one onto the other and names the motion or explains why none exists (different side lengths).

- **Q:** Two triangles have the same angles but different side lengths. Are they congruent?
  **A:** No — no rigid motion maps one onto the other because rigid motions preserve length.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Corresponding parts
*Student:* Write congruence statements and list all corresponding congruent parts (CPCTC).

*How to teach:* Model writing △ABC ≅ △DEF so that matching vertices are in the same order, then listing six pairs of congruent parts. Introduce CPCTC.

- **Q:** If △JKL ≅ △RST, which angle is congruent to ∠K and which side to LJ?
  **A:** ∠K ≅ ∠S, and LJ ≅ TR.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Triangle criteria
*Student:* Build triangles from given sides/angles with straws to test SSS, SAS, ASA, SSA and AAA.

*How to teach:* Give the student sets of straws and challenge them to build two different triangles for each case. SSS, SAS, ASA always produce one triangle; AAA and SSA may not.

- **Q:** Why does AAA not prove triangles congruent?
  **A:** Triangles can have the same angles but be different sizes (similar, not congruent).
- **Q:** Show SSA can fail.
  **A:** With sides 5 and 7 and a 30° angle not between them, the side of length 5 can swing to two different positions, making two different triangles.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Explain criteria
*Student:* Write a rigid-motion explanation of why SAS guarantees congruence.

*How to teach:* Talk through the argument together using patty paper, then the student writes a paragraph: map the included angle's vertex, rotate to line up one side, use equal lengths and equal angle to land the other vertices.

- **Q:** In SAS, why must the angle be the included angle?
  **A:** The angle between the two sides fixes the direction of both sides from the common vertex; a non-included angle does not lock the triangle in place.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 test
*Student:* Unit 2 test on transformations and congruence.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test from the assessment key; review errors together. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Name the four triangle congruence shortcuts for general triangles.
  **A:** SSS, SAS, ASA and AAS.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test with answers: 1) Rotate (−2, 5) 90° counterclockwise → (−5, −2). 2) Reflect (4, 1) over y = −x → (−1, −4). 3) Lines of symmetry of a regular octagon → 8. 4) △PQR ≅ △XYZ; list corresponding parts → PQ ≅ XY, QR ≅ YZ, PR ≅ XZ, ∠P ≅ ∠X, ∠Q ≅ ∠Y, ∠R ≅ ∠Z. 5) Which are valid criteria: SSS, SAS, ASA, SSA, AAA → SSS, SAS, ASA (and AAS); not SSA or AAA. 6) Short answer: explain with rigid motions why two triangles with SAS are congruent → translate to match the included vertex, rotate to align one side, the equal side lengths make endpoints match, reflect if needed; the equal included angle makes the second side lie on the same ray and its equal length makes the endpoints coincide, so all three vertices match. 7) Write a sequence mapping one given figure to another (any correct sequence). Mastery 80%.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Periodic table trends

**Prepare:** Make 20 element cards (name, symbol, atomic mass, a property such as metal/nonmetal, reactivity, formula of oxide). Gather graph paper or a spreadsheet with atomic radius and first ionization energy data for elements 1–20 (from PubChem or a textbook appendix). Queue an alkali-metals-in-water video (e.g., Royal Institution or Open University).

**Watch for:**
- Students think atoms get bigger across a period because they gain electrons. Across a period, added protons increase nuclear charge while shielding stays about the same, so electrons are pulled in and the radius shrinks.
- Students think metals 'want' to lose electrons. Atoms do not have wants; lower ionization energy means the outer electron is held less tightly and is removed more easily.
- Students reverse ionization energy and radius trends. Remind them that they are inverse: small atoms hold electrons tightly (high IE).

### Day 1 (Monday): Organizing elements
*Student:* Sort 20 element cards by properties to recreate Mendeleev's reasoning.

*How to teach:* Shuffle the element cards. The student sorts them into groups by similar properties, then by increasing atomic mass, and explains the arrangement. Tell how Mendeleev (1869) left gaps for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties.

- **Q:** Why was Mendeleev's table convincing to other scientists?
  **A:** He predicted properties of undiscovered elements (e.g., 'eka-silicon' = germanium) that were later found to match closely.
- **Q:** Today the table is ordered by what instead of atomic mass?
  **A:** Atomic number (number of protons), which fixes cases like tellurium/iodine.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Graph trends
*Student:* Graph atomic radius and first ionization energy vs. atomic number and describe the patterns.

*How to teach:* The student plots atomic radius and first ionization energy versus atomic number (1–20) and describes repeating patterns. Prompt: where are the peaks and valleys?

- **Q:** Which elements are at the peaks of the ionization energy graph?
  **A:** Noble gases: He, Ne, Ar.
- **Q:** What happens to atomic radius going from Li to Ne?
  **A:** It decreases.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Explain trends
*Student:* Explain trends using nuclear charge and shielding with diagrams.

*How to teach:* Draw Bohr-style shells for Na and Cl side by side. Explain effective nuclear charge (protons minus inner shielding electrons) and shielding. The student writes explanations for each trend using these two ideas.

- **Q:** Explain why K is larger than Na.
  **A:** K has an additional occupied electron shell (4 vs. 3), so its outer electron is farther from the nucleus, even though K has more protons.
- **Q:** Why is the first ionization energy of F greater than that of Li?
  **A:** F has a much larger effective nuclear charge pulling on electrons in the same shell, so its electrons are held more tightly.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Reactivity demo
*Student:* Watch alkali metal in water videos and predict reactivity down Group 1.

*How to teach:* Before showing the video, the student predicts the reaction of Li, Na and K with water, then watches and checks. Write the equation for sodium and connect reactivity to ionization energy.

- **Q:** Write the balanced equation for sodium and water.
  **A:** 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂
- **Q:** Predict how cesium reacts compared with potassium.
  **A:** More violently: cesium's outer electron is farther from the nucleus and easier to remove.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on periodic trends.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-question quiz and grade it together. Collect the graphs.

- **Q:** Which has a larger radius, Mg or Mg²⁺?
  **A:** Mg — losing the two 3s electrons removes the outer shell, so Mg²⁺ is smaller.

**Assessment:** Trend graphs and quiz.

**Answer key:** Graphs: full credit for correctly plotted radius and ionization energy vs. atomic number for Z = 1–20, axes labeled with units (pm, kJ/mol), and written patterns: IE peaks at noble gases (He, Ne, Ar) and drops sharply at alkali metals (Li, Na, K); radius peaks at alkali metals and decreases across each period. Quiz key: 1) Larger atom: Na or Cl? Na. 2) Larger atom: Li or K? K. 3) Higher first ionization energy: Mg or Ba? Mg. 4) Most electronegative element: fluorine. 5) More reactive: Na or K? K, its outer electron is farther from the nucleus and more shielded. 6) Explain the radius decrease across period 3 — more protons (greater effective nuclear charge) with the same number of shells/shielding. 7) Why does IE jump from Na to Ne? Ne has a full shell with electrons close to the nucleus; Na's lone 3s electron is far away and shielded. 8) Group 17 name — halogens; Group 18 — noble gases. 9) Mendeleev left gaps — he predicted undiscovered elements such as gallium and germanium. 10) Rank by electronegativity: Na < C < O.

## Modern World History: Industrial society: cities, labor and reform

**Prepare:** Sadler Committee testimony (1832; e.g., Elizabeth Bentley), Engels, The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845) excerpt on Manchester, Dickens, Hard Times Book I ch. 5 ('The Key-note,' Coketown) from Gutenberg. Parent note: testimony describes beatings and injuries of children.

**Watch for:**
- Assuming reforms happened quickly. Change was gradual: 1833 Factory Act, 1842 Mines Act, 1847 Ten Hours Act.
- Thinking only men worked in factories. Women and children were a large part of the textile workforce.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sadler Report
*Student:* Read child-labor testimony from the Sadler Committee and annotate conditions.

*How to teach:* Source the Sadler Committee (1832, a parliamentary inquiry by reformers). The student reads testimony and annotates conditions: hours, age, punishments, injuries. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What conditions did child workers describe to the Sadler Committee?
  **A:** Very long days (often 5 or 6 a.m. to 8 or 9 p.m. in busy times), beatings by overlookers when tired, injuries and deformities.
- **Q:** Why must historians consider the Committee's purpose?
  **A:** Sadler was a reformer pushing a ten-hour bill, so he chose witnesses who supported reform; testimony is valuable but may emphasize the worst cases.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cities
*Student:* Read Engels's description of Manchester and compare to a modern city.

*How to teach:* Read Engels's description of Manchester. The student lists five details about housing, sanitation and pollution and compares them to a modern city. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What did Engels observe about Manchester's working-class districts?
  **A:** Crowded, filthy housing, open sewage, polluted rivers and air, and poverty hidden away from middle-class streets.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dickens
*Student:* Read the Coketown chapter of Hard Times and identify social criticism.

*How to teach:* Read the Coketown chapter. The student identifies Dickens's criticism of industrial sameness and pollution. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** How does Dickens describe Coketown?
  **A:** A town of red brick blackened by smoke, with machinery, tall chimneys, a black canal, a river purple with dye, and streets and people all alike.
- **Q:** What is Dickens criticizing?
  **A:** The dehumanizing sameness and ugliness of industrial life, where people become like machines.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Reform
*Student:* Trace factory acts, unions, abolition of slavery and women's roles in a timeline.

*How to teach:* The student creates a timeline of reforms 1824–1847 and notes women's roles in industry and reform movements. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What did the Factory Act of 1833 do?
  **A:** Banned children under 9 from textile mills, limited hours for children 9–13, and created factory inspectors.

### Day 5 (Friday): Letter
*Student:* Write a letter to Parliament as a factory worker in 1833 using evidence.

*How to teach:* The student writes a letter to Parliament as a factory worker in 1833 using at least three pieces of evidence. Score with the rubric. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What would a worker likely ask Parliament for in 1833?
  **A:** Shorter working hours (a ten-hour day), limits on child labor, and safer conditions.

**Assessment:** Historical letter scored for evidence.

**Answer key:** Historical letter rubric (4-point): 4 = written in the voice of an 1833 factory worker; cites at least three specific details from the Sadler testimony, Engels or Dickens (e.g., 14–16 hour days, beatings by overlookers, injuries, polluted housing); makes a clear request (shorter hours, limits on child labor, inspectors); historically accurate; 3 = good but fewer specifics; 2 = generic; 1 = inaccurate. Reform timeline: Reform Act 1832; Factory Act 1833 (no children under 9 in textile mills; ages 9–13 limited to 9 hours a day; inspectors); Slavery Abolition Act 1833; Mines Act 1842; Ten Hours Act 1847; legalization of unions (Combination Acts repealed 1824–25).

## Music & Media Arts: Loops, bass lines and arrangement

**Prepare:** BandLab; a favorite song (clean) for form analysis; feedback form for family.

**Watch for:**
- Students write bass lines with random notes. Start with chord roots on beat 1, then add passing tones.
- Students stack loops that clash in key. Check loop key and tempo before layering.
- Students make songs with no change. Arrangement means adding and removing parts to create sections.

### Day 1 (Monday): Bass lines
*Student:* Write a bass line using root notes of a chord progression.

*How to teach:* The student writes a bass line using chord roots of a progression (e.g., C–G–Am–F).

- **Q:** What notes would a root-note bass line play over C–G–Am–F?
  **A:** C, G, A, F.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Loops
*Student:* Explore BandLab loop library and layer three loops.

*How to teach:* Explore the loop library and layer three loops in the same key and tempo.

- **Q:** Why check a loop's key?
  **A:** Loops in different keys can clash harmonically.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Song form
*Student:* Analyze form of a favorite song (intro, verse, chorus, bridge).

*How to teach:* The student analyzes a favorite song's form with timestamps.

- **Q:** What's the difference between a verse and a chorus?
  **A:** Verses change lyrics and tell the story; the chorus repeats the main hook and lyrics.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Arrange
*Student:* Arrange a 90-second instrumental track.

*How to teach:* Arrange a 90-second instrumental with clear sections.

- **Q:** How can you make a 'drop' or chorus feel bigger?
  **A:** Add layers (bass, drums, synths), raise energy, or remove parts just before it for contrast.

### Day 5 (Friday): Feedback
*Student:* Get feedback from family and revise.

*How to teach:* Get family feedback and revise.

- **Q:** What makes feedback useful?
  **A:** It's specific (time stamp and element) and suggests a change.

**Assessment:** Draft track.

**Answer key:** Draft track rubric: 90 seconds, at least three sections (e.g., intro, verse, drop/chorus) with clear changes; bass line following chord roots; three or more layered loops in the same key; revisions noted from family feedback. Form analysis key example: Intro – Verse 1 – Pre-chorus – Chorus – Verse 2 – Pre-chorus – Chorus – Bridge – Chorus – Outro.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Speed laws and following distance

**Prepare:** Read the handbook speed-limit section. Gather a stopwatch, calculator and graph paper. For Day 3 use these assumptions: reaction time 1.5 s; 1 mph = 1.467 ft/s; braking distance ≈ mph² ÷ 20 feet on dry pavement (a common approximation). Build the Unit 2 test from the key.

**Watch for:**
- Students think driving the posted limit is always legal. The Basic Speed Law says you may never drive faster than is safe for current conditions (weather, traffic, visibility), even below the limit.
- Students think doubling speed doubles stopping distance. Braking distance grows with the square of speed: doubling speed roughly quadruples braking distance.
- Students count 3 seconds from when they pass a fixed object regardless of conditions. Increase the gap in rain, at night, behind motorcycles or large trucks, and when being tailgated.

### Day 1 (Monday): Basic Speed Law
*Student:* Read and explain the Basic Speed Law: never drive faster than is safe for conditions.

*How to teach:* Read the Basic Speed Law together. The student lists five conditions that require slower speeds and explains why a driver going 60 in a 65 zone in fog could be ticketed.

- **Q:** Can you get a speeding ticket while driving under the posted limit?
  **A:** Yes — under the Basic Speed Law, if your speed is unsafe for conditions (rain, fog, heavy traffic).
- **Q:** Name three conditions that require slowing below the limit.
  **A:** Examples: rain or fog, heavy traffic, pedestrians/children nearby, sharp curves, night, construction zones.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Speed limits
*Student:* Make a chart of limits: 65 mph max on most highways, 55 for two-lane undivided roads and when towing, 25 in residential and school zones, 15 at blind intersections and railroad crossings.

*How to teach:* The student makes a speed-limit chart and quizzes a family member.

- **Q:** What is the speed limit within 100 feet of a railroad crossing where you can't see 400 feet down the tracks in both directions?
  **A:** 15 mph.
- **Q:** What is the limit in a school zone when children are present?
  **A:** 25 mph (unless otherwise posted; some zones are posted at 15 or 20 mph).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Stopping distance
*Student:* Calculate reaction and braking distance at 25, 45 and 65 mph and graph how distance grows with speed.

*How to teach:* Model: reaction distance = speed (ft/s) × 1.5 s; braking ≈ mph² ÷ 20. The student calculates totals at 25, 45 and 65 mph and graphs them.

- **Q:** Calculate the total stopping distance at 25 mph.
  **A:** Reaction: 25 × 1.467 × 1.5 ≈ 55 ft; braking: 625 ÷ 20 ≈ 31 ft; total ≈ 86 ft.
- **Q:** Calculate it at 65 mph.
  **A:** Reaction ≈ 143 ft; braking: 4225 ÷ 20 ≈ 211 ft; total ≈ 354 ft — more than a football field.
- **Q:** What happens to braking distance when speed doubles?
  **A:** It roughly quadruples.

### Day 4 (Thursday): 3-second rule
*Student:* As a passenger, practice counting 3 seconds behind the vehicle ahead and note when to increase following distance.

*How to teach:* As a passenger, the student picks a fixed object; when the car ahead passes it, counts 'one-thousand-one…'; if your car reaches it before 3, the gap is too small. Note situations needing more space.

- **Q:** When should you increase following distance beyond 3 seconds?
  **A:** In bad weather, at night, behind motorcycles or large trucks, when being tailgated, on slippery roads, or when towing.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 test
*Student:* Unit 2 test on signs, signals, right-of-way and speed.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test. Grade together.

- **Q:** What is the maximum speed on most California highways?
  **A:** 65 mph, unless signs post 70 mph.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test key (sample 15 items): 1) Octagon — stop. 2) Pennant — no passing zone. 3) Pentagon — school zone. 4) Flashing red — stop then proceed. 5) Signal out — all-way stop. 6) Double solid yellow — no passing. 7) Blue curb — disabled parking. 8) Two cars at a four-way stop together — car on the right goes first. 9) Roundabout — yield to traffic in the circle, counterclockwise. 10) School bus with flashing red lights — stop. 11) Basic Speed Law — never faster than safe for conditions. 12) Maximum on most California highways — 65 mph (70 where posted). 13) Two-lane undivided highway and when towing — 55 mph. 14) Residential and school zones (when children present) — 25 mph. 15) Blind intersections, railroad crossings within 100 feet, alleys — 15 mph. ## Computer Science Principles: Routing, redundancy and fault tolerance

**Prepare:** Learn how to run traceroute: Windows Command Prompt 'tracert google.com'; Mac/Linux Terminal 'traceroute google.com'. Gather string/yarn, tape and sticky notes for a 6–8 node network model. Read a short ARPANET history (Khan Academy or Code.org).

**Watch for:**
- Believing there is one central computer that runs the internet. The internet is decentralized; routers make local forwarding decisions.
- Thinking redundancy is waste. Redundant paths make the network fault tolerant — if one link fails, data can take another route.
- Confusing fault tolerance with 'never failing'. A fault-tolerant system keeps working when some parts fail; a single critical link can still disconnect part of the network.

### Day 1 (Monday): Routing
*Student:* Run a traceroute (tracert/traceroute) to two websites and map how many hops each path takes.

*How to teach:* Have the student run traceroute to a nearby site (e.g. a California university) and a distant one (e.g. a site in another country). Count hops and note the round-trip times. Explain each line is a router forwarding the packet.

- **Q:** What does each line of a traceroute output represent?
  **A:** One router (hop) along the path, with the time it took for a test packet to get there and back.
- **Q:** Why might a distant website need more hops?
  **A:** Packets must pass through more networks and routers to travel the greater distance and reach the destination's network.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Redundancy
*Student:* Build a string-and-sticky-note network model and count how many alternative paths exist between two nodes.

*How to teach:* Build a network on a table: sticky notes are routers (A–F), string pieces are links. Have the student count how many different paths exist from A to F without repeating a node.

- **Q:** Network: A–B, B–C, A–D, D–C. How many paths from A to C?
  **A:** Two: A→B→C and A→D→C.
- **Q:** What is redundancy in a network?
  **A:** Having more than one path between devices, so traffic can be rerouted.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fault tolerance
*Student:* Remove links from your model one at a time and determine which removals disconnect the network.

*How to teach:* Remove one string at a time and check if A can still reach F. Mark any link whose removal disconnects the network (a single point of failure).

- **Q:** In the network A–B, B–C, A–D, D–C, C–E, can the network survive the loss of B–C? Of C–E?
  **A:** Losing B–C: yes, A→D→C still works. Losing C–E: no, E is disconnected — C–E is a single point of failure.
- **Q:** Define fault tolerance.
  **A:** The ability of a system to keep operating even when some components fail.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Scalability
*Student:* Read about how the internet scaled from ARPANET and explain why open, decentralized design helped it grow.

*How to teach:* Read about ARPANET (1969, four nodes) and the creation of TCP/IP (Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn, 1970s). Discuss: because the protocols are open and no one controls the whole network, anyone can add networks, which let it scale to billions of devices.

- **Q:** Why did open protocols help the internet grow?
  **A:** Anyone can build devices or software that follow TCP/IP without permission or fees, so many companies and countries could connect their networks.
- **Q:** How many nodes did ARPANET start with in 1969?
  **A:** Four (UCLA, Stanford Research Institute, UC Santa Barbara and the University of Utah).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Complete AP-style questions on routing and fault tolerance using network diagrams.

*How to teach:* Draw a 6-node network and ask the AP-style questions in the assessment key. Have the student trace each path with a finger before answering.

- **Q:** Why is the internet's routing described as dynamic?
  **A:** Routers choose paths based on current conditions and can change routes when links fail or get congested.

**Assessment:** Network diagram questions.

**Answer key:** AP-style diagram questions (use a diagram you draw with nodes A–F): typical answers — 1) Count paths: list every distinct route without repeating nodes. 2) A connection that, if removed, disconnects a node (a single point of failure) shows the network is not fault tolerant for that node. 3) Minimum number of connections that must fail to stop A from reaching F = the smallest set of links that separates them. 4) Redundancy = extra paths; fault tolerance = continuing to work after a failure. 5) Routing = the process of choosing a path for packets; routers forward packets toward the destination using routing tables. 6) Scalability = ability to grow while still working well. Score: correct path counts and identification of single points of failure = mastery. (Grade the student's specific diagram by tracing each answer with them.)
