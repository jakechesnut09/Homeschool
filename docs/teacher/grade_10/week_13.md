# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 13

## English 10: Speech writing: persuasive oration

**Prepare:** Speech planning sheet, phone for video. Help the student identify a local issue and two credible sources (local news, city/county websites).

**Watch for:**
- Confusing a topic with a position. 'Parks' is a topic; 'Our city should fund a new skate park' is a position.
- Overloading devices. Each device should serve a point.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose an issue
*Student:* Choose a contemporary community issue and research two credible facts and one quotation to support a position.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm three local issues (parks, transit, library hours, school start times) and pick the one the student cares about most. The student writes a one-sentence position and finds two credible facts and one quotation, recording source, author and date for each.

- **Q:** How can you tell if a source is credible?
  **A:** Check the author's expertise, the publisher, the date, evidence cited and whether other reliable sources agree.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Draft speech
*Student:* Draft a 3–4 minute speech using at least anaphora, antithesis, a rhetorical question and a tricolon.

*How to teach:* Teach anaphora, antithesis, rhetorical question and tricolon with examples. The student drafts the speech, labeling each device. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Give an example of antithesis from Julius Caesar.
  **A:** 'Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more.'
- **Q:** What is a tricolon?
  **A:** A series of three parallel words or phrases, e.g., 'I came, I saw, I conquered.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Revise for audience
*Student:* Revise for audience and timing; mark the script for pauses and emphasis.

*How to teach:* The student revises for audience and time, marking pauses (/) and emphasis (underline). Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How long is a 3–4 minute speech, roughly?
  **A:** About 400–550 words at a typical speaking pace.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rehearse
*Student:* Rehearse twice on video and self-score eye contact, pace and volume.

*How to teach:* Record two run-throughs on a phone. The student watches each and self-scores eye contact, pace and volume on a 1–4 scale, then picks one thing to fix before the second take.

- **Q:** What is one strategy to slow down a rushed speech?
  **A:** Mark planned pauses after key lines and breathe at each one.

### Day 5 (Friday): Deliver
*Student:* Deliver the speech to family and answer two audience questions.

*How to teach:* Gather the family as an audience. The student delivers the speech and then takes two questions; score with the speech rubric and give one specific compliment and one goal. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How should a speaker handle a question they can't answer?
  **A:** Acknowledge it honestly, give what they do know, and offer to follow up.

**Assessment:** Speech rubric (content, rhetoric, delivery).

**Answer key:** Speech rubric: Content (4) — clear position, two credible facts with sources, a quotation, logical organization; Rhetoric (4) — anaphora, antithesis, rhetorical question and tricolon used effectively; Delivery (4) — eye contact, pace, volume, 3–4 minutes, answers questions thoughtfully. 10–12 = proficient.

## Geometry: Rectangles, rhombi, squares, trapezoids and kites

**Prepare:** GeoGebra, paper for a large Venn diagram/hierarchy, colored pencils.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a square is not a rectangle or rhombus. Use definitions: a square has four right angles (rectangle) and four congruent sides (rhombus).
- Thinking kite diagonals bisect each other. Only one diagonal is bisected (the one between the vertices where unequal sides meet); they are perpendicular.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rectangles
*Student:* Prove diagonals of a rectangle are congruent and use it to solve problems.

*How to teach:* Have the student prove rectangle diagonals congruent with SAS (two right angles, opposite sides congruent, shared side). Solve algebra problems using equal diagonals.

- **Q:** Rectangle ABCD has diagonal AC = 2x + 6 and BD = 4x − 10. Find x and AC.
  **A:** 2x + 6 = 4x − 10 → x = 8, AC = 22.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rhombi & squares
*Student:* Prove diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular and bisect angles.

*How to teach:* Prove rhombus diagonals are perpendicular using SSS on the four small triangles, and that they bisect the vertex angles. Practice problems combining the Pythagorean theorem with diagonals.

- **Q:** A rhombus has diagonals 12 and 16. Find a side length.
  **A:** Half-diagonals 6 and 8, so side = 10.
- **Q:** Why is every square a rhombus?
  **A:** A square has four congruent sides, which is the definition of a rhombus.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Trapezoids & kites
*Student:* Explore trapezoid midsegment and kite diagonal properties in GeoGebra.

*How to teach:* In GeoGebra, measure a trapezoid's midsegment and compare to the bases; measure the diagonals of a kite. The student writes conjectures.

- **Q:** A trapezoid's midsegment is 11 and one base is 8. Find the other base.
  **A:** (8 + b)/2 = 11 → b = 14.
- **Q:** Name a property of an isosceles trapezoid's diagonals.
  **A:** They are congruent.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Quadrilateral hierarchy
*Student:* Build a Venn diagram/hierarchy of quadrilaterals and justify placements.

*How to teach:* Build the hierarchy diagram together, asking 'Is every ___ a ___?' for each link. The student justifies each placement with a definition.

- **Q:** Is every rectangle a square? Is every square a rectangle?
  **A:** No; yes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on special quadrilaterals.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key and check the hierarchy diagram. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Which quadrilaterals have perpendicular diagonals?
  **A:** Rhombi (including squares) and kites.

**Assessment:** Quiz and hierarchy diagram.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Rectangle diagonals AC = 3x + 4, BD = 5x − 8 → x = 6, each 22. 2) Rhombus with diagonals 10 and 24 → side = √(5² + 12²) = 13. 3) In a rhombus, m∠ABD = 35° where BD is a diagonal → m∠ABC = 70°. 4) Trapezoid bases 10 and 16 → midsegment 13. 5) Kite diagonals → perpendicular, one bisects the other. 6) True/False: every square is a rhombus (T); every rhombus is a square (F); every rectangle is a parallelogram (T); a trapezoid is a parallelogram (F, under the exclusive definition). Hierarchy rubric: 3 = quadrilateral → parallelogram/trapezoid/kite; parallelogram → rectangle and rhombus; square in both; each placement justified by a definition; 2 = one misplacement; 1 = several.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Metallic bonding and designed materials

**Prepare:** Gather aluminum foil, white school glue (PVA), borax (or contact-lens saline with boric acid plus baking soda), measuring cups, food coloring, zip bags, an HDPE bag (crinkly grocery bag, recycling code 2) and an LDPE bag (bread or zip bag, code 4), and a ruler. Borax should not be ingested; wash hands after slime.

**Watch for:**
- Students think metals are malleable because metal atoms are soft. Metal cations sit in a sea of mobile electrons; layers can slide without breaking the bonding, unlike ionic crystals that shatter.
- Students think slime is a new compound like a reaction product. Borate ions cross-link existing PVA chains with weak, reversible bonds; that is why slime flows slowly yet stretches.
- Students think 'plastic' is one material. Different polymers have different chain structures and properties (HDPE vs. LDPE).

### Day 1 (Monday): Metals
*Student:* Model metallic bonding as a sea of electrons and explain malleability and conductivity.

*How to teach:* Model metallic bonding with a diagram of positive ions in a sea of electrons. Bend and fold aluminum foil and compare to tapping a salt crystal (which shatters). The student explains malleability, ductility and conductivity using the model.

- **Q:** Why do metals conduct electricity?
  **A:** Their valence electrons are delocalized and free to move through the metal.
- **Q:** Why does a salt crystal shatter when hit but a metal bends?
  **A:** Shifting an ionic lattice lines up like charges, which repel and split the crystal; in metals the electron sea keeps holding the ions together as layers slide.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Polymers
*Student:* Make slime with glue and borax and explain cross-linking of polymer chains.

*How to teach:* Mix ½ cup glue with ½ cup water, then slowly stir in a borax solution (1 tsp borax in 1 cup warm water) until slime forms. The student observes stretch, flow and bounce and draws polymer chains before and after cross-linking.

- **Q:** What does borax do to the glue?
  **A:** Borate ions form cross-links between long PVA polymer chains, turning the liquid into a stretchy gel.
- **Q:** Predict what adding more borax solution does.
  **A:** More cross-links make the slime stiffer and less stretchy (more rubbery).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Material testing
*Student:* Test the stretch of different plastic bags (HDPE vs. LDPE) and relate to chain branching.

*How to teach:* Cut equal 2 cm × 10 cm strips from an HDPE and an LDPE bag. The student pulls each slowly by hand, measuring how far it stretches before breaking, and compares. Draw linear vs. branched chains.

- **Q:** Which bag stretched more, and why?
  **A:** LDPE usually stretches more because its branched chains cannot pack tightly, so they slide past each other; HDPE's linear chains pack tightly, making it stiffer and stronger.
- **Q:** What does the 2 in a recycling triangle mean?
  **A:** The item is high-density polyethylene (HDPE).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Designed materials research
*Student:* Research one designed material (Kevlar, Gore-Tex, nonstick coating) and how structure gives function.

*How to teach:* The student researches one designed material using reliable sources and fills a chart: material, molecular structure, property, use, how structure causes property. Check the explanation is at the molecular level, not just 'it is strong'.

- **Q:** How does Kevlar's structure explain its strength?
  **A:** Long rigid chains align parallel and are held together by many hydrogen bonds between chains, so it resists pulling apart.
- **Q:** Why is nonstick PTFE coating slippery?
  **A:** Its surface is covered with fluorine atoms that are tightly held and have very weak attractions to other molecules.

### Day 5 (Friday): Present
*Student:* Present a 3-minute explanation of your material with a diagram.

*How to teach:* The student presents a 3-minute explanation with a diagram; you ask one question. Score with the rubric.

- **Q:** What is the general rule connecting structure and properties of materials?
  **A:** The arrangement of particles and the strength of forces between them determine bulk properties like strength, flexibility, melting point and conductivity.

**Assessment:** Material presentation rubric.

**Answer key:** Presentation rubric (4 levels): 4 = Names the material and its use; describes the molecular structure accurately (e.g., Kevlar: rigid, aligned polyamide chains with hydrogen bonds between chains → high tensile strength; Gore-Tex: expanded PTFE membrane with pores far smaller than liquid water droplets but larger than water vapor molecules → waterproof and breathable; PTFE nonstick: carbon chain shielded by fluorine atoms, very strong C–F bonds and weak surface attractions → nothing sticks); a clear diagram linking structure to function; within 3 minutes; answers one question. 3 = accurate but structure–function link vague. 2 = describes uses with little structure. 1 = inaccurate or incomplete.

## Modern World History: Causes of World War I

**Prepare:** Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia (July 23, 1914) and the 'Willy–Nicky' telegrams (July 29–August 1, 1914) — available from the Avalon Project or BYU WWI Document Archive. Map of 1914 alliances.

**Watch for:**
- Believing the assassination alone caused the war. It was the spark; the MAIN causes made a local crisis a world war.
- Thinking Italy fought with Germany. Italy was in the Triple Alliance but joined the Allies in 1915.

### Day 1 (Monday): MAIN causes
*Student:* Build a cause web of militarism, alliances, imperialism and nationalism.

*How to teach:* Draw a web with 'WWI' in the center and four bubbles: militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism. The student adds at least two specific examples to each (e.g., Anglo-German naval race; Triple Entente; Moroccan crises; Pan-Slavism) and draws arrows showing how causes connect.

- **Q:** Give an example of militarism before 1914.
  **A:** The Anglo-German naval race (dreadnoughts) or mass conscript armies and war plans like the Schlieffen Plan.
- **Q:** How did alliances turn a local conflict into a world war?
  **A:** Each country was bound to defend its partners, so the Austria-Serbia conflict pulled in Russia, Germany, France and Britain.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sarajevo
*Student:* Read accounts of Franz Ferdinand's assassination and map the July Crisis.

*How to teach:* Read two short accounts of the assassination in Sarajevo (June 28, 1914). The student builds a dated timeline of the July Crisis from the assassination to Britain's declaration of war on August 4, marking each alliance obligation that pulled a country in.

- **Q:** Who assassinated Franz Ferdinand?
  **A:** Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb nationalist linked to the Black Hand.
- **Q:** Why did Britain declare war?
  **A:** Germany invaded neutral Belgium as part of the Schlieffen Plan.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Leaders' arguments
*Student:* Analyze the Willy–Nicky telegrams and the ultimatum to Serbia.

*How to teach:* Source the documents: Austria-Hungary's ultimatum (July 23, 1914) and telegrams between Kaiser Wilhelm II and Tsar Nicholas II (July 29–August 1). The student summarizes each leader's position and identifies where each blames the other.

- **Q:** What did Austria's ultimatum demand?
  **A:** That Serbia suppress anti-Austrian groups and allow Austrian officials to take part in the investigation inside Serbia — demands meant to be hard to accept.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Blame debate
*Student:* Debate: Which nation bears most responsibility for war?

*How to teach:* Assign the student a nation (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Russia, Serbia, France or Britain) to blame and have them prepare three pieces of evidence; you argue another side. After a 15-minute debate, the student writes debate notes summarizing the strongest evidence on each side.

- **Q:** What evidence suggests Germany bears major responsibility?
  **A:** The 'blank check' to Austria and the Schlieffen Plan invasion of Belgium.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on causes.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key without notes (about 20 minutes). Grade it together; for each missed item the student finds the answer in their notes and writes a one-sentence correction.

- **Q:** What was the Schlieffen Plan?
  **A:** Germany's plan to defeat France quickly through Belgium, then turn to Russia.

**Assessment:** Debate notes and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz (answers): 1) MAIN → militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism. 2) Triple Entente → France, Russia, Britain; Triple Alliance → Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy. 3) Assassination → Archduke Franz Ferdinand and Sophie, June 28, 1914, Sarajevo, by Gavrilo Princip (Black Hand). 4) 'Blank check' → Germany's promise of unconditional support to Austria-Hungary. 5) Order of declarations → Austria on Serbia (July 28), Germany on Russia (Aug 1), Germany on France (Aug 3), Britain on Germany (Aug 4). 6) Why Britain entered → Germany invaded neutral Belgium. Debate notes: full credit for three cited pieces of evidence and a rebuttal.

## Spanish III: Mandatos informales y consejos

**Prepare:** Chart tú commands: affirmative = él form (habla, come); irregulars di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven; negative = no + subjunctive (no hables, no comas, no vayas). Prepare poster paper.

**Watch for:**
- Using the affirmative form in negatives (*No come): No comas.
- Forgetting the accent when attaching pronouns: Dímelo, Hazlo (one-syllable + one pronoun needs no accent), Cómelo.
- Confusing 've' (go, from ir) and 've' (see, from ver): context decides; 'sé' (be) has an accent.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mandatos afirmativos
*Student:* Learn affirmative tú commands including irregulars (di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven).

*How to teach:* Teach affirmative tú commands and the 8 irregulars with a chant. Your child gives commands to you and you obey.

- **Q:** Give affirmative tú commands for salir, tener, and ser.
  **A:** sal, ten, sé

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Negativos
*Student:* Learn negative tú commands.

*How to teach:* Teach negatives from the yo form; your child writes 10 negative commands.

- **Q:** Translate: 'Don't eat so much sugar.'
  **A:** No comas tanto azúcar.
- **Q:** Translate: 'Don't go to bed late.'
  **A:** No te acuestes tarde.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pronombres
*Student:* Attach and place pronouns with commands.

*How to teach:* Practice pronoun placement: attached to affirmatives, before negatives.

- **Q:** Rewrite with a pronoun: 'Bebe el agua.' and its negative.
  **A:** Bébela. / No la bebas.
- **Q:** Rewrite: 'Dime la verdad' as negative.
  **A:** No me digas la verdad.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Póster
*Student:* Create a health-advice poster with 10 commands.

*How to teach:* Your child creates a health-advice poster with 10 commands.

- **Q:** Write: 'Sleep eight hours.'
  **A:** Duerme ocho horas.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Commands quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the commands quiz.

- **Q:** Make negative: 'Hazlo ahora.'
  **A:** No lo hagas ahora.

**Assessment:** Poster and quiz.

**Answer key:** Commands quiz sample key: 1) decir — di / no digas 2) hacer — haz / no hagas 3) ir — ve / no vayas 4) ser — sé / no seas 5) poner — pon / no pongas 6) comer + lo — cómelo / no lo comas 7) acostarse — acuéstate / no te acuestes 8) tener — ten / no tengas. Poster rubric: 4 = 10 accurate commands with health advice and visuals; 3 = 1–2 errors; 2 = several errors; 1 = incomplete.

## Music & Media Arts: Songwriting project final

**Prepare:** BandLab, microphone, MuseScore/Noteflight for the lead sheet, and the song rubric.

**Watch for:**
- Students record one take and keep it. Record three or more and choose (or comp) the best.
- Students add heavy reverb to hide problems. Light reverb adds space; too much muddies the mix.
- Students leave chord symbols out of lead sheets. A lead sheet has melody, chord symbols above the staff and lyrics below.

### Day 1 (Monday): Record
*Student:* Record final vocal or lead instrument tracks, doing at least three takes and choosing the best.

*How to teach:* Record final vocal or lead tracks with at least three takes.

- **Q:** What helps a vocal recording sound clear?
  **A:** A quiet room with soft surfaces, consistent distance from the mic (about 6–12 inches), and a pop filter.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mix
*Student:* Mix the song: balance levels, pan supporting parts, and add light reverb.

*How to teach:* Mix: balance levels, pan supporting parts and add light reverb.

- **Q:** What does reverb simulate?
  **A:** The reflections of sound in a space, like a room or hall.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Lead sheet
*Student:* Create a lead sheet in MuseScore or Noteflight with melody, chord symbols and lyrics.

*How to teach:* Create a lead sheet with melody, chord symbols and lyrics.

- **Q:** Where do chord symbols go on a lead sheet?
  **A:** Above the staff, over the beat where each chord changes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Present
*Student:* Present the song to family and explain how melody, harmony and form support the lyric's meaning.

*How to teach:* Present the song and explain how melody, harmony and form support the lyric.

- **Q:** Give an example of music supporting lyrics.
  **A:** A rising melody on a hopeful line, or a minor chord on a sad word.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Write a 250-word reflection on your songwriting process and score yourself on the rubric.

*How to teach:* Write a 250-word reflection and score on the rubric.

- **Q:** What should a process reflection discuss?
  **A:** Challenges, decisions, feedback used and what you'd do differently.

**Assessment:** Song rubric.

**Answer key:** Song rubric (each 1–4): Lyrics (hook, imagery, prosody), Melody (motif, singable, cadences), Harmony (fitting progression, contrasting bridge), Form, Production (balance, panning, light reverb, no clipping), Lead sheet accuracy, Presentation and reflection (250 words). Proficient 21+/28.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Badminton or tennis strokes

**Prepare:** Get racquets (badminton or tennis), shuttlecocks or tennis balls, a flat wall, and a footwork ladder (or chalk one). Learn the basic grips: badminton forehand ('shake hands') and backhand (thumb on the flat side of the grip); tennis eastern or semi-western forehand.

**Watch for:**
- Students hit with a stiff wrist and arm only. Power comes from the legs, hip and shoulder rotation, then the forearm.
- Students stand facing the net on forehands and backhands. Turn sideways (shoulder to the net) to swing through.
- Students stop moving after hitting. Recover to the base position after every shot.

### Day 1 (Monday): Grip & forehand
*Student:* Practice grips and forehand against a wall (100 reps).

*How to teach:* Teach grips; the student hits 100 forehands against a wall.

- **Q:** Which part of the body starts a powerful forehand?
  **A:** The legs and hips rotate first, then the shoulders and arm.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Backhand
*Student:* Practice backhand technique.

*How to teach:* Teach backhand technique (one- or two-handed); practice against a wall.

- **Q:** Why turn your shoulders before a backhand?
  **A:** To load the body for a full swing and keep the racquet face square.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Overhead
*Student:* Practice clears and smashes.

*How to teach:* Practice overhead clears and smashes (badminton) or serves/overheads (tennis).

- **Q:** What is a badminton clear?
  **A:** A high, deep shot to the opponent's back court that buys time.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Footwork
*Student:* Do footwork ladder drills.

*How to teach:* Lead footwork ladder drills (in-in-out-out, lateral shuffles, crossover steps).

- **Q:** Why practice footwork?
  **A:** Getting to the shuttle/ball early and balanced makes every stroke better.

### Day 5 (Friday): Rally
*Student:* Rally for consistency and record longest rally.

*How to teach:* Rally for consistency and record the longest rally.

- **Q:** Name one adjustment to improve rally length.
  **A:** Hit at medium pace toward the center, keep eyes on the ball, and reset to ready position.

**Assessment:** Rally count.

**Answer key:** Rally count: record longest cooperative rally (wall or partner). Benchmarks — beginning: under 10; developing: 10–24; proficient: 25+. Technique checklist: correct grip, side-on stance, swing from low to high (tennis) or high contact (badminton clear), follow-through, recovery to base.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Hazardous conditions

**Prepare:** Read the handbook sections on night driving, rain, fog, skids and California hazards (wildfire smoke, high winds, mountain grades and chain controls). Check Caltrans QuickMap to show road conditions.

**Watch for:**
- Students use high beams in fog. High beams reflect off fog and reduce visibility; use low beams (and fog lights if equipped).
- Students brake hard when skidding. Ease off the gas and brake, steer gently where you want to go; with ABS, press firmly and steer, don't pump.
- Students think roads are most slippery in heavy rain. The first rain after a dry spell is often worst because oil and dust mix with the water.

### Day 1 (Monday): Night driving
*Student:* Learn headlight rules (30 min after sunset; when wipers are on) and high-beam use.

*How to teach:* The student learns headlight rules and high-beam courtesy.

- **Q:** When must you use headlights?
  **A:** From 30 minutes after sunset to 30 minutes before sunrise, when visibility is under 1,000 feet, and when using wipers continuously.
- **Q:** When must you dim your high beams?
  **A:** Within 500 feet of an oncoming vehicle and within 300 feet of a vehicle ahead of you.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rain & fog
*Student:* Learn about slippery first rain, hydroplaning and fog driving with low beams.

*How to teach:* The student learns about first rain, hydroplaning and fog driving.

- **Q:** Why is the first rain especially slippery?
  **A:** Oil and grime on the road mix with water, creating a slick film.
- **Q:** What should you do if you start hydroplaning?
  **A:** Ease off the gas, hold the wheel straight, and don't brake suddenly until the tires regain contact.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Skids
*Student:* Learn skid recovery: ease off pedals and steer where you want to go.

*How to teach:* The student learns skid recovery and explains ABS braking.

- **Q:** Your rear wheels skid to the right. Which way do you steer?
  **A:** Steer in the direction you want the front of the car to go (straight down your lane); ease off the pedals.
- **Q:** How do you brake hard with anti-lock brakes?
  **A:** Press firmly and hold; let ABS pulse; keep steering.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Wildfire smoke & wind
*Student:* Learn California-specific hazards: wildfire smoke, high winds and mountain driving.

*How to teach:* The student learns California-specific hazards: wildfire smoke, Santa Ana winds, mountain grades and chain controls.

- **Q:** On a narrow mountain road, which vehicle has the right-of-way when two cannot pass?
  **A:** The vehicle going uphill; the downhill vehicle backs up to a wider spot.
- **Q:** What should you do on a long, steep downgrade?
  **A:** Shift to a lower gear to let the engine slow the car and avoid overheating the brakes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Hazard scenario quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the hazard scenario quiz. Review.

- **Q:** You're driving in dense smoke and can barely see. What's the safest action?
  **A:** Slow down, use low beams, and if visibility is near zero, pull completely off the road, turn off lights except parking/hazards, and wait.

**Assessment:** Scenario quiz.

**Answer key:** Hazard scenario quiz key: 1) Headlights required — 30 minutes after sunset until 30 minutes before sunrise; when visibility is less than 1,000 feet; when windshield wipers are on continuously. 2) Dim high beams — within 500 feet of an oncoming vehicle and within 300 feet of a vehicle you're following. 3) Fog — slow down, low beams, increase following distance, pull off and stop if visibility is near zero. 4) Hydroplaning — ease off the gas, keep the wheel straight, don't brake hard. 5) Skid — ease off pedals, steer where you want to go. 6) Wildfire smoke — low beams, slow down, recirculate air, avoid closed roads, follow evacuation orders. 7) High wind — slow down, grip the wheel firmly, watch for debris and high-profile vehicles. 8) Mountain driving — use lower gear on long downgrades; uphill traffic has the right-of-way on narrow roads; carry chains when required. 9) Blinded by oncoming high beams — look toward the right edge of your lane.

## Computer Science Principles: Conditionals and Boolean logic

**Prepare:** Print a blank truth table and the AP reference sheet section on IF/ELSE and AND/OR/NOT. Prepare 15 Boolean expressions (examples in Day 1).

**Watch for:**
- Using AND where OR is needed (age < 13 AND age > 65 can never be true). Read the condition aloud with real numbers to test it.
- Overlapping or gapped ranges in if/else chains, e.g. age < 13 then age > 13, missing exactly 13. Test boundary values.
- Confusing = (assignment in JavaScript) with == / === (comparison). In pseudocode, = is comparison and ← is assignment.

### Day 1 (Monday): Booleans
*Student:* Evaluate 15 Boolean expressions using comparison and logical operators and build a truth table for AND/OR.

*How to teach:* Teach comparison operators (=, ≠, <, >, ≤, ≥) and AND/OR/NOT. Build the AND and OR truth tables together, then the student evaluates 15 expressions such as (4 < 9) AND (3 = 3).

- **Q:** Complete: true AND false = ?, true OR false = ?, NOT true = ?
  **A:** false, true, false.
- **Q:** Evaluate (10 > 5) AND NOT (3 > 1).
  **A:** true AND NOT true = true AND false = false.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): If-else
*Student:* Write a program that gives a different message for a user's age range.

*How to teach:* Model an age-range program: if age < 13 'kid', else if age < 18 'teen', else 'adult'. Have the student test 12, 13, 17, 18 to check the boundaries.

- **Q:** With the chain above, what prints for age 13? 18?
  **A:** 13 → 'teen'; 18 → 'adult'.
- **Q:** Why test boundary values like 13 and 18?
  **A:** Off-by-one errors (< vs ≤) show up at the edges of ranges.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Nested conditionals
*Student:* Build a 'Which Pet Should You Get?' quiz app that uses nested and compound conditionals.

*How to teach:* Build a 'Which Pet Should You Get?' quiz with dropdowns (e.g. space: small/large; time: little/lots). Use compound conditions like if (space == "large" && time == "lots") → dog. Check every combination gives an answer.

- **Q:** Two yes/no questions: how many combinations must your quiz handle?
  **A:** 4 (2 × 2).
- **Q:** Write a condition for 'small space OR little time' in JavaScript.
  **A:** space == "small" || time == "little"

### Day 4 (Thursday): Flowcharts
*Student:* Draw a flowchart for a movie-ticket pricing algorithm (age, day, student ID) before coding it, then implement it and compare code to chart.

*How to teach:* Draw a flowchart for movie-ticket pricing (e.g. under 12 or 65+ = $8; else student ID = $10; else $14; Tuesdays $2 off). Code it and compare the code's structure to the diamonds in the chart.

- **Q:** Using those rules, what does a 30-year-old with a student ID pay on Tuesday?
  **A:** $10 − $2 = $8.
- **Q:** What flowchart shape represents a decision?
  **A:** A diamond, with one arrow for yes/true and one for no/false.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Complete AP-style questions tracing conditionals in pseudocode.

*How to teach:* Give the 8-question conditionals quiz in the assessment key in AP pseudocode, and review the quiz app.

- **Q:** x ← 7. IF (x > 5 AND x < 10) DISPLAY "A" ELSE DISPLAY "B". Output?
  **A:** A.

**Assessment:** Conditionals quiz and quiz app.

**Answer key:** Conditionals quiz: 1) (5 > 3) AND (2 > 4) → false. 2) (5 > 3) OR (2 > 4) → true. 3) NOT (7 = 7) → false. 4) x ← 12; IF x MOD 2 = 0 display "even" ELSE "odd" → even. 5) age ← 15: IF age < 13 "child" ELSE IF age < 20 "teen" ELSE "adult" → teen. 6) a ← true, b ← false: (a OR b) AND NOT b → true. 7) Expression true only when t is between 60 and 80 inclusive: t ≥ 60 AND t ≤ 80. 8) Number of outputs from nested IF with 2 conditions → up to 4. Score 7–8 mastery. Quiz app checklist: at least 3 questions, uses nested and compound (AND/OR) conditions, every combination produces a result, tested with boundary answers.
