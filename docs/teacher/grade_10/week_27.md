# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 27

## English 10: Research paper: drafting and presenting

**Prepare:** Outline, notes, word processor, Purdue OWL MLA formatting guide. Plan a 5-minute presentation time with an audience.

**Watch for:**
- Paragraphs that are strings of quotes. Each body paragraph needs the student's analysis tying sources to the thesis.
- Works Cited errors: missing hanging indent, not alphabetized, or containing sources not cited in the paper.

### Day 1 (Monday): Draft body
*Student:* Draft body paragraphs with at least two sources per paragraph.

*How to teach:* Model a body paragraph that synthesizes two sources (topic sentence, source A, source B, analysis connecting them). The student drafts body paragraphs. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does 'synthesis' mean in a research paper?
  **A:** Combining information from multiple sources to make a point neither source makes alone.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Intro & conclusion
*Student:* Draft the introduction hook and conclusion that addresses 'so what?'

*How to teach:* Show hook types (striking fact, anecdote, question). The student drafts an introduction and a conclusion that answers 'So what?' Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What should a conclusion do besides restate the thesis?
  **A:** Explain the significance — why the findings matter today or what they suggest.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Revise & cite
*Student:* Revise, check every citation and build the Works Cited page.

*How to teach:* The student revises for logic and support, then checks every in-text citation against a Works Cited entry. Build the Works Cited page together using Purdue OWL. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How is a Works Cited page formatted?
  **A:** Titled 'Works Cited,' alphabetized by author's last name, double-spaced, with hanging indents.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Edit & submit
*Student:* Final edit for grammar and MLA format; submit the paper.

*How to teach:* The student does a final grammar edit (reading aloud backwards by sentence helps catch errors) and checks MLA format. Submit the paper and score with the research rubric. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What goes in the MLA header?
  **A:** Student name, teacher name, course, date — on separate lines at the top left.

### Day 5 (Friday): Present
*Student:* Give a 5-minute presentation of findings with Q&A.

*How to teach:* The student presents findings for 5 minutes with Q&A. Score with both rubrics. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How should a presenter use notes?
  **A:** Use brief cue cards with key points, not a script to read word for word.

**Assessment:** Research paper rubric and presentation rubric.

**Answer key:** Research paper rubric: Thesis and focus (4); Evidence — at least two sources per body paragraph, accurately cited (4); Analysis and synthesis (4); Organization and transitions (4); MLA format and Works Cited (4); Conventions (4). 20–24 = proficient. Presentation rubric: clear summary of findings, visual aid, 5 minutes, answers questions accurately (4-point).

## Geometry: Area of polygons and composite figures

**Prepare:** Graph paper, tape measure, calculator. For Day 4, choose a room and look up the price per square foot of a flooring option (e.g., laminate around $2–$5/ft²).

**Watch for:**
- Using the slant side instead of the height in parallelogram/triangle area. Height is perpendicular to the base.
- Forgetting to subtract cut-outs (or double-counting overlaps) in composite figures.

### Day 1 (Monday): Area review
*Student:* Review area formulas for triangles, parallelograms and trapezoids with derivations.

*How to teach:* Review derivations: parallelogram = rectangle rearranged; triangle = half a parallelogram; trapezoid = half a parallelogram made of two copies. The student derives each with sketches.

- **Q:** Find the area of a trapezoid with bases 8 and 12 and height 5.
  **A:** ½(8 + 12)·5 = 50.
- **Q:** A triangle has base 14 and area 63. Find the height.
  **A:** 63 = ½·14·h → h = 9.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Regular polygons
*Student:* Find the area of regular polygons using apothem and trig.

*How to teach:* Show area = ½·apothem·perimeter by splitting into n isosceles triangles; find the apothem with trig (a = s / (2 tan(180°/n))).

- **Q:** Find the area of a regular hexagon with side 6.
  **A:** Apothem 3√3; perimeter 36; area = ½·3√3·36 = 54√3 ≈ 93.5.
- **Q:** Find the area of a regular pentagon with side 10.
  **A:** a = 5/tan 36° ≈ 6.88; area ≈ ½·6.88·50 ≈ 172.0.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Composite figures
*Student:* Find areas of composite figures including circle parts.

*How to teach:* Split composite shapes into known pieces, adding or subtracting. The student finds areas of 6 figures with circle parts.

- **Q:** A 12 × 8 rectangle has a circular hole of radius 2. Find the remaining area.
  **A:** 96 − 4π ≈ 83.4.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Floor plan project
*Student:* Measure a room at home and compute flooring cost for a new design.

*How to teach:* The student measures a room, sketches it to scale, computes area, adds 10% for waste, and prices flooring.

- **Q:** A 12 ft × 15 ft room uses flooring at $3.50/ft² with 10% waste. What is the cost?
  **A:** 180 ft² × 1.1 = 198 ft²; 198 × 3.50 = $693.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on area.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and score the floor plan with the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** What is an apothem?
  **A:** The perpendicular segment from the center of a regular polygon to the midpoint of a side.

**Assessment:** Floor plan project and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Trapezoid bases 8 and 12, height 5 → 50. 2) Parallelogram base 9, height 4 → 36. 3) Regular hexagon side 6 → apothem 3√3, area 54√3 ≈ 93.5. 4) Square with a semicircle on one side (side 10) → 100 + 12.5π ≈ 139.3. 5) Rectangle 12 × 8 with a circle of radius 2 removed → 96 − 4π ≈ 83.4. Floor plan rubric: 4 = accurate scaled drawing with measurements, correct area including any irregular parts, waste allowance (~10%), cost with units; 3 = minor calculation error; 2 = missing waste or units, or drawing not to scale; 1 = incomplete.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Climate solutions

**Prepare:** Gather research sources: NREL or Department of Energy pages on solar and EVs, IEA or DOE pages on carbon capture, California Air Resources Board pages on cap-and-trade (renamed cap-and-invest when extended in 2025) and SB 32. Build the Unit 6 test from the key.

**Watch for:**
- Students think EVs produce zero emissions under all conditions. Their emissions depend on how electricity is generated and on battery manufacturing, though lifetime emissions are generally lower than gasoline cars.
- Students think one solution will solve climate change. Use the trade-off matrix to show that combinations are needed.
- Students confuse SB 32 targets with AB 32. AB 32 (2006): return to 1990 levels by 2020. SB 32 (2016): 40% below 1990 levels by 2030.

### Day 1 (Monday): Solutions survey
*Student:* Research three solutions (solar, EVs, carbon capture) and their chemistry.

*How to teach:* The student researches solar PV, EVs and carbon capture, filling a table: how it works chemically, CO₂ reduced, cost, limitations. Check that chemistry is explained (e.g., electrons freed in silicon by light).

- **Q:** How does a solar cell produce electricity?
  **A:** Photons give energy to electrons in a silicon semiconductor; an internal electric field at the p-n junction pushes them through a circuit.
- **Q:** How does a common carbon-capture method trap CO₂?
  **A:** Flue gas passes through an amine solution that chemically binds CO₂; heating later releases pure CO₂ for storage underground.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Trade-off matrix
*Student:* Build a decision matrix weighing cost, CO₂ reduction and feasibility.

*How to teach:* Build a decision matrix: rows = solutions, columns = criteria (cost, CO₂ reduction, feasibility, time to deploy), weighted by importance. The student scores each and totals.

- **Q:** Why weight criteria differently?
  **A:** Some criteria matter more to the decision-maker (e.g., CO₂ reduction may count double), so weights reflect priorities.
- **Q:** What is a trade-off?
  **A:** Giving up some of one desirable quality to gain another (e.g., higher cost for greater CO₂ reduction).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Policy connection
*Student:* Read about California's cap-and-trade program and SB 32 goals.

*How to teach:* Read about California's cap-and-trade program (started 2013, extended in 2025 as cap-and-invest) and the SB 32 goal. The student summarizes how a cap works and lists one argument for and one against.

- **Q:** What does SB 32 require?
  **A:** That California cut greenhouse gas emissions to 40% below 1990 levels by 2030.
- **Q:** How does cap-and-trade reduce emissions?
  **A:** The state sets a declining cap on total emissions and issues allowances; companies that cut emissions cheaply can sell spare allowances, so reductions happen where cheapest.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Position brief
*Student:* Write a 1-page brief recommending one solution for your city.

*How to teach:* The student writes a 1-page brief recommending one solution for the city, with evidence and trade-offs. Score with the rubric; revise once.

- **Q:** What should the brief's recommendation sentence include?
  **A:** The specific action, who should do it, and the main benefit (e.g., 'The city should install solar panels on all public buildings by 2030 to cut X tons of CO₂ per year').

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 6 test
*Student:* Unit 6 test on climate chemistry.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test. Grade together.

- **Q:** What is the chemistry behind an EV battery's charge and discharge?
  **A:** Lithium ions move from the anode to the cathode through the electrolyte while electrons flow through the circuit (discharge); charging reverses the flow.

**Assessment:** Position brief and unit test.

**Answer key:** Position brief rubric: 4 = Clear recommendation for the student's city; explains the chemistry (e.g., photovoltaic silicon converting light to electricity; lithium-ion batteries moving Li⁺ ions; amines binding CO₂); uses data (cost, tons of CO₂ avoided); acknowledges trade-offs; cites ≥3 sources. Unit 6 test key (sample): 1) Albedo of Earth ≈ 0.3. 2) Why CO₂ absorbs IR — vibrations change its dipole. 3) 1 ppm CO₂ ≈ 2.13 GtC; adding 4.26 GtC raises CO₂ by 2 ppm. 4) Largest active reservoir — deep ocean. 5) Ice-albedo feedback is positive. 6) SB 32 target — 40% below 1990 by 2030. 7) Cap-and-trade sets a declining limit on total emissions and lets companies buy/sell allowances. 8) One trade-off of carbon capture — high energy cost and expense. 9) The Keeling Curve's seasonal cycle comes from Northern Hemisphere photosynthesis. 10) Mitigation vs. adaptation: mitigation reduces emissions; adaptation copes with impacts.

## Modern World History: Decolonization in South Asia, Africa and the Middle East

**Prepare:** Gandhi's letter to Lord Irwin (March 2, 1930), Nehru's 'Tryst with Destiny' (August 14–15, 1947), Nkrumah's independence speech (March 6, 1957), the Balfour Declaration (November 2, 1917), apartheid law summaries.

*How to teach:* Read Gandhi's letter to Lord Irwin and Nehru's 'Tryst with Destiny.' The student analyzes nonviolent strategy and partition's consequences. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Africa
*Student:* Analyze Nkrumah's 1957 speech and map African independence dates.

*How to teach:* Read Nkrumah's 1957 speech. The student maps African independence dates by decade. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Why is 1960 called the 'Year of Africa'?
  **A:** Seventeen African nations gained independence.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Middle East
*Student:* Read the Balfour Declaration and explain how the Holocaust affected support for Israel; study Arab nationalism.

*How to teach:* Read the Balfour Declaration. Discuss how the Holocaust increased support for Israel and the rise of Arab nationalism. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

  **A:** It increased international support for a Jewish state.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Apartheid
*Student:* Analyze apartheid laws and Mandela's struggle.

*How to teach:* Read apartheid laws summaries. The student traces Mandela's struggle from the ANC to prison to presidency. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on decolonization.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key without notes (about 20 minutes). Grade it together; for each missed item the student finds the answer in their notes and writes a one-sentence correction.

- **Q:** Who led Ghana to independence?
  **A:** Kwame Nkrumah.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz (answers): 1) Salt March → 1930, Gandhi's 240-mile march to protest the salt tax. 2) Indian independence → August 15, 1947, with partition into India and Pakistan. 3) Partition → about 1 million killed, 10–15 million displaced. 4) Ghana → first sub-Saharan colony to gain independence (1957), led by Nkrumah. 5) Balfour Declaration → British support for 'a national home for the Jewish people' in Palestine while protecting non-Jewish communities' rights. 6) Israel → founded May 14, 1948. 7) Apartheid laws → Population Registration Act, Group Areas Act, pass laws. 8) Mandela → imprisoned 1964–1990, president 1994.

## Spanish III: La tecnología

**Prepare:** Find a Spanish article on teens and social media in Spain (e.g., from El País, 20minutos, or a government INE/INJUVE survey summary). Prepare a family tech-use survey and graph paper or a spreadsheet.

**Watch for:**
- Using English tech words with English grammar: Spanish verbs are often adapted (chatear, tuitear, descargar, subir una foto) and conjugated regularly.
- Confusing 'la red' (network/the internet) and 'la red social' (a social network); 'la contraseña' = password, not 'el pase'.

### Day 1 (Monday): Vocabulario
*Student:* Learn technology vocabulary.

*How to teach:* Teach 20 technology words; your child labels a screenshot of a phone and a computer.

- **Q:** Translate: 'I downloaded an app and created a password.'
  **A:** Descargué una aplicación y creé una contraseña.
- **Q:** What does 'subir una foto' mean?
  **A:** To upload (post) a photo.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Encuesta
*Student:* Survey family on tech use and graph the results with Spanish labels.

*How to teach:* Your child surveys family members (¿Cuántas horas usas el celular al día? ¿Qué aplicación usas más?) and graphs results with Spanish labels.

- **Q:** Report: 'Most of my family uses their phone three hours a day.'
  **A:** La mayoría de mi familia usa el celular tres horas al día.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Lectura
*Student:* Read an article on teen social media use in Spain.

*How to teach:* Read the article; your child summarizes the main statistics and the author's concern.

- **Q:** What reading strategy helps with statistics in Spanish?
  **A:** Look for numbers with por ciento, la mitad, la mayoría, uno de cada… and note what each refers to; remember Spain writes decimals with commas (2,5 = 2.5).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Debate
*Student:* Debate: ¿Las redes sociales nos unen o nos separan?

*How to teach:* Debate: ¿Las redes sociales nos unen o nos separan? Your child argues one side, then the other.

- **Q:** Give an argument that social networks unite us.
  **A:** Example: Las redes sociales nos unen porque podemos hablar con familiares que viven lejos.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the vocabulary quiz and score the debate.

- **Q:** Translate: 'Don't share your password.'
  **A:** No compartas tu contraseña.

**Assessment:** Quiz and debate.

**Answer key:** Vocabulary quiz sample key: la red social = social network; descargar = to download; subir = to upload; la aplicación = app; la contraseña = password; el enlace = link; la pantalla = screen; el teclado = keyboard; guardar = to save; borrar = to delete. Debate rubric: 4 = clear position, 3 reasons with evidence, rebuttal, uses opinion phrases and subjunctive (No creo que..., Es importante que...); 3 = minor gaps; 2 = few reasons; 1 = cannot sustain.

## Music & Media Arts: Film language

**Prepare:** Clips: Casablanca (1942) airport scene; a modern dialogue scene; the Odessa Steps sequence from Battleship Potemkin (1925; parental note: depicts violence against civilians, no gore by modern standards — preview). Storyboard template with 9 panels. Quiz stills.

**Watch for:**
- Students describe shots by camera distance only. Combine shot size (wide, medium, close-up) with angle (high, low, eye-level, Dutch).
- Students think editing just removes mistakes. Editing creates meaning, rhythm and controls time.
- Students storyboard art-perfect drawings. Stick figures are fine; clarity of shot type and angle matters.

### Day 1 (Monday): Shot types
*Student:* Watch a 3-minute scene from a classic film (e.g., Casablanca airport scene) and log each shot as wide, medium, close-up or extreme close-up.

*How to teach:* Log each shot of a 3-minute classic scene as wide, medium, close-up or extreme close-up.

- **Q:** Why use a close-up?
  **A:** To show emotion and focus attention on a face or detail.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Camera angles
*Student:* Photograph the same object at high, low, eye-level and Dutch angles and write what feeling each angle creates.

*How to teach:* Photograph an object at high, low, eye-level and Dutch angles and write the feeling each creates.

- **Q:** What does a low-angle shot usually suggest?
  **A:** Power or dominance of the subject.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Editing
*Student:* Compare continuity editing in a dialogue scene with Eisenstein's Odessa Steps montage and list how each controls time.

*How to teach:* Compare continuity editing in a dialogue scene with the Odessa Steps montage.

- **Q:** How does Eisenstein's montage treat time?
  **A:** It expands time, cutting many shots together so the event feels longer and more intense than real time.
- **Q:** What is the 180-degree rule?
  **A:** Keep the camera on one side of an imaginary line between characters so screen directions stay consistent.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Storyboard
*Student:* Draw a 9-panel storyboard for a 30-second scene with shot type, angle and sound noted under each panel.

*How to teach:* Draw a 9-panel storyboard for a 30-second scene with shot type, angle and sound.

- **Q:** Why note sound in a storyboard?
  **A:** Sound shapes meaning and must be planned for recording.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz identifying shot types and edits in stills, then revise the storyboard.

*How to teach:* Take a quiz identifying shot types and edits, then revise the storyboard.

- **Q:** What's a Dutch angle?
  **A:** A tilted camera angle that creates unease or disorientation.

**Assessment:** Quiz and storyboard.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: identify wide/establishing, medium, close-up, extreme close-up; high angle (subject looks small/vulnerable), low angle (powerful), eye level (neutral), Dutch/canted (unease); cut, match cut, cross-cutting, montage, shot/reverse shot, 180-degree rule (keep camera on one side of the action line). Storyboard rubric: 9 panels, shot type/angle/sound noted, clear 30-second story, logical continuity.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Tumbling fundamentals

**Prepare:** Use thick gymnastics mats or several stacked exercise mats on a soft surface, clear space with no hard edges nearby, and a wall for headstand prep. You serve as spotter; learn spotting positions from a reputable gymnastics video. Skip headstands if the student has neck problems.

**Watch for:**
- Students roll onto the top of their head. Tuck the chin to the chest so the back of the neck and shoulders contact the mat.
- Students land jumps with straight legs. Land on the balls of the feet, knees bent and aligned over toes, arms forward for balance.
- Students rush to harder skills. Master progressions (log roll → forward roll down an incline → flat forward roll).

### Day 1 (Monday): Safety & landings
*Student:* Learn safe landing positions, mat setup and spotting roles for tumbling.

*How to teach:* Teach safe landings, mat setup, spotting roles and progressions.

- **Q:** What's the safest landing position from a jump?
  **A:** Feet shoulder-width, knees bent and over toes, hips back, arms forward.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rolls
*Student:* Practice forward and backward rolls (and log roll progressions) 10 times each with a spotter.

*How to teach:* Practice forward and backward rolls 10 times each with a spotter, using log-roll progressions as needed.

- **Q:** Why tuck the chin in a forward roll?
  **A:** To roll on the back of the neck and shoulders and protect the head and neck.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Balances
*Student:* Practice tripod balance, headstand prep against a wall and partner balances.

*How to teach:* Practice tripod balance, wall headstand prep and partner balances.

- **Q:** What shape do your hands and head make in a tripod balance?
  **A:** A triangle, giving a wide base of support.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Center of gravity
*Student:* Explain with diagrams how a wide base and low center of gravity improve balance; test it with a push test.

*How to teach:* Explain base of support and center of gravity with diagrams; test with a gentle push test in narrow vs. wide stance.

- **Q:** Why is a wide, low stance harder to push over?
  **A:** The base of support is larger and the center of gravity lower, so it must be pushed farther before it moves outside the base.

### Day 5 (Friday): Skill check
*Student:* Demonstrate a roll, a balance and a jump with landing for a skill checklist.

*How to teach:* The student demonstrates a roll, a balance and a jump; score with the checklist.

- **Q:** When does a balance fail, in terms of physics?
  **A:** When the center of gravity moves outside the base of support.

**Assessment:** Skill checklist.

**Answer key:** Skill checklist: forward roll (hands shoulder-width, chin tucked, rounded back, rises to feet without hands), backward roll (or progression), tripod balance held 5 seconds, wall headstand prep held 5 seconds with control, jump with safe landing (bent knees, arms forward, stick 2 seconds). Proficient = all correct with control. Concept: balance improves with a wide base of support and low center of gravity, with the center of gravity over the base.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Alcohol, nicotine and vaping

**Prepare:** Read NIDA for Teens articles on alcohol, nicotine and vaping. Find two vape or e-cigarette ads (search older ad archives such as Stanford Research into the Impact of Tobacco Advertising). Parental note: discuss your family's expectations about substances.

**Watch for:**
- Students think vapes are just water vapor. E-cigarette aerosol contains nicotine, ultrafine particles, flavoring chemicals and metals such as nickel and lead.
- Students think beer or wine is safer than liquor. A standard drink of each (12 oz beer, 5 oz wine, 1.5 oz liquor) contains about the same alcohol (about 0.6 oz).
- Students think it's best to let a very drunk friend 'sleep it off'. Signs of alcohol poisoning require calling 911; BAC can keep rising after drinking stops.

### Day 1 (Monday): Alcohol
*Student:* Learn how alcohol affects the brain and body, BAC factors, and signs of alcohol poisoning (call 911).

*How to teach:* The student learns alcohol's effects, BAC factors and the signs of alcohol poisoning.

- **Q:** Name three signs of alcohol poisoning.
  **A:** Can't be awakened, vomiting while passed out, slow or irregular breathing, blue/pale skin, seizures, low body temperature.
- **Q:** What should you do?
  **A:** Call 911, turn them on their side to prevent choking, stay with them; California's medical amnesty law gives some protection to people under 21 who call for help.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Nicotine & vaping
*Student:* Read NIDA information on nicotine addiction and vaping ingredients; analyze a vape ad for targeting of teens.

*How to teach:* The student reads NIDA articles on nicotine and vaping and analyzes a vape ad for techniques that target teens (flavors, colors, influencers, freedom/rebellion themes).

- **Q:** Why do flavors matter in vape marketing?
  **A:** Sweet and fruity flavors appeal to young people and mask harshness, making it easier to start.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Teen brain
*Student:* Explain why the developing adolescent brain is more vulnerable to addiction.

*How to teach:* The student explains, with a simple brain diagram, why the adolescent brain is more vulnerable to addiction.

- **Q:** Why is the teen brain more vulnerable to addiction?
  **A:** The reward system is very sensitive while the prefrontal cortex (judgment, impulse control) is still developing until the mid-20s; substances can rewire developing circuits.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Influences
*Student:* List peer, family, media and advertising influences on substance use and rate their strength.

*How to teach:* The student lists peer, family, media and advertising influences and rates their strength for themselves.

- **Q:** Give an example of a protective influence.
  **A:** Friends who don't use, clear family expectations, sports or activities, accurate information.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Quiz on alcohol and nicotine effects.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Review.

- **Q:** What's the legal age to buy tobacco or vape products in California?
  **A:** 21.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Alcohol is a depressant that slows the central nervous system. 2) BAC factors — amount and speed of drinking, body size, sex, food in stomach, medications. 3) Alcohol poisoning signs — confusion, vomiting, seizures, slow or irregular breathing, blue or pale skin, low body temperature, can't be woken: call 911, keep them on their side, don't leave them. 4) Nicotine is highly addictive and harms the developing brain (attention, learning, impulse control). 5) One JUUL-type pod may contain about as much nicotine as a pack of cigarettes. 6) Vape aerosol contains nicotine, heavy metals, ultrafine particles and flavoring chemicals. 7) The prefrontal cortex matures into the mid-20s, so teens are more vulnerable to addiction. 8) Legal age for tobacco/vapes and alcohol in California — 21. 9) Standard drink — 12 oz beer, 5 oz wine, 1.5 oz distilled spirits. 10) Influences — peers, family, media and advertising.

## Computer Science Principles: Privacy and personal data

**Prepare:** Preview the student's browser privacy settings and a tracker visualizer (e.g. the browser's built-in privacy report or a tracker-blocker extension). Read the California AG's CCPA overview (oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa).

**Watch for:**
- 'I have nothing to hide.' Privacy protects against identity theft, discrimination, manipulation and stalking, not just hiding wrongdoing.
- Thinking deleting a post removes it everywhere. Copies, screenshots and backups may remain.
- Thinking a single harmless data point is safe. Combining many pieces of PII (birthdate + ZIP + gender) can identify a person.

### Day 1 (Monday): PII inventory
*Student:* List the personally identifiable information you have shared with apps and websites and rate the risk of each item.

*How to teach:* The student lists PII they've shared (name, birthdate, address, school, photos, location, phone) and rates each risk low/medium/high with a reason.

- **Q:** What is PII?
  **A:** Personally identifiable information — data that can identify a specific person, such as name, address, phone number, birthdate, Social Security number or biometric data.
- **Q:** Why is birthdate + ZIP code + gender risky together?
  **A:** Combined, they can uniquely identify most people, even without a name.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tracking
*Student:* Inspect cookies and site permissions in your browser, then use a tracker-visualizer extension or privacy report to count third-party trackers on a news site.

*How to teach:* Inspect cookies (browser settings → site data) and site permissions; use a tracker visualizer on a news site and count third-party trackers.

- **Q:** What's the difference between a first-party and a third-party cookie?
  **A:** First-party cookies come from the site you're visiting (e.g. keeping you logged in); third-party cookies come from other domains, often advertisers, and can track you across sites.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Privacy policies
*Student:* Read the privacy policy of an app you use and summarize what data it collects, who it shares with, and how to delete it.

*How to teach:* Read a privacy policy for an app the student uses and summarize: data collected, who it's shared with, how to delete it.

- **Q:** What three things should you look for in a privacy policy?
  **A:** What data is collected, who it's shared with or sold to, and how you can access or delete it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): California privacy law
*Student:* Learn the rights the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA/CPRA) gives residents and draft a data-deletion request email.

*How to teach:* Learn CCPA/CPRA rights (right to know, delete, correct, opt out of sale/sharing, limit use of sensitive data, non-discrimination). Draft a deletion request email.

- **Q:** Name three rights the CCPA/CPRA gives California residents.
