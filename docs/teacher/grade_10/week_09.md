# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 9

## English 10: Shakespeare's Rome: Julius Caesar Act 1

**Prepare:** Folger Julius Caesar (free online at folger.edu with audio), short reading on Caesar and the Roman Republic.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the play is mainly about Caesar. He dies in Act 3; the focus is Brutus and the conspiracy.
- Reading line by line rather than sentence by sentence. Teach reading to the punctuation, not to the line end.

### Day 1 (Monday): Context
*Student:* Read a short history of Julius Caesar and the Roman Republic and note why senators feared him.

*How to teach:* Read a short history of Caesar (crossing the Rubicon, dictator for life). The student notes why senators feared him. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Why did many senators fear Julius Caesar?
  **A:** He had gained enormous power — named dictator for life — and they feared he would become king and end the Republic.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Iambic pentameter
*Student:* Scan five lines of blank verse, marking stressed syllables, and notice when prose is used instead.

*How to teach:* Teach iambic pentameter (five da-DUM feet). The student scans five lines and notices commoners speak prose. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Scan: 'The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars.'
  **A:** the FAULT / dear BRU / tus IS / not IN / our STARS — five iambs (some readers stress 'not' instead of 'is').
- **Q:** Who speaks in prose in 1.1, and why?
  **A:** The commoners (cobbler, carpenter) — prose often marks lower-status characters or casual speech.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Act 1 scenes 1–2
*Student:* Read 1.1–1.2 with the Folger audio and chart how Cassius manipulates Brutus.

*How to teach:* Listen to 1.1–1.2 with Folger audio. The student charts Cassius's manipulation of Brutus. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Explain 'The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings.'
  **A:** Cassius says their low position is their own fault, not fate — implying they can act to change it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Act 1 scene 3
*Student:* Read 1.3 and list omens and their effect on mood.

*How to teach:* Read 1.3. The student lists omens and their effect on mood. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How do the omens affect the mood?
  **A:** They create fear and unease, suggesting the natural order is disturbed and something terrible is coming.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Act 1 quiz and journal: Is Cassius a patriot or a schemer?

*How to teach:* Give the Act 1 quiz and assign the journal on Cassius. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does Caesar say about Cassius's 'lean and hungry look'?
  **A:** Such men think too much and are dangerous; Caesar distrusts him.

**Assessment:** Act 1 quiz; scansion practice.

**Answer key:** Act 1 quiz (answers): 1) Why do Flavius and Marullus scold the commoners? → For celebrating Caesar's triumph over Pompey's sons when they once cheered Pompey. 2) What does the Soothsayer say? → 'Beware the ides of March.' 3) What does Casca report? → Antony offered Caesar a crown three times; he refused each time, then fell in an epileptic fit. 4) Cassius's 'fault... is not in our stars' means → people, not fate, are responsible for being underlings. 5) How does Cassius plan to sway Brutus? → by throwing forged letters from 'citizens' into his window. 6) Two omens in 1.3 → a slave's hand burning without harm, a lion near the Capitol, an owl hooting at noon, men on fire walking the streets. Scansion: full credit for correct stress marks on five lines (da-DUM ×5).

## Geometry: Proving theorems about lines and angles

**Prepare:** GeoGebra and protractor. Make a theorem reference sheet listing postulates the student may cite: Linear Pair Postulate, Corresponding Angles Postulate, and the definitions of congruent and supplementary angles.

**Watch for:**
- Citing a theorem's converse when using the theorem (e.g., using 'if lines are parallel...' to prove lines parallel). Ask, 'What do you know, and what are you trying to prove?'
- Assuming all angle pairs formed by a transversal are congruent; consecutive (same-side) interior angles are supplementary.

### Day 1 (Monday): Vertical angles proof
*Student:* Write a two-column proof that vertical angles are congruent.

*How to teach:* Label two intersecting lines with ∠1–∠4. Model the proof statement by statement, then have the student write it again from a blank page.

- **Q:** What postulate gives m∠1 + m∠2 = 180° when they form a linear pair?
  **A:** The Linear Pair Postulate (angles that form a linear pair are supplementary).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Transversals
*Student:* Explore angle pairs formed by parallel lines and a transversal in GeoGebra and record conjectures.

*How to teach:* In GeoGebra draw two parallel lines and a transversal; measure all 8 angles and drag the transversal. The student records which pairs stay congruent and which stay supplementary.

- **Q:** Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal; one angle is 65°. Name the measure of its alternate interior, corresponding, and same-side interior angles.
  **A:** Alternate interior 65°, corresponding 65°, same-side interior 115°.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Parallel line proofs
*Student:* Prove alternate interior angles congruent from the corresponding angles postulate.

*How to teach:* Prove alternate interior angles congruent using the corresponding angles postulate and vertical angles. The student then proves same-side interior angles are supplementary.

- **Q:** Outline why same-side interior angles are supplementary.
  **A:** One of them forms a linear pair with an angle that is congruent (alternate interior) to the other, so their measures add to 180°.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Converses
*Student:* Use converses to prove lines parallel and solve algebraic angle problems.

*How to teach:* Explain converses: congruent corresponding, alternate interior, or alternate exterior angles (or supplementary same-side interior) prove lines parallel. Solve algebra problems where the student finds x that makes lines parallel.

- **Q:** Alternate interior angles are (3x + 10)° and (5x − 30)°. What x makes the lines parallel, and what is each angle?
  **A:** 3x + 10 = 5x − 30 → x = 20; each angle is 70°.
- **Q:** Same-side interior angles are (2x)° and (x + 30)°. For what x are the lines parallel?
  **A:** 3x + 30 = 180 → x = 50.

### Day 5 (Friday): Proof quiz
*Student:* Write two proofs independently for a quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the two-proof quiz from the assessment key; the student works independently with the theorem reference sheet. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a postulate and a theorem?
  **A:** A postulate is accepted without proof; a theorem is proven from definitions, postulates and earlier theorems.

**Assessment:** Proof quiz.

**Answer key:** Proof quiz: Proof 1 (vertical angles congruent) — Statements/reasons: ∠1 and ∠2 are a linear pair, ∠2 and ∠3 are a linear pair (definition/diagram); m∠1 + m∠2 = 180, m∠2 + m∠3 = 180 (Linear Pair Postulate); m∠1 + m∠2 = m∠2 + m∠3 (Substitution); m∠1 = m∠3 (Subtraction); ∠1 ≅ ∠3 (def. of congruent). Proof 2 (lines parallel ⇒ alternate interior congruent) — ℓ ∥ m (Given); ∠1 ≅ ∠5 corresponding (Corresponding Angles Postulate); ∠5 ≅ ∠3 vertical (Vertical Angles Theorem); ∠1 ≅ ∠3 (Transitive). Rubric each proof: 4 = every statement valid with correct reason, logical order; 3 = one missing/incorrect reason; 2 = key step missing; 1 = statements without reasons.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Stars forge elements

**Prepare:** Bookmark NASA's 'Star Life Cycle' and 'The Sun' pages and print a periodic table color-coded by origin (search 'Jennifer Johnson nucleosynthesis periodic table' by NASA/SDSS) to check the student's version. Gather colored pencils.

**Watch for:**
- Students think stars 'burn' like fire. Stars release energy through nuclear fusion, not chemical combustion; there is no oxygen burning.
- Students think all elements are made in supernovae. Hydrogen and helium come from the Big Bang; carbon and nitrogen largely come from dying low-mass stars; elements up to iron form by fusion in massive stars; many heavy elements like gold come from neutron-star mergers.
- Students think the Sun will become a black hole. The Sun is too small; it will become a red giant, then a white dwarf.

### Day 1 (Monday): Fusion in the Sun
*Student:* Model the proton-proton chain and explain how mass converts to energy.

*How to teach:* Model the proton-proton chain simply: four hydrogen nuclei (protons) eventually fuse into one helium-4 nucleus, releasing positrons, neutrinos and energy. The products have about 0.7% less mass than the reactants; that mass becomes energy (E = mc²). The student draws a flow diagram.

- **Q:** Four hydrogen atoms have a mass of 4.0313 u; one helium-4 atom is 4.0026 u. What fraction of the mass is converted to energy?
  **A:** (4.0313 − 4.0026) ÷ 4.0313 = 0.0287 ÷ 4.0313 ≈ 0.0071, about 0.7%.
- **Q:** Why does fusion require the extreme temperature of the Sun's core (~15 million K)?
  **A:** Nuclei are positively charged and repel; only at very high speeds (temperature) and pressure can they get close enough for the strong force to fuse them.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Life cycle of stars
*Student:* Create a star life-cycle diagram for a Sun-like star and a massive star.

*How to teach:* Using NASA resources, the student draws two branching paths: a Sun-like star (nebula → protostar → main sequence → red giant → planetary nebula → white dwarf) and a massive star (… → red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole). Label what fuses in each stage.

- **Q:** What determines whether a star ends as a white dwarf or a supernova?
  **A:** Its mass; stars more than about 8 times the Sun's mass end in supernovae.
- **Q:** Where is the Sun in its life cycle?
  **A:** On the main sequence, about halfway through its roughly 10-billion-year lifetime.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Where elements come from
*Student:* Color-code a periodic table by element origin (Big Bang, stars, supernovae, mergers).

*How to teach:* The student color-codes a blank periodic table by origin: Big Bang (H, He, some Li), low-mass dying stars (e.g., C, N, many heavy elements via slow neutron capture such as barium and lead), massive stars and supernovae (O through Fe and others), neutron-star mergers (e.g., gold, platinum), and human-made (Tc, elements beyond U). Compare to the NASA version.

- **Q:** Where did the iron in your blood come from?
  **A:** Fusion and supernova explosions of stars (especially massive stars and white-dwarf supernovae).
- **Q:** Which two elements were made mainly in the Big Bang?
  **A:** Hydrogen and helium.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Communicate
*Student:* Write a 1-page explainer 'You are made of stardust' using evidence.

*How to teach:* The student writes the 1-page explainer 'You are made of stardust', using at least three specific elements in the human body (oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, calcium, iron) and their origins. Score with the rubric and have the student revise once.

- **Q:** Is the hydrogen in water molecules in your body 'stardust'? Explain.
  **A:** Not exactly: hydrogen formed in the Big Bang, before stars; most other elements in the body were formed in stars.
- **Q:** What evidence tells astronomers which elements are in distant stars?
  **A:** Their absorption/emission spectra — each element produces a unique set of lines.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 test
*Student:* Unit 2 test on atoms, periodic table, nuclear processes and stars.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test (50 minutes) with a periodic table. Grade together and log errors for review.

- **Q:** Write the configuration of sulfur and state its valence electrons.
  **A:** 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁴; 6 valence electrons.

**Assessment:** Explainer and Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Explainer rubric (4 levels): 4 = Explains that hydrogen in our bodies formed in the Big Bang, carbon/oxygen/nitrogen formed by fusion in stars and were spread by stellar winds and supernovae, iron is the endpoint of fusion in massive stars, and heavier elements (e.g., gold, iodine) form in supernovae and neutron-star mergers; cites at least two pieces of evidence (stellar spectra showing elements, solar fusion, observed 2017 neutron-star merger GW170817 with heavy-element signatures). Unit 2 test key (sample): 1) ²³⁹₉₄Pu alpha decay → ²³⁵₉₂U + ⁴₂He. 2) Half-lives in 15 days for a 5-day isotope: 3; fraction left 1/8. 3) Fe configuration [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶. 4) Larger radius: Ca or Br — Ca. 5) Ions: Mg²⁺ has 12 p, 10 e. 6) Average mass of Ga (60.1% Ga-69 at 68.93; 39.9% Ga-71 at 70.92) = 69.72. 7) The Sun's energy source: fusion of hydrogen into helium. 8) Fate of a star 20× the Sun's mass: supernova, then neutron star or black hole. 9) Why fusion stops at iron: fusing iron absorbs rather than releases energy. 10) Line spectra show which elements are in stars.

## Modern World History: Motives and methods of New Imperialism

**Prepare:** A blank world map, colored pencils, a 1914 reference map (Khan Academy or an atlas). Kipling's 'The White Man's Burden' (1899) and Henry Labouchère's 'The Brown Man's Burden' (1899). Three imperialist cartoons (e.g., 'The Rhodes Colossus').

**Watch for:**
- Seeing imperialism as only economic. Motives included nationalism, Social Darwinism, religion, and strategic bases.
- Thinking all of Africa was colonized by 1914. Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent.

### Day 1 (Monday): Motives
*Student:* Sort motives (raw materials, markets, nationalism, Social Darwinism, missionary) with examples.

*How to teach:* Give the student cards with examples (rubber in the Congo, Suez Canal, 'civilizing mission,' missionaries, coaling stations). They sort them under raw materials, markets, nationalism, Social Darwinism, and missionary/religious motives.

- **Q:** Why did industrial nations want colonies for raw materials?
  **A:** Factories needed rubber, cotton, palm oil, minerals and other resources not available at home.
- **Q:** What is Social Darwinism?
  **A:** A false application of 'survival of the fittest' to societies and races, used to justify domination of other peoples.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Map empires
*Student:* Color a 1914 world map showing British, French, German, Belgian, Dutch, Russian, Japanese and U.S. holdings.

*How to teach:* The student colors a 1914 map by empire using a reference map and adds a legend. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Which two African countries were independent in 1914?
  **A:** Ethiopia and Liberia.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Kipling & critics
*Student:* Read 'The White Man's Burden' and a response such as 'The Brown Man's Burden'; analyze perspectives.

*How to teach:* Read 'The White Man's Burden' and 'The Brown Man's Burden.' The student sources each and analyzes the perspectives. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What does Kipling mean by 'the White Man's burden'?
  **A:** The claimed duty of white Westerners to 'civilize' colonized peoples — a paternalistic, racist justification for empire.
- **Q:** How does 'The Brown Man's Burden' respond?
  **A:** It satirizes Kipling, exposing the violence and greed behind imperialism and the burden placed on colonized people.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cartoons
*Student:* Analyze three imperialist cartoons with a cartoon analysis worksheet.

*How to teach:* Model the cartoon analysis worksheet (symbols, labels, exaggeration, message, point of view) on one cartoon. The student analyzes three. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** In 'The Rhodes Colossus,' what does Rhodes straddling Africa represent?
  **A:** British ambition to control Africa from Cape Town to Cairo.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on motives and geography.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key and check the map. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Why did Belgium's King Leopold II want the Congo?
  **A:** For its wealth — especially ivory and later rubber.

**Assessment:** Map and quiz.

**Answer key:** Map key: Britain — India, Canada, Australia, Egypt, South Africa, Nigeria, Kenya, Malaya, Burma; France — Algeria, French West Africa, Madagascar, Indochina; Germany — Togo, Cameroon, German Southwest Africa (Namibia), German East Africa (Tanzania); Belgium — Congo; Netherlands — Dutch East Indies (Indonesia); Russia — Central Asia, Siberia; Japan — Korea, Taiwan; U.S. — Philippines, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, Guam. Quiz (answers): 1) Social Darwinism → the false idea that 'survival of the fittest' applied to nations and races, used to justify conquest. 2) Two economic motives → raw materials, new markets. 3) Independent African nations in 1914 → Ethiopia, Liberia. 4) Kipling's purpose → urge the U.S. to colonize the Philippines as a 'civilizing' duty. 5) Rhodes Colossus → Cecil Rhodes straddling Africa from Cape to Cairo.

## Spanish III: El futuro

- Using the future for immediate plans only: in conversation ir a + infinitive is more common for near plans; the future also expresses probability (¿Qué hora será? = I wonder what time it is).

## PE 10 (Course 2): Falls, stances and blocks

**Prepare:** Use an exercise mat (or a grassy area) and pool noodles. Partner drills must be slow and controlled. Have a phone to record skill videos.

**Watch for:**
- Students reach a hand back to break a fall. Arms straight back can break a wrist; tuck the chin and slap the mat with arms at about 45° to spread force.
- Students block with stiff, extended arms. Blocks use bent arms close to the body, with the forearm meeting the strike.
- Students strike with a closed fist. Palm-heel strikes reduce hand injuries and use the strong heel of the hand.

### Day 1 (Monday): Breakfalls
*Student:* Practice back and side breakfalls on a mat.

*How to teach:* Practice back and side breakfalls on a mat from sitting, then squatting.

- **Q:** Why tuck your chin during a backward fall?
  **A:** To keep the back of your head from hitting the ground.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Blocks
*Student:* Practice high and low blocks with a partner holding a pool noodle.

*How to teach:* Partner holds a pool noodle and slowly swings high and low; student practices high and low blocks.

- **Q:** What part of the arm meets the strike in a block?
  **A:** The forearm, with the arm bent.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Striking basics
*Student:* Practice palm-heel strikes and knee strikes on a pillow or pad.

*How to teach:* Practice palm-heel and knee strikes on a pillow or pad.

- **Q:** Why use a palm-heel strike instead of a fist?
  **A:** It protects the small bones of the hand while delivering force.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Conditioning
*Student:* Do a combatives conditioning circuit (shadowboxing rounds, sprawls).

*How to teach:* Lead a conditioning circuit: 3 × 2-minute shadowboxing rounds with sprawls, burpees and planks between.

- **Q:** What fitness component does shadowboxing train most?
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance (also coordination and muscular endurance).

### Day 5 (Friday): Skill check
*Student:* Demonstrate falls and blocks on video.

*How to teach:* Record the student demonstrating falls and blocks; review with the checklist.

- **Q:** What's the safe way to practice strikes with a partner?
  **A:** Slow speed, light contact on pads only, and a clear stop signal.

**Assessment:** Skill video.

**Answer key:** Skill video checklist: back breakfall (chin tucked, rounded back, arms slap at ~45°), side breakfall (both sides), high block and low block with each arm, palm-heel strike and knee strike with correct alignment and balance, return to stance after each. Proficient = all elements correct.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Passing and lane positioning

**Prepare:** Read the handbook sections on lane use, passing, being passed and center left-turn lanes. Prepare scenario cards.

**Watch for:**
- Students think passing is legal whenever the line is broken. Even with a broken line, passing is illegal when approaching hills or curves where you can't see, intersections, railroad crossings, or when you cannot return to your lane safely before oncoming traffic.
- Students speed up when someone passes them. Maintain or reduce speed and stay right so the passer can return safely.
- Students drive long distances in the center left-turn lane. Enter it no more than 200 feet before your turn, and never use it for passing.

### Day 1 (Monday): Lane choice
*Student:* Learn which lane to use for through traffic, passing and turning.

*How to teach:* The student studies lane choice and explains which lane to use for through traffic, passing and turning on multilane roads.

- **Q:** On a three-lane road in your direction, which lane is for faster traffic and passing?
  **A:** The left lane.
- **Q:** Which lane should you use if you plan to turn right soon?
  **A:** The right lane.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Passing
*Student:* Learn when passing is illegal (curves, hills, intersections, solid lines) and the steps to pass safely.

*How to teach:* The student lists when passing is illegal and writes the safe passing steps.

- **Q:** Name three places you may not pass.
  **A:** Hills or curves where you can't see far enough, within 100 feet of an intersection or railroad crossing, across a solid line on your side, where a no-passing sign is posted.
- **Q:** How do you know it's safe to return to your lane after passing?
  **A:** When you can see the front of the passed vehicle (both headlights) in your rearview mirror.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Being passed
*Student:* Learn what to do when another driver passes you.

*How to teach:* Discuss what to do when being passed and when a slow vehicle law applies.

- **Q:** What should you do when another driver passes you?
  **A:** Stay in your lane to the right and maintain or slightly reduce speed so they can return safely.
- **Q:** When must a slow driver on a two-lane road pull over?
  **A:** When five or more vehicles are lined up behind, at the nearest safe turnout.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Center turn lane
*Student:* Learn rules for two-way center left-turn lanes (no more than 200 feet).

*How to teach:* The student diagrams correct and incorrect uses of a two-way center left-turn lane.

- **Q:** How far may you drive in a center left-turn lane before turning?
  **A:** No more than 200 feet.
- **Q:** May you use the center lane to pass?
  **A:** No.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Scenario quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the scenario quiz. Review.

- **Q:** Can you pass on the shoulder to get around a car turning left?
  **A:** No — you may pass on the right only if there's room in a lane without driving off the paved roadway.

**Assessment:** Scenario quiz.

**Answer key:** Scenario quiz key: 1) Through traffic on a multilane road — use the right or middle lane; left lane for passing. 2) Passing on the right — only on multilane roads or when the driver ahead is turning left and there's room without leaving the pavement; never on the shoulder. 3) Passing near the crest of a hill — illegal/unsafe. 4) Steps to pass — check that the zone is legal and clear, signal, check mirror and blind spot, accelerate, pass, signal right, return when you can see the passed car's headlights in your rearview mirror. 5) Being passed — stay right, maintain or slow speed. 6) Center left-turn lane — enter within 200 feet of the turn; may also be used to turn left out of a driveway and wait to merge. 7) Slow vehicle with 5+ cars behind on a two-lane road — must pull over at a turnout. 8) Passing a bicycle — at least 3 feet.

## Computer Science Principles: The digital divide and internet impacts

**Prepare:** Bookmark the FCC National Broadband Map (broadbandmap.fcc.gov). Find a credible article on the 'homework gap' during remote learning (e.g. Pew Research Center). Prepare a shared Google Doc for Day 4. Prepare the Unit 2 test from the key below.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the digital divide is only about owning a device. It also includes access to fast, affordable broadband and the skills to use it.
- Thinking net neutrality is about whether the internet should be free of cost. It is about whether internet providers may treat some data differently (block, slow or charge extra for priority).
- Believing one government owns the internet. ICANN, a nonprofit, coordinates domain names and IP address allocation; no single entity controls the whole internet.

### Day 1 (Monday): Digital divide
*Student:* Use FCC broadband map data to compare internet access in a rural and an urban California county.

*How to teach:* Use the FCC map to compare broadband availability in a rural county (e.g. Modoc or Trinity) and an urban one (e.g. Los Angeles or San Francisco). Have the student record the percentage of locations with 100/20 Mbps service and the number of providers.

- **Q:** Why do rural areas often have less broadband access?
  **A:** Laying cable or fiber over long distances to few customers is expensive, so providers earn less per mile and are slower to build there.
- **Q:** Define the digital divide.
  **A:** The gap between those who have access to computers and reliable internet (and the skills to use them) and those who do not.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Effects
*Student:* Read an article on how the digital divide affected students during remote learning and summarize three impacts.

*How to teach:* Read an article on remote learning and the 'homework gap' and have the student summarize three effects (e.g. students doing homework in parking lots on fast-food Wi-Fi, missing class, lower grades or falling behind).

- **Q:** Name two ways the digital divide affected students during remote learning in 2020.
  **A:** Students without home broadband or devices missed live classes or couldn't submit work, and some had to share one device or use public Wi-Fi hotspots.
- **Q:** What is one solution communities used?
  **A:** Districts handed out laptops and mobile hotspots; some cities expanded public Wi-Fi.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Governance
*Student:* Research ICANN and net neutrality and write a pro/con table on net neutrality.

*How to teach:* Explain ICANN (manages the domain name system and IP address coordination globally) and net neutrality. Have the student make a pro/con table on net neutrality.

- **Q:** Give one argument for and one against net neutrality.
  **A:** For: prevents ISPs from favoring their own or paying companies' content, protecting free speech and competition for startups. Against: ISPs say charging for priority lets them manage traffic and fund network investment.
- **Q:** What does ICANN do?
  **A:** Coordinates the internet's naming and numbering systems — domain names and IP address allocation — so addresses are unique worldwide.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Global connections
*Student:* Use a collaborative document to compare internet access data with a peer or family member from another country or region.

*How to teach:* Create a shared doc with a peer or relative in another region; each adds data on internet access in their area (cost per month, typical speed, mobile vs. home use). Have the student write three observations comparing the data.

- **Q:** Why might mobile internet be more common than home broadband in some countries?
  **A:** Building cell towers is cheaper than wiring every home, and smartphones are more affordable than computers, so many people get online mainly through phones.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 2 assessment on internet structure, protocols and impacts.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test in the assessment key (about 40 minutes). Grade together and review any misses on protocols.

- **Q:** What happens when you type a URL? Name the protocols in order.
  **A:** DNS finds the server's IP address; TCP sets up a reliable connection; HTTP requests the page; IP routes the packets across networks.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 assessment.

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test answers (14 items): 1) Internet = network of networks; web = pages accessed via HTTP. 2) IPv4 32 bits / IPv6 128 bits. 3) Packets carry data plus header metadata. 4) Packets can arrive out of order and are reassembled using sequence numbers. 5) Routing = choosing a path for data. 6) Redundancy creates fault tolerance. 7) Single point of failure removal disconnects nodes. 8) TCP = reliable, UDP = fast/unreliable. 9) DNS maps names to IP addresses. 10) HTTP is used for web pages. 11) Bandwidth vs. latency definitions. 12) The open, decentralized design let the internet scale. 13) The digital divide = unequal access to computing devices and the internet across groups (rural/urban, income, age, country). 14) Net neutrality = ISPs must treat all data equally. Mastery: 11+/14.
