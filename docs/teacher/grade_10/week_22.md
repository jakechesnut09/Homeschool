# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 22

## English 10: Informative essay on witness literature

**Prepare:** Night, the WWI poems, and two USHMM Holocaust Encyclopedia articles (e.g., 'Auschwitz' and 'Deportations'). Show the student the Purdue OWL pages on MLA in-text citation and attributive tags. Prepare the Unit 5 test.

**Watch for:**
- Using first person ('I think') in informative writing. Model rewriting in third person with an objective tone.
- Dropping a fact into a paragraph without naming its source. Every borrowed idea needs an attributive tag or a citation.

### Day 1 (Monday): Prompt & thesis
*Student:* Plan an informative essay explaining how witness literature (Night and WWI poetry) preserves memory.

*How to teach:* Discuss what 'witness literature' means and brainstorm what Night and the WWI poems preserve that textbooks may not (individual voices, sensory detail, emotion). The student writes a working thesis and a three-part plan.

- **Q:** What is witness literature?
  **A:** Writing by people who lived through or saw historical atrocities, created to preserve memory and testify for those who cannot.
- **Q:** Draft a sample thesis for this essay.
  **A:** For example: 'Through personal testimony and vivid imagery, Night and the poems of Wilfred Owen preserve the human reality of mass suffering so that later generations cannot forget or deny it.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Source notes
*Student:* Take paraphrase and quote notes from two USHMM articles, citing each in MLA.

*How to teach:* Model the difference between paraphrase (your words, same length), summary (shorter) and quote (exact words) using one USHMM paragraph. The student takes labeled notes from two articles with full MLA source information.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a paraphrase and a summary?
  **A:** A paraphrase restates a specific passage in your own words at about the same length; a summary condenses the main ideas into a shorter form.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft
*Student:* Draft a 900-word essay with attributive tags for every source.

*How to teach:* Show three attributive tags ('According to the USHMM...', 'Wiesel recalls...', 'Owen depicts...'). The student drafts the 900-word essay, highlighting each tag to check that every source is introduced. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Rewrite with an attributive tag: 'Over 1 million people were murdered at Auschwitz.'
  **A:** 'According to the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum, approximately 1.1 million people were murdered at Auschwitz.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revise
*Student:* Revise for academic vocabulary and objective tone; remove first person.

*How to teach:* Read a paragraph aloud and circle every 'I,' 'you,' slang word or vague word ('stuff,' 'really'). The student revises for academic vocabulary and objective tone throughout. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Revise for objective tone: 'I think Night is really sad and you can't stop reading it.'
  **A:** 'Night conveys profound grief in a narrative that compels the reader forward.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Final & test
*Student:* Submit final essay and take the Unit 5 test.

*How to teach:* The student submits the final essay, then takes the Unit 5 test from the assessment key. Grade the test together and discuss any missed text details. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Why is Night classified as a memoir rather than a novel?
  **A:** It is Elie Wiesel's own account of his experiences in the Holocaust, written in the first person about real events.

**Assessment:** Informative essay rubric and Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Informative essay rubric (W.9-10.2): 4 = clear thesis explaining how witness literature preserves memory; organized body paragraphs; accurate evidence from Night, at least one WWI poem and two USHMM articles; an attributive tag for every source; objective tone; MLA Works Cited; 3 = minor gaps in evidence or citation; 2 = thin evidence or opinionated tone; 1 = incomplete. Unit 5 test (sample answers): 1) Memoir vs. novel → a memoir is the author's true account of their own experience. 2) Imagery in 'Dulce' → the gassed soldier 'drowning' under a 'green sea.' 3) Speaker of 'In Flanders Fields' → the dead soldiers. 4) Significance of Madame Schächter's vision → foreshadows the crematoria fires. 5) Wiesel's view of silence → it helps the tormentor, never the tormented. 6) Tone of Brooke's 'The Soldier' → idealistic, patriotic.

## Geometry: Laws of Sines and Cosines

**Prepare:** Scientific calculator in degree mode; Unit 6 test from the assessment key. Review the formulas: Area = ½ab sin C; a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C; c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C.

**Watch for:**
- Using the Law of Sines for SAS or SSS (it needs an angle-opposite-side pair). Choose Law of Cosines for SAS and SSS.
- Missing the second solution in the SSA ambiguous case. If sin B gives angle B, also check 180° − B.

### Day 1 (Monday): Area formula
*Student:* Derive A = ½ab sin C by drawing an altitude and use it on oblique triangles.

*How to teach:* Draw an oblique triangle, drop the altitude from B to side b; h = a sin C, so Area = ½ab sin C. The student applies it to several triangles.

- **Q:** Find the area of a triangle with sides 8 and 10 and included angle 30°.
  **A:** ½·8·10·sin 30° = 20.
- **Q:** Sides 12 and 15 with included 110°. Area?
  **A:** ½·12·15·sin 110° ≈ 84.6.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Law of Sines
*Student:* Derive and apply the Law of Sines including the ambiguous SSA case.

*How to teach:* Derive the Law of Sines from two expressions for the same altitude. Solve AAS/ASA problems, then show SSA can give 0, 1, or 2 triangles.

- **Q:** A = 40°, B = 60°, a = 10. Find b.
  **A:** b = 10·sin 60°/sin 40° ≈ 13.47.
- **Q:** a = 6, b = 8, A = 30°. How many triangles?
  **A:** sin B = 8 sin 30°/6 ≈ 0.667 → B ≈ 41.8° or 138.2°; both work (138.2 + 30 < 180), so two triangles.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Law of Cosines
*Student:* Derive the Law of Cosines and use it for SAS and SSS triangles.

*How to teach:* Derive the Law of Cosines by placing C at the origin and using the distance formula (or the altitude). Note it becomes the Pythagorean theorem when C = 90°.

- **Q:** a = 7, b = 8, C = 60°. Find c.
  **A:** c² = 49 + 64 − 112·0.5 = 57 → c ≈ 7.55.
- **Q:** Sides 5, 7, 8. Find the angle opposite 7.
  **A:** cos θ = (25 + 64 − 49)/(2·5·8) = 0.5 → θ = 60°.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Surveying problems
*Student:* Solve land-surveying and navigation problems choosing the right law.

*How to teach:* Give mixed surveying/navigation word problems; the student sketches, identifies given parts (SAS, SSS, ASA, AAS, SSA) and chooses the law.

- **Q:** Two hikers leave camp; one walks 3 km, the other 5 km, with 70° between their paths. How far apart are they?
  **A:** c² = 9 + 25 − 30 cos 70° ≈ 34 − 10.26 = 23.74 → c ≈ 4.87 km.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 6 test
*Student:* Unit 6 test on right-triangle trig and oblique triangles.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test from the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** What does the Law of Cosines reduce to when the angle is 90°?
  **A:** The Pythagorean theorem, since cos 90° = 0.

**Assessment:** Unit 6 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 6 test with answers: 1) sin 52° ≈ 0.788. 2) Right triangle angle 25°, hypotenuse 30 → opposite ≈ 12.68. 3) Angle of elevation for a 6 ft rise over 20 ft → tan⁻¹(0.3) ≈ 16.7°. 4) Area with a = 8, b = 10, C = 30° → 20. 5) A = 40°, B = 60°, a = 10 → b ≈ 13.47. 6) a = 7, b = 8, C = 60° → c = √57 ≈ 7.55. 7) Sides 5, 7, 8 → angle opposite 7 = 60°. 8) SSA: a = 6, b = 8, A = 30° → sin B = 0.667, B ≈ 41.8° or 138.2° (two triangles). Mastery 80%.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Limiting reactants

**Prepare:** Gather bread and cheese slices (or cards labeled as such), baking soda, vinegar (5% acetic acid), five identical bottles and balloons, a measuring spoon or scale, and a flexible measuring tape. Open PhET 'Reactants, Products and Leftovers'.

**Watch for:**
- Students assume the reactant with the smaller mass is limiting. Compare moles needed vs. moles available using the balanced ratio.
- Students think that once one reactant is used up the other keeps reacting. The reaction stops when the limiting reactant is gone; the excess remains.
- Students calculate product from both reactants and add them. The smaller amount of product is the correct (theoretical) yield.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sandwich analogy
*Student:* Use a sandwich-making analogy to identify limiting ingredients.

*How to teach:* Model making sandwiches (2 bread + 1 cheese). Give different quantities and have the student find how many sandwiches can be made, what runs out, and what is left over.

- **Q:** You have 12 slices of bread and 4 slices of cheese. How many sandwiches, and what is limiting?
  **A:** 4 sandwiches; cheese is limiting; 4 slices of bread are left.
- **Q:** Relate this to a chemical reaction.
  **A:** The balanced equation is the recipe; the reactant that runs out first limits how much product forms.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): PhET
*Student:* Use PhET Reactants, Products and Leftovers to model limiting reactants.

*How to teach:* In PhET, the student completes the sandwich and molecule screens, predicting products and leftovers before checking. Record three cases.

- **Q:** With 4 N₂ and 9 H₂ molecules (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃), what forms and what is left?
  **A:** H₂ limits: 9 H₂ make 6 NH₃ using 3 N₂; 1 N₂ is left over.
- **Q:** With 3 CH₄ and 4 O₂ (CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O), what forms?
  **A:** O₂ limits: 2 CH₄ react making 2 CO₂ and 4 H₂O; 1 CH₄ left over.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Balloon lab
*Student:* Add increasing baking soda amounts to equal vinegar in balloons and graph balloon size to find the limiting point.

*How to teach:* Put 100 mL of vinegar in each of five bottles. Put 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 g of baking soda into balloons, stretch each over a bottle, then lift to dump. After reactions stop, measure balloon circumference and graph.

- **Q:** Why do the last balloons end up about the same size?
  **A:** The vinegar ran out (became limiting), so extra baking soda could not make more CO₂.
- **Q:** Estimate the baking soda mass at which 100 mL of 5% vinegar becomes limiting.
  **A:** ≈ 5 g acetic acid ÷ 60.05 = 0.083 mol → 0.083 × 84.01 ≈ 7 g baking soda.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Problems
*Student:* Solve limiting reactant problems for combustion of fuels.

*How to teach:* Model a limiting reactant problem for methane combustion; the student solves four more for fuels.

- **Q:** 10.0 g CH₄ reacts with 32.0 g O₂. Which is limiting and how much CO₂ forms?
  **A:** CH₄: 0.623 mol needs 1.25 mol O₂, but only 1.00 mol O₂ is available → O₂ limits. CO₂ = 1.00 ÷ 2 = 0.500 mol × 44.01 = 22.0 g.
- **Q:** In a car engine with too little air, what forms instead of CO₂, and why is it dangerous?
  **A:** Carbon monoxide (CO) and soot from incomplete combustion; CO binds to hemoglobin and blocks oxygen transport.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on limiting reactants.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Grade together and collect the balloon graph.

- **Q:** 4.0 mol Al reacts with 4.0 mol Cl₂ (2Al + 3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃). Which is limiting and how much AlCl₃ forms?
  **A:** Cl₂ limits (4.0 mol Al needs 6.0 mol Cl₂); AlCl₃ = 4.0 × 2/3 = 2.7 mol.

**Assessment:** Balloon lab graph and quiz.

**Answer key:** Balloon lab: full credit for a graph of baking soda mass (x) vs. balloon circumference (y) that rises and then levels off; identification of the leveling point. With 100 mL vinegar (≈0.083 mol acetic acid), growth should stop near 7 g baking soda (0.083 × 84.01). Explanation: before that point baking soda is limiting; after it vinegar is limiting. Quiz key: 1) 10 slices of bread and 8 cheese, sandwich = 2 bread + 1 cheese: 5 sandwiches, bread limiting, 3 cheese left. 2) 10.0 g CH₄ + 32.0 g O₂: O₂ limiting (needs 1.25 mol O₂, has 1.00 mol); CO₂ = 0.500 mol = 22.0 g; leftover CH₄ = 1.98 g. 3) 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O with 4.0 mol H₂ and 3.0 mol O₂: H₂ limiting; 4.0 mol H₂O; 1.0 mol O₂ left. 4) N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ with 2.0 mol N₂ and 3.0 mol H₂: H₂ limiting; 2.0 mol NH₃.

## Modern World History: The Holocaust

**Prepare:** USHMM primary sources (photographs, documents), Wannsee Conference minutes excerpt (January 20, 1942), survivor testimony (USC Shoah Foundation IWitness). Parent preview: graphic content; allow processing time and coordinate with Night in English.

**Watch for:**
- Seeing the Holocaust as sudden. It escalated step by step from 1933 discrimination to 1941–45 mass murder.
- Assuming victims did not resist. Resistance included armed uprisings, smuggling and preserving culture.

### Day 1 (Monday): Persecution
*Student:* Build a timeline from 1933 boycotts to Kristallnacht using USHMM sources.

*How to teach:* Using USHMM sources, the student builds a timeline from the April 1933 boycott through the Nuremberg Laws (1935) to Kristallnacht (November 9–10, 1938), noting how persecution escalated step by step.

- **Q:** What happened on Kristallnacht?
  **A:** Nazi-organized attacks destroyed synagogues and Jewish businesses; about 30,000 Jewish men were sent to camps.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ghettos & killing
*Student:* Analyze ghetto photographs and Einsatzgruppen reports.

*How to teach:* Show ghetto photographs (e.g., Warsaw or Łódź) and a short Einsatzgruppen report excerpt from USHMM (preview first). The student sources each and describes what it shows about conditions and policy.

- **Q:** Who were the Einsatzgruppen?
  **A:** Mobile killing units that shot Jews and others in the occupied USSR after June 1941.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Final Solution
*Student:* Read Wannsee minutes excerpt and analyze euphemistic language.

*How to teach:* Read the Wannsee Conference (January 20, 1942) minutes excerpt. The student underlines euphemisms and rewrites each in plain language, then explains why officials used such language. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What euphemisms appear in the Wannsee minutes?
  **A:** 'Final Solution,' 'evacuation to the East,' 'treated accordingly.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Resistance & rescue
*Student:* Research Warsaw Ghetto Uprising and rescuers (Denmark, Sugihara).

*How to teach:* The student researches the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising (April–May 1943) and two rescuers or rescue efforts (Danish rescue of about 7,000 Jews; Chiune Sugihara's visas). They write a paragraph on forms of resistance and rescue.

- **Q:** What happened in Denmark in 1943?
  **A:** Danes ferried about 7,000 Jews to safety in Sweden.
- **Q:** Who was Chiune Sugihara?
  **A:** A Japanese diplomat in Lithuania who issued thousands of visas to Jewish refugees.

### Day 5 (Friday): Testimony
*Student:* Watch a survivor testimony and write a response connecting to Night.

*How to teach:* Watch a survivor testimony on IWitness (about 10–15 minutes). The student writes a one-page response connecting specific moments to Night from English class. Allow time to talk. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** How many Jews were murdered in the Holocaust?
  **A:** About 6 million.

**Assessment:** Testimony response.

**Answer key:** Testimony response rubric (4-point): summarizes the survivor's experience accurately; connects to specific events in Night; reflects on the importance of testimony; uses respectful language.

## Spanish III: El cuento: un cuento corto

**Prepare:** Print Horacio Quiroga's 'El almohadón de plumas' (1907, public domain) with a glossary. Preview: a short horror story with a gradual illness, death, and a blood-drinking parasite. Chart the pluperfect: había, habías, había, habíamos, habíais, habían + participle. Write the Unit 6 test (present perfect, pluperfect, art and literature vocabulary, a short reading).

**Watch for:**
- Using the preterite where the pluperfect is needed: an action before another past action uses había + participle (Cuando llegué, la película ya había empezado).
- Thinking the narrator explains everything early: Quiroga withholds the cause of Alicia's illness until the final paragraphs to create suspense.
- Writing the summary in the present: the task asks for past tenses (preterite for events, imperfect for description).

### Day 1 (Monday): Pluscuamperfecto
*Student:* Learn the past perfect (había + participle).

*How to teach:* Teach the pluperfect with a timeline; your child writes 10 sentences about things that had already happened before a past moment.

- **Q:** Translate: 'When I arrived, my brother had already eaten.'
  **A:** Cuando llegué, mi hermano ya había comido.
- **Q:** Translate: 'They had never seen snow.'
  **A:** Nunca habían visto la nieve.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Lectura
*Student:* Read 'El almohadón de plumas' by Horacio Quiroga.

*How to teach:* Read 'El almohadón de plumas' aloud together with the glossary; stop after each section to check comprehension.

- **Q:** Describe the house and the marriage at the start.
  **A:** The house is cold, white, and silent like an enchanted palace; Jordán is stern and undemonstrative, and Alicia, shy and timid, is unhappy and grows thin.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Análisis
*Student:* Answer comprehension and analysis questions about suspense.

*How to teach:* Discuss how Quiroga builds suspense: the cold setting, Alicia's worsening illness, doctors who cannot explain it, and the hallucinations.

- **Q:** What do the doctors find, and why can't they cure her?
  **A:** Severe anemia that worsens each day; they cannot explain the cause because it is hidden in the pillow.
- **Q:** What is revealed at the end?
  **A:** A monstrous parasite inside the feather pillow had been sucking Alicia's blood every night through her temples.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Resumen
*Student:* Write a 200-word summary in past tenses.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a 200-word summary in past tenses using at least three pluperfect verbs.

- **Q:** Write a sentence with the pluperfect about the story.
  **A:** Example: La sirvienta descubrió que el animal había chupado la sangre de Alicia.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Unit 6 test.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test.

- **Q:** Fill in: Alicia ya ___ (morir) cuando encontraron el parásito.
  **A:** había muerto

**Assessment:** Unit 6 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 6 test sample key: 1) Cuando llegamos, el tren ya había salido 2) Ella nunca había visto un mural 3) he escrito / habían vuelto 4) Reading items: Alicia and Jordán are newlyweds; Alicia becomes weak and pale (anemia) and has hallucinations; she dies; the maid notices stains on the feather pillow; Jordán cuts it open and finds a swollen parasite that had been sucking Alicia's blood at night. Summary rubric (200 words): 4 = main events in order, correct preterite/imperfect/pluperfect, own words; 3 = minor errors; 2 = gaps or many errors; 1 = incomplete.

## Music & Media Arts: Copyright, fair use and media ethics

**Prepare:** Creative Commons website (creativecommons.org) and search (openverse.org); summaries of sampling cases: Grand Upright Music v. Warner Bros. (1991, Biz Markie), Bridgeport Music v. Dimension Films (2005), VMG Salsoul v. Ciccone (2016, Madonna's 'Vogue'); examples of edited news photos. Build the quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Students think anything online is free to use. Most works are copyrighted automatically when created.
- Students think giving credit makes any use legal. Credit doesn't replace permission or a license unless the use is fair use or the license allows it.
- Students think fair use has a fixed 'seconds rule'. There's no set number of seconds; fair use is judged on four factors.

### Day 1 (Monday): Copyright
*Student:* Learn copyright basics and fair use factors.

*How to teach:* Learn copyright basics and the four fair-use factors.

- **Q:** When does a song you write become copyrighted?
  **A:** Automatically as soon as it's fixed — recorded or written down.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Creative Commons
*Student:* Find CC-licensed music and images and attribute them correctly.

*How to teach:* Find CC-licensed music and images and attribute them correctly.

- **Q:** Write a correct attribution.
  **A:** 'Sunset Beach' by Jane Doe (openverse link), licensed under CC BY 4.0.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Case studies
*Student:* Analyze music sampling lawsuits.

*How to teach:* Analyze music sampling lawsuits.

- **Q:** What did Bridgeport Music v. Dimension Films (2005) decide?
  **A:** In the 6th Circuit, any unlicensed sample of a sound recording, however small, could infringe — 'Get a license or do not sample.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Media bias
*Student:* Analyze how photos are edited to influence viewers.

*How to teach:* Analyze how photos are edited to influence viewers.

- **Q:** Give one ethical rule for news photo editing.
  **A:** Don't add or remove content; only minor adjustments like exposure and cropping that don't change meaning.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on copyright and ethics.

*How to teach:* Give the copyright and ethics quiz.

- **Q:** What does CC BY-NC mean?
  **A:** You may use it with attribution, but not for commercial purposes.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Copyright is automatic once a work is fixed in tangible form. 2) Term — generally the author's life + 70 years. 3) Four fair-use factors — purpose/character (transformative, educational), nature of the work, amount used, effect on the market. 4) Public domain — no copyright (expired, e.g., U.S. works published in 1930 or earlier as of 2026, or dedicated via CC0). 5) CC BY — use with attribution; SA — share alike; NC — noncommercial; ND — no derivatives. 6) Attribution format TASL — Title, Author, Source, License. 7) Grand Upright (1991) — unlicensed sample ruled infringement. 8) Bridgeport (2005) — even tiny sound-recording samples need a license (6th Circuit: 'Get a license or do not sample'). 9) VMG Salsoul (2016, 9th Circuit) — a de minimis sample was allowed. 10) Misleading photo edits violate journalistic ethics.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Injuries and sudden illness

**Prepare:** Gather gloves, gauze, a rolled towel (for packing practice), a commercial tourniquet if available (or a triangular bandage and a stick for improvised practice), a rolled magazine, triangular bandage, and an elastic bandage. Queue the Stop the Bleed overview and an epinephrine auto-injector trainer video. Parental note: some materials show injuries.

**Watch for:**
- Students apply a tourniquet loosely or remove it to check. Tighten until bleeding stops, note the time, and leave it on for EMS to remove.
- Students put ice, butter or ointment on burns. Cool with cool running water (not ice) for at least 10 minutes (20 for chemical burns), then cover loosely.
- Students wait to see if stroke symptoms go away. Call 911 immediately and note the time symptoms started; treatment is time-critical.

### Day 1 (Monday): Bleeding control
*Student:* Practice direct pressure, wound packing on a rolled towel, and applying a commercial or improvised tourniquet 2–3 inches above a wound (Stop the Bleed method).

*How to teach:* Teach Stop the Bleed: direct pressure, wound packing, tourniquet. The student practices pressure and packing on a rolled towel and applies a tourniquet to a family member's arm or a pillow (don't tighten painfully on a person).

- **Q:** Where do you place a tourniquet?
  **A:** 2–3 inches above the wound (between the wound and the heart), not over a joint.
- **Q:** What should you do after applying a tourniquet?
  **A:** Note the time and leave it in place for EMS.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Burns
*Student:* Learn first aid for thermal, chemical and electrical burns: cool with running water 10+ minutes, cover loosely, and know when to call 911.

*How to teach:* The student learns first aid for thermal, chemical and electrical burns and when to call 911.

- **Q:** What's the first aid for a minor thermal burn?
  **A:** Cool it under cool running water for at least 10 minutes, then cover loosely with a sterile dressing.
- **Q:** Why must you turn off power before helping someone with an electrical injury?
  **A:** So the rescuer isn't electrocuted; the person may still be in contact with the current.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fractures & sprains
*Student:* Splint a 'forearm fracture' with a rolled magazine and triangular bandage and practice RICE for sprains.

*How to teach:* The student splints a 'forearm fracture' with a rolled magazine and triangular bandage sling, then practices RICE on an ankle.

- **Q:** How do you check that a splint isn't too tight?
  **A:** Check fingers for warmth, color, sensation and movement before and after splinting.
- **Q:** What does RICE stand for?
  **A:** Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sudden illness
*Student:* Learn signs and responses for stroke (BE-FAST), heart attack, seizures, diabetic emergencies and anaphylaxis using an epinephrine auto-injector trainer video.

*How to teach:* The student learns BE-FAST for stroke, signs of heart attack, seizure care, diabetic emergencies and anaphylaxis; watch the auto-injector trainer video.

- **Q:** What should you do for someone having a seizure?
  **A:** Protect them from injury (move objects, cushion the head), don't restrain them or put anything in the mouth, time the seizure, roll them onto their side after, and call 911 if it lasts over 5 minutes or it's their first.
- **Q:** A diabetic person is confused and sweaty but can swallow. What could help?
  **A:** Give sugar (juice, regular soda, glucose tablets) for suspected low blood sugar; call 911 if they don't improve or can't swallow.

### Day 5 (Friday): Scenario quiz
*Student:* Respond to 10 written emergency scenarios, listing the correct first aid steps for each.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-scenario quiz. Review.

- **Q:** A friend stung by a bee has trouble breathing and swelling lips. What do you do?
  **A:** Call 911 and help them use their epinephrine auto-injector in the outer thigh; keep monitoring and be ready for CPR.

**Assessment:** Scenario quiz.

**Answer key:** Scenario quiz key (example items): 1) Severe arm bleeding — call 911, gloves, firm direct pressure; if life-threatening and not controlled, tourniquet 2–3 inches above the wound (not on a joint), note time. 2) Deep wound in the thigh/armpit area — pack the wound with gauze and hold pressure. 3) Kitchen burn — cool running water 10+ min, remove rings, cover loosely, call 911 if large, on face/hands/genitals, or deep. 4) Chemical burn — brush off dry chemical, flush with water 20+ minutes, remove contaminated clothing. 5) Electrical burn — turn off power first, call 911 (possible internal injury and heart rhythm problems). 6) Suspected forearm fracture — splint in position found, padding, check circulation beyond the splint. 7) Sprain — RICE: rest, ice (20 min with cloth), compression, elevation. 8) Stroke — BE-FAST: Balance, Eyes, Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call 911. 9) Heart attack — call 911, help the person rest, aspirin if not allergic and directed; be ready for CPR. 10) Anaphylaxis — call 911, help use epinephrine auto-injector in the outer thigh, second dose after 5–15 min if needed.

## Computer Science Principles: Unsolvable problems, heuristics and parallel computing

**Prepare:** Draw 6 points (cities) on paper with a distance table between them. Read a short explainer of the halting problem (Khan Academy AP CSP 'Undecidable problems'). Get a deck of cards and a timer for Day 4. Prepare the Unit 5 test.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing 'unreasonable time' and 'undecidable'. Unreasonable problems can be solved but take too long for large inputs; undecidable problems cannot be solved by any algorithm for all inputs.
- Thinking a heuristic gives the best answer. It gives a good-enough answer quickly, with no guarantee of optimality.
- Expecting speedup to equal the number of helpers. Coordination and sequential parts limit speedup.

### Day 1 (Monday): Traveling salesperson
*Student:* Try to find the shortest route through 6 cities and count the possibilities.

*How to teach:* With 6 cities, have the student try several routes and compute their lengths. Then count the possibilities: starting at a fixed city, 5! = 120 orderings (60 if a route and its reverse count as the same).

- **Q:** How many routes visit 6 cities starting from city A (counting direction)?
  **A:** 5! = 120 (60 if reverse routes are considered the same).
- **Q:** How many for 11 cities from a fixed start?
  **A:** 10! = 3,628,800 — the count explodes, which is why brute force becomes unreasonable.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Heuristics
*Student:* Use a nearest-neighbor heuristic and compare to the best route.

*How to teach:* Apply nearest-neighbor: from the start, always go to the closest unvisited city. Compare its route length to the best route found on Day 1.

- **Q:** What is a heuristic?
  **A:** A problem-solving approach that finds a good-enough (approximate) solution in reasonable time when finding the exact best solution is impractical.
- **Q:** Does nearest-neighbor always find the shortest route?
  **A:** No — an early greedy choice can force a long final leg, so it may be longer than optimal.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Undecidable problems
*Student:* Read about Turing's halting problem and write a paragraph explaining in your own words why no program can decide whether every program halts.

*How to teach:* Read about Alan Turing's halting problem (1936). The student writes a paragraph explaining why no program can decide for every program and input whether it halts.

- **Q:** In your own words, why is the halting problem undecidable?
  **A:** If a program H could always decide halting, we could build a program that asks H about itself and does the opposite (loops if H says halt, halts if H says loop). That contradiction shows no such H can exist.
- **Q:** What does 'undecidable' mean?
  **A:** No algorithm can always give a correct yes/no answer for every possible input.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Parallel computing
*Student:* Time sorting cards alone vs. with helpers and calculate speedup.

*How to teach:* Time the student sorting a full deck alone; then split the deck among 2–3 helpers who each sort a part, then merge. Speedup = sequential time ÷ parallel time.

- **Q:** Alone: 120 s. With two helpers (three people) the parallel time is 60 s. Speedup?
  **A:** 120 ÷ 60 = 2.
- **Q:** Why isn't the speedup 3 with three people?
  **A:** The merge step must be done sequentially and coordination takes time, limiting how much work runs in parallel.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take the Unit 5 assessment on procedures, libraries, search algorithms, efficiency and parallel computing.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test in the assessment key (about 40 minutes).

- **Q:** What's the difference between parallel and distributed computing?
  **A:** Parallel computing splits a program into parts run simultaneously on multiple processors; distributed computing uses multiple separate computers connected over a network.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 assessment.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test (12 items): 1) Parameter vs. argument. 2) RETURN sends a value back to the caller. 3) PROCEDURE sq(n){RETURN n*n}; sq(5)+1 → 26. 4) Procedural abstraction hides implementation. 5) Library and API definitions. 6) Linear search worst case for 500 items → 500. 7) Binary search requires sorted data. 8) Binary search of 64 items → about 7 checks. 9) 2ⁿ → unreasonable. 10) Halting problem is undecidable. 11) Heuristic → approximate solution in reasonable time. 12) Sequential takes 60 s; parallel with two processors 35 s → speedup = 60/35 ≈ 1.71.
