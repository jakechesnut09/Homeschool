# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 5

## English 10: Greek theater and Antigone, Prologue–Ode 1

**Prepare:** Get a translation of Antigone (the Fitts and Fitzgerald translation uses Prologue/Parodos/Scene/Ode/Exodos labels). Find a short video on Greek theater (e.g., TED-Ed or Crash Course Theater). Parent preview: the play includes suicides offstage.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Chorus is a narrator. It is a group of Theban elders who comment, react and represent community values.
- Mixing up family members. Keep the family tree visible all unit.

### Day 1 (Monday): Greek theater background
*Student:* Watch a short video on Greek theater (masks, chorus, Dionysia) and take Cornell notes on Aristotle's elements of tragedy.

*How to teach:* Watch a 5–10 minute video on Greek theater together (TED-Ed or Crash Course Theater). The student takes Cornell notes — questions on the left, notes on the right, summary at the bottom — covering masks, chorus, the Dionysia festival and Aristotle's elements of tragedy; quiz each other on the terms at the end.

- **Q:** Define hamartia and catharsis.
  **A:** Hamartia is the tragic hero's error or flaw; catharsis is the release of pity and fear the audience feels.
- **Q:** What role did the chorus play in Greek tragedy?
  **A:** A group that sang and danced between scenes, commenting on action and voicing community views.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Oedipus family backstory
*Student:* Read a summary of the Theban myth and create a family tree of Oedipus, Jocasta, Antigone, Ismene, Polyneices, Eteocles and Creon.

*How to teach:* Read a one-page summary of the Oedipus myth together (Oedipus unknowingly kills his father and marries his mother, Jocasta). The student builds a family tree that includes Creon as Jocasta's brother and the four children, then explains aloud how the curse on the family leads into Antigone.

- **Q:** How are Antigone and Creon related?
  **A:** Creon is Antigone's uncle — brother of her mother, Jocasta.
- **Q:** Who are Antigone's siblings?
  **A:** Ismene, Polyneices and Eteocles.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Read Prologue
*Student:* Read the Prologue aloud in two voices and chart how Antigone and Ismene differ in values, citing three lines each.

*How to teach:* Read the Prologue aloud, parent as Ismene. The student charts differences in values with three lines each. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does Antigone ask Ismene to do, and how does Ismene respond?
  **A:** To help bury Polyneices; Ismene refuses, saying they are women and cannot defy the king's law.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Read Parodos & Scene 1
*Student:* Read the Parodos and Scene 1 and paraphrase Creon's opening speech on loyalty to the state.

*How to teach:* Read the Parodos and Scene 1. The student paraphrases Creon's speech. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Paraphrase Creon's view of loyalty in Scene 1.
  **A:** A ruler and citizen must put the state first; anyone who values a friend above the country is worthless, and enemies of the state (like Polyneices) get no honor.
- **Q:** What is the Parodos about?
  **A:** The Chorus celebrates Thebes' victory over the attacking Argive army.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check & reflect
*Student:* Take a reading-check quiz and write a journal entry: which law matters more, divine law or civic law?

*How to teach:* Give the reading-check quiz (assessment key). The student writes a journal entry on divine vs. civic law. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Which law does Antigone follow, and which does Creon defend?
  **A:** Antigone follows divine law (the gods' law requiring burial of the dead); Creon defends civic law (his decree for the state).

**Assessment:** Reading quiz; character-values chart.

**Answer key:** Reading quiz (answers): 1) What has Creon decreed? → Eteocles is buried with honor; Polyneices must lie unburied, and anyone who buries him will be put to death. 2) Why do the brothers die? → They killed each other fighting over Thebes (Polyneices attacked with an Argive army). 3) Why does Ismene refuse to help? → She fears death and says they are women who cannot defy the state. 4) Creon's main idea in his first speech → loyalty to the state comes before loyalty to friends or family. 5) What does the Sentry report? → Someone has sprinkled dust on Polyneices' body. Values chart: full credit for three cited lines each showing Antigone values divine law/family duty/honor and Ismene values safety/obedience/caution.

## Geometry: Rotations and symmetry

**Prepare:** Protractor, patty paper, compass, phone camera for Day 4, GeoGebra. Memorize the counterclockwise rotation rules about the origin: 90° (−y, x), 180° (−x, −y), 270° (y, −x).

**Watch for:**
- Rotating clockwise when the problem says 90° (positive rotations are counterclockwise). Have the student turn the paper to check direction.
- Saying a rectangle has 4 lines of symmetry (its diagonals are not lines of symmetry unless it is a square).

### Day 1 (Monday): Rotations about origin
*Student:* Rotate figures 90°, 180°, 270° about the origin and derive the coordinate rules.

*How to teach:* Rotate a triangle 90° about the origin with patty paper (pin at origin, turn counterclockwise) and record coordinates in a table to find each rule.

- **Q:** Rotate (3, 1) 90° counterclockwise about the origin.
  **A:** (−1, 3).
- **Q:** What is the rule for a 180° rotation about the origin?
  **A:** (x, y) → (−x, −y).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rotations about a point
*Student:* Rotate a triangle about a point that isn't the origin with protractor and compass.

*How to teach:* Model rotating about point P: draw segment from P to each vertex, use the protractor to make the rotation angle at P, and use the compass to keep the same distance. The student rotates a triangle 60° about a non-origin point.

- **Q:** When rotating point A about P, what must be equal for A and its image A'?
  **A:** PA = PA' (same distance from the center), and m∠APA' equals the rotation angle.
- **Q:** Rotate (5, 2) 180° about P(1, 1).
  **A:** Shift so P is the origin: (4, 1) → (−4, −1); shift back: (−3, 0).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Symmetry
*Student:* Find all lines of symmetry and rotation angles for regular polygons, rectangles and rhombi.

*How to teach:* Use cut-out shapes to find lines of symmetry by folding and rotation symmetry by turning in place. Summarize in a table for regular polygons: an n-gon has n lines and rotations by multiples of 360°/n.

- **Q:** What rotations carry a regular hexagon onto itself?
  **A:** Multiples of 60°: 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, 300° (and 360°).
- **Q:** How many lines of symmetry does a non-square rhombus have?
  **A:** Two — its diagonals.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Real-world symmetry
*Student:* Photograph three objects at home with symmetry (hubcap, tile, logo) and describe each symmetry.

*How to teach:* The student photographs three symmetric objects at home and writes the line and/or rotational symmetry of each. Discuss one together to model precise wording.

- **Q:** A hubcap with 5 identical spokes has what rotational symmetry?
  **A:** 72° (and multiples of 72°); it also has 5 lines of symmetry if each spoke is itself symmetric.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on rotation rules and symmetry.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and score the photo set using the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Rotate (0, 4) 270° counterclockwise about the origin.
  **A:** (4, 0).

**Assessment:** Quiz and symmetry photo set.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Rotate (2, 5) 90° counterclockwise about the origin → (−5, 2). 2) Rotate (−3, 1) 180° → (3, −1). 3) Rotate (4, −2) 270° counterclockwise → (−2, −4). 4) Lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry: regular pentagon → 5 lines, 72° rotations; rectangle (non-square) → 2 lines, 180°; rhombus (non-square) → 2 lines (diagonals), 180°; square → 4 lines, 90°. Photo set rubric: 3 = three objects, each symmetry correctly named (line count and/or rotation angle); 2 = one error; 1 = symmetries named vaguely; 0 = missing.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Atomic structure and history of the atom

**Prepare:** Open PhET 'Build an Atom' and test it. Gather marbles, a large piece of cardboard propped on books, and a hidden shape cut from cardboard (triangle or circle). For 'beanium' gather three kinds of dried beans (e.g., 20 pinto, 15 kidney, 5 lima) and the kitchen scale. Have a periodic table.

**Watch for:**
- Students think the mass number is on the periodic table. The decimal number shown is the average atomic mass; the mass number (protons + neutrons) is a whole number for one isotope.
- Students think isotopes of an element have different numbers of protons. Isotopes have the same protons (same element) but different neutrons.
- Students treat ions as changes in protons. Ions form by gaining or losing electrons; the number of protons never changes in a chemical reaction.

### Day 1 (Monday): Atomic history timeline
*Student:* Make an illustrated timeline of Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr and the quantum model.

*How to teach:* Have the student research each scientist and make an illustrated timeline: Dalton (c. 1803, solid indivisible spheres), Thomson (1897 discovered the electron; plum-pudding model), Rutherford (1911 gold foil; nucleus), Bohr (1913 electrons in fixed energy levels), Schrödinger/quantum model (1920s electron cloud/orbitals). For each, note the evidence that changed the model.

- **Q:** What evidence led Thomson to propose that atoms contain smaller particles?
  **A:** Cathode-ray experiments showed beams of negatively charged particles much lighter than any atom, deflected by electric and magnetic fields — electrons.
- **Q:** What was wrong with the plum-pudding model?
  **A:** It predicted alpha particles would pass straight through gold foil; Rutherford found a few bounced back, showing charge and mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rutherford analogy
*Student:* Roll marbles at a hidden shape under cardboard to infer its shape, modeling the gold-foil experiment.

*How to teach:* Hide a shape under the cardboard without letting the student see it. The student rolls marbles under it from many directions, marks where each enters and exits, and infers the shape. Connect: Rutherford never saw the nucleus either; he inferred it from deflections.

- **Q:** In Rutherford's experiment, what did it mean that most alpha particles went straight through?
  **A:** The atom is mostly empty space.
- **Q:** How is the marble activity like the gold-foil experiment?
  **A:** Both use the paths of projectiles to infer the size and shape of something that cannot be seen directly.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Build an Atom
*Student:* Use PhET Build an Atom to construct atoms and ions and record charge and mass.

*How to teach:* In PhET Build an Atom, the student builds H, He, C, O and Na, then makes ions (e.g., O²⁻, Na⁺) and records protons, neutrons, electrons, charge and mass number in a table. Ask what determines the element's identity.

- **Q:** An atom has 8 protons, 8 neutrons and 10 electrons. What is it?
  **A:** An oxide ion, O²⁻ (oxygen-16 with a 2− charge).
- **Q:** What determines which element an atom is?
  **A:** The number of protons (atomic number).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Isotopes
*Student:* Calculate average atomic mass from isotope abundances using 'beanium' (three kinds of beans).

*How to teach:* Sort the beans by type, count each type and weigh each group. Model the weighted average: (fraction of type A × average mass of A) + … The student calculates the atomic mass of beanium and explains why it is not simply the average of the three masses.

- **Q:** Chlorine is about 75% Cl-35 and 25% Cl-37. Estimate its average atomic mass.
  **A:** 0.75 × 35 + 0.25 × 37 = 35.5 u (the table lists 35.45).
- **Q:** Why is boron's average atomic mass (10.81) closer to 11 than to 10?
  **A:** Boron-11 is much more abundant (about 80%) than boron-10.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on atomic structure and isotopes.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-item quiz and grade it together; collect the beanium lab sheet. Have the student redo missed isotope calculations.

- **Q:** How many neutrons are in uranium-238 (Z = 92)?
  **A:** 238 − 92 = 146 neutrons.

**Assessment:** Beanium lab and quiz.

**Answer key:** Beanium lab: full credit if mass of each bean type, count, percent abundance, and weighted average are correct. Example: 20 beans at 0.50 g, 15 at 0.80 g, 5 at 1.20 g (40 beans) → abundances 50%, 37.5%, 12.5%; average = 0.50(0.50) + 0.375(0.80) + 0.125(1.20) = 0.70 g. Quiz key: 1) Protons, neutrons, electrons in ⁵⁶Fe: 26, 30, 26. 2) Electrons in Na⁺: 10. 3) Electrons in O²⁻: 10. 4) Why most alpha particles passed through gold foil — atoms are mostly empty space. 5) Why some bounced back — a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus. 6) Define isotope — atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. 7) Copper: 69.15% ⁶³Cu (62.93 u) and 30.85% ⁶⁵Cu (64.93 u); average = 63.55 u. 8) Order the models Dalton → Thomson → Rutherford → Bohr → quantum (electron cloud). 9) Mass number of an atom with 17 protons and 20 neutrons — 37 (chlorine-37). 10) Which subatomic particle has almost no mass — the electron.

## Modern World History: The French Revolution and Napoleon

**Prepare:** The 1789 cartoon of a Third Estate peasant carrying a clergyman and nobleman; Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789); Olympe de Gouges's Declaration of the Rights of Woman (1791) excerpt; Robespierre's 'On the Principles of Political Morality' (February 1794) excerpt; map of Napoleon's empire (1812). Prepare a 5–6 document DBQ set from these sources.

**Watch for:**
- Seeing the Revolution as one event. It moved through phases: moderate (1789–91), radical/Terror (1792–94), Directory (1795–99), Napoleon (1799–1815).
- Viewing Napoleon only as a conqueror. He also spread legal equality (Napoleonic Code) while limiting liberty.

### Day 1 (Monday): Causes
*Student:* Analyze the 'Third Estate carrying the clergy and nobility' cartoon and list causes of the revolution.

*How to teach:* Analyze the cartoon with the student: who is carrying whom, and what does it say? Then list causes of the Revolution: unequal estates and taxes, royal debt (including from aiding the American Revolution), bread prices, and Enlightenment ideas.

- **Q:** What does the cartoon of the peasant carrying the clergy and noble mean?
  **A:** The Third Estate bore the burden of taxes and labor while the privileged First and Second Estates paid little.
- **Q:** Name two economic causes of the French Revolution.
  **A:** Royal debt from wars (including the American Revolution) and rising bread prices after poor harvests, with an unfair tax system.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rights of Man
*Student:* Compare the Declaration of the Rights of Man with de Gouges's response.

*How to teach:* Read selected articles of the Declaration of the Rights of Man and de Gouges's response side by side. The student charts how de Gouges rewrites each article. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What does Article 1 of the Declaration of the Rights of Man state?
  **A:** Men are born and remain free and equal in rights.
- **Q:** What was de Gouges's main argument?
  **A:** Women are born free and equal to men in rights; if a woman can mount the scaffold (be executed), she must also have the right to mount the speaker's rostrum.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Terror
*Student:* Read Robespierre's speech on virtue and terror and evaluate his justification.

*How to teach:* Read Robespierre's speech excerpt and source it (February 1794, to the National Convention, during war and the Terror). The student evaluates his justification of terror. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** How does Robespierre connect virtue and terror?
  **A:** He says virtue without terror is powerless and terror without virtue is fatal; terror is 'prompt, severe, inflexible justice' that flows from virtue.
- **Q:** About how many people were officially executed during the Terror?
  **A:** About 17,000 official executions (with many more deaths in prison and in civil war).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Napoleon
*Student:* Weigh Napoleon's achievements and abuses; locate his empire on a map; explain the Congress of Vienna.

*How to teach:* The student makes a T-chart of Napoleon's achievements (Napoleonic Code, Concordat, Bank of France, merit-based careers, public schools) and abuses (dictatorship, censorship, wars, restoring slavery in the colonies in 1802). Map his empire at its 1812 height, then explain the Congress of Vienna's goals.

- **Q:** What were the goals of the Congress of Vienna (1814–1815)?
  **A:** Restore a balance of power, return legitimate monarchs, and contain France to prevent future revolutions and wars (led by Metternich).
- **Q:** What did the Napoleonic Code establish?
  **A:** Equality of male citizens before the law, religious toleration, and protection of property — but it limited women's rights.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 DBQ
*Student:* Write a document-based essay: Did the French Revolution achieve its goals?

*How to teach:* Give the DBQ: Did the French Revolution achieve its goals? Allow 10 minutes to read documents and plan, 40 minutes to write. Score with the DBQ rubric. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What were the Revolution's stated goals?
  **A:** 'Liberty, equality, fraternity' — ending privilege, securing rights, and creating government by the people.

**Assessment:** DBQ essay.

**Answer key:** DBQ rubric (4-point per row): Thesis — directly answers the question with a defensible claim; Evidence — uses at least 4 documents accurately plus outside knowledge; Sourcing — explains author, purpose, audience or context for at least 2 documents; Analysis — groups documents and explains how they support the claim; Writing — organized, clear. 17–20 = proficient. Strong thesis example: 'The Revolution partly achieved its goals: it ended feudal privilege and spread the idea of rights through the Declaration and Napoleonic Code, but the Terror and Napoleon's dictatorship betrayed liberty, and women were excluded, as de Gouges protested.'

## Spanish III: El imperfecto y la niñez

**Prepare:** Gather 3 childhood photos. Make an imperfect chart (regular -aba/-ía; irregular ser, ir, ver). Arrange an interview with a parent or grandparent.

**Watch for:**
- Using the preterite for habits (*De niño, fui a la escuela a pie): repeated past habits use the imperfect — iba.
- Missing accents on -ía forms and on -ábamos.

### Day 1 (Monday): Imperfecto
*Student:* Learn imperfect forms (regular, ser, ir, ver) and write 8 sentences about childhood habits.

*How to teach:* Your child learns the forms and writes 8 sentences about childhood habits with siempre, a menudo, todos los días.

- **Q:** Translate: 'When I was little, I always went to my grandmother's house.'
  **A:** Cuando era pequeño/a, siempre iba a la casa de mi abuela.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Fotos
*Student:* Describe three childhood photos: where you lived, what you were like, what you used to do.

*How to teach:* Your child describes three photos: where they lived, what they were like, what they used to do.

- **Q:** Translate: 'I was shy and I had long hair.'
  **A:** Era tímido/a y tenía el pelo largo.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Entrevista
*Student:* Interview a parent or grandparent about their childhood using 10 imperfect questions.

*How to teach:* Your child interviews a parent or grandparent with 10 imperfect questions (¿Dónde vivías? ¿A qué escuela ibas? ¿Qué hacías los domingos?).

- **Q:** Ask: 'What did you used to do on Sundays?'
  **A:** ¿Qué hacías los domingos?

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparación
*Student:* Write a paragraph comparing your childhood with theirs.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a comparison paragraph: 'Mi abuelo jugaba en la calle, pero yo jugaba videojuegos.'

- **Q:** Translate: 'My father didn't have a cell phone; I had a tablet.'
  **A:** Mi papá no tenía celular; yo tenía una tableta.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Present your interview findings in a 2-minute talk.

*How to teach:* Your child presents the interview findings for 2 minutes.

- **Q:** Fill in: Mi abuela ___ (ser) muy trabajadora y ___ (ir) al mercado cada día.
  **A:** era; iba

**Assessment:** Interview presentation.

**Answer key:** Interview presentation rubric (2 minutes): 4 = summarizes 6+ facts about the interviewee's childhood in the imperfect (Mi abuela vivía en..., jugaba..., era...) with a comparison to your own; 3 = minor errors; 2 = few facts or mixed tenses; 1 = incomplete.

## Music & Media Arts: Rhythms of the world

**Prepare:** Buckets, pots and wooden spoons for drums; claves or two sticks; salsa recordings; Smithsonian Folkways recordings of Brazilian samba, Ghanaian drumming and Indian classical tala.

**Watch for:**
- Students think syncopation is random off-beat notes. It's deliberate emphasis on weak beats or off-beats against a steady pulse.
- Students play 3-against-2 by guessing. Use 'nice cup of tea' or count 6 pulses: 3-beat part on 1, 3, 5; 2-beat part on 1, 4.
- Students think clave patterns change direction randomly. The 3-2 or 2-3 direction is set by the song and stays consistent.

### Day 1 (Monday): Syncopation
*Student:* Clap and count syncopated rhythms.

*How to teach:* Clap and count syncopated rhythms (e.g., eighth-quarter-eighth patterns, ties across beats).

- **Q:** What is syncopation?
  **A:** Emphasis on normally weak beats or off-beats.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Clave
*Student:* Learn the son clave and play it with salsa recordings.

*How to teach:* Learn the son clave (3-2: beats 1, 2&, 4 | 2, 3) and play it with salsa recordings.

- **Q:** Write the counts of a 3-2 son clave in two bars of 4/4.
  **A:** Bar 1: 1, 2&, 4; Bar 2: 2, 3.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): West African polyrhythm
*Student:* Layer 3-against-2 polyrhythms with family on household drums.

*How to teach:* Layer 3-against-2 polyrhythms with family on household drums.

- **Q:** How do you count 3 against 2?
  **A:** Use six pulses: one part plays on 1, 3, 5 (three notes); the other plays on 1 and 4 (two notes).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Listening log
*Student:* Listen to rhythms from Brazil, Ghana and India (tala) and note features.

*How to teach:* Listen to rhythms from Brazil, Ghana and India and log features.

- **Q:** What is a tala in Indian classical music?
  **A:** A repeating rhythmic cycle of a fixed number of beats (e.g., teental has 16).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Perform a polyrhythm ensemble.

*How to teach:* Perform a polyrhythm ensemble.

- **Q:** What is an ostinato?
  **A:** A short pattern repeated persistently.

**Assessment:** Ensemble performance.

**Answer key:** Ensemble performance rubric: steady pulse maintained by all parts; correct polyrhythm (3:2 aligned on beat 1); son clave played correctly (3-2: beats 1, 2&, 4 | 2, 3); dynamics/balance; 1–2 minutes without breaking. Proficient = all parts locked with at most minor slips. Listening log key features: samba (Brazil) — surdo bass drums, interlocking percussion, 2/4 feel; Ghana (Ewe) — bell timeline, layered polyrhythms, master drum; India — tala cycles such as teental (16 beats), tabla, improvisation within the cycle.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Map, compass and orienteering

**Prepare:** Get a baseplate orienteering compass and print a topographic map of a local park (USGS or CalTopo). Look up local magnetic declination (about 11–14° east in most of California; check NOAA's calculator). Set up 8 controls (flags or cones with letters) in a park and make a control map/answer card.

**Watch for:**
- Students hold the compass near metal or phones. Metal and electronics deflect the needle; hold it level and away from them.
- Students read close contour lines as flat. Close lines mean steep terrain; widely spaced lines mean gentle slopes.
- Students forget declination. True north and magnetic north differ; in California, magnetic north is east of true north, so adjust map bearings accordingly (or use a compass with adjustable declination).

### Day 1 (Monday): Compass parts
*Student:* Learn compass parts and take bearings to five objects in the yard.

*How to teach:* Teach compass parts (baseplate, direction-of-travel arrow, rotating bezel, orienting arrow, magnetic needle). The student takes bearings to five objects in the yard.

- **Q:** How do you take a bearing to an object?
  **A:** Point the direction-of-travel arrow at the object, rotate the bezel until the orienting arrow lines up under the red north needle ('red in the shed'), then read the degrees at the index line.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Map reading
*Student:* Read contour lines and symbols on a topographic map.

*How to teach:* The student reads contour lines and map symbols on a topo map and identifies hills, valleys and steep slopes.

- **Q:** What do V-shaped contour lines pointing uphill indicate?
  **A:** A valley or stream drainage (the V points upstream).
- **Q:** If the contour interval is 40 feet and you cross 5 lines, how much elevation do you gain?
  **A:** 200 feet.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pace count
*Student:* Measure your pace count over 100 m.

*How to teach:* The student walks 100 m three times counting every left-foot step and averages the pace count.

- **Q:** If your pace count is 64 per 100 m, how many paces for 250 m?
  **A:** 160 paces.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Orienteering course
*Student:* Complete a homemade park orienteering course with 8 controls at a jog.

*How to teach:* Run the 8-control park course at a jog; the student navigates with map and compass and records control codes.

- **Q:** What should you do if you realize you're lost?
  **A:** Stop, think, observe, plan (STOP): return to the last known point or use landmarks and the map to relocate.

### Day 5 (Friday): Debrief
*Student:* Reflect on navigation errors and teamwork.

*How to teach:* The student reflects on navigation errors and teamwork.

- **Q:** Name one common navigation error and its fix.
  **A:** Not checking the map often enough — stop at each feature and confirm your position before moving on.

**Assessment:** Orienteering completion time and accuracy.

**Answer key:** Orienteering course: score time and accuracy — 8 of 8 controls with correct codes = full accuracy; subtract time penalties (e.g., 5 minutes) per missed or wrong control. Proficient: all controls found, bearings within ±5°, pace counts within 10% of actual distance.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Traffic signals and pavement markings

**Prepare:** Have the handbook sections on signals and pavement markings, and an online map with street view. Pick three local intersections for Day 4.

**Watch for:**
- Students think a flashing yellow arrow means the left turn is protected. It means turns are permitted but you must yield to oncoming traffic and pedestrians.
- Students think solid yellow lines are never crossed. You may cross a double yellow line to turn left into or out of a driveway or private road (and to make a legal U-turn), but not to pass.
- Students forget what to do when a signal is dark. Treat it as an all-way stop.

### Day 1 (Monday): Signals
*Student:* Study solid, flashing and arrow signals and what to do when a signal is out (treat as all-way stop).

*How to teach:* The student studies each signal type and writes the required action. Quiz orally with quick scenarios.

- **Q:** You approach a flashing red light. What do you do?
  **A:** Come to a complete stop, then go when it is safe, as at a stop sign.
- **Q:** The traffic signals at an intersection are not working. What do you do?
  **A:** Treat it as an all-way stop.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Lane lines
*Student:* Diagram yellow vs. white lines, broken vs. solid, double solid and two-way left-turn lanes.

*How to teach:* The student diagrams yellow vs. white lines, broken vs. solid, double solid, and the two-way center left-turn lane, labeling what each allows.

- **Q:** Can you pass a car across a solid yellow line on your side?
  **A:** No — a solid yellow line on your side means no passing.
- **Q:** What do white lines separate?
  **A:** Lanes of traffic moving in the same direction.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Curb colors
*Student:* Learn curb colors: red no stopping, yellow loading, white passenger loading, green limited time, blue disabled parking.

*How to teach:* The student makes a curb color chart with meanings and one example of each.

- **Q:** What does a white curb mean?
  **A:** Stop only long enough to pick up or drop off passengers or mail.
- **Q:** Can you park at a green curb?
  **A:** Yes, for the limited time posted or painted on the curb.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Street view
*Student:* Use an online street-view map to identify markings at three local intersections.

*How to teach:* Using street view, the student identifies markings at three local intersections (crosswalks, stop lines, turn arrows, bike lanes) and explains what a driver must do.

- **Q:** Where must you stop at a red light if there is a limit line?
  **A:** Before the limit line (white stop line); if none, before the crosswalk; if none, before entering the intersection.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Quiz on signals and markings.

*How to teach:* Give the signals and markings quiz. Review misses.

- **Q:** What does a yellow curb mean?
  **A:** Loading zone: stop only as long as allowed to load or unload passengers or freight; noncommercial drivers usually must stay with the vehicle.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Solid red — stop; right turn after stopping is allowed unless a sign prohibits it. 2) Flashing red — stop, then proceed when safe (like a stop sign). 3) Flashing yellow — proceed with caution. 4) Solid yellow — the signal is about to turn red; stop if you can do so safely. 5) Green arrow — protected turn; still yield to vehicles/pedestrians in the intersection. 6) Signal out — all-way stop. 7) Yellow center lines separate traffic moving in opposite directions; white lines separate lanes in the same direction. 8) Broken line — passing/lane changes allowed when safe. 9) Double solid yellow — no passing. 10) Two sets of double yellow lines two or more feet apart — a barrier; do not cross. 11) Two-way left-turn lane — center lane with solid outer and broken inner yellow lines. 12) Curbs: red no stopping/standing/parking; yellow commercial loading; white passenger/mail loading; green limited-time parking; blue disabled parking only.

## Computer Science Principles: Intellectual property and digital information project

**Prepare:** Preview the U.S. Copyright Office's fair use page and creativecommons.org/licenses. Prepare 10 scenarios to sort (examples in Day 1). Have Creative Commons search (openverse.org) ready for Day 2. Prepare or print a 15-question Unit 1 test drawn from the key below.

**Watch for:**
- Believing anything online is free to use. Online works are copyrighted automatically when created; you need permission, a license, fair use, or public-domain status.
- Thinking giving credit makes any use legal. Attribution is required by CC licenses but does not replace permission for copyrighted work.
- Assuming 'open source' means no rules. Open-source licenses have conditions (e.g. GPL requires sharing derivative source code under the GPL; MIT requires keeping the copyright notice).

### Day 1 (Monday): Copyright basics
*Student:* Read about copyright and fair use and sort 10 scenarios as likely allowed or not allowed.

*How to teach:* Read a short overview of copyright (automatic protection for original work) and fair use (four factors: purpose, nature, amount, market effect). Have the student sort 10 scenarios such as: quoting two lines of a song in a review (likely allowed), uploading a whole movie to YouTube (not allowed), using a 1920s public-domain photo (allowed), a teacher showing a short clip in class to critique it (likely allowed), selling T-shirts with a Disney character (not allowed).

- **Q:** Is copying an entire paid e-book and posting it free online fair use? Why?
  **A:** No — it copies the whole work and harms the market for the original, failing the amount and market-effect factors.
- **Q:** Name the four fair-use factors.
  **A:** Purpose and character of the use, nature of the copyrighted work, amount used, and effect on the market for the original.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Creative Commons
*Student:* Find three images with different Creative Commons licenses and write the correct attribution for each.

*How to teach:* Explain the CC elements: BY (attribution), SA (share-alike), NC (noncommercial), ND (no derivatives), plus CC0 (no rights reserved). Have the student find three images with different licenses on Openverse and write attributions in TASL format: Title, Author, Source, License.

- **Q:** Can you edit an image licensed CC BY-ND and post your edited version?
  **A:** No — ND means no derivatives; you may share it only unchanged (with attribution).
- **Q:** Write a sample TASL attribution.
  **A:** Example: "Golden Gate at Dusk" by J. Smith, via Openverse/Flickr, licensed CC BY 2.0. (Must include title, author, source and license.)

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Open source
*Student:* Compare MIT, GPL and proprietary software licenses and explain how each could help or hurt innovation.

*How to teach:* Compare licenses: MIT (permissive — anyone can use, modify, sell if they keep the copyright notice), GPL (copyleft — derivative works that are distributed must also be released under the GPL with source code), proprietary (code is secret, use restricted by the license agreement). Have the student explain how each could help or hurt innovation.

- **Q:** A company wants to use a library in its closed-source product. Which license is easier for them, MIT or GPL? Why?
  **A:** MIT — it allows use in closed-source products as long as the copyright notice is kept; the GPL would require them to release their product's source code under the GPL if they distribute it.
- **Q:** How can proprietary licensing help innovation?
  **A:** It lets companies earn money from their work, funding research and development; it can hurt by preventing others from building on the code.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Project
*Student:* Create a one-page explainer (infographic or slide) on how one type of digital information is represented, using only properly licensed images.

*How to teach:* Have the student create a one-page infographic or slide explaining how one type of digital information (e.g. images as pixels with RGB values) is represented, using only CC or public-domain images with correct attributions. Check every image's license before they finish.

- **Q:** Where should image attributions go on your infographic?
  **A:** Near the image or in a credits section on the page, with title, author, source and license for each image.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 1 assessment (AP-style multiple choice) on binary, data representation, compression and intellectual property.

*How to teach:* Give the 15-item Unit 1 test in the assessment key (about 40 minutes). Grade together and log missed topics for review in Week 36.

- **Q:** What is the decimal value of 11001₂, and how many bits are needed to show 200?
  **A:** 25; 8 bits (2^7 = 128 is too few, 2^8 = 256 is enough).

**Assessment:** Unit 1 assessment and explainer project.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test (15 items): 1) 1011₂ = 11. 2) 42 = 101010₂. 3) 5 bits give 32 patterns. 4) 1111 + 1 in 4 bits = 0000, overflow. 5) 0.1 + 0.2 ≠ 0.3 because of round-off error. 6) ASCII 'B' = 66. 7) Unicode supports many more characters than ASCII. 8) 50×50 at 24 bpp = 60,000 bits = 7,500 bytes. 9) #0000FF = blue. 10) RLE of WWWWBB = 4W 2B. 11) Lossless: original exactly recoverable; lossy: some data permanently removed. 12) Best for a photo on a website: lossy (JPEG). 13) Abstraction hides unnecessary details to manage complexity. 14) CC BY means reuse allowed with attribution. 15) Public domain works can be used freely without permission. Mastery 12+/15. Explainer project rubric: 4 = accurately explains how one type of data (text/images/sound/numbers) is represented in bits with an example, all images properly licensed and attributed (title, author, source, license); 3 = accurate with one attribution missing; 2 = minor errors in explanation or several missing attributions; 1 = inaccurate or uses unlicensed images.
