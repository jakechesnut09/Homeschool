# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 1

## English 10: Suspense and structure: 'The Most Dangerous Game'

**Prepare:** Print or bookmark 'The Most Dangerous Game' (CommonLit), set up a reading journal (composition book or doc), sticky notes. Read the story yourself first (about 30 minutes). Parent preview: the story involves a hunter who hunts people; violence is mostly implied.

**Watch for:**
- Students write summaries that include opinions ('This exciting story...'). Model an objective summary: who, what, where, how it ends — no judgments.
- Confusing foreshadowing with any description. Foreshadowing is a detail that hints at a later event (e.g., the island's sinister name).

### Day 1 (Monday): Course launch & reading survey
*Student:* Complete a reading-history survey, set up a reading journal, and write a one-page 'reader autobiography' as a writing baseline.

*How to teach:* Walk through the reading survey together and help set up the journal (sections for vocabulary, quotes, responses). The student writes a one-page reader autobiography; save it unmarked as the baseline writing sample for comparison in Weeks 18 and 35.

- **Q:** What should a reader autobiography include?
  **A:** Memorable books and when they were read, reading habits, strengths and struggles as a reader and writer, and a goal for this year.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Read Connell, part 1
*Student:* Read the first half of 'The Most Dangerous Game' and annotate every foreshadowing clue on Ship-Trap Island with sticky notes or margin marks.

*How to teach:* Read the opening conversation aloud together, pausing to model one annotation ('Ship-Trap — a name that warns of a trap'). The student reads to the midpoint (Zaroff's explanation of his hunting) and marks every foreshadowing clue.

- **Q:** What does Whitney say about Ship-Trap Island, and why is it foreshadowing?
  **A:** He says sailors have a curious dread of the place and it has an evil name; it hints that something dangerous awaits on the island.
- **Q:** Early on Rainsford says the world is made up of 'two classes—the hunters and the huntees.' How does this line foreshadow the plot?
  **A:** Rainsford, a hunter, is soon forced to become the hunted, testing his own belief.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Read Connell, part 2
*Student:* Finish the story and chart the rising tension on a plot diagram, quoting one sentence for each stage.

*How to teach:* Review the plot diagram stages. The student finishes the story and places one quoted sentence at each stage; check that the climax is the final bedroom confrontation rather than the leap into the sea.

- **Q:** Which traps does Rainsford build, and what does each do?
  **A:** A Malay man-catcher (injures Zaroff's shoulder), a Burmese tiger pit (kills one of the hounds), and a Ugandan knife trap (kills Ivan).
- **Q:** What does the final line, 'He had never slept in a better bed,' tell the reader?
  **A:** That Rainsford won the fight and Zaroff is dead — the outcome is implied, not stated.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: phrases
*Student:* Identify prepositional, participial and appositive phrases in ten sentences from the story, then write five sentences imitating Connell's participial openers.

*How to teach:* Teach the three phrase types with one example each (prepositional: 'on the island'; participial: 'Gripping the rail, he...'; appositive: 'Zaroff, a Cossack aristocrat, ...'). The student labels ten sentences, then writes five sentences starting with participial phrases.

- **Q:** Label the phrase: 'Bracing himself, Rainsford waited.'
  **A:** 'Bracing himself' is a participial phrase modifying Rainsford.
- **Q:** Label the phrase in 'Ivan, a giant of a man, carried a revolver.'
  **A:** 'a giant of a man' is an appositive phrase renaming Ivan.

### Day 5 (Friday): Summary & discussion
*Student:* Write a 150-word objective summary (no opinion) and discuss with a parent: is Rainsford changed by the end? Cite two lines.

*How to teach:* The student writes the 150-word objective summary, then discusses whether Rainsford is changed, citing two lines. Score the summary with the assessment key. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Is Rainsford changed by the end? Give one piece of evidence.
  **A:** Model answer: Yes — at the start he says 'Who cares how a jaguar feels?' but while hunted he realizes he 'knew now how an animal at bay feels.' Yet he ends by killing Zaroff and sleeping in his bed, so readers can argue he has also become a hunter of men.

**Assessment:** Objective summary scored for accuracy and no opinion; plot diagram with quoted evidence.

**Answer key:** Objective summary (model content): Big-game hunter Sanger Rainsford falls off a yacht near Ship-Trap Island and swims ashore. He finds the chateau of General Zaroff, a Russian aristocrat who, bored with hunting animals, hunts shipwrecked men. Rainsford refuses to join him and is forced to become the prey for three days. He uses traps (a Malay man-catcher, a Burmese tiger pit, a Ugandan knife trap) that injure Zaroff, kill a hound and kill Zaroff's servant Ivan, then leaps into the sea. That night he surprises Zaroff in his bedroom; they fight, and Rainsford sleeps in Zaroff's bed, implying Zaroff is dead. Score with: Rubric (4-point): 4 = accurate, complete, objective (no opinion or 'I'), in the student's own words, key events in order; 3 = one minor inaccuracy or stray opinion; 2 = missing major events or retells too much detail; 1 = inaccurate or mostly opinion. Plot diagram: full credit for a quotation at each stage — exposition (yacht/Whitney conversation), rising action (Zaroff's offer; the hunt), climax (bedroom confrontation), falling action/resolution ('He had never slept in a better bed, Rainsford decided.').

## Geometry: Points, lines, planes and precise definitions

**Prepare:** Gather compass, straightedge, protractor and a notebook for a vocabulary/theorem log. Preview Khan Academy 'Geometry foundations' videos. Write the 7 vocabulary words on cards.

**Watch for:**
- Students treat a line and a segment as the same thing. Insist on notation: line AB has arrows on both ends and no length; segment AB has endpoints and a length.
- Reading a protractor from the wrong scale (getting 130° for a 50° angle). Have the student first decide 'acute or obtuse?' before reading the number.

### Day 1 (Monday): Undefined terms
*Student:* Sketch and label points, lines and planes, then write definitions of segment, ray and angle using only undefined terms.

*How to teach:* Explain that point, line and plane are 'undefined' — we describe them but every other term is built from them. Model sketching and labeling each (point A, line AB or line ℓ, plane ABC or plane M), then have the student write definitions of segment, ray and angle using only those words. Check that each definition mentions points and lines only.

- **Q:** Using only undefined terms, define a segment.
  **A:** A segment AB is the part of a line consisting of two points A and B and all the points between them.
- **Q:** How many points determine a plane, and what condition must they meet?
  **A:** Three points that are not on the same line (noncollinear) determine exactly one plane.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Segment measure
*Student:* Use the Segment Addition Postulate and the distance on a number line to solve 10 problems like AB + BC = AC with algebra.

*How to teach:* Draw a number line and show AB + BC = AC when B is between A and C; distance is the absolute value of the difference of coordinates. Work one algebra example together, then assign the 10 problems, checking that the student substitutes x back to find the actual lengths asked.

- **Q:** On a number line A is at −3 and C is at 9. What is AC, and where is the midpoint?
  **A:** AC = |9 − (−3)| = 12; the midpoint is at 3.
- **Q:** B is between A and C. AB = 3x − 1, BC = 2x + 4, AC = 23. Find x and BC.
  **A:** (3x − 1) + (2x + 4) = 23 → 5x + 3 = 23 → x = 4, so BC = 12.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Angles
*Student:* Measure and classify angles with a protractor and solve angle-addition problems with expressions like (3x + 5)°.

*How to teach:* Model placing the protractor's center on the vertex and the zero line on one ray, then reading the scale that starts at 0 on that ray. Classify angles as acute, right, obtuse or straight, then solve angle-addition equations together before independent practice.

- **Q:** m∠PQS = (3x + 5)°, m∠SQR = (x + 15)°, and m∠PQR = 100°. Find x and m∠PQS.
  **A:** 4x + 20 = 100 → x = 20, so m∠PQS = 65°.
- **Q:** Classify a 90°, a 135° and a 180° angle.
  **A:** Right, obtuse, straight.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Angle pairs
*Student:* Find complementary, supplementary, vertical and linear-pair angles in diagrams and write equations to find unknowns.

*How to teach:* Define each pair with a quick sketch: complementary sum to 90°, supplementary to 180°, vertical angles are congruent, a linear pair is adjacent and supplementary. Have the student label a diagram of two intersecting lines and then write and solve an equation for each problem.

- **Q:** Two angles are complementary. One is 4 times the other. Find both.
  **A:** x + 4x = 90 → x = 18; the angles are 18° and 72°.
- **Q:** Two lines intersect and one angle is 47°. Give the other three angles.
  **A:** 133°, 47° (its vertical angle), and 133°.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Quiz on definitions, segment and angle addition, and angle pairs.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key without notes (about 25 minutes). Grade together, and have the student redo any missed item and explain the error aloud.

- **Q:** Are all linear pairs supplementary? Are all supplementary angles linear pairs?
  **A:** Yes, every linear pair is supplementary. No — supplementary angles need not be adjacent, so they are not always a linear pair.

**Assessment:** Quiz on vocabulary and angle relationships.

**Answer key:** Quiz items and answers: 1) Define a ray using only undefined terms — part of a line that starts at one point and extends forever in one direction through another point. 2) B is between A and C; AB = 2x + 3, BC = x + 1, AC = 19. Find x and AB — 3x + 4 = 19, x = 5, AB = 13. 3) D is in the interior of ∠ABC; m∠ABD = (3x + 5)°, m∠DBC = 2x°, m∠ABC = 75°. Find x — 5x + 5 = 75, x = 14. 4) Complement and supplement of 38° — 52° and 142°. 5) Vertical angles measure (4x − 10)° and (2x + 30)°. Find each — x = 20, each 70°. 6) A linear pair measures (5x + 10)° and (3x + 10)° — x = 20; 110° and 70°. Score 1 point each; 5–6 = mastery, 3–4 = reteach angle equations.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Lab safety, measurement and the phenomenon of fire

**Prepare:** Gather a kitchen scale, measuring cups, a thermometer, safety goggles and a notebook to use as a lab notebook all year. Locate the home fire extinguisher and print or write a simple safety contract (goggles on, long hair tied back, no eating in lab, adult present for flames, know where water and the extinguisher are). Preview Khan Academy 'significant figures' and 'scientific notation' videos.

**Watch for:**
- Students think more digits always means more accurate. Distinguish precision (how close repeated measurements are) from accuracy (how close to the true value), and show that a calculator answer cannot be more precise than the least precise measurement.
- Trailing and leading zeros confuse students (counting 0.0045 as 4 significant figures). Teach the rules: leading zeros never count, captive zeros always count, trailing zeros count only if there is a decimal point.
- Students drop units mid-calculation. Require every number in a dimensional-analysis setup to carry its unit and cancel units visibly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Lab safety
*Student:* Complete a home-lab safety contract, locate a fire extinguisher, and identify hazards in five lab scenarios.

*How to teach:* Walk through the safety contract line by line and have the student sign it. Tour the 'lab' (kitchen) together, physically locating the extinguisher, water source and first-aid kit. Then read five hazard scenarios aloud and have the student name the hazard and the correct action for each.

- **Q:** A student heats a liquid in a sealed jar. What is the hazard?
  **A:** Pressure builds as the liquid and air heat and the jar can burst; never heat a closed container.
- **Q:** Why do we wear goggles even when only mixing vinegar and baking soda?
  **A:** Any splash can reach the eyes, and even mild acids or bases can injure eyes; goggles are worn whenever chemicals or flames are used.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Measurement
*Student:* Measure mass, volume and temperature of water samples three times each and calculate mean and percent error.

*How to teach:* Model reading a scale and a thermometer to one estimated digit beyond the smallest marking. The student measures the mass, volume and temperature of the same water sample three times, records all trials, and computes the mean and percent error = |measured − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100. Check that units are recorded on every number.

- **Q:** Three volume readings are 99, 101 and 100 mL for a 100.0 mL sample. What is the mean and the percent error?
  **A:** Mean = 100 mL; percent error = 0%. The readings are both precise (close together) and accurate.
- **Q:** Readings of 92.1, 92.0 and 92.2 g for a 100.0 g mass are precise or accurate?
  **A:** Precise but not accurate: they agree closely with each other but are about 8% off the true value, which suggests a systematic error such as an untared scale.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sig figs & scientific notation
*Student:* Practice significant figures and scientific notation with 20 problems using your measurement data.

*How to teach:* Teach the significant-figure rules with examples on a whiteboard, then the rules for calculations (multiply/divide: fewest sig figs; add/subtract: fewest decimal places). The student completes the 20 problems using his or her own measurement data, showing the rounding step. Check five problems together before the rest.

- **Q:** How many significant figures are in 0.02030?
  **A:** 4 (the leading zeros do not count; the 2, 0, 3 and trailing 0 after the decimal do).
- **Q:** Calculate the density of a 25.3 g sample with a volume of 12 mL, with correct sig figs.
  **A:** 25.3 ÷ 12 = 2.108… → 2.1 g/mL (2 sig figs, limited by 12 mL).
- **Q:** Write 6,020,000 in scientific notation with 3 significant figures.
  **A:** 6.02 × 10⁶

### Day 4 (Thursday): Anchoring phenomenon
*Student:* Observe a candle burning and write 10 questions: where does the wax go? Start a driving-question board.

*How to teach:* Light a candle (adult supervision, goggles, hair tied back) and have the student observe silently for five minutes, recording at least 10 observations. Then the student writes 10 questions, including 'Where does the wax go?', on sticky notes and starts a driving-question board that will be revisited all year. Do not answer the questions yet.

- **Q:** Is the burning of a candle a physical change, a chemical change, or both?
  **A:** Both: wax melting is a physical change, while wax vapor burning to form carbon dioxide and water is a chemical change.
- **Q:** What evidence suggests that the candle is getting lighter?
  **A:** The candle gets shorter over time and wax does not pile up underneath it; the matter is leaving as invisible gases.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on safety, units, sig figs and dimensional analysis.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz closed-book (calculator allowed) and grade it together immediately, having the student correct each missed item in a different color. Collect the measurement lab sheet and check it against the key.

- **Q:** Convert 450 mg to grams.
  **A:** 450 mg × 1 g/1000 mg = 0.450 g.
- **Q:** Why do scientists use SI units?
  **A:** So measurements are consistent and comparable worldwide; SI units are based on powers of ten, which makes conversions easy.

**Assessment:** Quiz and measurement lab sheet.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Name two items of PPE for a home lab — safety goggles and closed-toe shoes or gloves/apron. 2) What to do if clothing catches fire — stop, drop and roll (or smother with a blanket); never run. 3) Sig figs in 0.00450 — 3. 4) Sig figs in 3.040 — 4. 5) Write 0.000365 in scientific notation — 3.65 × 10⁻⁴. 6) 4.56 × 1.4 = 6.4 (2 sig figs). 7) 12.11 + 18.0 + 1.013 = 31.1 (round to the tenths place). 8) Convert 2.5 L to mL — 2.5 × 10³ mL (2500 mL). 9) Convert 65 mi/h to m/s (1 mi = 1609 m) — 65 × 1609 ÷ 3600 = 29 m/s. 10) A student measures 98.5 g for a 100.0 g standard; percent error — |98.5 − 100.0| ÷ 100.0 × 100 = 1.5%. Lab sheet: full credit when all three trials are recorded with units, the mean is correct, and percent error is calculated with the formula shown. 9–10 = mastery; 7–8 = review sig-fig rules; below 7 = reteach.

## Modern World History: Greek and Roman roots of democracy

**Prepare:** Set up a history notebook (sections for notes, sources, vocabulary). Print excerpts of Pericles' Funeral Oration (from Thucydides), Plato's Allegory of the Cave, and Aristotle's Politics (Book III classification). Find a modern news photograph for sourcing practice.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Athenian democracy included everyone. Only adult male citizens voted — women, enslaved people and foreigners (metics) were excluded.
- Equating 'republic' and 'democracy.' Rome was a republic: citizens elected representatives; Athens practiced direct democracy.

### Day 1 (Monday): Course launch
*Student:* Set up a history notebook and practice SOURCING a document (author, date, audience, purpose) with a modern photograph.

*How to teach:* Teach the SOURCING routine with a modern photograph: Who made it? When? For what audience? Why? The student sets up the notebook and sources the photo, then predicts how the source's perspective might shape what it shows.

- **Q:** What four questions make up 'sourcing' a document?
  **A:** Who created it (author), when, for what audience, and for what purpose.
- **Q:** Why does sourcing matter?
  **A:** A source's author, date and purpose shape what it includes and leaves out, so historians weigh reliability and perspective.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Athens
*Student:* Read Pericles' Funeral Oration excerpt and list what Athenians valued about democracy.

*How to teach:* Source the Funeral Oration first: given by Pericles in 431 BCE to honor Athenian war dead, recorded by Thucydides. The student reads the excerpt and lists what Athenians valued about democracy, then notes who was excluded.

- **Q:** According to Pericles, why is Athens called a democracy?
  **A:** Power is in the hands of the many, not a few; everyone is equal before the law, and advancement depends on merit, not class.
- **Q:** What was the purpose and audience of the speech?
  **A:** To honor the war dead and inspire Athenians to keep fighting in the Peloponnesian War — so it praises Athens and may idealize it.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Plato & Aristotle
*Student:* Read the Allegory of the Cave and Aristotle's classification of governments; chart good and corrupt forms.

*How to teach:* Read the Allegory of the Cave aloud and sketch it. Then read Aristotle's classification and have the student fill a 3×2 chart of rule by one/few/many in good and corrupt forms.

- **Q:** What does Plato's allegory suggest about who should rule?
  **A:** Those who have escaped ignorance and seen the truth — philosophers — should rule (philosopher-kings), not the uninformed majority.
- **Q:** What is the corrupt form of rule by the few in Aristotle's scheme?
  **A:** Oligarchy (the good form is aristocracy).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rome
*Student:* Read about the Roman Republic, Twelve Tables and Cicero; diagram checks and balances in Rome.

*How to teach:* Read a short overview of the Roman Republic, the Twelve Tables and Cicero's idea of natural law. The student diagrams consuls, Senate, assemblies and tribunes with arrows showing who checks whom.

- **Q:** How did the Roman Republic prevent one person from gaining too much power?
  **A:** Two consuls served one-year terms and could veto each other; the Senate advised and controlled funds; tribunes could veto actions harmful to plebeians.
- **Q:** Why were the Twelve Tables important?
  **A:** They put laws in writing and posted them publicly, so laws applied to all and could not be changed at a judge's whim.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz and paragraph: Which Greek or Roman idea most shapes U.S. government today?

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key (about 20 minutes). Then the student writes a constructed-response paragraph: which Greek or Roman idea most shapes U.S. government today? Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Name one Roman institution that resembles part of the U.S. government.
  **A:** The Senate (U.S. Senate), or the veto power (presidential veto), or elected representatives (republic).

**Assessment:** Quiz and constructed response.

**Answer key:** Quiz (answers): 1) Direct democracy → citizens vote on laws themselves (Athens). 2) Who could participate in Athenian democracy? → Adult male citizens only. 3) In the Allegory of the Cave, the shadows represent → appearances/ignorance; leaving the cave represents → education/philosophy and seeing truth. 4) Aristotle's good forms and corrupt forms → monarchy/tyranny, aristocracy/oligarchy, polity/democracy (mob rule). 5) Roman checks → two consuls who could veto each other, a Senate, tribunes who could veto on behalf of plebeians. 6) Twelve Tables → Rome's first written laws (c. 450 BCE), posted publicly. Constructed response rubric (4-point): clear claim naming one idea (e.g., Roman republicanism/checks and balances, Greek citizen participation, written law); two specific connections to U.S. government (Senate, veto, elected representatives, written Constitution); explanation of influence.

## Spanish III: Repaso: presentaciones y el presente

**Prepare:** Print a Spanish I–II placement review (present, preterite, ser/estar, gustar) and set up the notebook and Conjuguemos account. Prepare family-tree paper and a phone for recording.

**Watch for:**
- Using estar with personality adjectives (*Mi tía está simpática): personality uses ser — Mi tía es simpática.
- Forgetting stem changes or yo-go forms (*yo teno, *ella quere): tengo, quiere.
- Saying 'Me llevo bien a mi hermano': the verb is llevarse bien CON — Me llevo bien con mi hermano.

### Day 1 (Monday): Diagnóstico
*Student:* Take a placement review of Spanish I–II grammar and write a 10-sentence self-introduction as a baseline.

*How to teach:* Your child takes the placement review without notes, then writes a 10-sentence self-introduction as a baseline. Mark errors by category (verb form, agreement, ser/estar) to plan reteaching.

- **Q:** Write a sentence with ser and one with estar about yourself.
  **A:** Example: Soy alto y simpático. Hoy estoy un poco cansado.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Presente irregular
*Student:* Drill stem-changing and yo-go verbs on Conjuguemos and write 10 sentences about your daily routine.

*How to teach:* Review boot verbs (e→ie, o→ue, e→i) and yo-go verbs. Your child drills on Conjuguemos and writes 10 routine sentences.

- **Q:** Conjugate preferir for yo and nosotros.
  **A:** prefiero; preferimos
- **Q:** Give the yo form of traer, decir, and conocer.
  **A:** traigo, digo, conozco

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Árbol genealógico
*Student:* Create a labeled family tree and describe five relatives' personalities using ser and adjectives.

*How to teach:* Your child draws a family tree with Spanish kinship terms (el abuelo, la tía, el primo, el sobrino, la cuñada) and describes five relatives' personalities.

- **Q:** Translate: 'My cousin is funny and hard-working.'
  **A:** Mi primo es gracioso y trabajador.
- **Q:** What is 'el apodo'?
  **A:** A nickname.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Entrevista
*Student:* Record a 3-minute interview with a family member or tutor asking 8 questions about likes and routines.

*How to teach:* Your child records a 3-minute interview with 8 questions (¿Qué te gusta hacer los fines de semana? ¿Te llevas bien con tus hermanos?).

- **Q:** Ask 'Do you get along well with your siblings?'
  **A:** ¿Te llevas bien con tus hermanos?

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Quiz on present tense and give a 1-minute spoken self-introduction.

*How to teach:* Give the present-tense quiz and listen to the 1-minute self-introduction, scoring with the checklist.

- **Q:** Correct: 'Mi abuela está muy generosa y tene 70 años.'
  **A:** Mi abuela es muy generosa y tiene 70 años.

**Assessment:** Quiz and speaking checklist.

**Answer key:** Present quiz sample key: querer/ella — quiere; dormir/yo — duermo; pedir/tú — pides; hacer/yo — hago; salir/yo — salgo; conocer/yo — conozco; jugar/ellos — juegan; poder/nosotros — podemos; ir/tú — vas; ser/ustedes — son. Speaking checklist (1-minute self-introduction): name, age, origin, personality (ser + adjectives), likes (gustar), family, routine; 4 = all items, accurate verbs, fluent; 3 = 1–2 items missing or a few errors; 2 = several missing; 1 = minimal.

## Music & Media Arts: Pitch, staff and clefs

**Prepare:** Bookmark musictheory.net (Lessons and Exercises → Note Identification). Install a free keyboard app or use a piano/keyboard. Print a blank grand staff and a 30-note quiz (mix treble and bass, include middle C and a few ledger-line notes). Read the student's musical autobiography prompt yourself.

**Watch for:**
- Students read bass clef as if it were treble (seeing a G line and calling it E). Teach bass clef separately: lines G-B-D-F-A ('Good Boys Do Fine Always'), spaces A-C-E-G ('All Cows Eat Grass').
- Students think middle C is in the middle of the treble staff. Middle C sits on one ledger line below the treble staff (or above the bass staff).
- Students think an octave is eight different note names. An octave spans eight letter steps from one note to the next with the same name (C to C), seven distinct letters.

### Day 1 (Monday): Musical autobiography
*Student:* Write a one-page musical autobiography listing the music that shaped you and set goals for the year.

*How to teach:* The student writes a one-page musical autobiography: songs from childhood, family music, favorite artists, any instruments, and three goals for the year. Discuss it together.

- **Q:** What's one goal that is specific and measurable for this course?
  **A:** Example: 'Name any note on the grand staff within 3 seconds by Week 5' or 'Record an original song by Week 13.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Treble clef
*Student:* Complete the musictheory.net treble-clef lessons and drill until 90% accuracy.

*How to teach:* The student completes musictheory.net treble-clef lessons and drills until 90% accuracy.

- **Q:** Name the notes on the treble-clef lines from bottom to top.
  **A:** E, G, B, D, F.
- **Q:** Name the treble-clef spaces.
  **A:** F, A, C, E.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Bass clef
*Student:* Complete bass-clef lessons and drill; label notes on a printed grand staff.

*How to teach:* The student completes bass-clef lessons and drills, then labels a printed grand staff.

- **Q:** Name the bass-clef lines bottom to top.
  **A:** G, B, D, F, A.
- **Q:** Where is middle C on the grand staff?
  **A:** On the ledger line between the staves — one ledger line below treble, one above bass.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Keyboard
*Student:* Find all C's, F's and G's on a keyboard app and play a C-major scale with correct fingering.

*How to teach:* The student finds all C's (left of the two black keys), F's (left of the three black keys) and G's on a keyboard, then plays a C-major scale with fingering: right hand 1-2-3-1-2-3-4-5 (thumb tucks under after E); left hand 5-4-3-2-1-3-2-1.

- **Q:** How do you find C on a keyboard?
  **A:** It is the white key just to the left of each group of two black keys.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Note-naming quiz (30 notes in 2 minutes).

*How to teach:* Give the 30-note, 2-minute quiz; review misses with mnemonics.

- **Q:** A note sits on the top line of the bass staff. What is it?
  **A:** A.

**Assessment:** Note-naming quiz.

**Answer key:** Note-naming quiz: 30 notes in 2 minutes; 27+ correct (90%) = mastery, 21–26 = more drill, under 21 = reteach with mnemonics. Answer key reference: treble lines E-G-B-D-F, spaces F-A-C-E; bass lines G-B-D-F-A, spaces A-C-E-G; middle C = one ledger line below treble/above bass; treble ledger below staff: D (space below), C (first ledger line); bass ledger above: B (space above), C (first ledger line).

## PE 10 (Course 2): FITNESSGRAM baseline testing

**Prepare:** Measure and mark a 20-meter course with cones (or a measured 1-mile route/track). Download the FITNESSGRAM PACER audio (free from the Cooper Institute/FitnessGram or on YouTube) and cadence audio for curl-ups and push-ups. Get a measuring tape, a yardstick for the sit-and-reach (or a box), a ruler, a bathroom scale and a log sheet. Print the FITNESSGRAM Healthy Fitness Zone (HFZ) chart for the student's age and sex. Check for any health conditions that limit testing.

**Watch for:**
- Students sprint the first PACER laps and burn out. Pace with the beeps; the test gets faster each minute.
- Students bounce during sit-and-reach. Reach slowly and hold the farthest point for one second; bouncing can cause injury and inflates scores.
- Students think BMI measures fitness. BMI is a rough screen of weight relative to height; it doesn't distinguish muscle from fat or measure fitness.

### Day 1 (Monday): Warm-up & safety
*Student:* Learn a dynamic warm-up routine (10 moves) and safety expectations for home and park workouts.

*How to teach:* Teach a 10-move dynamic warm-up (jog, high knees, butt kicks, walking lunges, leg swings, arm circles, inchworms, skips, lateral shuffles, carioca) and safety expectations (hydration, footwear, stop if chest pain/dizziness, buddy or adult nearby at parks).

- **Q:** Why use dynamic stretches before exercise instead of long static holds?
  **A:** Dynamic movements raise heart rate, muscle temperature and range of motion to prepare for activity; long static holds are better after exercise.
- **Q:** Name two warning signs to stop exercising immediately.
  **A:** Chest pain, dizziness or fainting, severe shortness of breath, or sharp joint pain.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Aerobic test
*Student:* Complete the 20-m PACER or 1-mile run and record laps/time.

*How to teach:* After warming up, the student completes the 20-m PACER (or 1-mile run) while you count laps or time it.

- **Q:** When does the PACER end?
  **A:** The second time the student fails to reach the line before the beep.
- **Q:** What fitness component does the PACER measure?
  **A:** Aerobic capacity (cardiorespiratory endurance).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Strength tests
*Student:* Complete curl-ups and 90° push-up tests with correct cadence.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate correct curl-up form (knees bent ~140°, fingertips slide along a measuring strip) and 90° push-up form (body straight, elbows to 90°). The student tests to cadence while you count.

- **Q:** What's the cadence for curl-ups and push-ups?
  **A:** One repetition every 3 seconds.
- **Q:** What counts as a push-up form error?
  **A:** Not lowering to a 90° elbow angle, sagging or piking hips, not fully extending arms, or stopping to rest.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Flexibility & trunk lift
*Student:* Complete back-saver sit-and-reach and trunk lift; measure height/weight for BMI.

*How to teach:* Administer the back-saver sit-and-reach (each side) and trunk lift; measure height and weight and calculate BMI.

- **Q:** Why is the trunk lift capped at 12 inches?
  **A:** Lifting higher can strain the lower back; the test measures healthy extension, not maximum.
- **Q:** Calculate BMI for 130 lb and 5 ft 6 in.
  **A:** 130 × 703 ÷ 66² = 91,390 ÷ 4,356 ≈ 21.0.

### Day 5 (Friday): Analyze
*Student:* Compare results to Healthy Fitness Zones and write a strengths/needs summary.

*How to teach:* The student compares each result to the HFZ chart and writes a strengths/needs summary with one priority to work on.

- **Q:** If your PACER score is below the HFZ, what type of training should you add?
  **A:** Regular aerobic exercise (running, cycling, swimming) at moderate to vigorous intensity, 3–5 days per week.

**Assessment:** Baseline FITNESSGRAM log.

**Answer key:** Baseline log: full credit when every test is recorded with units and date, compared with the HFZ chart, and labeled 'Needs Improvement', 'Healthy Fitness Zone' or above. Protocol checks: PACER — 20 m laps to the beep, test ends at the second missed beep; 1-mile — time in minutes:seconds; curl-ups — cadence of 1 every 3 seconds, maximum 75, ends at second form error; 90° push-ups — 1 every 3 seconds, elbows to 90°, ends at second form correction; back-saver sit-and-reach — one leg bent, record each side to the nearest half inch, maximum 12 inches; trunk lift — face down, lift upper body slowly and hold, measure chin height, maximum 12 inches (HFZ 9–12 in); BMI = weight (lb) × 703 ÷ height (in)². Example: 130 lb, 66 in → 130 × 703 ÷ 4356 = 21.0.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Graduated driver licensing in California

**Prepare:** Download the current California Driver Handbook (dmv.ca.gov) and open the DMV 'Driver's Licenses' and 'Teen Drivers' pages. Read the licensing section yourself first, since rules are occasionally updated — the handbook is the authority if anything here differs. Print a blank driving log template.

**Watch for:**
- Students think the provisional license is a full license. For the first 12 months, provisional drivers may not drive 11 p.m.–5 a.m. or carry passengers under 20 unless accompanied by a licensed parent/guardian, a licensed driver 25 or older, or a certified driving instructor (limited exceptions apply).
- Students think the 50 practice hours can be any time of day. At least 10 of the 50 supervised hours must be at night.
- Students think finishing driver education alone earns a permit. They must also apply, pass the vision and knowledge tests, and have a parent/guardian sign.

### Day 1 (Monday): Why GDL?
*Student:* Read teen crash statistics and explain why California uses graduated licensing; write three questions you want answered this semester.

*How to teach:* Share a teen crash statistic from NHTSA or the CDC (drivers aged 16–19 have the highest crash rate per mile driven of any age group). Discuss how graduated licensing works in stages. The student writes a paragraph on why California uses GDL and three questions to answer this semester.

- **Q:** Why do teens have higher crash rates?
  **A:** Inexperience (poor hazard recognition), higher risk-taking, distraction, and peer passengers; GDL limits the riskiest situations while experience builds.
- **Q:** Name the stages of California's graduated licensing.
  **A:** Instruction permit → provisional license (with restrictions) → full license at 18.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Permit requirements
*Student:* Read the handbook licensing section and list every requirement to get a permit at 15½ (driver ed, application, vision and knowledge tests, parent signature).

*How to teach:* The student reads the handbook licensing section and lists every permit requirement in order: age 15½, driver education, application (online DL 44 form), identity, documents, fee, thumbprint and photo, vision test, knowledge test, parent signature.

- **Q:** What tests must be passed to get a permit?
  **A:** A vision test and a written/online knowledge test on traffic laws and signs.
- **Q:** Who must sign the application for a minor?
  **A:** A parent or legal guardian (both if there is joint custody).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Provisional rules
*Student:* Create a one-page chart of provisional license restrictions: 11 p.m.–5 a.m. limit, no passengers under 20 without a 25+ licensed adult for 12 months, and exceptions.

*How to teach:* The student creates a one-page chart of provisional license restrictions and exceptions. Quiz orally from the chart.

- **Q:** You are 16 with a provisional license for 4 months. Can you drive your 17-year-old friend to the movies alone?
  **A:** No — for the first 12 months you cannot carry passengers under 20 without a licensed parent/guardian, a licensed driver 25 or older, or an instructor.
- **Q:** Can you drive home from work at 11:30 p.m.?
  **A:** Only with a signed note from your employer (a work exception), or with an accompanying licensed adult 25+.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Practice hours plan
*Student:* With a parent, draft a 50-hour supervised driving log template including 10 night hours and behind-the-wheel training sessions.

*How to teach:* Together, build a practice log template with columns for date, time, hours, night (Y/N), conditions and skills practiced, and plan when the 50 hours (10 at night) and 6 hours of professional training will happen.

- **Q:** How many supervised hours are required, and how many at night?
  **A:** 50 hours, including at least 10 at night.
- **Q:** How long must you hold your permit before taking the drive test?
  **A:** At least 6 months.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Take a 15-question handbook-style quiz on licensing rules.

*How to teach:* Give the 15-question quiz. Grade together; the student finds the handbook page for each missed item.

- **Q:** When do the provisional restrictions on passengers and night driving end?
  **A:** After 12 months of holding the provisional license.

**Assessment:** Licensing quiz.

**Answer key:** Licensing quiz key (15 items): 1) Minimum age for a permit — 15½. 2) Driver education required before the permit — yes, completed (or enrolled in) a state-approved course. 3) Parent/guardian signature required — yes. 4) Tests at the permit appointment — vision and knowledge test. 5) Minimum time holding a permit before the drive test — 6 months. 6) Supervised practice — 50 hours, 10 at night. 7) Professional behind-the-wheel training — 6 hours (required for applicants under 17½). 8) Minimum age for a provisional license — 16. 9) Who must accompany a permit driver — a California-licensed parent, guardian, spouse, adult 25+, or certified instructor. 10) Provisional night restriction — no driving 11 p.m.–5 a.m. for the first 12 months. 11) Passenger restriction — no passengers under 20 for the first 12 months unless accompanied by a licensed driver 25+ (or parent/guardian or instructor). 12) Exceptions — medical necessity, school or school activity, work (with signed notes), or transporting an immediate family member when necessary. 13) Restrictions end — after 12 months (provisional status continues until 18). 14) Why GDL exists — new teen drivers have the highest crash rates; gradual experience reduces crashes. 15) Under-18 phone rule — no use of any wireless device, even hands-free. Score: 14–15 mastery; 12–13 review; below 12 reteach.

## Computer Science Principles: Representing information and abstraction

**Prepare:** Create a Code.org account (code.org/educate/csp) and set up a CSP journal (notebook or doc). Have index cards and a working flashlight. Preview the Code.org Unit 1 opening lessons and the 'Bits and patterns' 2^n table so you can check it.

**Watch for:**
- Students think 'abstraction' means 'vague' or 'abstract art'. In computing it means hiding details you don't need so you can focus on what matters — ask 'what details are hidden here, and who is spared from thinking about them?'
- Students think a computer 'understands' letters or pictures directly. Stress that everything is stored as bits; letters, images and sounds are interpretations (abstractions) layered on top of 0s and 1s.
- Doubling confusion: students add 2 per extra bit instead of doubling. Show that each new bit doubles the patterns because every old pattern can now end in 0 or 1.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is computing?
*Student:* Set up a Code.org CSP account and journal; list five computing devices you used today and the input, processing and output of each.

*How to teach:* Ask the student what they think 'computing' means, then have them list five devices used today (phone, microwave, car, TV remote, game console) and fill in a three-column table: input, processing, output. Model one row first (phone camera: light hits sensor / processor converts and stores image / photo on screen). Check that 'processing' is something the device computes, not just 'it works'.

- **Q:** For a digital thermostat, name the input, processing and output.
  **A:** Input: temperature sensor reading and user's setpoint; processing: compare the room temperature with the setpoint; output: turn the heater/AC on or off and show the temperature on the display.
- **Q:** Is a non-digital light switch a computing device? Why or why not?
  **A:** No — it just completes a circuit; it does not take input data, process it with instructions and produce output.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Representing with symbols
*Student:* Invent a code using a flashlight (on/off) to send a 4-choice answer to a partner and record how many on/off signals each message needed.

*How to teach:* Explain that the student must send one of four answers (A, B, C, D) using only on/off flashes. Let them invent a code, then ask: what's the fewest signals needed per answer? Guide them to realize 2 on/off signals give 4 patterns (off-off, off-on, on-off, on-on). Record the number of signals each message needed.

- **Q:** How many on/off signals are needed to send one of 4 choices? One of 8?
  **A:** 2 signals give 4 patterns; 3 signals give 8 patterns.
- **Q:** Why does a code with a fixed length per message (e.g. always 2 flashes) make decoding easier?
  **A:** The receiver knows exactly where each message ends, so it doesn't need a pause or special end signal to separate messages.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Abstraction
*Student:* Compare a subway map to a real street map and write how each hides details; then describe two abstractions in your phone's interface.

*How to teach:* Show a subway (transit) map next to a street map of the same city. Ask what the subway map leaves out (exact distances, streets, buildings, curves) and why that helps a rider. Then have the student name two abstractions on their phone (app icons hide program files; a 'battery %' hides voltage chemistry). Check that each answer names the hidden detail.

- **Q:** What does a subway map hide, and why is that useful?
  **A:** It hides real distances, street layout and exact geography so riders can focus on stops, lines and transfers — the only details they need to navigate.
- **Q:** Give one example of abstraction in a phone's interface and the detail it hides.
  **A:** Example: the trash-can icon for 'delete' hides the file-system operations that remove the file's data/pointer; the volume slider hides the digital audio amplitude values. Any example naming a hidden detail is correct.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Bits and patterns
*Student:* Calculate how many patterns 1–8 bits can make (2^n) and complete a table showing why 8 bits make 256 values.

*How to teach:* Build a table with columns n (bits) and patterns. Have the student list all patterns for 1, 2 and 3 bits by hand, notice doubling, then extend with 2^n up to 8 bits. Ask why each extra bit doubles the count.

- **Q:** Complete: 1 bit → 2, 2 → 4, 3 → 8, 4 → ?, 5 → ?, 8 → ?
  **A:** 4 bits → 16, 5 bits → 32, 8 bits → 256.
- **Q:** What is the smallest number of bits needed to give each of 100 students a unique ID?
  **A:** 7 bits, because 2^6 = 64 is too few and 2^7 = 128 is enough.
- **Q:** Why do 8 bits make 256 values but the largest number is 255?
  **A:** The 256 patterns include 0 (00000000), so they represent 0 through 255.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a 10-question check on bits and abstraction and write a paragraph explaining how a touchscreen tap is an abstraction.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-question check (assessment key) closed-notes, about 20 minutes, then have the student write the touchscreen paragraph. Grade together and have the student correct any wrong items aloud.

- **Q:** In one sentence, how is a touchscreen tap an abstraction?
  **A:** The user simply taps an icon, but underneath the screen senses a change in electrical charge, converts the location to coordinates (bits), and the operating system interprets them as a 'tap' event — all hidden from the user.

**Assessment:** Quiz and abstraction paragraph.

**Answer key:** 10-question check: 1) A bit is a binary digit, a single 0 or 1. 2) 3 bits make 2^3 = 8 patterns. 3) 8 bits (one byte) make 256 patterns. 4) To represent 26 letters you need at least 5 bits (2^4 = 16 is too few, 2^5 = 32 is enough). 5) Abstraction = reducing complexity by hiding unnecessary details. 6) Example of abstraction: a 'Send' button hides the networking steps (any valid example). 7) Input/processing/output of a calculator: key presses / arithmetic by the processor / number on the screen. 8) Adding one bit doubles the number of patterns. 9) A flashlight on/off is a binary (two-state) signal. 10) 10 bits make 1,024 patterns. Paragraph rubric (4 pts): 4 = explains that the tap is converted to electrical signals and coordinates (bits) that the OS interprets as a touch event, names what details the user doesn't see, and uses the word abstraction correctly; 3 = correct idea with one detail missing; 2 = says 'it hides details' without explaining what; 1 = misuses abstraction.
