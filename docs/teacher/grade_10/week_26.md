# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 26

## English 10: Research paper: question, sources and notes

**Prepare:** Public library card and database access (e.g., Gale, EBSCO), Purdue OWL MLA pages, note-taking template. Help the student narrow a topic (e.g., the Civil Rights Act, the Rwandan genocide, apartheid's end, the Universal Declaration).

**Watch for:**
- Choosing a topic too broad ('human rights'). Narrow it to a question that can be answered in 5–7 pages.
- Trusting a source because it looks professional. Apply the CRAAP test every time.

### Day 1 (Monday): Question
*Student:* Choose a human-rights topic from the 20th–21st century and draft a focused research question.

*How to teach:* Model narrowing: topic → subtopic → question. The student drafts a focused research question. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Make this question more focused: 'What about human rights in Africa?'
  **A:** For example: 'How did international sanctions contribute to the end of apartheid in South Africa?'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Source search
*Student:* Locate six sources using library databases and evaluate each with the CRAAP test.

*How to teach:* Show how to search a database with keywords and filters. The student finds six sources and evaluates each with CRAAP. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does each letter of CRAAP stand for?
  **A:** Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Annotated bibliography
*Student:* Write MLA annotated bibliography entries for four sources.

*How to teach:* Model one annotated entry. The student writes four. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What three things should an annotation do?
  **A:** Summarize the source, evaluate its credibility, and explain how it will be used in the paper.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Note-taking
*Student:* Take organized notes, labeling paraphrase, summary and direct quote.

*How to teach:* Show a note-card or digital template with source, page and type (P/S/Q). The student takes organized notes from at least four sources, labeling paraphrase, summary and direct quote. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Why label notes P, S or Q?
  **A:** To avoid accidental plagiarism — you'll know which words are the author's and need quotation marks.

### Day 5 (Friday): Thesis & outline
*Student:* Write a thesis and detailed outline with source assignments.

*How to teach:* The student writes a thesis and detailed outline, assigning sources to each section. Check the annotated bibliography with the rubric. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What should a research thesis do?
  **A:** Answer the research question with an arguable or explanatory claim that previews the main points.

**Assessment:** Annotated bibliography check.

**Answer key:** Annotated bibliography check: each entry has a correct MLA citation (Author. 'Title.' Container, Publisher, Date, URL/DOI) and a 3–5 sentence annotation summarizing the source, evaluating credibility (CRAAP: Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose), and stating how it will be used. 4 = four entries all complete and correctly formatted; 3 = minor formatting errors; 2 = annotations lack evaluation; 1 = fewer than four sources.

## Geometry: Equations of circles and parabolas

**Prepare:** Desmos, graph paper. Prepare the Unit 7 test (assessment key).

**Watch for:**
- Sign errors with the center: (x + 3)² means h = −3. Rewrite as (x − (−3))².
- Forgetting to add the same constants to both sides when completing the square.

### Day 1 (Monday): Circle equation
*Student:* Derive (x – h)² + (y – k)² = r² from the distance formula.

*How to teach:* Take a point (x, y) on a circle with center (h, k) and radius r; the distance formula squared gives the equation. Practice writing equations and reading center/radius.

- **Q:** Write the equation of the circle with center (2, −3) and radius 5.
  **A:** (x − 2)² + (y + 3)² = 25.
- **Q:** Give the center and radius of (x + 1)² + (y − 4)² = 49.
  **A:** Center (−1, 4), radius 7.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Completing the square
*Student:* Convert general form to standard form to find center and radius.

*How to teach:* Model completing the square for both x and y terms, adding (b/2)² to both sides. The student converts several equations and checks in Desmos.

- **Q:** Find the center and radius of x² + y² − 6x + 4y − 12 = 0.
  **A:** (x − 3)² + (y + 2)² = 12 + 9 + 4 = 25: center (3, −2), radius 5.
- **Q:** Find the center and radius of x² + y² + 8x − 9 = 0.
  **A:** (x + 4)² + y² = 25: center (−4, 0), radius 5.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Proving points on circles
*Student:* Prove or disprove that a point lies on a given circle.

*How to teach:* Substitute the point into the equation, or compute its distance from the center. The student proves or disproves points on circles, including one with radius √ form.

- **Q:** Does (1, √3) lie on the circle centered at the origin with radius 2?
  **A:** 1 + 3 = 4 = 2², so yes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Parabola
*Student:* Derive the equation of a parabola from a focus and directrix and graph in Desmos.

*How to teach:* Define a parabola as all points equidistant from a focus and a directrix. Set distance to focus equal to distance to directrix and simplify; graph in Desmos.

- **Q:** Find the equation of the parabola with focus (0, 2) and directrix y = −2.
  **A:** x² + (y − 2)² = (y + 2)² → x² = 8y, or y = x²/8.
- **Q:** Where is the vertex of that parabola?
  **A:** At (0, 0), halfway between focus and directrix.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 test
*Student:* Unit 7 test on circles.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test from the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** What does r² represent in the circle equation?
  **A:** The square of the radius — the squared distance from every point on the circle to the center.

**Assessment:** Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 7 test with answers: 1) Inscribed angle on a 110° arc → 55°. 2) Tangent from a point 10 from center of radius 6 → 8. 3) Arc length r = 9, 40° → 2π ≈ 6.28. 4) Sector area r = 4, 135° → 6π ≈ 18.85. 5) Equation of circle center (2, −3), radius 5 → (x − 2)² + (y + 3)² = 25. 6) x² + y² − 6x + 4y − 12 = 0 → (x − 3)² + (y + 2)² = 25; center (3, −2), r = 5. 7) Is (6, 2) on that circle? → 9 + 16 = 25, yes. 8) Parabola focus (0, 2), directrix y = −2 → x² = 8y. 9) Convert 225° to radians → 5π/4.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Climate data and feedbacks

**Prepare:** Download NOAA Mauna Loa annual mean CO₂ (gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends) and Vostok ice core CO₂ and temperature data (NOAA Paleoclimatology or CDIAC). Open NASA Climate 'Vital Signs' and Cal-Adapt (cal-adapt.org) for California projections. Prepare a spreadsheet.

**Watch for:**
- Students think correlation in ice cores proves CO₂ alone drove past cycles. In ice ages, orbital changes started warming and CO₂ then amplified it (a feedback); today CO₂ is rising first because of human emissions.
- Students think the yearly Keeling sawtooth is pollution changing seasonally. It is Northern Hemisphere plants taking up CO₂ in summer and releasing it in winter.
- Students think 'positive feedback' means good. Positive feedback amplifies a change; negative feedback dampens it.

### Day 1 (Monday): Keeling Curve
*Student:* Graph Mauna Loa CO₂ data and calculate the average yearly increase.

*How to teach:* The student graphs Mauna Loa annual mean CO₂ from 1959 to the latest year, calculates the average yearly increase overall and for the last ten years, and describes the shape (accelerating). Discuss the monthly sawtooth.

- **Q:** CO₂ was about 317 ppm in 1960 and 425 ppm in 2024. What is the average annual increase?
  **A:** (425 − 317) ÷ 64 ≈ 1.7 ppm/yr.
- **Q:** Why does CO₂ drop each Northern Hemisphere summer?
  **A:** Plants in the Northern Hemisphere, which has most of the land, absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis during the growing season.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ice core data
*Student:* Analyze Vostok ice core CO₂ and temperature data for correlation.

*How to teach:* Graph Vostok CO₂ and temperature on the same time axis (or a scatter plot). The student describes the correlation and compares the maximum ice-core value with today.

- **Q:** What relationship do the Vostok data show?
  **A:** A strong positive correlation: warm interglacials have high CO₂ (~280–300 ppm) and ice ages have low CO₂ (~180 ppm).
- **Q:** How do the bubbles in ice record past CO₂?
  **A:** Air trapped in the ice as snow compacts preserves samples of the ancient atmosphere.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Feedbacks
*Student:* Diagram the ice-albedo and permafrost feedbacks.

*How to teach:* The student draws loop diagrams for the ice-albedo and permafrost feedbacks, with + signs on each arrow, and one negative feedback for contrast (e.g., warmer Earth radiates more infrared).

- **Q:** Explain the permafrost feedback.
  **A:** Warming thaws frozen soil; microbes decompose organic matter, releasing CO₂ and methane, which cause more warming.
- **Q:** Give an example of a negative feedback.
  **A:** A warmer Earth emits more infrared radiation to space, which tends to cool it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Forecast
*Student:* Use NASA projections to forecast CA temperature and snowpack in 2050 under two scenarios.

*How to teach:* Use Cal-Adapt or NASA projections to compare a lower-emissions and higher-emissions scenario for the student's region in 2050 (temperature, extreme-heat days, Sierra snowpack). The student writes a short forecast with numbers and sources.

- **Q:** Why do forecasts give two or more scenarios?
  **A:** Future warming depends on how much greenhouse gas humans emit, which is uncertain; scenarios show outcomes for different emission paths.
- **Q:** Why does Sierra snowpack matter to California?
  **A:** It stores water that melts in spring and summer, supplying a large share of the state's water; less snowpack means less stored water when it's needed.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Data-analysis quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the data-analysis quiz (graph reading and calculations). Grade together.

- **Q:** A graph shows global temperature rising 0.2 °C per decade. How much warming is that over 30 years?
  **A:** 0.6 °C.

**Assessment:** Forecast write-up and quiz.

**Answer key:** Forecast write-up rubric: 4 = Uses NASA/Cal-Adapt projections to state a 2050 California temperature change under lower and higher emissions (roughly +2 to +4 °F above late-20th-century averages, larger under high emissions) and the direction of snowpack change (substantial decline, larger under high emissions), cites the data source and explains uncertainty and the role of feedbacks. Quiz key: 1) Mauna Loa CO₂ rose from about 317 ppm (1960) to about 425 ppm (2024): average ≈ 1.7 ppm/yr; recent decade ≈ 2.4–2.5 ppm/yr. 2) Vostok CO₂ ranged ~180–300 ppm over 400,000 years. 3) Today's ~425 ppm is far above any value in the 800,000-year ice-core record. 4) Ice-albedo feedback: warming melts ice → lower albedo → more absorption → more warming (positive). 5) Permafrost feedback: thawing releases CO₂ and CH₄ → more warming (positive). 6) The seasonal cycle is caused by Northern Hemisphere photosynthesis/respiration.

## Modern World History: China's revolution and the Cold War in Asia

**Prepare:** Excerpts of Mao's Quotations ('Little Red Book'), Cultural Revolution posters, Red Scarf Girl (Ji-li Jiang) excerpt, famine data for the Great Leap Forward, Korean War map.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking China's communists won quickly. The civil war lasted decades.
- Assuming Deng abandoned communism. He kept one-party rule while allowing markets.

### Day 1 (Monday): Civil war
*Student:* Trace the Nationalist–Communist conflict and the Long March.

*How to teach:* Trace the Nationalist–Communist conflict from the 1920s through the Long March (1934–35) to the Communist victory in 1949. The student builds a timeline. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What was the Long March?
  **A:** A 1934–35 retreat of about 6,000 miles by the Communists; it made Mao the leader.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Korea
*Student:* Map the Korean War and its outcome.

*How to teach:* The student maps the Korean War's phases (North invasion 1950, Inchon, Chinese entry, stalemate, 1953 armistice). Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** How did the Korean War end?
  **A:** With an armistice in 1953 near the 38th parallel.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Great Leap
*Student:* Analyze the Great Leap Forward and famine data.

*How to teach:* Read about the Great Leap Forward and look at famine data. The student explains how policy caused famine. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What was the Great Leap Forward?
  **A:** Mao's 1958–62 plan for rapid industrialization; it caused a famine killing tens of millions.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cultural Revolution
*Student:* Analyze posters and a memoir excerpt (e.g., Red Scarf Girl).

*How to teach:* Show Cultural Revolution posters and read a Red Scarf Girl excerpt. The student analyzes the propaganda and the memoir's perspective. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

### Day 5 (Friday): Deng's reforms
*Student:* Compare Mao's and Deng's economic policies.

*How to teach:* The student completes the Mao vs. Deng comparison chart from the assessment key. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What were special economic zones?
  **A:** Areas open to foreign investment and markets, like Shenzhen.

**Assessment:** Comparison chart.

**Answer key:** Comparison chart (Mao vs. Deng): Mao — collectivization, communes, Great Leap Forward (1958–62, famine killed an estimated 30–45 million), Cultural Revolution (1966–76), ideology first. Deng — reforms from 1978, household responsibility system, special economic zones (Shenzhen), foreign investment, rapid growth, but one-party rule and Tiananmen Square crackdown (1989).

## Spanish III: Mi futuro profesional

**Prepare:** Chart adverbial clauses of time: cuando, tan pronto como, en cuanto, hasta que, después de que + subjunctive for future events; + indicative for habits or past events. Antes de que always takes the subjunctive. Write the Unit 7 test (careers, emotion/doubt subjunctive, adverbial clauses, community).

**Watch for:**
- Using the future after cuando (*Cuando tendré 25 años): Cuando tenga 25 años, viviré en...
- Using the subjunctive for habits: 'Cuando llego a casa, como' (habit = indicative).
- Using 'después de que' with the same subject: use después de + infinitive (Después de graduarme, trabajaré).

### Day 1 (Monday): Cláusulas adverbiales
*Student:* Learn cuando, tan pronto como, hasta que + subjunctive for future events.

*How to teach:* Teach the rule: pending/future action → subjunctive; habitual or completed → indicative. Your child completes 15 sentences.

- **Q:** Translate: 'When I graduate, I will travel.'
  **A:** Cuando me gradúe, viajaré.
- **Q:** Choose: 'Siempre como cuando ___ (llegar) a casa.'
  **A:** llego (habit → indicative)

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plan
*Student:* Write a plan for your future career using future and subjunctive.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a career plan with 10 sentences using the future and adverbial clauses.

- **Q:** Translate: 'As soon as I have money, I will buy a car.'
  **A:** Tan pronto como tenga dinero, compraré un carro (coche).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Video
*Student:* Record a 3-minute video 'Mi futuro'.

*How to teach:* Your child records a 3-minute video 'Mi futuro'.

- **Q:** Translate: 'I will work until I find my ideal job.'
  **A:** Trabajaré hasta que encuentre mi trabajo ideal.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Retroalimentación
*Student:* Peer/parent feedback and revision.

*How to teach:* Give feedback on the video (content, grammar, pronunciation); your child revises and re-records weak parts.

- **Q:** Correct: 'Después de que me graduaré, buscaré trabajo.'
  **A:** Después de que me gradúe, buscaré trabajo (or Después de graduarme, ...).

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Unit 7 test.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test.

- **Q:** Fill in: Te llamaré en cuanto ___ (saber) los resultados.
  **A:** sepa

**Assessment:** Video and unit test.

**Answer key:** Unit 7 test sample key: 1) Cuando me gradúe, iré a la universidad 2) Tan pronto como llegues, llámame 3) Trabajaré hasta que tenga suficiente dinero 4) Cuando era niño, jugaba (past habit = indicative) 5) Antes de que salgas, cierra la puerta 6) Me alegro de que consigas el trabajo 7) No creo que sea fácil. Video rubric (3 minutes, 'Mi futuro'): 4 = clear career plan, 6+ future verbs, 4+ adverbial clauses with correct subjunctive, fluent; 3 = minor errors; 2 = short or many errors; 1 = incomplete.

**Prepare:** Audacity, the draft and feedback, a professional podcast for comparison, the podcast rubric, CC attribution examples.

- Students normalize peaks and think loudness is fixed. Loudness normalization (e.g., about −16 LUFS for podcasts) matches perceived volume across episodes.

*How to teach:* Re-edit using the three most important notes, tightening segments over 2 minutes.

*How to teach:* Write show notes with summary, timestamps and CC attributions.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Loudness check

*How to teach:* Normalize the episode in Audacity and export MP3; compare against a professional podcast.

- **Q:** What loudness target do many podcast platforms recommend?

### Day 4 (Thursday): Critique

*How to teach:* Score your episode and a professional one on the same rubric.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect

**Assessment:** Podcast rubric.

**Answer key:** Podcast rubric (each 1–4): Story (hook, structure, clear focus), Audio quality (clean, consistent loudness ~−16 LUFS, no clipping), Pacing (segments ≤2 min of drag, transitions), Voice (natural delivery), Show notes (50-word summary, timestamps, CC attributions). Proficient 15+/20.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Suicide prevention

**Prepare:** Parental preview required: read the 988 Lifeline site (988lifeline.org) and a reliable warning-signs page (e.g., American Foundation for Suicide Prevention or NIMH). Check in privately with your teen. If you're ever worried about your teen's immediate safety, call 911 or 988. Keep discussions factual and caring; it is safe and helpful to talk about suicide directly.

**Watch for:**
- Students think asking about suicide puts the idea in someone's head. Research shows asking directly does not increase risk and can open the door to help.
- Students think they should keep a friend's suicidal thoughts secret. Safety comes before secrecy; always tell a trusted adult.
- Students think people who talk about suicide won't do it. Talk of suicide is a warning sign and must be taken seriously.

### Day 1 (Monday): Warning signs
*Student:* Learn suicide warning signs and risk and protective factors from a reliable source.

*How to teach:* The student learns warning signs and risk and protective factors from a reliable source and makes a chart.

- **Q:** Name three warning signs of suicide.
  **A:** Talking about wanting to die or being a burden, withdrawing from others, giving away possessions, increased substance use, saying goodbye, extreme mood changes.
- **Q:** Name two protective factors.
  **A:** Strong connections with family and friends, access to mental health care, problem-solving skills, limited access to lethal means.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ask, care, tell
*Student:* Practice asking directly and caringly ('Are you thinking about suicide?') and listening without judgment through scripted role-plays.

*How to teach:* Model: 'I've noticed you seem really down. Are you thinking about suicide?' Practice listening without judgment in scripted role-plays.

- **Q:** What should you do after a friend says yes, they're thinking about suicide?
  **A:** Stay calm, thank them for telling you, listen, don't leave them alone if in danger, and connect them with a trusted adult or 988 right away.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): 988 and resources
*Student:* Learn how the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline works (call, text, chat) and list school, county and online resources.

*How to teach:* The student learns how 988 works (call, text, chat) and lists school, county and online resources.

- **Q:** How can someone reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline?
  **A:** Call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org; free, confidential and available 24/7.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Safety plan
*Student:* Complete a sample safety plan template and identify who you would tell if a friend were at risk.

*How to teach:* The student completes a sample safety plan template (not necessarily about themselves) and names who they would tell if a friend were at risk.

- **Q:** Why is reducing access to lethal means part of a safety plan?
  **A:** Suicidal crises are often brief; making methods harder to reach gives time for the crisis to pass and saves lives.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit test
*Student:* Unit 7 test on mental health, coping and suicide prevention.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test. Review, and remind the student of trusted adults and 988.

- **Q:** If a friend asks you to promise not to tell anyone they're suicidal, what do you do?
  **A:** Don't promise; tell a trusted adult right away because their safety matters more than secrecy.

**Assessment:** Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 7 test key (sample): 1) Warning signs — talking about wanting to die or being a burden, looking for ways to end one's life, withdrawing, giving away possessions, extreme mood swings, increased substance use, saying goodbye, sudden calm after depression. 2) Risk factors — depression or other mental illness, previous attempt, substance use, access to lethal means, family history, bullying, loss. 3) Protective factors — connection to family/friends/school, access to care, coping skills, reasons for living, restricted access to lethal means. 4) What to say — 'Are you thinking about suicide?' 5) Next steps — listen, stay with them, remove access to means if safe, tell a trusted adult, call/text 988 or 911 if in immediate danger. 6) 988 — call, text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org; 24/7, free, confidential. 7) Coping strategies and I-statements from Unit 7. 8) Depression vs. normal sadness — 2+ weeks, affects functioning. 9) Stigma prevents help-seeking. 10) Safety plan parts — warning signs, coping strategies, people/places for distraction, people to ask for help, professionals/crisis lines, making the environment safe.

## Computer Science Principles: Bias in data and machine learning

**Prepare:** Open Teachable Machine (teachablemachine.withgoogle.com) and test the image project with a webcam. Choose a credible article on algorithmic bias (e.g. coverage of the 2018 MIT 'Gender Shades' facial-recognition study or the reported Amazon hiring-tool bias). Visit zooniverse.org. Prepare the Unit 6 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking computers are neutral so algorithms can't be biased. Algorithms learn from data created by people; biased data or design produces biased results.
- Thinking more data always fixes bias. More of the same unrepresentative data repeats the bias; data must be representative.
- Believing bias only enters through training data. It can enter in problem definition, data collection, labeling, design choices and how results are used.

### Day 1 (Monday): Bias in data
*Student:* Identify the sampling bias in five survey scenarios (e.g., an online-only poll about internet access) and rewrite one to fix it.

*How to teach:* Present five survey scenarios and have the student name the bias, e.g. an online-only poll about internet access (excludes people offline), a gym survey about exercise habits (sample already exercises), a landline phone poll (misses younger people). Rewrite one.

- **Q:** Why is an online-only poll about home internet access biased?
  **A:** Only people with internet access can answer, so it overestimates access.
- **Q:** How would you fix it?
  **A:** Sample randomly from the whole population using several methods (mail, phone, in person), not just online.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Machine learning
*Student:* Train an image classifier in Teachable Machine with deliberately unbalanced training data, then retrain with balanced data and compare accuracy.

*How to teach:* In Teachable Machine, train two classes (e.g. 'thumbs up' vs. 'thumbs down') with 50 images of one and 5 of the other, under one lighting condition. Test, then retrain with balanced, varied data and compare accuracy.

- **Q:** What happened to accuracy for the class with only 5 examples?
  **A:** It was lower — the model had too few examples to learn that class, so it often predicted the larger class.
- **Q:** What is training data?
  **A:** The labeled examples a machine-learning model uses to learn patterns.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Algorithmic bias
*Student:* Read a credible news article on facial recognition or hiring-algorithm bias and identify where in the pipeline the bias entered.

*How to teach:* Read the article and have the student identify where bias entered the pipeline (data collection, labeling, model design, deployment).

- **Q:** In the Gender Shades study, what did researchers find about commercial facial-analysis systems?
  **A:** They were much less accurate for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men, largely because training and benchmark data underrepresented darker-skinned faces.
- **Q:** Where did bias enter in a hiring algorithm trained on past hires from a male-dominated field?
  **A:** In the training data — it learned patterns from historically biased hiring decisions and penalized résumés associated with women.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Crowdsourcing and fixes
*Student:* Explore a citizen-science project (e.g., Zooniverse) and write a checklist developers can use to test an app for bias.

*How to teach:* Explore a Zooniverse project and discuss crowdsourcing/citizen science. Then the student writes a bias-testing checklist for developers.

- **Q:** What is citizen science?
  **A:** Scientific research in which members of the public help collect or analyze data, often online.
- **Q:** Name one benefit and one risk of crowdsourcing data.
  **A:** Benefit: many people can process huge amounts of data quickly. Risk: contributors may not represent everyone, or quality may vary.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 6 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 6 assessment on data collection, visualization, simulation and bias.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test in the assessment key.

- **Q:** Give one step to reduce bias in a machine-learning app.
  **A:** Collect training data that represents all user groups, and test accuracy separately for each group before release.

**Assessment:** Unit 6 assessment and bias checklist.

**Answer key:** Unit 6 test (12 items): 1) Cleaning = making data consistent and correct. 2) Metadata = data about data. 3) Line chart for change over time. 4) Correlation does not prove causation. 5) Pivot table summarizes by group. 6) Simulation = model of a real process; uses randomness and assumptions. 7) Simulations can be refined using real data. 8) Sampling bias = sample not representative of the population. 9) An online poll about internet access is biased because it excludes people without internet. 10) Training data = examples a machine-learning model learns from. 11) Unbalanced training data makes a model less accurate for underrepresented groups. 12) Crowdsourcing = obtaining input/data from many people online. Mastery 10+/12. Bias checklist (proficient) includes: test with diverse users and data; check training data represents all user groups; measure accuracy separately for each group; examine who could be harmed; get outside review; allow users to report problems.
