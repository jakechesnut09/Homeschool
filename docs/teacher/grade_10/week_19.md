# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 19

## English 10: Poetry of the Great War

**Prepare:** Print the five poems (Poetry Foundation). Find two WWI recruitment posters (Library of Congress). Parent preview: Owen and Sassoon describe gas death and a soldier's suicide.

**Watch for:**
- Assuming all war poems share the same view. Brooke's is idealistic (1914); Owen and Sassoon wrote from the trenches with bitter realism.
- Confusing poet and speaker. Refer to 'the speaker.'

### Day 1 (Monday): Context
*Student:* Read about WWI trench warfare and recruitment propaganda; examine two recruitment posters (Library of Congress).

*How to teach:* Read about trench warfare and recruitment. The student analyzes two posters for appeals. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What appeal does a poster saying 'Your country needs YOU' use?
  **A:** Pathos — patriotism and guilt — plus ethos of national authority.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Brooke vs. Owen
*Student:* Read 'The Soldier' and 'Dulce et Decorum Est' and chart contrasting tone words and images.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Soldier' and 'Dulce et Decorum Est.' The student charts tone words and images. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What is the tone of Brooke's 'The Soldier'?
  **A:** Patriotic, peaceful, idealistic — dying for England makes a foreign field 'for ever England.'
- **Q:** What is 'the old Lie' in Owen's poem?
  **A:** 'Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori' — it is sweet and fitting to die for one's country.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Imagery
*Student:* Analyze sensory imagery in Owen's gas attack stanza and its effect on the reader.

*How to teach:* Read the gas attack stanza aloud twice. The student lists sensory images by sense (sight, sound, touch) and writes a paragraph on how the imagery puts the reader in the scene and prepares for 'the old Lie.'

- **Q:** What image does Owen use for the gassed soldier?
  **A:** He sees the man drowning 'as under a green sea' — 'guttering, choking, drowning.'
- **Q:** What is the effect of 'Gas! GAS! Quick, boys!'?
  **A:** The sudden exclamations and dashes create panic and urgency, pulling the reader into the moment.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sassoon & McCrae
*Student:* Read 'Suicide in the Trenches' and 'In Flanders Fields'; identify the speaker's attitude in each.

*How to teach:* Read Sassoon and McCrae. The student identifies each speaker's attitude. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Who speaks in 'In Flanders Fields,' and what do they ask?
  **A:** The dead soldiers ('We are the Dead'); they ask the living to take up the torch and continue the fight.
- **Q:** What is Sassoon's attitude toward the 'smug-faced crowds'?
  **A:** Bitter scorn — they cheer soldiers but never know 'the hell where youth and laughter go.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Comparative response
*Student:* Write a 400-word comparison of how two poets use imagery and tone to convey a view of war.

*How to teach:* The student plans with a T-chart of images and tone words for two poets, then writes a 400-word comparison with a thesis about each poet's view of war. Grade with the rubric.

- **Q:** Name a contrast between Brooke's and Owen's imagery.
  **A:** Brooke uses gentle English nature images; Owen uses graphic, physical images of suffering.

**Assessment:** Comparative poetry response.

**Answer key:** Comparative response rubric: 4 = thesis compares two poets' views; analyzes specific images and tone words (e.g., Brooke's 'some corner of a foreign field / That is for ever England' — peaceful and patriotic — vs. Owen's 'guttering, choking, drowning' and 'the old Lie' — horrified and bitter); explains how imagery creates tone; 3 = accurate but less precise; 2 = summary; 1 = off-topic.

## Geometry: Pythagorean theorem and special right triangles

**Prepare:** Square tiles or graph paper, scientific calculator. Know √2 ≈ 1.414 and √3 ≈ 1.732.

**Watch for:**
- Using a² + b² = c² with c as a leg. The hypotenuse (longest side, opposite the right angle) is always c.
- Mixing up the 30-60-90 ratios: the short leg is opposite 30°, the hypotenuse is twice the short leg, the long leg is short leg × √3.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pythagorean review
*Student:* Solve applied problems (ladders, TV diagonals) with the Pythagorean theorem.

*How to teach:* Review the theorem with a tile square-on-each-side model, then solve applied problems: draw a sketch, label the right angle and hypotenuse, write the equation.

- **Q:** A 13-ft ladder's base is 5 ft from a wall. How high up the wall does it reach?
  **A:** √(169 − 25) = √144 = 12 ft.
- **Q:** A TV screen is 32 in wide and 18 in tall. What is the diagonal?
  **A:** √(1024 + 324) = √1348 ≈ 36.7 in.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Converse
*Student:* Use the converse and inequalities to classify triangles as acute, right or obtuse.

*How to teach:* Teach: if c² = a² + b² right; c² > a² + b² obtuse; c² < a² + b² acute (c longest). The student classifies a list of triangles.

- **Q:** Classify a triangle with sides 6, 8, 11.
  **A:** 121 > 36 + 64 = 100, so obtuse.
- **Q:** Classify sides 5, 6, 7.
  **A:** 49 < 25 + 36 = 61, so acute.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): 45-45-90
*Student:* Derive 45-45-90 ratios from a square's diagonal and solve problems.

*How to teach:* Draw a square with side 1 and its diagonal: √(1 + 1) = √2. Generalize to leg x → hypotenuse x√2, and practice both directions.

- **Q:** A square has side 8. Find its diagonal.
  **A:** 8√2 ≈ 11.31.
- **Q:** A 45-45-90 triangle has hypotenuse 6. Find a leg.
  **A:** 6/√2 = 3√2 ≈ 4.24.

### Day 4 (Thursday): 30-60-90
*Student:* Derive 30-60-90 ratios from an equilateral triangle and solve problems.

*How to teach:* Cut an equilateral triangle of side 2 in half: short leg 1, hypotenuse 2, long leg √3. Generalize and practice.

- **Q:** In a 30-60-90 triangle the long leg is 9. Find the short leg and hypotenuse.
  **A:** Short leg 9/√3 = 3√3 ≈ 5.20; hypotenuse 6√3 ≈ 10.39.
- **Q:** Find the height of an equilateral triangle with side 10.
  **A:** 5√3 ≈ 8.66.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on Pythagorean theorem and special right triangles.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Is a triangle with sides 8, 15, 17 a right triangle?
  **A:** Yes: 64 + 225 = 289 = 17².

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Legs 9 and 12 → hypotenuse 15. 2) Ladder 13 ft, base 5 ft from wall → reaches 12 ft. 3) Classify 6, 8, 11 → 121 > 100 → obtuse. 4) 7, 24, 25 → right. 5) 45-45-90 with leg 5 → hypotenuse 5√2 ≈ 7.07. 6) 45-45-90 with hypotenuse 10 → legs 5√2 ≈ 7.07. 7) 30-60-90 with short leg 4 → long leg 4√3 ≈ 6.93, hypotenuse 8. 8) 30-60-90 with hypotenuse 14 → short leg 7, long leg 7√3 ≈ 12.12.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Chemical equations and conservation of mass

**Prepare:** Gather baking soda, vinegar, a sturdy zip-top bag, a small cup that fits inside the bag, a balloon, a bottle, and the kitchen scale (0.1 g if possible). Open PhET 'Balancing Chemical Equations'. Print 20 skeleton equations (key below includes the core ones).

**Watch for:**
- Students balance by changing subscripts (making H₂O into H₂O₂). Changing a subscript changes the substance; only coefficients may change.
- Students think mass is lost in the open-system reaction. The 'missing' mass left as CO₂ gas; in a closed bag the mass stays the same.
- Students forget that a coefficient multiplies every atom in the formula (2H₂O has 4 H and 2 O).

### Day 1 (Monday): Closed system lab
*Student:* React baking soda and vinegar in a sealed zip bag on a scale and compare mass before and after; repeat open.

*How to teach:* Put 1 tablespoon of baking soda in the bag and 50 mL of vinegar in a small cup standing upright inside; seal, weigh, then tip the cup to react and reweigh. Repeat in an open cup. The student records both masses and explains the difference.

- **Q:** Why did the closed bag's mass stay the same?
  **A:** No matter could enter or leave; the CO₂ produced stayed in the bag, so total mass was conserved.
- **Q:** Write the word equation for baking soda and vinegar.
  **A:** Sodium bicarbonate + acetic acid → sodium acetate + water + carbon dioxide.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Balancing
*Student:* Balance 20 equations using particle diagrams and atom inventories.

*How to teach:* Model the atom-inventory method with a table of each element on each side, adjusting coefficients one at a time (save H and O for last; balance polyatomic ions as units). The student balances 20 equations, drawing particle diagrams for three.

- **Q:** Balance: Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃
  **A:** 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃
- **Q:** Balance: C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
  **A:** C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

### Day 3 (Wednesday): PhET Balancing
*Student:* Use PhET Balancing Chemical Equations game, levels 1–3.

*How to teach:* The student plays PhET Balancing Chemical Equations levels 1–3 and records any equations that took more than two tries, then explains the fix for each.

- **Q:** Balance: Fe + Cl₂ → FeCl₃
  **A:** 2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃
- **Q:** How many oxygen atoms are in 3Ca(NO₃)₂?
  **A:** 18 (3 × 2 × 3).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Explain
*Student:* Write a CER on why the open-system mass decreased.

*How to teach:* The student writes a CER explaining why the open system lost mass, using Day 1 data and the balanced equation. Score with the rubric.

- **Q:** If the open cup lost 2.3 g, what does that number represent?
  **A:** The mass of CO₂ gas that escaped into the air (plus a little evaporated water).
- **Q:** How could you show the gas has mass?
  **A:** Collect it in a balloon over the bottle; the inflated balloon traps it, or weigh the sealed system.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on balancing.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-equation balancing quiz. Grade together and have the student check each answer by atom inventory.

- **Q:** Balance: H₂O₂ → H₂O + O₂
  **A:** 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂

**Assessment:** CER and balancing quiz.

**Answer key:** CER rubric: 4 = Claim that the open system lost mass because CO₂ gas escaped; evidence with numbers (e.g., closed: 152.4 g before, 152.4 g after; open: 152.4 g → 150.1 g, a 2.3 g loss; bubbling observed); reasoning that atoms are rearranged, not destroyed (NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → NaCH₃COO + H₂O + CO₂), so mass is conserved in a closed system and the gas carried mass away in the open one. Balancing quiz key: 1) 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. 2) 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃. 3) C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. 4) 2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂. 5) N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. 6) 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂. 7) CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. 8) 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl. 9) Ca(OH)₂ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O. 10) C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O.

## Modern World History: Fascism in Italy and Nazi Germany

**Prepare:** Unemployment data 1929–1933 (Germany, Britain, U.S.), Mussolini's 'The Doctrine of Fascism' (1932) excerpt, USHMM pages on Nazi propaganda and the Nuremberg Laws, 1923 hyperinflation photographs. Prepare the Unit 6 test and a blank totalitarian-regimes comparison chart.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Hitler seized power in a coup. He was appointed chancellor legally (January 1933), then used the Reichstag fire and Enabling Act to destroy democracy.
- Treating fascism and communism as the same. Both were totalitarian, but fascism was ultra-nationalist and kept private property; communism aimed at a classless society and state ownership.

### Day 1 (Monday): Great Depression
*Student:* Graph unemployment in Germany, Britain and the U.S. 1929–1933.

*How to teach:* The student graphs unemployment for Germany, Britain and the U.S. 1929–1933 and explains how economic crisis made extremist promises appealing. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Why did the Great Depression help extremist parties?
  **A:** Mass unemployment and despair made people lose faith in democratic governments and turn to leaders promising jobs, order and national pride.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mussolini
*Student:* Read 'The Doctrine of Fascism' excerpt and list fascism's core ideas.

*How to teach:* Source the Doctrine of Fascism (1932, Mussolini and Giovanni Gentile). The student lists fascism's core ideas. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What is fascism's view of the individual and the state?
  **A:** The individual exists for the state: 'Everything within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state.'
- **Q:** When and how did Mussolini come to power?
  **A:** 1922, after the March on Rome, when the king appointed him prime minister.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hitler's rise
*Student:* Trace Weimar crises, the 1933 elections, the Enabling Act and the Reichstag fire.

*How to teach:* Trace the Weimar crises (Versailles, 1923 hyperinflation, Depression), the 1932–33 elections, Hitler's appointment, the Reichstag fire and the Enabling Act on a timeline. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** How did Hitler use the Reichstag fire?
  **A:** He blamed communists and had emergency decrees suspend civil liberties, then arrested opponents.
- **Q:** What did the Enabling Act do?
  **A:** Allowed Hitler's cabinet to pass laws without the Reichstag — effectively creating a dictatorship.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Nazi state
*Student:* Analyze propaganda and the Nuremberg Laws from USHMM.

*How to teach:* Analyze Nazi propaganda and the Nuremberg Laws using USHMM sources. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What did the Nuremberg Laws (1935) do?
  **A:** Stripped German Jews of citizenship and banned marriage and relationships between Jews and non-Jews.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 6 test
*Student:* Compare totalitarian regimes in a chart and take the unit test.

*How to teach:* The student completes the totalitarian regimes comparison chart, then takes the Unit 6 test. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Name three methods totalitarian regimes used to control people.
  **A:** Secret police, propaganda, censorship, terror and purges, and control of education and youth groups.

**Assessment:** Unit 6 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 6 test (sample answers): 1) Peak unemployment → Germany about 30% (6 million registered, 1932), U.S. about 25% (1933), Britain about 20%. 2) Fascism's core ideas → extreme nationalism, the state above the individual ('Everything within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state'), glorification of war, anti-communism, anti-liberalism, a single leader. 3) Hitler appointed chancellor → January 30, 1933. 4) Reichstag fire → February 27, 1933; used to suspend civil liberties. 5) Enabling Act → March 1933, let Hitler make laws without the Reichstag. 6) Nuremberg Laws (1935) → stripped Jews of citizenship and banned marriage between Jews and non-Jews. Comparison chart (Stalin, Mussolini, Hitler): leader, ideology, economic system, methods of control (secret police, propaganda, censorship, terror), targets.

## Spanish III: El arte hispano

**Prepare:** Open high-resolution images of Velázquez's Las Meninas (Museo del Prado), a Frida Kahlo self-portrait (e.g., Autorretrato con collar de espinas y colibrí), Diego Rivera murals, Chicano murals in San Francisco's Mission District (Balmy Alley, the Women's Building) and Los Angeles (Judy Baca's Great Wall of Los Angeles), and Picasso's Guernica (Museo Reina Sofía). Preview: Guernica shows wartime death and suffering; Kahlo's work depicts pain and injury.

**Watch for:**
- Using 'en el fondo' and 'al fondo' loosely: al fondo = in the background (location in the picture); en el fondo = deep down/basically.
- Describing paintings only with 'es': use hay (there is), está/están (location), se ve/se ven (one sees), and representa/simboliza for meaning.
- Thinking Guernica shows a battle between armies: it depicts the 1937 aerial bombing of a civilian town.

### Day 1 (Monday): Vocabulario de arte
*Student:* Learn art vocabulary and describe Las Meninas.

*How to teach:* Teach art vocabulary (el cuadro, el autorretrato, el primer plano, al fondo, a la izquierda/derecha, representar). Display Las Meninas; your child writes 6 sentences describing who and what appears where.

- **Q:** Who is the girl at the center of Las Meninas, and who are 'las meninas'?
  **A:** The Infanta Margarita Teresa (daughter of King Philip IV); the meninas are her maids of honor attending her.
- **Q:** Where do the king and queen appear in the painting?
  **A:** Reflected in the mirror at the back (al fondo) of the room; Velázquez himself stands at a large canvas on the left.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Frida Kahlo
*Student:* Read a short biography and analyze a self-portrait.

*How to teach:* Read a short Spanish biography of Frida Kahlo together. Your child analyzes one self-portrait: symbols, colors, expression, and what it says about identity.

- **Q:** What event shaped Frida Kahlo's life and art when she was 18?
  **A:** A bus accident (1925) that badly injured her spine and pelvis; she began painting while recovering and lived with pain all her life.
- **Q:** Translate: 'In her self-portraits she wears traditional Mexican clothing.'
  **A:** En sus autorretratos ella lleva ropa tradicional mexicana.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Muralismo
*Student:* Study Diego Rivera and Chicano murals in Los Angeles and San Francisco (Mission District).

*How to teach:* Show Diego Rivera murals and Chicano murals in the Mission District and Los Angeles. Discuss in Spanish why murals are public art and whom they speak to.

- **Q:** Why did Mexican muralists like Rivera paint on public walls?
  **A:** To teach history and celebrate workers and Indigenous people to everyone, including people who could not read, after the Mexican Revolution.
- **Q:** Name a famous Chicano mural project in Los Angeles.
  **A:** The Great Wall of Los Angeles, directed by Judy Baca, a half-mile mural of California history.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Guernica
*Student:* Interpret Picasso's Guernica and its historical context.

*How to teach:* Your child studies Guernica and its context, then identifies at least four symbols and offers interpretations in Spanish.

- **Q:** What happened at Guernica in 1937?
  **A:** During the Spanish Civil War, German (and Italian) planes supporting Franco bombed the Basque town of Guernica on April 26, 1937, killing many civilians.
- **Q:** Why is the painting in black, white, and gray?
  **A:** Possible answer: it looks like a newspaper photo and expresses death, mourning, and horror without the distraction of color.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Write a museum label for one artwork.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a museum label for one artwork and reads it aloud as a docent would.

- **Q:** Translate: 'In the background one sees a mirror.'
  **A:** Al fondo se ve un espejo.

**Assessment:** Museum label.

**Answer key:** Museum label rubric (60–100 words): 4 = title, artist, date, medium, museum; accurate description using hay/se ve/representa; one sentence of historical context and one of interpretation; correct agreement; 3 = one element missing or a few errors; 2 = mostly description with errors; 1 = incomplete. Model label: 'Guernica (1937), Pablo Picasso. Óleo sobre lienzo. Museo Reina Sofía, Madrid. Picasso pintó este mural después del bombardeo de la ciudad vasca de Guernica durante la Guerra Civil española. En el cuadro se ven un toro, un caballo herido y una madre que grita con su hijo muerto. Los colores grises, negros y blancos representan el dolor y el horror de la guerra.'

## Music & Media Arts: Photography: composition

**Prepare:** A phone camera; three media artworks to analyze (e.g., a short film, a music video, a photograph); a composition guide with examples.

**Watch for:**
- Students always center the subject. The rule of thirds often creates more dynamic images.
- Students think more photos mean better photography. Intentional composition and careful selection matter.
- Students confuse depth of field with focus. Depth of field is how much of the scene front-to-back appears sharp.

### Day 1 (Monday): Media arts intro
*Student:* Define media arts and analyze three media works.

*How to teach:* Define media arts (art made with technology: photography, film, sound, digital design, animation) and analyze three works.

- **Q:** What makes something 'media arts'?
  **A:** It's created and shared using technology — cameras, computers, audio and video tools.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Composition
*Student:* Learn rule of thirds, leading lines and framing.

*How to teach:* Learn rule of thirds, leading lines and framing with examples.

- **Q:** What are leading lines?
  **A:** Lines in the image (roads, fences, rivers) that guide the viewer's eye to the subject.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Photo walk
*Student:* Shoot 30 photos applying composition rules.

*How to teach:* The student shoots 30 photos applying composition rules.

- **Q:** How do you get shallow depth of field on a phone?
  **A:** Use portrait mode or get very close to the subject with a distant background.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Select
*Student:* Select the best five and explain choices.

*How to teach:* Select the best five and explain choices.

- **Q:** How should you choose your best photos?
  **A:** By clear subject, strong composition, sharp focus and good light.

### Day 5 (Friday): Critique
*Student:* Critique photos with criteria.

*How to teach:* Critique photos with criteria.

- **Q:** Give one criterion for critique.
  **A:** Composition, lighting, focus, emotional impact, or storytelling.

**Assessment:** Photo set.

**Answer key:** Photo set rubric: five best photos each demonstrating a named technique (rule of thirds, leading lines, framing, depth of field, perspective), with a 2–3 sentence explanation per photo; technical quality (focus, exposure); critique uses criteria. Proficient = all five techniques clearly used and explained.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Soccer skills

**Prepare:** Soccer ball, 10 cones, tape for targets on a wall or fence or a goal, timer. Find a park field.

**Watch for:**
- Students look down at the ball the whole time. Use quick glances at the ball and head up to see space.
- Students pass with the toe. Use the inside of the foot for accurate short passes, ankle locked.
- Students shoot with the toe. Use the laces (instep) with toe pointed down and plant foot beside the ball.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dribbling
*Student:* Dribble through a 10-cone slalom using inside/outside touches, timing three runs.

*How to teach:* Dribble a 10-cone slalom with inside/outside touches; time three runs.

- **Q:** Which parts of the foot should you use to dribble?
  **A:** Inside, outside and sole — small, frequent touches with both feet.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Passing
*Student:* Complete 100 wall passes with each foot, focusing on the inside-foot technique and first touch.

*How to teach:* 100 wall passes with each foot, focusing on inside-foot technique and first touch.

- **Q:** Where should your plant foot point when passing?
  **A:** At the target, beside the ball.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Shooting
*Student:* Shoot 30 instep shots at taped targets in a goal or fence, recording accuracy.

*How to teach:* 30 instep shots at taped targets, recording accuracy.

- **Q:** Why keep your knee over the ball when shooting?
  **A:** It keeps the shot low and on target.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Small-sided game
*Student:* Play 2v2 or 3v3 games to small goals for 30 minutes.

*How to teach:* Play 2v2 or 3v3 games to small goals for 30 minutes.

- **Q:** Why use small-sided games?
  **A:** More touches and decisions per player.

### Day 5 (Friday): Skill test
*Student:* Skill test: slalom time, 20 passes to target, 10 shots on goal.

*How to teach:* Give the skill test.

- **Q:** What makes a good first touch?
  **A:** It cushions the ball and moves it into space away from pressure, ready for the next action.

**Assessment:** Skill test.

**Answer key:** Skill test: slalom time (record best of three); 20 passes to a 2-yard target from 10 yards — 14+ proficient; 10 shots on goal (instep) — 6+ on target proficient. Technique checklist: close control with both feet, inside-foot passing with locked ankle, first touch out of feet into space, instep shooting with plant foot beside the ball.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Emergency action steps

**Prepare:** Install the American Red Cross First Aid app or open its website. Gather the home first aid kit and a car kit (or a bag to build one), plus a printed kit checklist. Parental note: first aid content includes injuries; keep discussion calm and practical.

**Watch for:**
- Students rush to help before checking the scene. The first step is always to check the scene for safety (traffic, fire, wires, violence); an injured rescuer can't help.
- Students think they need a parent's permission to call 911. Anyone should call 911 in a true emergency immediately.
- Students fear being sued for helping. California's Good Samaritan law protects people who give emergency care in good faith, without compensation, at the scene of an emergency, unless grossly negligent.

### Day 1 (Monday): Check-Call-Care
*Student:* Learn the emergency action steps and practice scene-safety checks.

*How to teach:* Teach Check–Call–Care. Walk through three scenes (kitchen fall, roadside crash, a person collapsed in a park) and have the student say what to check for safety first.

- **Q:** What's the first thing to do when you arrive at an emergency?
  **A:** Check the scene for safety before approaching.
- **Q:** How do you check if an adult is responsive?
  **A:** Tap the shoulder and shout 'Are you OK?'; check for breathing for no more than 10 seconds.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Calling 911
*Student:* Practice what to tell a 911 dispatcher.

*How to teach:* Role-play a 911 call: you play the dispatcher. The student gives location, what happened, the person's condition and care given, and stays on the line.

- **Q:** What's the most important information to give a 911 dispatcher?
  **A:** Your exact location (address, cross streets or landmarks), then what happened and the person's condition.
- **Q:** When should you hang up?
  **A:** Only when the dispatcher tells you to.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Good Samaritan
*Student:* Learn California's Good Samaritan protections and consent.

*How to teach:* The student reads a summary of California's Good Samaritan law and the rules of consent.

- **Q:** Do you need consent to help an unresponsive adult?
  **A:** No — consent is implied when a person can't respond.
- **Q:** What does the Good Samaritan law protect?
  **A:** People who give emergency care in good faith at the scene of an emergency, without expecting payment, from civil liability unless they act with gross negligence or willful misconduct.

### Day 4 (Thursday): First aid kit
*Student:* Build or inventory a home and car first aid kit.

*How to teach:* The student inventories the home kit against a checklist and builds or restocks a car kit, noting expiration dates.

- **Q:** Why include gloves in a first aid kit?
  **A:** To protect both rescuer and victim from bloodborne infections.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Review.

- **Q:** List the three emergency action steps.
  **A:** Check, Call, Care.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Emergency action steps — Check (scene, then person), Call (911 / have someone call and get an AED), Care (give care until help arrives). 2) Scene hazards — traffic, fire, electrical wires, water, unstable structures, violence, chemicals. 3) Tell the dispatcher — exact location, what happened, number of people, their condition, what help is being given; stay on the line until told to hang up. 4) Consent — ask a responsive adult; implied consent for an unresponsive person; for a child, ask a parent/guardian if present. 5) Good Samaritan — protects good-faith emergency care without pay (not gross negligence). 6) Standard precautions — gloves, wash hands, avoid contact with blood. 7) Basic kit items — gloves, assorted bandages, gauze pads and roller gauze, tape, elastic bandage, triangular bandage, antiseptic wipes, scissors, tweezers, cold pack, emergency blanket, CPR face shield, tourniquet, first aid guide, emergency contacts, personal medications. 8) Check for responsiveness — tap and shout 'Are you OK?'. 9) Check breathing — no more than 10 seconds. 10) When to call 911 — unresponsive, trouble breathing, chest pain, severe bleeding, seizure, signs of stroke, severe burns, suspected poisoning.

## Computer Science Principles: Procedures with parameters and return values

**Prepare:** Pick an earlier app of the student's with repeated code to refactor. Have the AP reference sheet section on PROCEDURE and RETURN. Prepare 10 tracing questions (examples in the key).

**Watch for:**
- Confusing parameters and arguments. A parameter is the variable in the definition; an argument is the actual value passed in the call.
- Displaying a result inside a function instead of returning it. A returned value can be stored or used in an expression; a displayed one cannot.
- Thinking code after RETURN still runs. RETURN ends the procedure immediately.

### Day 1 (Monday): Procedures
*Student:* Find repeated code in an earlier app and refactor it into a named procedure, confirming the app behaves identically.

*How to teach:* Open an earlier app, find repeated lines (e.g. code that resets the screen in three handlers), and move them into function resetGame() { … }, calling it from each place. Run to confirm the app behaves identically.

- **Q:** Give two benefits of moving repeated code into a procedure.
  **A:** The code is shorter and easier to read, and a fix or change only has to be made in one place.
- **Q:** What is procedural abstraction?
  **A:** Using a procedure by name and knowing what it does without needing to know how it is implemented.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Parameters
*Student:* Write procedures with parameters for drawing shapes of different sizes.

*How to teach:* Write function drawSquare(size) and call it with different arguments (drawSquare(50), drawSquare(100)). Emphasize the parameter is a placeholder.

- **Q:** In drawSquare(80), what is the argument and what is the parameter?
  **A:** 80 is the argument; size is the parameter.
- **Q:** Why add a parameter instead of writing drawSmallSquare and drawBigSquare?
  **A:** One general procedure handles any size, reducing duplicated code.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Return values
*Student:* Write functions that return values (tip calculator, letter grade, area of a circle) and use the returned values in expressions.

*How to teach:* Write functions that return values: tip(bill, percent) returns bill * percent / 100; letterGrade(score) returns a letter; circleArea(r) returns Math.PI * r * r. Use returned values in expressions, e.g. total = bill + tip(bill, 20).

- **Q:** What does tip(50, 18) return?
  **A:** 9 (50 × 18 / 100).
- **Q:** What is circleArea(3) to two decimals?
  **A:** π × 9 ≈ 28.27.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Decomposition
*Student:* Decompose a game into procedures on a diagram before coding.

*How to teach:* For a simple game (e.g. a clicker or guessing game), draw a box diagram breaking it into procedures (startGame, checkGuess, updateScore, endGame) before coding.

- **Q:** What is decomposition?
  **A:** Breaking a large problem into smaller, more manageable subproblems (often each becomes a procedure).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Complete 10 AP-style questions tracing PROCEDURE calls with parameters and RETURN values.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-question procedure quiz in the assessment key.

- **Q:** PROCEDURE add3(n) { RETURN n + 3 }. What is add3(add3(1))?
  **A:** 7.

**Assessment:** Procedure quiz.

**Answer key:** Procedure quiz (10 items): 1) PROCEDURE double(x) { RETURN x * 2 }; double(7) → 14. 2) double(double(3)) → 12. 3) PROCEDURE f(a, b) { RETURN a − b }; f(10, 4) → 6; f(4, 10) → −6. 4) PROCEDURE g(n) { IF n > 0 RETURN "pos" ; RETURN "not pos" }; g(0) → "not pos". 5) y ← double(5) + 1 → 11. 6) In 'double(7)', 7 is the argument and x is the parameter. 7) PROCEDURE sq(n) { RETURN n * n }; sq(3) + sq(4) → 25. 8) Procedural abstraction lets you use a procedure knowing only what it does, not how. 9) PROCEDURE m(L) { t ← 0; FOR EACH v IN L { t ← t + v }; RETURN t } with [1,2,3] → 6. 10) A procedure with no RETURN used in an expression returns no value (it can't be used that way). Score 8+ mastery.
