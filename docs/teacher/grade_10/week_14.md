# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 14

## English 10: Things Fall Apart Part 1: Igbo society

**Prepare:** Things Fall Apart (library), map of Nigeria. Parent preview: the novel includes domestic violence, the killing of a boy (Ikemefuna), and later a suicide.

**Watch for:**
- Reading Okonkwo as the voice of Igbo culture. Achebe shows many Igbo characters (Obierika, Nwoye, Ezeudu) who question or temper his rigidity.
- Seeing Igbo society as 'primitive.' Achebe shows its law, religion, art, commerce and debate.

### Day 1 (Monday): Context
*Student:* Read about Nigeria, the Igbo people and British colonization; locate Igboland on a map.

*How to teach:* Read a short background article on the Igbo and British colonization of Nigeria (late 1800s to 1960 independence). The student locates Igboland in southeastern Nigeria on a map and lists three things they expect the novel to show about Igbo life before colonization.

- **Q:** When did Achebe publish the novel, and what was he responding to?
  **A:** 1958; he wanted to tell African history from an African point of view, responding to colonial-era novels that portrayed Africans negatively.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ch. 1–4
*Student:* Read chapters 1–4 and record Okonkwo's fears and their roots in his father Unoka.

*How to teach:* The student reads chapters 1–4 (about 45 minutes) and keeps a two-column log: Okonkwo's fears and actions on the left, how each connects to Unoka on the right. Discuss the log, asking for page numbers.

- **Q:** Why does Okonkwo work so hard and rule his household harshly?
  **A:** He fears failure and weakness and is ashamed of his father, Unoka, who was idle and died in debt.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Proverbs
*Student:* Collect eight proverbs from the novel and explain what each reveals about Igbo values.

*How to teach:* Explain how proverbs work in oral cultures and model one. The student collects eight proverbs with page numbers and writes what each means and what Igbo value it reveals; discuss the two most interesting.

- **Q:** What does 'Proverbs are the palm-oil with which words are eaten' mean?
  **A:** Proverbs make speech smooth and meaningful; skillful talk in Igbo culture uses them.
- **Q:** What does 'If a child washes his hands he could eat with kings' reveal?
  **A:** Igbo society values achievement — a person can rise by their own effort.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Ch. 5–8
*Student:* Read chapters 5–8 and write a journal entry on Ikemefuna's death and Okonkwo's choice.

*How to teach:* The student reads chapters 5–8. Because Ikemefuna's death is upsetting, discuss it together first; then the student writes a journal entry on why Okonkwo strikes the blow and how it affects Nwoye and Okonkwo himself.

- **Q:** Why does Okonkwo strike the blow that kills Ikemefuna?
  **A:** He is afraid of being thought weak.
- **Q:** How does Nwoye react?
  **A:** Something gives way inside him; he grows distant from his father (this later contributes to his conversion).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Reading quiz and character chart for Okonkwo, Nwoye and Ekwefi.

*How to teach:* Give the reading quiz from the assessment key. Then check the character chart for Okonkwo, Nwoye and Ekwefi: each trait needs an example with a page number. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Who is Ezinma?
  **A:** Okonkwo and Ekwefi's daughter, the only one of Ekwefi's children to survive; Okonkwo's favorite.

**Assessment:** Quiz and proverb log.

**Answer key:** Reading quiz (answers): 1) How did Okonkwo become famous? → He threw Amalinze the Cat in a wrestling match. 2) What is Okonkwo's greatest fear? → Failure and weakness — becoming like his father, Unoka. 3) What was Unoka like? → Lazy, in debt, loved music and his flute; he died without titles. 4) Why does Ikemefuna come to Umuofia? → As compensation from Mbaino after a man from Mbaino killed an Umuofia woman (wife of Ogbuefi Udo). 5) Who kills Ikemefuna? → Okonkwo, because he was afraid of being thought weak. 6) Who warned him not to take part? → Ogbuefi Ezeudu. Character chart: full credit for traits with page-cited examples for Okonkwo, Nwoye, Ekwefi. Proverb log: 8 proverbs each with page and explanation.

## Geometry: Coordinate proofs

**Prepare:** Graph paper, Desmos, calculator. Prepare the Unit 4 test from the assessment key.

**Watch for:**
- Making the perpendicular slope only negative or only reciprocal. Both: slope 2/3 → −3/2.
- Using a single property to classify (e.g., 4 equal sides ⇒ square). Students must also show right angles (perpendicular slopes) to prove a square.

### Day 1 (Monday): Slope criteria
*Student:* Show parallel lines have equal slopes and perpendicular slopes are negative reciprocals using rotation.

*How to teach:* Rotate a line 90° in GeoGebra or on graph paper: a slope triangle with run a and rise b becomes run −b and rise a, so m becomes −a/b. Have the student verify with several examples.

- **Q:** Line ℓ has slope 3/4. Give the slope of a parallel line and a perpendicular line.
  **A:** Parallel 3/4; perpendicular −4/3.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Writing lines
*Student:* Write equations of lines parallel or perpendicular to a given line through a point.

*How to teach:* Model writing lines in point-slope form, then converting to slope-intercept. The student writes several and checks with Desmos.

- **Q:** Write the line parallel to y = −3x + 2 through (1, 5).
  **A:** y − 5 = −3(x − 1) → y = −3x + 8.
- **Q:** Write the line perpendicular to y = ½x − 4 through (2, 1).
  **A:** Slope −2: y − 1 = −2(x − 2) → y = −2x + 5.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Classify by coordinates
*Student:* Prove a quadrilateral with given vertices is a rectangle, rhombus or square.

*How to teach:* Show the checklist: compute all side lengths (distance) and slopes. Rectangle: parallelogram + perpendicular sides; rhombus: 4 equal sides; square: both. The student classifies several quadrilaterals with full justification.

- **Q:** Classify A(1, 1), B(4, 2), C(3, 5), D(0, 4).
  **A:** Slopes AB = 1/3, BC = −3, CD = 1/3, DA = −3, so adjacent sides are perpendicular; lengths all √10. It is a square.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Area on the plane
*Student:* Compute the area of polygons on the plane with decomposition.

*How to teach:* For an irregular polygon, show the 'box' method: draw a rectangle around it and subtract right triangles, or split it into triangles. The student finds areas of three polygons.

- **Q:** Find the area of the triangle with vertices (1, 1), (5, 1), (3, 6).
  **A:** Base 4, height 5, area = ½·4·5 = 10.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 test
*Student:* Unit 4 test on quadrilaterals and coordinate proof.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 4 test from the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** How do you show two segments are perpendicular on the coordinate plane?
  **A:** Show their slopes are negative reciprocals (product −1), or one is horizontal and the other vertical.

**Assessment:** Unit 4 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 4 test with answers: 1) Slope of the line through (1, 2) and (4, 8) → 2; perpendicular slope → −1/2. 2) Line parallel to y = 2x + 1 through (3, 4) → y = 2x − 2. 3) Line perpendicular to y = 2x + 1 through (3, 4) → y = −½x + 5.5. 4) Prove A(0, 0), B(4, 0), C(4, 3), D(0, 3) is a rectangle → opposite sides parallel (slopes 0, 0, undefined, undefined) and adjacent sides perpendicular (horizontal and vertical). 5) Classify P(0, 0), Q(3, 4), R(8, 4), S(5, 0) → all sides 5, diagonals PR = √80 and QS = √20 unequal → rhombus, not a square. 6) Area of triangle (0, 0), (6, 0), (2, 5) → 15. 7) Exterior angle of a regular pentagon → 72°. Mastery 80%.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Naming, moles and molar mass

**Prepare:** Gather uncooked rice, a measuring cup, sugar, salt, water, and the kitchen scale. Make a periodic table available with atomic masses. Write key numbers on a card: 1 mol = 6.022 × 10²³ particles; molar mass of water 18.02 g/mol, NaCl 58.44 g/mol, sucrose (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) 342.30 g/mol.

**Watch for:**
- Students think a mole is a mass. A mole is a count (6.022 × 10²³); its mass depends on the substance.
- Students add atomic masses without multiplying by subscripts (H₂O = 1 + 16 = 17). Multiply each atomic mass by the number of atoms: 2(1.01) + 16.00 = 18.02.
- Students flip conversion factors. Set up units so they cancel: grams × (1 mol / g) → mol.

### Day 1 (Monday): Counting by weighing
*Student:* Estimate the number of rice grains in a cup by weighing 100 grains, as an analogy for the mole.

*How to teach:* Weigh 100 grains of rice, then weigh a full cup. The student calculates the number of grains in the cup without counting, then connects to how chemists count atoms by weighing.

- **Q:** 100 rice grains weigh 2.0 g and a cup of rice weighs 185 g. How many grains are in the cup?
  **A:** 185 ÷ 2.0 × 100 = 9,250 grains (about 9,300).
- **Q:** Why do chemists count atoms by weighing?
  **A:** Atoms are far too small and numerous to count; if you know the mass of one mole, weighing tells you how many particles you have.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Molar mass
*Student:* Calculate molar masses of 15 compounds.

*How to teach:* Model molar mass: sum (atomic mass × number of atoms). The student calculates 15 molar masses; check H₂O, CO₂, NaCl, C₆H₁₂O₆ and Ca(NO₃)₂ first.

- **Q:** Molar mass of CO₂?
  **A:** 12.01 + 2(16.00) = 44.01 g/mol.
- **Q:** Molar mass of Ca(NO₃)₂?
  **A:** 40.08 + 2(14.01) + 6(16.00) = 164.10 g/mol.
- **Q:** Molar mass of C₆H₁₂O₆?
  **A:** 6(12.01) + 12(1.008) + 6(16.00) = 180.16 g/mol.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Conversions
*Student:* Convert grams ↔ moles ↔ particles with dimensional analysis.

*How to teach:* Draw the mole map: grams ⇄ moles (molar mass) ⇄ particles (Avogadro's number). Model two problems with full units, then assign ten.

- **Q:** How many moles are in 36.0 g of water?
  **A:** 36.0 g × (1 mol / 18.02 g) = 2.00 mol.
- **Q:** How many molecules is that?
  **A:** 2.00 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.20 × 10²⁴ molecules.
- **Q:** What is the mass of 0.250 mol of NaCl?
  **A:** 0.250 × 58.44 = 14.6 g.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mole of sugar
*Student:* Measure one mole of sugar, salt and water and compare volumes.

*How to teach:* Weigh out one mole each of sugar (342 g), salt (58.4 g) and water (18.0 g, which is 18 mL) and compare their volumes in measuring cups. Discuss why the same number of particles takes up different amounts of space.

- **Q:** Why does a mole of sugar take up far more space than a mole of water?
  **A:** Each sucrose molecule is much larger and heavier (342 g/mol vs. 18 g/mol), so the same number of molecules has more mass and volume.
- **Q:** How many molecules are in the 18 mL of water?
  **A:** About 6.02 × 10²³ (one mole).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 test
*Student:* Unit 3 test on bonding, properties and the mole.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 test with a periodic table and polyatomic ion card. Grade together and log errors.

- **Q:** How many moles of NaCl are in 117 g?
  **A:** 117 ÷ 58.44 = 2.00 mol.

**Assessment:** Unit 3 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 3 test key (sample): 1) NaCl is ionic; CH₄ covalent; Cu metallic. 2) Formula of calcium phosphate — Ca₃(PO₄)₂. 3) Shape of H₂O — bent; polar. 4) Highest boiling point: H₂O, H₂S, CH₄ — H₂O (hydrogen bonding). 5) Molar mass of CaCO₃ — 100.09 g/mol. 6) 36.0 g of H₂O = 2.00 mol = 1.20 × 10²⁴ molecules. 7) Mass of 0.500 mol NaCl — 29.2 g. 8) Moles in 90.0 g glucose (180.16 g/mol) — 0.500 mol. 9) Why salt water conducts but sugar water does not — mobile ions vs. neutral molecules. 10) Why metals are malleable — mobile electron sea allows layers to slide. 11) Molecules in 1.00 g H₂O: 3.34 × 10²² (1.00 ÷ 18.02 × 6.022 × 10²³). Score: 90%+ mastery; reteach mole conversions if items 6–8 are missed.

## Modern World History: Fighting the Great War

**Prepare:** WWI map, Library of Congress WWI posters from three nations, soldier letters (Library of Congress Veterans History or BYU archive), Zimmermann Telegram (archives.gov).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Western Front moved a lot. It was largely a stalemate of trenches from late 1914 to 1918.
- Forgetting other fronts (Eastern, Ottoman/Gallipoli).

### Day 1 (Monday): Fronts
*Student:* Map the Western, Eastern and Ottoman fronts and key battles (Marne, Verdun, Somme, Gallipoli).

*How to teach:* On a map of Europe and the Middle East, the student draws the Western Front (Belgium to Switzerland), the Eastern Front, and the Ottoman fronts, and labels the Marne (1914), Verdun (1916), the Somme (1916) and Gallipoli (1915–16) with one fact each.

- **Q:** Why did trench warfare develop?
  **A:** After the Marne, both sides dug in; machine guns and artillery made attacks so deadly that neither side could break through.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Trench life
*Student:* Read soldier letters and connect to WWI poetry from English class.

*How to teach:* Read two or three soldier letters from the Library of Congress or a WWI archive. The student lists details of trench life and finds matching images in the poems from English class (e.g., Owen's 'Dulce et Decorum Est').

- **Q:** What details of trench life appear in letters and poems?
  **A:** Mud, rats, lice, trench foot, constant shelling, poison gas, and fear.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Total war
*Student:* Analyze home-front propaganda posters from three nations.

*How to teach:* Show propaganda posters from three nations (e.g., U.S., Britain, Germany). For each, the student identifies audience, emotional appeal, symbols and message, and explains what 'total war' required of civilians. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What is total war?
  **A:** A war in which nations mobilize their entire economies and populations.

### Day 4 (Thursday): U.S. entry & Russia
*Student:* Analyze the Zimmermann Telegram and explain how Russia's exit and U.S. entry changed the war.

*How to teach:* Source the Zimmermann Telegram (January 1917, German Foreign Secretary to the ambassador in Mexico, intercepted by Britain). The student explains why it angered Americans, then writes a paragraph on how Russia's exit (Brest-Litovsk, March 1918) and U.S. entry (April 1917) changed the war.

- **Q:** What did the Zimmermann Telegram propose?
  **A:** A German-Mexican alliance; Mexico would regain Texas, New Mexico and Arizona.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on the course of the war.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key without notes (about 20 minutes). Grade it together; for each missed item the student finds the answer in their notes and writes a one-sentence correction. Then score the poster analysis.

- **Q:** When did the armistice take effect?
  **A:** November 11, 1918.

**Assessment:** Quiz and poster analysis.

**Answer key:** Quiz (answers): 1) First Battle of the Marne (1914) → stopped the German advance on Paris; led to trench warfare. 2) Verdun (1916) → long French-German battle of attrition. 3) Somme (July 1, 1916) → about 57,000 British casualties on the first day. 4) Gallipoli (1915–16) → failed Allied attempt to take the Dardanelles; heavy ANZAC losses. 5) Zimmermann Telegram → Germany proposed an alliance with Mexico, offering Texas, New Mexico, Arizona. 6) Why the U.S. entered (April 1917) → unrestricted submarine warfare and the telegram. 7) Russia's exit → Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918). Poster analysis: full credit for identifying the nation, audience, emotional appeal and message for three posters.

## Music & Media Arts: Western art music: Baroque to Romantic

**Prepare:** Recordings: Bach, Brandenburg Concerto No. 3 in G (mvt. 1); Mozart, Symphony No. 40 in G minor, K. 550 (mvt. 1); Chopin, Nocturne in E-flat, Op. 9 No. 2. A timeline template. A listening quiz of 8–10 short excerpts.

**Watch for:**
- Students think 'classical' means all old music. The Classical period (c. 1750–1820) is one era; others include Baroque (c. 1600–1750) and Romantic (c. 1820–1900).
- Students confuse texture terms. Polyphonic = several independent melodies (Bach); homophonic = melody with accompaniment (much Mozart, Chopin).
- Students think sonata form is a piece for one instrument. Sonata form is a structure (exposition–development–recapitulation) used in symphony and sonata movements.

### Day 1 (Monday): Baroque
*Student:* Listen to Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 3 and identify texture.

*How to teach:* Listen to Brandenburg No. 3 and describe the texture.

- **Q:** What texture dominates the Brandenburg Concerto No. 3?
  **A:** Polyphonic (contrapuntal) — several string parts weaving independent lines, with continuo.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Classical
*Student:* Listen to Mozart's Symphony 40 movement 1 and map sonata form.

*How to teach:* Listen to Mozart's Symphony No. 40, mvt. 1 and map sonata form with timestamps.

- **Q:** What are the three main sections of sonata form?
  **A:** Exposition, development, recapitulation (often plus a coda).
- **Q:** In the recapitulation of a minor-key symphony like No. 40, what key does the second theme return in?
  **A:** The home key, G minor.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Romantic
*Student:* Listen to Chopin's Nocturne Op. 9 No. 2 and describe expression.

*How to teach:* Listen to Chopin's Nocturne Op. 9 No. 2 and describe its expression.

- **Q:** What is rubato?
  **A:** Flexible tempo — stretching and pushing the beat for expression.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Timeline
*Student:* Create a timeline connecting periods to world history events.

*How to teach:* Create a timeline linking periods to world events (e.g., Bach — Enlightenment beginnings; Mozart — American Revolution era; Chopin — revolutions of 1830/1848).

- **Q:** Name one historical event during Mozart's lifetime.
  **A:** The American Revolution (1775–1783) or the start of the French Revolution (1789).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Listening identification quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the listening identification quiz.

- **Q:** Which period features harpsichord continuo?
  **A:** Baroque.

**Assessment:** Listening quiz.

**Answer key:** Listening quiz key: identify period and composer with one musical clue — Baroque/Bach: continuous motor rhythm, polyphony, terraced dynamics, harpsichord continuo; Classical/Mozart: balanced phrases, clear homophony, contrasting themes in sonata form; Romantic/Chopin: expressive rubato, wide dynamics, lyrical ornamented melody over broken-chord accompaniment. Sonata form map (Mozart 40, mvt. 1): exposition (first theme G minor; transition; second theme B♭ major), development (fragments modulate through keys), recapitulation (both themes return in G minor), coda. Timeline: Baroque c. 1600–1750; Classical c. 1750–1820; Romantic c. 1820–1900.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Strategy in net games

**Prepare:** Racquets and balls/shuttles; a pro match clip (pickleball or badminton) and a shot-charting sheet.

**Watch for:**
- Students hit everything hard. Shot selection (placement, depth, angle) wins more points than power.
- Students in doubles both chase the same ball. Communicate ('mine', 'yours') and use a formation.
- Students stay where they hit. Recover to the base position (center of the possible returns).

### Day 1 (Monday): Singles strategy
*Student:* Learn singles base position and practice a cross-court/down-the-line pattern to move an opponent side to side.

*How to teach:* Teach singles base position and the cross-court/down-the-line pattern to move the opponent.

- **Q:** Why hit cross-court first?
  **A:** Cross-court travels over the lower part of the net with more court length, so it's safer and pulls the opponent wide, opening the down-the-line space.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Doubles formations
*Student:* Learn up-and-back vs. side-by-side doubles and drill switching on a lob.

*How to teach:* Teach up-and-back (attack) vs. side-by-side (defense) badminton doubles formations and switching on a lob.

- **Q:** When do badminton doubles partners switch to side-by-side?
  **A:** When defending, e.g., after hitting a lift/clear, to cover the court against smashes.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Game analysis
*Student:* Watch a pro pickleball or badminton match clip and chart 20 shots by type and placement.

*How to teach:* Watch a pro clip and chart 20 shots by type and placement.

- **Q:** What pattern did you see most often?
  **A:** Answers vary; strong answers cite counts from the chart.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Match play
*Student:* Play three games, choosing one strategy goal per game and tallying points won with it.

*How to teach:* Play three games, each with one strategy goal; tally points won with it.

- **Q:** Why set one strategy goal per game?
  **A:** To isolate which tactic works by measuring its results.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflection
*Student:* Write which strategy worked best and why, using your tally data.

*How to teach:* The student writes which strategy worked best using tally data.

- **Q:** What makes a conclusion convincing?
  **A:** Specific numbers from your tallies plus a reason the strategy worked.

**Assessment:** Match analysis.

**Answer key:** Match analysis rubric: 4 = charts 20 shots by type and placement correctly; tallies points won using each strategy goal across three games; concludes which strategy worked best using numbers (e.g., 'cross-court then down-the-line won 7 of 10 points'); explains why. 3 = data present, reasoning weak. 2 = incomplete data. 1 = opinion only.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Defensive driving and the Move Over law

**Prepare:** Read the handbook on scanning, space cushion and the Move Over law. Find dash-cam hazard clips (search 'hazard perception test clips'; preview for graphic content and choose ones without injuries). Make a hazard perception answer sheet.

**Watch for:**
- Students stare at the car directly ahead. Scan 10–15 seconds ahead in the city (about a block) and check mirrors every 5–8 seconds.
- Students think the Move Over law means stop. It means move over a lane if safe, or slow down, when passing stationary emergency or tow vehicles or Caltrans vehicles with flashing lights (the law has been expanded to other stopped vehicles with hazards on — check the current handbook).
- Students think work zones only matter when workers are present. Fines can be doubled in posted work zones, and lane shifts or equipment can be hazards anytime.

### Day 1 (Monday): Scanning
*Student:* Practice looking 10–15 seconds ahead while a passenger and call out hazards.

*How to teach:* As a passenger, the student scans ahead and calls out hazards aloud (commentary driving): pedestrians, cars backing out, brake lights, signals.

- **Q:** How far ahead should you scan in city driving?
  **A:** 10–15 seconds ahead, about one block.
- **Q:** How often should you check mirrors?
  **A:** Every 5–8 seconds, and before slowing or changing lanes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Space cushion
*Student:* Learn to keep space ahead, behind and to the sides.

*How to teach:* Explain the space cushion on all four sides. The student draws a car with its cushion and escape routes.

- **Q:** What should you do if someone tailgates you?
  **A:** Increase your following distance ahead, move right if possible, and let them pass; don't brake-check.
- **Q:** Why avoid driving in another driver's blind spot?
  **A:** They may change lanes into you without seeing you.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hazard perception
*Student:* Watch dash-cam clips and identify hazards before they happen.

*How to teach:* Watch dash-cam clips, pausing to have the student predict what could happen next and what to do.

- **Q:** A ball rolls into the street ahead. What should you anticipate?
  **A:** A child may chase it; slow down and be ready to stop.
- **Q:** A parked car's front wheels turn and the exhaust is visible. What could happen?
  **A:** It may pull out; cover the brake and give space.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Move Over & work zones
*Student:* Learn the Move Over law and double fines in work zones.

*How to teach:* The student learns the Move Over law and work-zone rules.

- **Q:** You approach a stopped tow truck with flashing amber lights on the freeway shoulder. What does the law require?
  **A:** Move over a lane away from it if safe; if not, slow down.
- **Q:** What is the penalty structure in posted work zones?
  **A:** Fines can be doubled.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Hazard perception test.

*How to teach:* Give the hazard perception test using 10 clips. Review missed hazards.

- **Q:** What's an escape route?
  **A:** An open space you can steer into to avoid a collision, such as an open lane or shoulder.

**Assessment:** Hazard perception test.

**Answer key:** Hazard perception test: show 10 clips; score 1 point per hazard identified before it develops plus 1 point for a correct response (e.g., cover the brake, change lanes, increase space). 16–20 = proficient; 11–15 = developing; below 11 = more practice. Key concepts: scan 10–15 seconds ahead; keep a space cushion ahead (3+ sec), behind, and to the sides; identify escape routes; Move Over law; double fines in work zones; reduce speed in work zones.

## Computer Science Principles: Debugging and pair programming project

**Prepare:** Arrange a pair-programming partner (sibling, parent or online). Set a 15-minute timer for role switching. Have a rubber duck or stuffed animal for 'rubber-duck debugging'. Print the Unit 3 project rubric (assessment key).

**Watch for:**
- Changing many things at once while debugging. Change one thing, test, and observe.
- Navigator just watching. The navigator reviews each line, thinks ahead, and catches errors; the driver types.
- Mixing up syntax and logic errors: a syntax error stops the program (missing parenthesis); a logic error runs but gives the wrong result.

### Day 1 (Monday): Debugging guide
*Student:* Write a debugging checklist (read the error, isolate, print/console.log, rubber duck) for others to use.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm what to do when code doesn't work, then have the student write a numbered debugging checklist someone else could follow. Introduce syntax vs. logic errors with an example of each.

- **Q:** Is a missing closing parenthesis a syntax or logic error? What about a tip calculator that adds instead of multiplies?
  **A:** Missing parenthesis = syntax error; adding instead of multiplying = logic error.
- **Q:** What is 'rubber duck debugging'?
  **A:** Explaining your code line by line out loud (even to an object) — doing so often reveals where your thinking and the code differ.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pair programming
*Student:* Pair program with a sibling, parent or online partner, switching driver/navigator every 15 minutes, on a decision-maker app.

*How to teach:* Explain driver (types) and navigator (reads, plans, spots errors). Pair program a decision-maker app (e.g. 'What should I eat?'), switching every 15 minutes.

- **Q:** What does the navigator do during pair programming?
  **A:** Reviews code as it's typed, catches errors, thinks about the next steps and the big picture, and communicates with the driver.
- **Q:** Name one benefit of pair programming.
  **A:** Fewer bugs, shared knowledge, better design decisions because two people review the code.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Build
*Student:* Continue building the app, adding at least two screens, variables and conditionals.

*How to teach:* Continue building, adding at least two screens, variables and conditionals. Stop every 20 minutes to run and test.

- **Q:** Why test after each small addition instead of at the end?
  **A:** If something breaks, you know it was the most recent change, making bugs much easier to find.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Test and refine
*Student:* Have a user test the app while you silently observe, log every bug or confusion in a table, and fix the top three.

*How to teach:* A user tests the app while the student silently observes, logging each bug or confusion in a table (what happened, expected, severity). Fix the top three.

- **Q:** How should you choose which three bugs to fix first?
  **A:** By severity and frequency — fix bugs that crash the app or block the main task before cosmetic issues.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 project
*Student:* Submit the app with a short write-up of design decisions and debugging.

*How to teach:* The student submits the app and write-up; score with the rubric in the assessment key.

- **Q:** Describe one bug you found this week and how you fixed it.
  **A:** Answers vary; proficient answers name the symptom, how it was isolated (e.g. console.log), the cause and the fix.

**Assessment:** Unit 3 app project rubric.

**Answer key:** Unit 3 app project rubric (4 levels): 4 = at least two screens, uses variables and conditionals meaningfully, no bugs found in testing, user-test bug table with top three fixes, write-up explains design choices and at least two debugging stories; 3 = works with minor bugs, write-up complete; 2 = partly working or missing variables/conditionals; 1 = doesn't run. Debugging checklist (proficient) includes: read the error message and line number, reproduce the bug, isolate it (comment out code/test small parts), use console.log to check values, check IDs/spelling, explain the code aloud (rubber duck), change one thing at a time and retest.
