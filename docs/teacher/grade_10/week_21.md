## English 10: Night sections 4–9

**Prepare:** Night. Parent preview: sections 4–9 include hangings, beatings, a son killing his father for bread, and the father's death. Read Wiesel's 1986 Nobel speech (nobelprize.org).

**Watch for:**
- Assuming Eliezer failed his father. Discuss guilt and the impossible conditions.
- Thinking 'silence' means just not speaking. Wiesel means the indifference of bystanders and nations.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sections 4–5
*Student:* Read sections 4–5 and record moments of dehumanization and resistance.

*How to teach:* Read sections 4–5. The student records dehumanization and resistance. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Describe the hanging of the young pipel.
  **A:** The boy dies slowly; a man asks 'Where is God now?' and Eliezer hears a voice answer that God is hanging on the gallows.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sections 6–7
*Student:* Read sections 6–7 (the march and train) and chart Eliezer's responsibility toward his father.

*How to teach:* Read sections 6–7. Chart Eliezer's responsibility toward his father. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What happens with Rabbi Eliahu's son?
  **A:** He runs ahead and abandons his father during the march; Eliezer prays never to do the same.
- **Q:** What does Juliek do in the barracks at Gleiwitz?
  **A:** Plays Beethoven on his violin; he is found dead the next morning.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sections 8–9
*Student:* Finish the book and analyze the mirror image in the final paragraph.

*How to teach:* Finish the book. Analyze the mirror image. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does Eliezer see in the mirror at the end?
  **A:** 'A corpse' gazing back — the look never left him.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Nobel speech
*Student:* Read Wiesel's 1986 Nobel acceptance speech and connect his call 'to take sides' to the memoir.

*How to teach:* Read the key section of Wiesel's 1986 Nobel Peace Prize acceptance speech aloud. The student underlines his call to take sides and writes a paragraph connecting it to two moments of silence or indifference in Night.

- **Q:** Quote Wiesel's Nobel statement about neutrality.
  **A:** 'We must always take sides. Neutrality helps the oppressor, never the victim. Silence encourages the tormentor, never the tormented.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Seminar
*Student:* Socratic seminar: Why does Wiesel say silence helps the tormentor?

*How to teach:* Hold a 20-minute Socratic seminar: 'Why does Wiesel say silence helps the tormentor?' The student brings three cited passages and asks at least one follow-up question; end with a written exit response.

- **Q:** When was Buchenwald liberated?
  **A:** April 11, 1945.

**Assessment:** Seminar participation and written exit response.

**Answer key:** Seminar rubric (4-point): claim about silence; at least three cited passages (e.g., the world's silence, the hanging of the pipel, the Nobel line 'Silence encourages the tormentor, never the tormented'); responds to others; asks questions. Exit response model: 'Wiesel argues that silence helps tormentors because victims are abandoned when bystanders say nothing; in Night, the world's inaction allowed deportations to continue.'

## Geometry: Solving right triangles and applications

**Prepare:** Clinometer materials: protractor, straw, string, a washer or nut, tape. Tape measure. Pick a building or tree with flat ground in front. Look up ADA ramp rule: maximum slope 1:12.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting to add eye height in the clinometer lab. The trig calculation gives height above eye level.
- Reading the clinometer angle directly when the string shows the complement. Depending on construction, the angle of elevation may be 90° minus the reading; test with a level line (should read 0° elevation).

### Day 1 (Monday): Inverse trig
*Student:* Find missing angles with inverse trig and completely solve right triangles.

*How to teach:* Introduce sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹, tan⁻¹ as 'what angle has this ratio?' Model completely solving a right triangle (all sides and angles).

- **Q:** Legs 7 and 10. Find the angle opposite the 7.
  **A:** tan⁻¹(7/10) ≈ 35.0°.
- **Q:** Hypotenuse 10, one leg 6. Find the angle opposite the 6.
  **A:** sin⁻¹(0.6) ≈ 36.9°.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Build a clinometer
*Student:* Build a clinometer from a protractor, straw and weighted string.

*How to teach:* Build the clinometer: tape a straw along the protractor's straight edge, hang the weighted string from the center hole. Test: sighting a level line should give 0° elevation. Make a conversion note if the string reads 90°.

- **Q:** Your clinometer string reads 60° on the protractor (measured from the straw's side). What is the angle of elevation?
  **A:** 90° − 60° = 30° (for a design where level reads 90°).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Field lab
*Student:* Measure the height of a building or tree using the clinometer and trig; write a lab report.

*How to teach:* Outside, the student measures distance to the base, sights the top, records the angle and eye height, and repeats 3 times to average. Compute height = d·tan θ + eye height and write the lab report.

- **Q:** Standing 40 ft from a tree with angle of elevation 32° and eye height 5 ft, how tall is the tree?
  **A:** 40 tan 32° + 5 ≈ 25.0 + 5 = 30.0 ft.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Applications
*Student:* Solve ramp-design problems meeting ADA slope requirements and navigation problems.

*How to teach:* Work ADA ramp problems (1 in rise per 12 in run maximum) and navigation problems using sketches.

- **Q:** A porch is 24 inches high. What minimum ramp length (horizontal run) meets ADA?
  **A:** 24 × 12 = 288 in = 24 ft.
- **Q:** A plane descends at 3° and is 1,500 ft high. How far horizontally until touchdown?
  **A:** 1500/tan 3° ≈ 28,600 ft (about 5.4 miles).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on solving right triangles.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Which inverse function do you use if you know the opposite and hypotenuse?
  **A:** Inverse sine, sin⁻¹(opposite/hypotenuse).

**Assessment:** Lab report and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Opposite 5, adjacent 12 → angle tan⁻¹(5/12) ≈ 22.6°. 2) Solve right triangle with legs 7 and 10 → hypotenuse ≈ 12.21, angles ≈ 35.0° and 55.0°. 3) Angle of elevation 35° from 50 ft away, eye height 5 ft → 50 tan 35° + 5 ≈ 40.0 ft. 4) ADA ramp (1:12) rising 30 in → 360 in = 30 ft run; angle ≈ 4.76°. 5) A ship sails 40 km at bearing that makes 30° east of north; distance east → 40 sin 30° = 20 km. Lab report rubric: 4 = diagram with angle, distance, eye height; correct tan calculation; final height with units; 2+ error sources; 3 = minor slips; 2 = eye height omitted or wrong ratio; 1 = incomplete.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Stoichiometry

**Prepare:** Gather baking soda, vinegar, a cup, balloons, and the kitchen scale (0.01 g ideal). Formula card: NaHCO₃ = 84.01 g/mol; CO₂ = 44.01 g/mol; NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → NaCH₃COO + H₂O + CO₂.

**Watch for:**
- Students convert grams of A directly to grams of B using the coefficients. Coefficients are mole ratios; always convert to moles first.
- Students use the mole ratio upside down. Write the ratio with the unknown substance on top and the known on the bottom.
- Students think percent yield over 100% is great. It signals an error (e.g., wet product or measurement error).

### Day 1 (Monday): Mole ratios
*Student:* Write mole ratios from balanced equations and solve mole-to-mole problems.

*How to teach:* Model reading mole ratios from coefficients. The student writes all ratios for N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ and solves mole-to-mole problems.

- **Q:** How many moles of NH₃ form from 6.0 mol H₂ (with enough N₂)?
  **A:** 6.0 mol H₂ × (2 mol NH₃ / 3 mol H₂) = 4.0 mol NH₃.
- **Q:** In 2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O, how many mol O₂ burn 1.0 mol octane?
  **A:** 12.5 mol O₂.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mass-to-mass
*Student:* Solve gram-to-gram stoichiometry problems.

*How to teach:* Model the three-step road map: grams A → moles A → moles B → grams B. Work two examples, then assign eight problems.

- **Q:** How many grams of water form when 16.0 g of CH₄ burns completely?
  **A:** 16.0 ÷ 16.04 = 0.998 mol CH₄ × 2 = 1.995 mol H₂O × 18.02 = 35.9 g.
- **Q:** How many grams of NH₃ form from 28.0 g of N₂?
  **A:** 28.0 ÷ 28.02 = 1.00 mol N₂ → 2.00 mol NH₃ × 17.03 = 34.1 g.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Predict & test
*Student:* Predict the mass of CO₂ lost when 5.0 g baking soda reacts with excess vinegar; measure it.

*How to teach:* The student predicts the mass of CO₂ from 5.0 g baking soda, then weighs a cup of excess vinegar plus the baking soda on paper, adds the baking soda slowly, waits until bubbling stops, swirls, and reweighs everything (including the paper). Mass lost = CO₂ released.

- **Q:** Predict the mass of CO₂ produced from 5.0 g of NaHCO₃.
  **A:** 5.0 ÷ 84.01 = 0.0595 mol; 1:1 ratio → 0.0595 × 44.01 = 2.6 g CO₂.
- **Q:** Why add the baking soda slowly?
  **A:** To avoid foam overflowing, which would lose liquid mass and make the CO₂ loss appear too large.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Percent yield
*Student:* Calculate percent yield and analyze sources of error.

*How to teach:* The student calculates percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100 and analyzes errors.

- **Q:** You measured 2.1 g of CO₂ lost; the theoretical value is 2.6 g. What is the percent yield?
  **A:** 2.1 ÷ 2.6 × 100 ≈ 81%.
- **Q:** Why might the measured CO₂ be low?
  **A:** Some CO₂ stays dissolved in the liquid; also the reaction may not be complete or readings may be imprecise.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Stoichiometry quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the stoichiometry quiz with a periodic table. Grade together and redo missed problems.

- **Q:** How many grams of O₂ are needed to burn 16.0 g of CH₄?
  **A:** 0.998 mol CH₄ × 2 = 1.995 mol O₂ × 32.00 = 63.9 g.

**Assessment:** Lab and quiz.

**Answer key:** Lab: full credit for predicted CO₂ (5.0 g ÷ 84.01 = 0.0595 mol → 0.0595 × 44.01 = 2.6 g), measured mass loss, percent yield, and two error sources (CO₂ staying dissolved in the liquid, splashing, water evaporating, incomplete reaction). Quiz key: 1) In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, mol H₂O from 4.0 mol O₂ — 8.0 mol. 2) In N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, mol H₂ needed for 5.0 mol N₂ — 15 mol. 3) Grams NH₃ from 28.0 g N₂ — 34.1 g. 4) Grams H₂O from 16.0 g CH₄ burned — 35.9 g. 5) Grams O₂ needed to burn 16.0 g CH₄ — 63.9 g (≈ 64 g). 6) Theoretical yield 2.6 g, actual 2.1 g — 81% (80.8%). 7) Grams CO₂ from 10.0 g C₃H₈ (C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O): 10.0 ÷ 44.09 = 0.227 mol × 3 × 44.01 = 29.9 g.

## Modern World History: The course of World War II

**Prepare:** WWII maps of Europe and the Pacific, FDR's 'Day of Infamy' speech audio (December 8, 1941), Eisenhower's D-Day Order of the Day and his unused 'in case of failure' note (National Archives / Eisenhower Library).

**Watch for:**
- Seeing the war as won mainly on the Western Front. The Eastern Front saw most German casualties.
- Thinking Pearl Harbor caused U.S. entry into the European war directly. Germany declared war on the U.S. on December 11, 1941.

### Day 1 (Monday): Blitzkrieg to Britain
*Student:* Map 1939–1941 Axis conquests and Battle of Britain.

*How to teach:* The student maps Axis conquests 1939–1941 (Poland, Denmark, Norway, Low Countries, France, Balkans, invasion of the USSR) and explains blitzkrieg. Read a short account of the Battle of Britain and discuss why it mattered.

- **Q:** Why was the Battle of Britain significant?
  **A:** The RAF prevented German air superiority, so Hitler postponed invading Britain.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Eastern Front
*Student:* Analyze Stalingrad as a turning point.

*How to teach:* Read a short account of Stalingrad (August 1942–February 1943) and look at a map of the Eastern Front. The student writes a paragraph explaining why it was a turning point using two pieces of evidence.

- **Q:** Why was Stalingrad a turning point?
  **A:** Germany lost an entire army, and the Soviets began pushing west.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pacific
*Student:* Listen to FDR's 'Day of Infamy' speech and map Pearl Harbor, Midway and island hopping.

*How to teach:* Play FDR's 'Day of Infamy' speech (about 7 minutes). The student notes its rhetoric, then maps Pearl Harbor, Midway and the island-hopping route toward Japan. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What phrase did FDR use?
  **A:** 'A date which will live in infamy.'
- **Q:** Why was Midway a turning point?
  **A:** The U.S. sank four Japanese aircraft carriers.

### Day 4 (Thursday): D-Day
*Student:* Analyze Eisenhower's order of the day and his unused failure note.

*How to teach:* Read Eisenhower's Order of the Day ('You are about to embark upon the Great Crusade') and his unused failure note. The student compares tone and purpose and explains what the note reveals about leadership.

- **Q:** What did Eisenhower's unused note say?
  **A:** The landings failed and 'if any blame or fault attaches to the attempt it is mine alone.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on turning points and leaders.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key without notes (about 20 minutes). Grade it together; for each missed item the student finds the answer in their notes and writes a one-sentence correction.

- **Q:** When did Japan surrender?
  **A:** August 15, 1945 (formally signed September 2, 1945).

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz (answers): 1) Blitzkrieg → 'lightning war' using tanks and aircraft. 2) Battle of Britain (1940) → RAF stopped Germany's air campaign, preventing invasion. 3) Operation Barbarossa → German invasion of the USSR, June 22, 1941. 4) Stalingrad → German 6th Army surrendered February 1943; turning point in the East. 5) Pearl Harbor → December 7, 1941. 6) Midway (June 1942) → U.S. sank four Japanese carriers; Pacific turning point. 7) Island hopping → capturing key islands while bypassing others. 8) D-Day → June 6, 1944, Normandy. 9) V-E Day → May 8, 1945.

## Spanish III: La poesía

**Prepare:** Locate José Martí's 'Yo soy un hombre sincero' (Versos sencillos I, public domain), Pablo Neruda's 'Oda al tomate' (Odas elementales; use a library or licensed copy), and a Gabriela Mistral poem (e.g., from Ternura). Preview a sung version of 'Guantanamera', which uses lines from Martí.

**Watch for:**
- Counting syllables like English: Spanish meter counts syllables with sinalefa (vowels across words join) — Martí's lines are octosyllabic (8 syllables).
- Confusing metaphor and simile: a simile uses como; a metaphor states it directly.
- Reading poems line by line as separate sentences: follow punctuation across lines (encabalgamiento).

### Day 1 (Monday): Martí
*Student:* Read 'Yo soy un hombre sincero' and identify imagery.

*How to teach:* Read 'Yo soy un hombre sincero' aloud twice; your child marks images of nature and the poet's identity, then listens to 'Guantanamera'.

- **Q:** Where does the speaker say he comes from?
  **A:** 'De donde crece la palma' — from where the palm tree grows (Cuba).
- **Q:** What does the speaker want to do before he dies?
  **A:** Share (echar) the verses of his soul — express his deepest feelings in poetry.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Neruda
*Student:* Read 'Oda al tomate' and list its metaphors.

*How to teach:* Read Neruda's 'Oda al tomate'; your child lists metaphors and personifications and explains each in Spanish.

- **Q:** How does Neruda treat an ordinary tomato in the ode?
  **A:** He celebrates it as something marvelous, personifying it and comparing it to a star or sun (an 'astro de tierra') that fills kitchens and summer meals.
- **Q:** What is an 'oda'?
  **A:** A poem of praise addressed to a person or thing; Neruda's Odas elementales praise everyday objects.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mistral
*Student:* Read a Gabriela Mistral poem and discuss its themes.

*How to teach:* Read a Gabriela Mistral poem; discuss themes and learn that she was the first Latin American to win the Nobel Prize in Literature (1945).

- **Q:** Name two common themes in Mistral's poetry.
  **A:** Love for children and motherhood, nature, loss and grief, and her Chilean/Latin American identity.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mi oda
*Student:* Write an 'Oda a…' about an everyday object.

*How to teach:* Your child writes an 'Oda a…' about an everyday object (calcetín, cuchara, mochila) using short lines and metaphors.

- **Q:** Write two lines of an ode with a metaphor.
  **A:** Example: Oh, cuchara, / pequeña luna de metal.

### Day 5 (Friday): Recital
*Student:* Recite your ode and one memorized stanza of Martí.

*How to teach:* Your child recites the ode and one memorized stanza of Martí; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** How many syllables are in each line of 'Yo soy un hombre sincero'?
  **A:** Eight (octosílabos).

**Assessment:** Recital and ode.

**Answer key:** Recital and ode rubric: Ode — 4 = 20+ short lines addressed to an everyday object, 4+ metaphors/similes, sensory images, accurate Spanish; 3 = fewer images or a few errors; 2 = list-like with errors; 1 = incomplete. Recital — 4 = memorized Martí stanza and ode delivered with clear pronunciation, pauses at punctuation, expression; 3 = minor slips; 2 = reads with hesitation; 1 = not prepared.

*How to teach:* Draw three offensive plays (slant-flat, curl, post) with routes and timing.

*How to teach:* Practice man-to-man vs. zone coverage with a partner, switching every 5 minutes.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Choking and child/infant CPR

**Prepare:** Gather a doll for infant practice, a manikin or firm pillow, and a sturdy chair. Practice choking techniques only on a pillow or chair, never with real thrusts on a person. Review the 2025 American Heart Association guideline update: for infants, compressions with two thumbs (encircling hands) or the heel of one hand; for choking adults and children, alternating 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts is now recommended (the Red Cross also teaches this).

**Watch for:**
- Students do abdominal thrusts on infants. Infants (under 1 year) get back blows and chest thrusts only.
- Students give back blows to a person who can cough forcefully. If they can cough or speak, encourage coughing and stay with them; act only if they can't breathe, cough or speak.
- Students compress infants too deeply or too shallowly. Infant compressions are about 1.5 inches (4 cm), one-third the chest depth.

### Day 1 (Monday): Adult choking
*Student:* Practice abdominal thrusts on a pillow-wrapped chair back and back blows, then self-rescue thrusts against a chair.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate on a pillow strapped to a chair back: back blows, then abdominal thrusts; then self-rescue by thrusting the abdomen against a chair back.

- **Q:** How can you tell someone is truly choking?
  **A:** They can't cough, speak or breathe and often clutch the throat.
- **Q:** Where do you place your fist for abdominal thrusts?
  **A:** Just above the navel, below the breastbone, thumb side in.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Child CPR
*Student:* Learn child CPR differences (one or two hands, about 2 inches deep) and practice 5 cycles of 30:2 on a manikin or pillow.

*How to teach:* Explain child CPR (age 1 to puberty) differences and practice five 30:2 cycles.

- **Q:** How deep are compressions for a child?
  **A:** About 2 inches, about one-third the depth of the chest.
- **Q:** If you're alone and didn't see a child collapse, when do you call 911?
  **A:** Give about 2 minutes (5 cycles) of CPR first, then call (unless you have a phone to call on speaker immediately).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Infant CPR
*Student:* Practice infant CPR on a doll using two fingers or two thumbs, about 1.5 inches deep, at 100–120 per minute.

*How to teach:* Practice infant CPR on the doll with the two-thumb encircling technique (or two fingers as older materials show), counting aloud.

- **Q:** How deep and fast are infant compressions?
  **A:** About 1.5 inches at 100–120 per minute.
- **Q:** How do you give breaths to an infant?
  **A:** Cover the infant's mouth and nose with your mouth and give gentle puffs, each about 1 second, until the chest rises.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Infant choking
*Student:* Practice infant choking relief with 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts on a doll, supporting the head.

*How to teach:* Practice infant choking relief on the doll: 5 back blows, 5 chest thrusts, supporting the head and keeping it lower than the chest.

- **Q:** Why not use abdominal thrusts on infants?
  **A:** They can injure an infant's internal organs; back blows and chest thrusts are used instead.
- **Q:** What do you do if the infant becomes unresponsive?
  **A:** Call 911 if not done, begin CPR, and look in the mouth for the object before giving breaths (remove only if seen).

### Day 5 (Friday): Skills check
*Student:* Demonstrate adult, child and infant choking relief and child/infant CPR for a parent using a skills checklist.

*How to teach:* The student demonstrates adult, child and infant choking relief and child/infant CPR; score with the checklist.

- **Q:** What should you do if a choking adult becomes unresponsive?
  **A:** Lower them to the ground, call 911, and begin CPR starting with compressions; check the mouth for the object before breaths.

**Assessment:** Choking and pediatric CPR skills checklist.

**Answer key:** Skills checklist key: Adult/child choking — confirm choking (can't cough/speak/breathe; hands at throat), get consent, call 911 or have someone call, give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades and 5 abdominal thrusts (fist just above the navel, quick inward-upward), repeat until object out or person unresponsive (then begin CPR, checking mouth for object before breaths). Self-rescue — thrust abdomen against a chair back. Infant choking — support head and jaw, face-down on forearm with head lower than chest, 5 back blows, turn over, 5 chest thrusts on the center of the chest just below the nipple line, repeat. Child CPR — 30:2 (single rescuer), one or two hands, about 2 inches (one-third chest depth), 100–120/min. Infant CPR — 30:2, two thumbs (encircling) or heel of one hand per 2025 AHA, or two fingers per older training; about 1.5 inches; 100–120/min. Proficient = all steps correct.

## Computer Science Principles: Searching and algorithm efficiency

**Prepare:** Get a deck of 52 cards. Prepare a sheet with list sizes 8, 16, 32 … 1,024 for graphing. Have graph paper or a spreadsheet.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking binary search works on any list. It only works on sorted data.
- Thinking a faster computer fixes an unreasonable algorithm. Exponential growth outruns any hardware speedup.
- Calling binary search 'twice as fast'. It's dramatically faster for large lists: about log₂(n) steps vs. n steps.

### Day 1 (Monday): Linear search
*Student:* Search for a target card in a shuffled deck of 52 cards one at a time, counting steps over five trials.

*How to teach:* Shuffle the deck. The student searches for a target card one at a time, counting checks; repeat five trials and average. Discuss best case (1), worst case (52) and average (about 26).

- **Q:** In the worst case, how many checks does linear search need for 52 cards?
  **A:** 52 (or 52 if the card is last; still 52 checks if it's not there at all).
- **Q:** Does linear search need the list to be sorted?
  **A:** No — it checks every element in order, so it works on any list.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Binary search
*Student:* Search a sorted deck using binary search and compare the steps.

*How to teach:* Sort one suit (13 cards) or a larger ordered set. Model binary search: check the middle; if the target is higher, discard the lower half; repeat. Count steps and compare to linear search.

- **Q:** Using binary search on 13 sorted cards, what is the maximum number of checks?
  **A:** 4 (13 → 6 → 3 → 1).
- **Q:** Why must the data be sorted for binary search?
  **A:** It decides which half to throw away by comparing to the middle; that only works if smaller values are all on one side.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Efficiency
*Student:* Graph worst-case steps vs. list size (8 to 1,024) for linear and binary search and describe the growth as linear vs. logarithmic.

*How to teach:* Make a table of worst-case steps for n = 8, 16, 32, …, 1,024: linear = n; binary ≈ log₂(n) + 1. Graph both and describe the shapes (straight line vs. flattening curve).

- **Q:** Worst-case binary search steps for 1,024 items?
  **A:** About 11 (log₂ 1024 = 10, plus one final check).
- **Q:** If the list grows from 1,024 to 2,048 items, how do linear and binary worst cases change?
  **A:** Linear doubles (1,024 → 2,048); binary increases by just one step.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Across disciplines
*Student:* Show how binary search applies to a dictionary, debugging and guessing games.

*How to teach:* Show binary search in real life: finding a word in a paper dictionary, 'git bisect'-style debugging by halving the code, and the 'guess a number 1–100' game (7 guesses max).

- **Q:** What's the maximum number of guesses to find a number from 1–100 with binary search?
  **A:** 7 (2^7 = 128 ≥ 100).
- **Q:** How can binary search help debugging?
  **A:** Comment out half the code to see which half has the bug, then halve that half, and so on.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Complete AP-style questions classifying algorithms as reasonable (polynomial) or unreasonable (exponential) time.

*How to teach:* Give the efficiency questions in the assessment key.

- **Q:** Is an algorithm that takes n² steps reasonable or unreasonable? 2ⁿ?
  **A:** n² is reasonable (polynomial); 2ⁿ is unreasonable (exponential).

**Assessment:** Efficiency questions.

**Answer key:** Efficiency questions: 1) Worst-case linear search of 1,000 items → 1,000 checks. 2) Binary search of 1,024 sorted items → about 10–11 checks (log₂ 1024 = 10). 3) Doubling list size adds 1 step to binary search, doubles linear. 4) Binary search requires a sorted list. 5) Classify n² → reasonable (polynomial). 6) 2ⁿ → unreasonable (exponential). 7) n³ → reasonable. 8) Checking every subset of n items (2ⁿ) → unreasonable. 9) An algorithm that looks at each pair of n items (n²) → reasonable. 10) A heuristic is used when the exact solution takes unreasonable time. Score 8+ mastery.
