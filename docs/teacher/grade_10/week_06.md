# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 6

## English 10: Antigone: Creon vs. Antigone

**Prepare:** Antigone text, character chart from Week 5. Plan to read Scene 2 aloud with the student.

**Watch for:**
- Seeing Creon as purely evil. He has a real argument about order after civil war; his flaw is pride and refusal to listen.
- Treating Haemon as only a love interest. His argument is political: the city sympathizes with Antigone.

### Day 1 (Monday): Read Scene 2
*Student:* Read Scene 2 (the confrontation) and annotate each rhetorical move Antigone and Creon make.

*How to teach:* Read Scene 2 aloud. The student annotates rhetorical moves (appeal to the gods, accusation, insult, appeal to loyalty). Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What argument does Antigone make for defying Creon?
  **A:** She says the decree was not made by the gods, and the gods' unwritten, unchanging laws matter more than a mortal king's edict.
- **Q:** How does Creon respond to Antigone's defiance?
  **A:** He treats it as a challenge to his authority and pride, saying he won't be ruled by a woman, and condemns her.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Stichomythia practice
*Student:* Perform the rapid line exchange aloud with a partner and explain how the form heightens conflict.

*How to teach:* Explain stichomythia: rapid alternating single lines. Perform the exchange; then the student explains its effect. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How does stichomythia heighten conflict?
  **A:** Quick one-line exchanges feel like a duel — each speaker snaps back, showing neither will yield.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ode 2 & figurative language
*Student:* Read Ode 2 and identify the extended metaphor of the curse as a storm; explain its effect in a short paragraph.

*How to teach:* Read Ode 2. The student identifies the extended metaphor of the curse as a storm/sea and writes a short paragraph on its effect. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does the storm imagery in Ode 2 suggest about the curse on Oedipus's house?
  **A:** The curse is like waves of a storm rolling over the family generation after generation — overwhelming and unstoppable.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Read Scene 3 – Haemon
*Student:* Read Scene 3 and evaluate Haemon's arguments to his father; list his three strongest points.

*How to teach:* Read Scene 3 aloud. The student lists Haemon's three strongest points. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Which image does Haemon use to argue his father should yield?
  **A:** Trees that bend in a flooding stream survive while stiff ones are torn up (and a sailor who never slackens sail capsizes).
- **Q:** How does the conversation between Creon and Haemon end?
  **A:** In anger; Haemon leaves, warning that Antigone's death will cause another, and that Creon will never see him again.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz & journal
*Student:* Complete a quiz on Scenes 2–3 and a journal entry on whether Creon is a tyrant or a ruler doing his duty.

*How to teach:* Give the Scenes 2–3 quiz from the assessment key and check the annotations. Then the student writes a half-page journal entry taking a side — is Creon a tyrant or a ruler doing his duty? — supported by at least one quoted line.

- **Q:** Give one line of evidence that Creon is a tyrant.
  **A:** He tells Haemon the city belongs to the ruler / he refuses to listen to anyone, insisting the state is his to command.

**Assessment:** Quiz and annotation check.

**Answer key:** Quiz on Scenes 2–3 (answers): 1) How is Antigone caught? → The Sentry's guards catch her returning to the body after it was uncovered. 2) Antigone's defense → Creon's decree is not the gods' law; the unwritten laws of the gods are higher. 3) What does Ismene try to do? → Claim a share of the guilt; Antigone refuses. 4) What is Creon's sentence? → Antigone will be sealed alive in a stone vault/tomb. 5) Haemon's key arguments → the city mourns Antigone; a wise man listens; a ruler who thinks only he is right is empty; trees that bend in a flood survive. 6) Haemon's parting threat → her death will cause another death. Annotation check: full credit for at least 8 labeled rhetorical moves across Scenes 2–3.

## Geometry: Compositions of transformations

**Prepare:** Graph paper, colored pencils, scissors, tape, cardstock for tessellation; GeoGebra. Look up a few M.C. Escher tessellations to show the student.

**Watch for:**
- Assuming order doesn't matter in compositions. Show a translation then reflection versus the reverse can give different images.
- Forgetting that two reflections over parallel lines give a translation of twice the distance between the lines.

### Day 1 (Monday): Compositions
*Student:* Perform a translation followed by a reflection and compare to reversing the order.

*How to teach:* Have the student translate a triangle and then reflect it, and then do the steps in the reverse order. Compare final images and discuss that compositions are applied one after another like functions.

- **Q:** Point (2, 3): translate by ⟨0, −5⟩, then reflect over the x-axis. Where does it end up?
  **A:** (2, −2) after translating, then (2, 2) after reflecting.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Two reflections
*Student:* Show reflecting over two parallel lines is a translation and over intersecting lines is a rotation.

*How to teach:* In GeoGebra reflect a figure over x = 1 and then x = 4; measure the shift (6 units = twice the distance). Then reflect over two lines meeting at 40° and measure the rotation (80°).

- **Q:** Two reflections over intersecting lines that meet at 50° equal what single transformation?
  **A:** A rotation of 100° about the intersection point.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Find the sequence
*Student:* Given a figure and its image, write a sequence of rigid motions mapping one onto the other (8 problems).

*How to teach:* Model working backward: compare orientation (a flip means a reflection is needed), then match one vertex with a translation or rotation. The student writes sequences for the 8 problems and checks by performing them.

- **Q:** If the image has the opposite orientation (clockwise vs. counterclockwise labels), what must the sequence include?
  **A:** An odd number of reflections.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Tessellation project
*Student:* Design a translation or rotation tessellation on paper using a modified square.

*How to teach:* Show how to cut a piece from one side of a square and tape it to the opposite side (translation) or rotate it about a corner (rotation). The student traces the tile repeatedly to fill a page and colors it.

- **Q:** Why does a modified square still tessellate?
  **A:** Whatever is removed from one side is added to the matching side, so the tiles still fit together without gaps or overlaps.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Short quiz plus tessellation explanation.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and have the student explain their tessellation aloud: which transformation, and why it fills the plane. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Can every composition of rigid motions be described as a single rigid motion?
  **A:** Yes — a composition of rigid motions is itself a rigid motion (a translation, rotation, reflection or glide reflection).

**Assessment:** Tessellation and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Translate (1, 2) by ⟨3, 0⟩ then reflect over the y-axis → (−4, 2); reverse order → (2, 2) — different. 2) Reflect over x = 1 then x = 4 → equivalent to a translation 6 units right. 3) Reflect over two lines meeting at 35° → rotation of 70° about their intersection. 4) Give a sequence mapping triangle A(1, 1), B(3, 1), C(1, 2) to A'(−1, −1), B'(−3, −1), C'(−1, −2) → a 180° rotation about the origin (or reflect over the x-axis then y-axis). Tessellation rubric: 4 = tiles fit without gaps/overlaps, correct transformation named and explained, neat color; 3 = tiles fit, explanation minor gaps; 2 = gaps or wrong transformation named; 1 = incomplete.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Electrons and light

**Prepare:** Build the spectroscope ahead with the student: a cereal box, an old CD (or DVD) as the diffraction grating, a narrow slit cut from foil, and tape (search 'CD spectroscope cereal box' for a template). Find incandescent, LED and fluorescent light sources at home. Queue a flame-test demonstration video and PhET 'Models of the Hydrogen Atom'. Print a blank periodic table.

**Watch for:**
- Students think the color in a flame test comes from the flame itself. The colored light comes from electrons in the metal ions dropping from excited energy levels to lower levels.
- Students fill 3d before 4s or forget the order. Use the diagonal (Aufbau) chart: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p.
- Students count all electrons as valence electrons. Valence electrons are in the outermost s and p sublevels (main-group elements: the group number's ones digit, e.g., Cl has 7).

### Day 1 (Monday): Build a spectroscope
*Student:* Build a spectroscope from a CD and cereal box and observe incandescent, LED and fluorescent lights.

*How to teach:* Assemble the spectroscope and have the student look at a bright incandescent bulb, a white LED, and a fluorescent tube or CFL (never look at the Sun). The student sketches each spectrum with colored pencils and describes the differences.

- **Q:** Which light source shows a continuous spectrum, and which shows bright lines?
  **A:** Incandescent shows a continuous spectrum (a hot glowing filament); fluorescent shows bright lines from excited mercury atoms.
- **Q:** What does the CD do in the spectroscope?
  **A:** Its closely spaced tracks act as a diffraction grating, spreading light into its component wavelengths.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Emission spectra
*Student:* Watch flame-test video demonstrations and match colors to elements.

*How to teach:* Watch a flame-test video, pausing before each reveal so the student predicts. The student records element and color, then uses a reference table to identify two 'unknown' flame colors you describe.

- **Q:** An unknown salt gives a crimson-red flame. Which ions could it contain?
  **A:** Lithium (crimson) or strontium (red).
- **Q:** Why are fireworks different colors?
  **A:** Different metal salts are added; each metal's electrons emit characteristic wavelengths when excited (e.g., strontium red, barium green, copper blue, sodium yellow).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Energy levels
*Student:* Use the Bohr model to explain why each element has a unique spectrum.

*How to teach:* Use PhET Models of the Hydrogen Atom (Bohr view) to show an electron jumping up when absorbing a photon and dropping down emitting one. Model on paper: a larger drop gives a higher-energy, shorter-wavelength photon. The student explains in writing why spectra act like fingerprints.

- **Q:** In hydrogen, does a drop from level 3 to 2 or from level 4 to 2 give the higher-energy photon?
  **A:** 4 → 2, because it is a larger energy difference (it produces blue-green light, 486 nm, versus red, 656 nm, for 3 → 2).
- **Q:** Why can't a hydrogen atom emit any color it wants?
  **A:** Electrons can only occupy specific energy levels, so only specific energy differences — specific photon energies — are possible.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Electron configurations
*Student:* Write configurations and orbital notation for elements 1–36.

*How to teach:* Teach the Aufbau order and orbital diagrams (boxes with up/down arrows, Hund's rule: fill singly before pairing). The student writes configurations for elements 1–36, using noble gas shorthand after neon, and highlights valence electrons. Point out the exceptions Cr ([Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵) and Cu ([Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰).

- **Q:** Write the electron configuration and orbital diagram for nitrogen.
  **A:** 1s² 2s² 2p³; the three 2p electrons each occupy a separate box with parallel spins (Hund's rule).
- **Q:** Write the configuration of calcium in noble gas notation.
  **A:** [Ar] 4s²

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on configurations and spectra.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and grade it together. Collect the spectroscope sketches and observation write-up.

- **Q:** How many valence electrons does phosphorus have?
  **A:** 5 (3s² 3p³).

**Assessment:** Spectroscope observations and quiz.

**Answer key:** Spectroscope observations: full credit for sketches of each light — incandescent: continuous rainbow; fluorescent: continuous dim background with distinct bright lines (violet, green, yellow-orange lines from mercury); white LED: smooth band strongest in blue and yellow-green with a weak gap — and a sentence explaining continuous vs. line spectra. Quiz key: 1) Configuration of O: 1s² 2s² 2p⁴. 2) Na: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹. 3) Fe: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶ ([Ar] 4s² 3d⁶). 4) Br valence electrons: 7. 5) Why each element has a unique spectrum — each has a unique set of energy levels, so the energy differences (photon colors) are unique. 6) Higher-energy photon: violet or red? Violet. 7) Flame color of sodium — yellow-orange; copper — blue-green; strontium/lithium — red. 8) What happens when an electron absorbs energy — it jumps to a higher energy level (excited state). 9) Noble gas notation for K — [Ar] 4s¹. 10) Element with configuration [Ar] 4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p⁵ — bromine.

## Modern World History: Origins of the Industrial Revolution

**Prepare:** Maps of British coalfields, rivers and ports; images of the spinning jenny, water frame, Watt steam engine and early railways (e.g., Stephenson's Rocket). A textile mill case study (e.g., Arkwright's Cromford Mill).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Industrial Revolution began with factories. It began with agricultural changes that freed labor and grew the population.
- Crediting James Watt with inventing the steam engine. Newcomen built an earlier engine (1712); Watt greatly improved its efficiency (patent 1769).

### Day 1 (Monday): Agricultural revolution
*Student:* Read about enclosure and crop rotation and explain links to population growth.

*How to teach:* Explain enclosure and new methods (Jethro Tull's seed drill, crop rotation with turnips and clover). The student draws a cause-effect chain from these changes to population growth and a workforce for factories.

- **Q:** How did enclosure contribute to industrialization?
  **A:** It made farming more efficient but pushed many small farmers off the land, creating a labor force that moved to cities and factories.
- **Q:** Why did crop rotation increase food supply?
  **A:** Planting crops like turnips and clover restored soil nutrients, so fields didn't need to lie fallow and fed more livestock.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Why Britain?
*Student:* Map Britain's coal, iron, rivers and ports and list five factors that favored industrialization.

*How to teach:* The student maps Britain's coal and iron deposits, navigable rivers and major ports, then lists five factors favoring early industrialization. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Why were rivers important to early industry?
  **A:** They provided water power for mills and cheap transport for heavy goods like coal.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Technology
*Student:* Create an illustrated chart of inventions (spinning jenny, water frame, steam engine, railroad).

*How to teach:* The student builds an illustrated chart: invention, inventor, date, what it did, and its effect (flying shuttle 1733, spinning jenny c. 1764, water frame 1769, Watt's improved steam engine 1769/1776, power loom 1785, Stephenson's railways 1825–1830).

- **Q:** Why was the steam engine so important?
  **A:** It provided reliable power anywhere, not just near rivers, driving factories, locomotives and steamships.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Factors of production
*Student:* Apply land, labor, capital and entrepreneurship to a textile mill case study.

*How to teach:* Define land, labor, capital and entrepreneurship. Apply them to a textile mill case study: the student identifies each factor in Arkwright's mill. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** In a textile mill, give an example of capital and of entrepreneurship.
  **A:** Capital: the building and machines (and money to buy them). Entrepreneurship: the owner (e.g., Richard Arkwright) organizing resources and taking the risk.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on causes and inventions.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key. Review answers, and have the student explain any missed item using their chart. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What were the 'factors of production' Britain had in abundance?
  **A:** Land with natural resources (coal, iron), labor (displaced farmers), capital (banks and investors), and entrepreneurs.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz (answers): 1) Enclosure → fencing common lands into private farms, pushing small farmers to cities. 2) Crop rotation → alternating crops (e.g., turnips, clover) to restore soil instead of leaving fields fallow. 3) Five factors favoring Britain → coal and iron, rivers and ports, colonies/markets, capital and banks, stable government and navy (also labor supply). 4) Spinning jenny → Hargreaves, spun many threads at once. 5) Water frame → Arkwright, water-powered spinning, led to factories. 6) Watt → improved steam engine efficiency. 7) First major passenger railway → Liverpool–Manchester (1830).

## Spanish III: Pretérito vs. imperfecto

**Prepare:** Draw a timeline graphic: wavy line for ongoing imperfect background, X marks for preterite events. Prepare a 30-item cloze passage (a short story with blanks) and 6-panel comic strips.

**Watch for:**
- Equating English 'was' with the imperfect every time: 'I was sick for a week' (completed) = Estuve enfermo una semana.
- Translating 'used to' and 'would (habitual)' with the conditional: habitual 'would' = imperfect (Every summer we would swim = Cada verano nadábamos).
- Verbs that change meaning: conocí (I met) vs. conocía (I knew); supe (I found out) vs. sabía (I knew); quise (I tried) vs. quería (I wanted).

### Day 1 (Monday): Reglas
*Student:* Learn the rules of preterite vs. imperfect (background vs. action) with a timeline graphic.

*How to teach:* Explain background (imperfect) vs. events (preterite) with the timeline. Model a short story labeling each verb.

- **Q:** Which tense and why: 'It was 8:00 when the movie started'?
  **A:** Eran las ocho (time = imperfect) cuando empezó la película (event = preterite).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Práctica
*Student:* Complete a 30-item cloze passage choosing preterite or imperfect.

*How to teach:* Your child completes the 30-item cloze passage and writes the reason beside each choice.

- **Q:** Choose: 'Yo ___ (leer) cuando mi hermano ___ (entrar).'
  **A:** leía; entró

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cómic
*Student:* Narrate a six-panel comic strip in the past using both tenses.

*How to teach:* Your child narrates a 6-panel comic using both tenses and connectors (mientras, de repente, entonces).

- **Q:** Translate: 'While the kids were playing, it suddenly started to rain.'
  **A:** Mientras los niños jugaban, de repente empezó a llover.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparar con inglés
*Student:* Compare 'was/used to/did' in English with Spanish aspect.

*How to teach:* Compare English 'was/used to/did' with Spanish aspect; translate 10 sentences and discuss.

- **Q:** Translate: 'I knew her' vs. 'I met her.'
  **A:** La conocía. / La conocí.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Quiz on preterite vs. imperfect.

*How to teach:* Give the preterite vs. imperfect quiz.

- **Q:** Choose: 'Ayer yo (quería/quise) llamarte, pero no (podía/pude).'
  **A:** Context-dependent; most natural: quería llamarte, pero no pude (I wanted to but didn't manage).

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz sample key: 1) Eran las diez cuando llegaron — eran (time), llegaron (event) 2) Mientras cocinaba, sonó el teléfono 3) De niño, nadaba todos los días 4) Ayer conocí a tu primo (met) 5) Cuando supe la noticia, lloré 6) Hacía frío y nevaba (description). Score each with the correct reason.

## Music & Media Arts: Drum patterns and beat-making

**Prepare:** Set up a free BandLab account (parent-managed for under-18 privacy settings) and explore the Studio and drum sequencer. Headphones help.

**Watch for:**
- Students put snare on every beat in a rock beat. Standard rock: kick on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4 (backbeat), hi-hat eighth notes.
- Students quantize everything to 100%, making grooves stiff. Swing or slight humanization creates feel, especially in hip-hop.
- Students confuse BPM with loudness. BPM is tempo (beats per minute).

### Day 1 (Monday): DAW tour
*Student:* Set up a BandLab account and learn the interface.

*How to teach:* Tour the DAW interface: tracks, timeline, transport, mixer, instruments.

- **Q:** What does DAW stand for?
  **A:** Digital Audio Workstation.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rock beat
*Student:* Program a basic rock beat at 100 bpm.

*How to teach:* Program a basic rock beat at 100 bpm.

- **Q:** On which beats does the snare play in a rock beat?
  **A:** Beats 2 and 4 (the backbeat).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hip-hop beat
*Student:* Program a hip-hop beat with swing.

*How to teach:* Program a hip-hop beat with swing.

- **Q:** What does swing do to eighth or sixteenth notes?
  **A:** Delays the off-beat notes slightly, creating a long-short lilt.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Genre beats
*Student:* Program reggaeton dembow and a four-on-the-floor house beat.

*How to teach:* Program a reggaeton dembow and a four-on-the-floor house beat.

- **Q:** What does 'four-on-the-floor' mean?
  **A:** The kick drum hits on every quarter-note beat.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Share four beats with a labeled screenshot.

*How to teach:* Share four beats with labeled screenshots.

- **Q:** What is quantizing?
  **A:** Snapping recorded notes to the nearest grid position to correct timing.

**Assessment:** Beat portfolio.

**Answer key:** Beat portfolio: four beats with labeled screenshots. Rock (100 bpm): kick 1 and 3, snare 2 and 4, closed hi-hat eighths. Hip-hop (85–95 bpm): kick pattern syncopated, snare/clap on 2 and 4, hi-hats with swing (~55–65%). Reggaeton dembow (~90–100 bpm): kick on every beat; snare on the 'a' of 1, '&' of 2, 'a' of 3, '&' of 4. House (120–128 bpm): kick on every beat (four-on-the-floor), clap/snare on 2 and 4, open hi-hat on the off-beats. Proficient = correct patterns, tempos and labels.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Climbing and balance challenges

**Prepare:** Find a local climbing gym (many have intro sessions) or a sturdy low playground structure for traverses. Get a slackline or use a curb or 2x4 on the ground as a balance beam. Verify waivers and staff instruction at gyms.

**Watch for:**
- Students climb with their arms. Good climbers push with their legs and keep arms straight to save energy.
- Students think spotting means catching the climber. A spotter guides the climber's fall to protect the head and neck and land feet-first on the pad.
- Students climb higher than a safe height without a rope. Keep bouldering low (feet no higher than about 3 feet on home structures) and over padding.

### Day 1 (Monday): Grip & core
*Student:* Do a grip and core strength workout (dead hangs, planks, farmer carries).

*How to teach:* Lead a grip and core workout: dead hangs (3 × max), planks (3 × 30–60 s), farmer carries (3 × 30 m).

- **Q:** Why does grip strength matter in climbing?
  **A:** Holding onto holds depends on finger and forearm strength; fatigue there ends the climb.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Climbing technique
*Student:* Learn footwork and three points of contact via video and practice on low structures.

*How to teach:* Watch a footwork video; the student practices three points of contact and precise foot placements on low structures.

- **Q:** What does 'three points of contact' mean?
  **A:** Always keeping two hands and one foot, or two feet and one hand, on the structure while moving one limb.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Bouldering or traverse
*Student:* Visit a climbing gym or practice low traverses with spotting.

*How to teach:* At a gym or low structure, the student boulders/traverses with a trained spotter.

- **Q:** How should you land if you fall while bouldering?
  **A:** Feet first with knees bent, then roll back onto the pad; don't put arms out to catch yourself.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Balance
*Student:* Practice slackline or balance-beam challenges.

*How to teach:* Practice slackline or balance-beam challenges (walk forward, backward, turn, single-leg hold).

- **Q:** What helps balance on a slackline?
  **A:** Eyes on a fixed point ahead, soft knees, arms up and out, and small adjustments from the hips.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflection
*Student:* Write about managing fear and risk.

*How to teach:* The student writes about managing fear and risk while climbing.

- **Q:** What's the difference between perceived risk and actual risk?
  **A:** Perceived risk is how dangerous something feels; actual risk is the real chance of harm. Safety gear and spotting lower actual risk even when it feels scary.

**Assessment:** Skill checklist.

**Answer key:** Skill checklist: proficient = maintains three points of contact, quiet precise footwork on edges, arms straight when resting, hips close to the wall, controlled down-climb or safe fall landing (bent knees, roll back), correct spotting stance (hands up, near the hips/torso), completes a traverse of at least 15 feet, and 30+ seconds on a slackline or balance beam. Developing = 1–2 cues needed. Beginning = 3+.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Right-of-way at intersections

**Prepare:** Draw intersections on large paper (four-way stop, uncontrolled, T, roundabout) and gather toy cars. Read the handbook right-of-way section.

**Watch for:**
- Students think right-of-way is something you take. The law says who must yield; no one has an absolute right, and drivers must yield whenever needed to avoid a collision.
- Students think at a four-way stop the larger road goes first. The first vehicle to stop goes first; if two arrive together, the vehicle on the right goes first.
- Students think pedestrians only have right-of-way in marked crosswalks. Pedestrians have the right-of-way in marked and unmarked crosswalks at intersections.

### Day 1 (Monday): Intersections
*Student:* Use toy cars on paper intersections to model four-way stops, uncontrolled and T-intersections.

*How to teach:* Using toy cars, model four-way stops, uncontrolled intersections and T-intersections. The student explains who goes first in each arrangement.

- **Q:** Car A and Car B stop at a four-way stop at the same time. Car B is on Car A's right. Who goes first?
  **A:** Car B — when two vehicles arrive at the same time, the driver on the left yields to the vehicle on the right.
- **Q:** At a T-intersection with no signs, who has the right-of-way?
  **A:** Vehicles on the through road.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Roundabouts
*Student:* Study roundabout rules (yield to traffic in the circle, travel counterclockwise) and diagram entering and exiting.

*How to teach:* The student diagrams entering and exiting a roundabout: slow down, yield to traffic in the circle and to pedestrians, enter when there is a gap, travel counterclockwise, signal right before exiting.

- **Q:** Which direction do you travel in a roundabout?
  **A:** Counterclockwise (to the right).
- **Q:** Who do you yield to when entering?
  **A:** Traffic already in the roundabout and pedestrians in crosswalks.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pedestrians
*Student:* Learn pedestrian right-of-way at marked and unmarked crosswalks and blind pedestrians with white canes or guide dogs.

*How to teach:* The student reads pedestrian rules and lists where pedestrians have the right-of-way.

- **Q:** Does a pedestrian have the right-of-way at a corner with no painted crosswalk?
  **A:** Yes — every intersection corner has an unmarked crosswalk.
- **Q:** What should you do when you see a pedestrian with a white cane?
  **A:** Stop and let the person cross; do not honk or call out directions, which can confuse them.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Emergency vehicles & school buses
*Student:* Learn to pull right and stop for emergency vehicles and to stop for flashing red school bus lights.

*How to teach:* The student learns responses to emergency vehicles and school buses, then explains them in his or her own words.

- **Q:** An ambulance with siren and lights approaches from behind. What do you do?
  **A:** Drive to the right edge of the road and stop until it passes (if in an intersection, continue through first, then pull over).
- **Q:** A school bus ahead has flashing red lights. What do you do?
  **A:** Stop and remain stopped as long as the red lights flash (unless it is on the other side of a divided highway or multilane highway with two or more lanes in each direction).

### Day 5 (Friday): Scenario quiz
*Student:* Solve 15 right-of-way scenarios and explain each answer.

*How to teach:* Give 15 written right-of-way scenarios with diagrams; the student answers and explains each.

- **Q:** You are turning left on a green light and an oncoming car is approaching. Who yields?
  **A:** You do — left-turning drivers yield to oncoming traffic close enough to be a hazard.

**Assessment:** Scenario quiz.

**Answer key:** Scenario quiz key (examples; full credit requires the correct answer and a reason): 1) Two cars arrive at a four-way stop at the same time — the car on the right goes first. 2) Uncontrolled intersection, cars arrive together — yield to the car on the right. 3) T-intersection without signs — vehicles on the through road have the right-of-way. 4) Turning left at a green light — yield to oncoming traffic and pedestrians. 5) Entering a roundabout — yield to traffic already in the circle; travel counterclockwise. 6) Pedestrian in an unmarked crosswalk at a corner — stop and yield. 7) Blind pedestrian with a white cane or guide dog — always yield; do not honk. 8) Emergency vehicle with siren behind you — pull to the right edge and stop until it passes (do not stop in an intersection). 9) School bus with flashing red lights on a two-lane road — stop from either direction until the lights stop flashing. 10) School bus on a divided highway with a median, you are on the opposite side — you do not have to stop. 11) Merging from a driveway — yield to traffic on the road. 12) Entering from an alley — stop and yield to pedestrians and traffic. 13) Car already in the intersection — yield to it. 14) Funeral procession — yield. 15) Left turn at a four-way stop when another car arrived first — let it go first.

## Computer Science Principles: Networks, IP addresses and packets

**Prepare:** Watch Code.org's 'How the Internet Works' video series (Wires, Cables and Wi-Fi; IP Addresses and DNS; Packets, Routing and Reliability) yourself first. Open the Code.org Internet Simulator. Gather 6 index cards or postcards for Day 4.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the internet and the World Wide Web are the same. The internet is the network of networks; the web is one service (web pages via HTTP) that runs on it.
- Believing a message travels as one piece along one fixed path. Data is split into packets that may take different routes and arrive out of order, then are reassembled.
- Thinking IPv6 just adds a few more addresses. IPv6 has 2^128 addresses versus 2^32 for IPv4 — about 7.9 × 10^28 times as many.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is the internet?
*Student:* Watch the Code.org 'How the Internet Works' video on wires, cables and Wi-Fi, then draw your home network.

*How to teach:* Watch the video together and pause to define network (connected devices that send data) and internet (network of networks). Have the student draw the home network: devices → Wi-Fi router → modem → internet service provider. Check that wired and wireless links are labeled.

- **Q:** What physical ways can bits travel between devices?
  **A:** Electricity through copper wire, light through fiber-optic cable, and radio waves (Wi-Fi, cellular).
- **Q:** What is the difference between the internet and the web?
  **A:** The internet is the global network of connected networks; the web is a system of linked pages and resources accessed over the internet with HTTP.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): IP addresses
*Student:* Find your device's IP address and compare IPv4 (32-bit) and IPv6 (128-bit) address spaces mathematically.

*How to teach:* Have the student find their device's IP address (Settings → Wi-Fi details, or 'what is my IP' for the public address) and note that home devices often have private addresses like 192.168.x.x. Calculate IPv4 = 2^32 and IPv6 = 2^128 address counts.

- **Q:** How many IPv4 addresses are possible?
  **A:** 2^32 = 4,294,967,296 (about 4.3 billion).
- **Q:** Why did the world need IPv6?
  **A:** The number of internet-connected devices grew beyond ~4.3 billion, so IPv4 addresses ran out; IPv6's 128 bits give about 3.4 × 10^38 addresses.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Packets
*Student:* Use the Code.org Internet Simulator to send a message split into packets and observe packets arriving out of order.

*How to teach:* In the Internet Simulator, have the student send a long message split into several packets and watch them arrive. Ask why they may arrive out of order (different routes, different delays).

- **Q:** Why might packets of one message arrive out of order?
  **A:** Each packet is routed independently, so they can take different paths with different delays.
- **Q:** What information must every packet carry besides the data?
  **A:** At least the destination and source addresses and a sequence number (plus total number of packets).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Packet reassembly
*Student:* Design a packet header with sequence numbers and send a 'postcard' message to a family member split across 6 cards.

*How to teach:* Have the student design a header format (to, from, packet # of total) and split a sentence across 6 cards. Shuffle and hand them to a family member to reassemble using only the headers. Discuss what happens if one card is lost.

- **Q:** If packet 4 of 6 is lost, how would the receiver know?
  **A:** The sequence numbers show 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 of 6 — number 4 is missing, so the receiver can request it again.
- **Q:** Why include the total number of packets in the header?
  **A:** So the receiver knows when it has all of them.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Answer 8 questions on addressing and packets and explain why messages are split into packets.

*How to teach:* Give the 8-question quiz in the assessment key and have the student write 2–3 sentences on why messages are split into packets.

- **Q:** Give two reasons the internet splits messages into packets.
  **A:** Packets let many users share the same links fairly, can be rerouted around broken or busy paths, and only lost pieces need to be resent instead of the whole message.

**Assessment:** Internet Simulator log and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) An IP address is a unique number identifying a device on a network so data can be delivered to it. 2) IPv4 = 32 bits ≈ 4.3 billion (4,294,967,296) addresses. 3) IPv6 = 128 bits ≈ 3.4 × 10^38 addresses. 4) A packet is a small chunk of data plus metadata (source, destination, sequence number). 5) Packets can arrive out of order because they take different routes. 6) Sequence numbers let the receiver reassemble packets in order. 7) A protocol is an agreed-upon set of rules for how data is sent. 8) Bandwidth is the maximum amount of data sent per second (bits per second). Explanation item: messages are split into packets so many users can share links, packets can be rerouted around failures, and only lost packets need resending. Internet Simulator log: complete if it shows each packet with header info and notes on out-of-order arrival.
