# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 10

## English 10: Julius Caesar Acts 2–3.1: Conspiracy and assassination

**Prepare:** Folger Julius Caesar. Parent preview: the assassination scene is violent but stylized.

**Watch for:**
- Accepting Brutus's reasoning at face value. Point out he admits Caesar hasn't done wrong yet — he kills for what Caesar might become.
- Thinking Brutus is simply a villain. He believes he acts for Rome; that's what makes him tragic.

### Day 1 (Monday): Brutus's soliloquy
*Student:* Read 2.1 and paraphrase Brutus's 'It must be by his death' soliloquy line by line, identifying his flawed reasoning.

*How to teach:* Read 2.1. The student paraphrases the soliloquy line by line and identifies the flaw in reasoning. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What is the flaw in Brutus's reasoning?
  **A:** He admits Caesar has not abused power yet; he kills him for what he might do — speculation, not evidence.
- **Q:** What does the 'serpent's egg' metaphor mean?
  **A:** Caesar is like a snake's egg: harmless now, but dangerous once hatched, so it should be killed in the shell.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Portia and Calpurnia
*Student:* Read 2.2–2.4 and compare the two wives' roles and warnings.

*How to teach:* Read 2.2–2.4. The student compares Portia and Calpurnia. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How does Portia prove she can be trusted with Brutus's secret?
  **A:** She shows a voluntary wound she gave herself in the thigh.
- **Q:** What famous line does Caesar say about cowards?
  **A:** 'Cowards die many times before their deaths; / The valiant never taste of death but once.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Act 3.1
*Student:* Read the assassination scene aloud and track stage directions; discuss why Brutus lets Antony speak.

*How to teach:* Read 3.1 aloud. The student tracks stage directions and discusses why Brutus lets Antony speak. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Why does Brutus allow Antony to speak at the funeral?
  **A:** He believes it will look honorable and that he can speak first and set terms; he underestimates Antony.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Motive debate
*Student:* Debate with a parent: Was the assassination justified? Each side cites three lines.

*How to teach:* Assign sides (switch halfway if time allows). Each side prepares three cited lines and a one-minute opening, then debate for 10–15 minutes with rebuttals; the student keeps debate notes for the assessment and ends by stating which side had stronger evidence.

- **Q:** Cite a line the conspirators could use to justify the killing.
  **A:** Brutus: 'Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more' (3.2), or his serpent's egg reasoning.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on Acts 2–3.1 and a timeline of the conspiracy.

*How to teach:* Give the Acts 2–3.1 quiz from the assessment key. The student then builds a timeline of the conspiracy from the forged letters to Antony's arrival after the assassination, checking order against the play.

- **Q:** What does Antony vow in his soliloquy over Caesar's body?
  **A:** Revenge and civil war — 'Cry havoc and let slip the dogs of war.'

**Assessment:** Quiz and debate notes.

**Answer key:** Quiz on Acts 2–3.1 (answers): 1) Brutus's reasoning in 'It must be by his death' → Caesar might be corrupted by the crown, so kill him like a serpent's egg before it hatches. 2) Why does Brutus refuse to kill Antony? → He wants them to be 'sacrificers, but not butchers.' 3) Calpurnia's dream → Caesar's statue spouting blood as Romans bathed their hands in it. 4) How does Decius change Caesar's mind? → He reinterprets the dream as Rome drawing revival from Caesar, and says the Senate will offer a crown. 5) Caesar's last words → 'Et tu, Brute?—Then fall, Caesar.' 6) Why does Cassius object to Antony speaking? → Antony may stir the people against them. Timeline: full credit for correct order — forged letters, conspirators meet at Brutus's house, Calpurnia's dream, Artemidorus's warning, assassination, Antony's arrival.

## Geometry: Proving theorems about triangles

**Prepare:** Patty paper, scissors, paper triangles to tear, GeoGebra, graph paper. Review what centroid, incenter and circumcenter are.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing which segments meet at which center. Memory aid: medians → centroid; angle bisectors → incenter (center of inscribed circle); perpendicular bisectors → circumcenter.
- Thinking the exterior angle equals the adjacent interior angle. The exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.

### Day 1 (Monday): Angle sum
*Student:* Prove that triangle angles sum to 180° using a parallel line, and apply the Exterior Angle Theorem.

*How to teach:* Have the student tear the three corners of a paper triangle and line them up to form a straight angle. Then model the formal proof: draw a line through one vertex parallel to the opposite side and use alternate interior angles. Apply the Exterior Angle Theorem.

- **Q:** A triangle has angles (2x)°, (3x)° and (4x)°. Find each angle.
  **A:** 9x = 180 → x = 20; angles 40°, 60°, 80°.
- **Q:** An exterior angle measures 130° and one remote interior angle is 55°. Find the other remote interior angle.
  **A:** 75°.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Isosceles triangles
*Student:* Prove the base angles of an isosceles triangle are congruent using a reflection or SAS.

*How to teach:* Fold an isosceles triangle along its line of symmetry to show base angles match. Then write the proof: draw the bisector of the vertex angle and use SAS to get congruent triangles, so the base angles are congruent by CPCTC.

- **Q:** In isosceles △ABC with AB = AC, m∠B = 52°. Find m∠A.
  **A:** m∠C = 52°, so m∠A = 180 − 104 = 76°.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Midsegment
*Student:* Explore and prove the midsegment is parallel to the third side and half as long (coordinate proof).

*How to teach:* Place a triangle at A(0, 0), B(2a, 0), C(2b, 2c). Find the midpoints of AC and BC, then show the segment joining them has slope 0 (parallel to AB) and length a, half of AB.

- **Q:** In the coordinate proof, why use 2a, 2b, 2c instead of a, b, c?
  **A:** So the midpoints come out without fractions: midpoints (b, c) and (a + b, c).
- **Q:** A midsegment of a triangle is 7.5 cm. How long is the parallel side?
  **A:** 15 cm.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Concurrency
*Student:* Construct medians, angle bisectors and perpendicular bisectors and locate centroid, incenter and circumcenter.

*How to teach:* Construct medians, angle bisectors and perpendicular bisectors of one triangle each (or in GeoGebra) and locate the centroid, incenter and circumcenter. Balance the cardboard triangle on a pencil at the centroid.

- **Q:** Which point of concurrency is the center of the circle that passes through all three vertices?
  **A:** The circumcenter (intersection of the perpendicular bisectors).
- **Q:** The centroid divides each median in what ratio?
  **A:** 2:1, with the longer part from the vertex to the centroid.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on triangle theorems.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Where is the circumcenter of a right triangle?
  **A:** At the midpoint of the hypotenuse.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Two angles of a triangle are 48° and 77°; the third → 55°. 2) Remote interior angles 40° and 65°; the exterior angle → 105°. 3) An isosceles triangle's vertex angle is 40°; base angles → 70° each. 4) A midsegment is parallel to a side of length 18; its length → 9. 5) Median from vertex A is 12 long; distance from A to the centroid → 8. 6) Which center is equidistant from the three vertices? → circumcenter. From the three sides? → incenter. 7) Short proof: triangle angle sum using a line through one vertex parallel to the opposite side (alternate interior angles + straight angle = 180°).

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Ionic bonding

**Prepare:** Gather table salt, a magnifier, a jar, string or a pencil for crystal growth, and parts for a conductivity tester: a 9-V battery, an LED, two insulated wires (or alligator clips), and tape. Prepare distilled or tap water, sugar and salt. Make a polyatomic ion reference card (nitrate NO₃⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, hydroxide OH⁻, phosphate PO₄³⁻, ammonium NH₄⁺, acetate C₂H₃O₂⁻).

**Watch for:**
- Students write NaCl as a molecule. Ionic compounds are lattices of alternating ions; the formula shows the simplest ratio (a formula unit).
- Students forget parentheses for multiple polyatomic ions, writing CaNO₃₂. Polyatomic ions stay intact: Ca(NO₃)₂.
- Students think solid salt conducts because it is made of ions. Ions must be free to move (dissolved or melted) to conduct.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ion formation
*Student:* Use Lewis dot diagrams to show electron transfer in NaCl, MgO and CaCl₂.

*How to teach:* Model Lewis dot diagrams for Na and Cl, then draw an arrow showing the electron transfer and the resulting ions in brackets with charges. The student repeats for MgO and CaCl₂, checking that total charge is zero.

- **Q:** Show electron transfer in CaCl₂.
  **A:** Ca (2 valence electrons) gives one electron to each of two Cl atoms → Ca²⁺ and 2 Cl⁻; charges total zero.
- **Q:** Why does sodium form Na⁺ rather than Na⁻?
  **A:** Na has one valence electron that is easily lost (low ionization energy), leaving a stable neon-like configuration.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Formulas & names
*Student:* Write formulas and names for 30 ionic compounds including polyatomic ions.

*How to teach:* Teach the criss-cross method and naming rules (metal name + nonmetal ending in -ide; polyatomic names unchanged; Roman numerals for transition metals). The student writes and names 30 compounds; check the first five together.

- **Q:** Write the formula for aluminum sulfate.
  **A:** Al₂(SO₄)₃
- **Q:** Name Cu₂O.
  **A:** Copper(I) oxide.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Salt crystals
*Student:* Grow salt crystals from saturated solution and examine shapes with a magnifier.

*How to teach:* Dissolve salt in hot water until no more dissolves (saturated). Pour into a shallow dish and leave a few days (or hang a string). Examine crystals with a magnifier and sketch their cubic shape. Relate the shape to the lattice.

- **Q:** Why do salt crystals form cubes?
  **A:** Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions stack in a repeating cubic lattice; the visible crystal reflects that arrangement.
- **Q:** What makes a solution 'saturated'?
  **A:** It holds the maximum dissolved solute at that temperature; extra solute will not dissolve.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Conductivity
*Student:* Build an LED conductivity tester and test dry salt, salt water and sugar water.

*How to teach:* Build the tester: battery to LED to one probe; second probe from the battery's other terminal (adult checks LED polarity). Test dry salt, salt water, sugar water and plain water, recording brightness. The student explains each result.

- **Q:** Why does salt water light the LED but sugar water does not?
  **A:** Salt dissociates into mobile Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions that carry charge; sugar dissolves as neutral molecules.
- **Q:** Predict whether melted salt would conduct.
  **A:** Yes — in the liquid state the ions can move.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on ionic compounds.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-item quiz with the polyatomic ion card allowed. Grade together and collect the conductivity data table.

- **Q:** Write the formula for sodium hydroxide and for barium hydroxide.
  **A:** NaOH; Ba(OH)₂.

**Assessment:** Conductivity data table and quiz.

**Answer key:** Conductivity table: dry salt — LED off (ions locked in a lattice); salt water — LED on (mobile ions); sugar water — LED off (molecules, no ions); tap water — dim or off. Quiz key: 1) Formula of magnesium chloride — MgCl₂. 2) Aluminum oxide — Al₂O₃. 3) Calcium nitrate — Ca(NO₃)₂. 4) Ammonium sulfate — (NH₄)₂SO₄. 5) Name Na₂CO₃ — sodium carbonate. 6) Name Fe₂O₃ — iron(III) oxide. 7) Name K₃PO₄ — potassium phosphate. 8) Lewis diagram of MgO: Mg transfers 2 electrons to O, forming Mg²⁺ and O²⁻. 9) Why ionic compounds have high melting points — strong electrostatic attractions among many ions in a lattice. 10) Name CuSO₄ — copper(II) sulfate.

## Modern World History: The Scramble for Africa

**Prepare:** A map of African states and societies before 1880 (e.g., Ethiopia, Asante, Sokoto Caliphate, Zulu kingdom, Buganda); Berlin Conference General Act excerpt; Congo Free State photographs (preview: images of mutilated people) and Casement Report summary; readings on Adwa, the Herero, and the Zulu. Link to Things Fall Apart from English.

**Watch for:**
- Viewing pre-colonial Africa as empty or without states. Many complex kingdoms and trade networks existed.
- Assuming colonial borders followed ethnic lines. Many split groups or joined rivals.

### Day 1 (Monday): Africa before 1880
*Student:* Study a map of African states and societies before colonization.

*How to teach:* Study a map of Africa before 1880. The student lists five states or societies with one fact each. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Name two African states that existed before the Scramble.
  **A:** Ethiopia, the Asante (Ashanti) kingdom, the Sokoto Caliphate, the Zulu kingdom, or Buganda.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Berlin Conference
*Student:* Analyze the Berlin Conference and draw how borders cut across ethnic groups.

*How to teach:* Read about the Berlin Conference (1884–85). The student draws modern borders over ethnic regions and notes where groups are split. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Who attended the Berlin Conference?
  **A:** European powers (and the U.S.) — no African representatives.
- **Q:** What principle did the conference set?
  **A:** 'Effective occupation' — a power had to control territory to claim it.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Congo
*Student:* Examine photographs and the Casement Report on King Leopold's Congo.

*How to teach:* Examine photographs and the Casement Report on the Congo. Discuss how forced labor and quotas led to atrocities. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What abuses occurred in the Congo Free State?
  **A:** Forced labor for rubber quotas, hostage-taking, mutilation (cutting off hands), and millions of deaths.
- **Q:** What happened after the Casement Report (1904)?
  **A:** International outrage forced Leopold to give up the Congo to Belgium in 1908.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Resistance
*Student:* Compare Ethiopia's victory at Adwa with the Herero and Zulu resistance.

*How to teach:* Compare Ethiopia's victory at Adwa (1896) with the Herero (1904–08) and Zulu (1879) resistance. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Why did Ethiopia win at Adwa?
  **A:** Emperor Menelik II modernized his army with modern rifles and artillery and had a large, united force.
- **Q:** What happened to the Herero?
  **A:** German forces killed most of the Herero in the first genocide of the 20th century.

### Day 5 (Friday): Response
*Student:* Write a document-based paragraph on colonizer vs. colonized perspectives.

*How to teach:* The student writes a document-based paragraph on colonizer vs. colonized perspectives. Score with the rubric. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Why is sourcing important for colonial documents?
  **A:** Colonizers' documents often justify rule, while colonized people's voices may be missing or recorded by outsiders.

**Assessment:** Paragraph scored for sourcing.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric (sourcing): 4 = claim comparing colonizer and colonized perspectives; cites at least two documents with author, date and purpose (e.g., Berlin Conference Act — European diplomats, 1885, to regulate colonization; Casement Report — British consul, 1904, exposing abuses); explains how perspective shapes each; 3 = sources one document well; 2 = summary; 1 = no evidence.

## Music & Media Arts: Melody writing

**Prepare:** Install MuseScore or open Noteflight (free). Queue Beethoven's Symphony No. 5, mvt. 1 opening and a pop song with a clear motif. Print staff paper for contour sketches.

**Watch for:**
- Students write melodies with constant leaps. Good melodies mostly move by step with occasional leaps, often followed by a step in the opposite direction.
- Students end melodies anywhere. Strong endings resolve to the tonic (do) over the I chord (authentic cadence).
- Students think a motif must be long. A motif can be 2–4 notes, like Beethoven's short-short-short-long.

### Day 1 (Monday): Motifs
*Student:* Analyze motifs in Beethoven's Fifth and a pop song.

*How to teach:* Analyze motifs in Beethoven's Fifth (short-short-short-long: G-G-G-E♭) and a pop song.

- **Q:** Describe the rhythm of Beethoven's Fifth opening motif.
  **A:** Three short notes followed by a long one (short-short-short-long).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Contour
*Student:* Sketch melodic contours and turn them into notation.

*How to teach:* Sketch melodic contours (arch, wave, ascending) and turn them into notation.

- **Q:** What is melodic contour?
  **A:** The overall shape of a melody — rising, falling, arch, wave.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compose
*Student:* Compose an 8-bar melody over I-IV-V-I.

*How to teach:* Compose an 8-bar melody over I–IV–V–I.

- **Q:** Which notes fit well over a C major chord on a strong beat?
  **A:** Chord tones C, E and G.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revise
*Student:* Revise for cadence and singability.

*How to teach:* Revise for cadence and singability.

- **Q:** What is an authentic cadence?
  **A:** V to I, ending on the tonic; it sounds final.

### Day 5 (Friday): Share
*Student:* Share notation and a recording.

*How to teach:* Share notation and a recording.

- **Q:** What makes a melody memorable?
  **A:** A clear motif that repeats with variation, a singable range and a strong rhythm.

**Assessment:** Melody composition.

**Answer key:** Melody composition rubric: 8 bars over I–IV–V–I (e.g., C–F–G–C, two bars each), uses a motif that repeats or varies, mostly stepwise with purposeful leaps, chord tones on strong beats, singable range (about an octave), ends on the tonic with an authentic cadence (V–I); notation correct; recording included. Proficient = all criteria with minor issues.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Escapes and leverage

**Prepare:** Mat, a cooperative partner (parent), and slow, controlled practice. Agree on a tap-out/stop word.

**Watch for:**
- Students pull straight back against a wrist grab. Rotate the wrist toward the attacker's thumb, the weakest point of the grip.
- Students rely on strength. Leverage and body position (base) let a smaller person escape a larger one.
- Students rush escapes. Practice slowly for correct mechanics; speed comes later.

### Day 1 (Monday): Leverage
*Student:* Learn how levers and base explain escapes.

*How to teach:* Explain levers (force, fulcrum, load) and base (wide, low stance). Demonstrate how lowering center of gravity makes you harder to lift.

- **Q:** How does lowering your center of gravity help in an escape?
  **A:** It widens your base and makes you more stable and harder to lift or push.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Wrist releases
*Student:* Practice wrist-grab releases toward the thumb.

*How to teach:* Practice wrist-grab releases toward the thumb, both sides, slowly.

- **Q:** Why release toward the thumb?
  **A:** The gap between thumb and fingers is the weakest part of the grip.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Bear hug escapes
*Student:* Practice front and rear bear-hug escapes slowly with a partner.

*How to teach:* Practice front and rear bear-hug escapes slowly with a partner.

- **Q:** What's the first move in a rear bear-hug escape?
  **A:** Drop your weight (lower base) to make it harder to be lifted.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Ground escapes
*Student:* Practice the shrimp and bridge-and-roll from the ground.

*How to teach:* Practice the shrimp (hip escape) and bridge-and-roll from the ground.

- **Q:** What's the goal of the shrimp movement?
  **A:** To create space between you and the person on top so you can recover your guard or get up.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Demonstrate four escapes.

*How to teach:* The student demonstrates four escapes; score with the checklist.

- **Q:** After escaping, what should you do?
  **A:** Run to safety and call for help.

**Assessment:** Skill checklist.

**Answer key:** Skill checklist (four escapes): wrist release toward the thumb (same-side and cross grab); front bear-hug escape (drop weight, create space, strike/knee pad, escape); rear bear-hug escape (drop base, step to the side, elbow/back step, turn out); ground escape — bridge-and-roll (trap arm and foot, bridge hips up and roll) or shrimp (hip escape) to create space and stand up. Proficient = correct mechanics on both sides and moving to escape afterward.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Parking

**Prepare:** Gather a toy car, a book ramp and a 'curb' (another book). Read the handbook parking section. Find a parallel-parking demonstration video.

**Watch for:**
- Students forget the curb direction for hill parking. Uphill with a curb: turn wheels away from the curb (left). Downhill: turn wheels toward the curb (right). No curb (up or down): turn wheels toward the shoulder (right) so the car would roll away from traffic.
- Students think it's fine to briefly park in a disabled space. It is illegal without a placard/plate, with large fines; also never park in the crosshatched access aisle.
- Students park too close to hydrants. The minimum is 15 feet.

### Day 1 (Monday): Parking rules
*Student:* List places you may never park (within 15 feet of a fire hydrant, in crosswalks, bike lanes, etc.).

*How to teach:* The student lists places where parking is prohibited from the handbook and explains the reason for three.

- **Q:** How close may you park to a fire hydrant?
  **A:** No closer than 15 feet.
- **Q:** Why not park in a bike lane?
  **A:** It forces cyclists into traffic and is illegal.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Hill parking
*Student:* Model hill parking with a toy car on a book ramp: uphill/downhill, with and without curb.

*How to teach:* Use the book ramp and toy car to model the four hill situations. The student turns the 'wheels' correctly and explains each.

- **Q:** Parking uphill next to a curb: which way do you turn your wheels?
  **A:** Away from the curb (to the left), then let the car roll back so the rear of the front tire rests against the curb.
- **Q:** Parking downhill with a curb?
  **A:** Toward the curb (to the right).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Parallel parking
*Student:* Watch a demonstration and write the step-by-step parallel parking procedure.

*How to teach:* Watch a parallel-parking demonstration and the student writes the step-by-step procedure.

- **Q:** How close to the curb must your car be when parallel parked?
  **A:** Within 18 inches.
- **Q:** What do you do before backing into the space?
  **A:** Signal, check mirrors and over your shoulder for traffic, cyclists and pedestrians.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Accessible parking
*Student:* Learn disabled-parking rules and penalties.

*How to teach:* The student learns disabled-parking rules and penalties.

- **Q:** Can you park in the blue-striped crosshatched area next to a disabled space?
  **A:** No — it is an access aisle for wheelchairs and lifts; parking there is illegal.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Parking quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the parking quiz. Review.

- **Q:** You park on a hill with no curb. Which way do you turn the wheels?
  **A:** Toward the side of the road (right), so the car rolls away from traffic if the brakes fail.

**Assessment:** Parking quiz.

**Answer key:** Parking quiz key: 1) Distance from a fire hydrant — 15 feet. 2) Never park — in an intersection, crosswalk, on a sidewalk, in a bike lane, within 3 feet of a sidewalk ramp, in a tunnel or on a bridge (unless permitted), in front of a driveway, on a freeway (except emergencies), double-parked, in a disabled space or crosshatched area without a placard, within 15 feet of a fire station driveway, next to a red curb. 3) Uphill with curb — wheels away from curb (turned left), roll back gently to touch the curb. 4) Downhill with curb — wheels toward curb (right). 5) No curb — wheels toward the shoulder (right). 6) Always set the parking brake and leave in Park. 7) Parallel parking — signal, stop alongside the front car about 2 feet away with rear bumpers aligned, back slowly turning the wheel sharply right until at ~45°, straighten then turn left to swing in, end within 18 inches of the curb. 8) Disabled parking fine — at least $250 and can be much higher (check local amounts). 9) Exit a parallel space — signal, check mirrors and over the shoulder, merge when safe.

## Computer Science Principles: User-centered design

**Prepare:** Gather paper, markers and sticky notes for prototype screens. Line up two family members willing to be interviewed (Day 2) and one to test the prototype (Day 4). Skim the Code.org Unit 3 design lessons.

**Watch for:**
- Designing for yourself instead of the user. Require every design decision to tie back to something the persona said or needs.
- Treating the first prototype as final. Paper prototypes are meant to be cheap so they can be changed after testing.
- Explaining the app to testers while they test. Stay quiet and watch; confusion you have to explain away is a design problem to fix.

### Day 1 (Monday): Design process
*Student:* Learn the design process (empathize, define, prototype, test) and analyze three apps you use for good and bad design.

*How to teach:* Introduce the design process: empathize (understand users), define (state the problem), prototype (build a rough version), test (watch users try it), repeat. Have the student evaluate three apps they use: one good design feature and one frustrating feature each, naming which user need is or isn't met.

- **Q:** What are the four stages of the design process used this week?
  **A:** Empathize, define, prototype, test (then iterate).
- **Q:** Why is 'iterate' important in design?
  **A:** Testing reveals problems you didn't anticipate, so you revise and test again to improve the design step by step.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): User personas
*Student:* Interview two family members about a daily problem and write a user persona for each.

*How to teach:* Model good interview questions (open-ended: 'Tell me about the last time…', 'What's frustrating about…?'). The student interviews two family members about a daily problem and writes a persona for each: name, age/role, goals, frustrations, tech comfort.

- **Q:** Why is 'Would you use an app that does X?' a weak interview question?
  **A:** It's a yes/no leading question that suggests the answer; open questions about real past experiences reveal true needs.
- **Q:** What is a user persona?
  **A:** A fictional but research-based profile of a typical user, summarizing their goals, needs and frustrations, used to guide design decisions.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Paper prototype
*Student:* Sketch a paper prototype for an app that solves one persona's problem with at least three screens.

*How to teach:* The student sketches at least three screens on paper (one per sheet or sticky note) for an app that solves one persona's problem, labeling every button and drawing arrows showing which screen each button leads to.

- **Q:** Why prototype on paper before coding?
  **A:** Paper is fast and cheap to change, so you can test ideas with users before spending hours on code.
- **Q:** What should each screen show?
  **A:** Its purpose, all user interface elements (buttons, labels, inputs) with labels, and where each button goes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): User testing
*Student:* Test the paper prototype with a user, record feedback, and revise the design.

*How to teach:* The user tries a task (e.g. 'add a reminder') while the student acts as the 'computer', swapping papers when buttons are 'tapped'. The student stays silent, records where the user hesitates or gets confused, then revises.

- **Q:** During testing the user taps the wrong button twice. What should you do?
  **A:** Don't explain — note it as a usability problem and redesign (e.g. clearer label or placement) after the test.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Submit the prototype and design log documenting feedback and changes.

*How to teach:* Review the full design log against the rubric in the assessment key. Have the student point to at least two changes and the feedback that caused them.

- **Q:** Describe one change you made and the feedback that led to it.
  **A:** Answers vary; a proficient answer names a specific observation (e.g. 'Grandma couldn't find the add button') and the specific change ('moved Add to the top and made it larger').

**Assessment:** Design log scored with rubric.

**Answer key:** Design log rubric (4 levels): 4 = log includes the problem statement, two personas based on real interviews (name, goals, frustrations, needs), at least three prototype screens with navigation arrows, user-test notes in the user's words, and at least two specific changes tied to feedback; 3 = all parts present but feedback-to-change link is thin; 2 = personas or testing missing; 1 = only sketches.
