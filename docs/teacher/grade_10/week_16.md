# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 16

## English 10: Things Fall Apart Parts 2–3: Colonial arrival

**Prepare:** Things Fall Apart, Yeats's 'The Second Coming' (Poetry Foundation).

**Watch for:**
- Seeing Mr. Brown and Reverend Smith as the same. Brown is accommodating; Smith is rigid and provokes conflict.
- Thinking the ending is neutral. The Commissioner's single 'reasonable paragraph' shows colonizers erasing African history.

### Day 1 (Monday): Yeats allusion
*Student:* Read 'The Second Coming' and explain how Achebe transforms Yeats's line into his title.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Second Coming.' The student explains how Achebe uses its line as his title. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What line from Yeats gives Achebe his title?
  **A:** 'Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold.'
- **Q:** How does the title fit the novel?
  **A:** Igbo society and Okonkwo's life break apart under colonial and missionary pressure.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Part 2
*Student:* Read chapters 14–19 and track Nwoye's conversion and why Christianity appeals to outcasts.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 14–19. The student tracks Nwoye's conversion. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What advice does Uchendu give Okonkwo in exile?
  **A:** That 'Mother is supreme' — a man finds refuge in his motherland in sorrow, so Okonkwo should not despair.
- **Q:** Why does Christianity attract outcasts?
  **A:** It welcomes osu, mothers of twins and others rejected by traditional society.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Part 3
*Student:* Read chapters 20–25 and annotate the actions of Mr. Brown versus Reverend Smith.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 20–25. The student annotates Mr. Brown vs. Reverend Smith. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How does Mr. Brown win converts?
  **A:** By respecting the clan, discussing religion with Akunna, and building a school and hospital.

### Day 4 (Thursday): The ending
*Student:* Analyze the District Commissioner's final paragraph: what is Achebe saying about who writes history?

*How to teach:* Read the last paragraph aloud slowly. The student underlines word choices that reveal the Commissioner's attitude ('primitive,' 'pacification,' 'a reasonable paragraph') and writes a paragraph on what Achebe is saying about who writes history.

- **Q:** What is the title of the Commissioner's book, and why is it ironic?
  **A:** 'The Pacification of the Primitive Tribes of the Lower Niger' — Achebe's whole novel shows a complex society, and 'pacification' disguises conquest.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Quiz on Parts 2–3 and a cause-effect chart of colonial change.

*How to teach:* Give the Parts 2–3 quiz from the assessment key. Then the student completes a cause-effect chart of colonial change (missionaries → converts → divided clan → government courts → Okonkwo's final act).

- **Q:** Why don't the clansmen fight after Okonkwo kills the messenger?
  **A:** They let the other messengers escape and ask 'Why did he do it?' — the clan is too divided; Okonkwo realizes they will not go to war.

**Assessment:** Quiz and final-paragraph analysis.

**Answer key:** Quiz (answers): 1) Where does Okonkwo spend his exile? → Mbanta, his mother's village. 2) Why does Nwoye join the Christians? → The hymns and the new religion answer his questions about twins thrown away and Ikemefuna's killing. 3) Who are the osu? → Outcasts who are drawn to the church. 4) What happens to Abame? → It is wiped out after villagers kill a white man. 5) How does Mr. Brown differ from Reverend Smith? → Brown is respectful, learns about Igbo religion, builds a school and hospital; Smith is strict and confrontational. 6) What does Enoch do? → Unmasks an egwugwu. 7) How does Okonkwo die? → He kills a court messenger, then hangs himself. Final-paragraph analysis rubric: 4 = explains that the Commissioner reduces Okonkwo's life to 'a reasonable paragraph' in 'The Pacification of the Primitive Tribes of the Lower Niger' and links it to Achebe's purpose of writing Africa's own history; 3 = correct idea, little evidence; 2 = summary; 1 = off-topic.

## Geometry: Similar triangles

**Prepare:** Small mirror, tape measure, a sunny day for shadows (Day 3), notebook for lab report. Pick a tree or flagpole outdoors.

**Watch for:**
- Mismatching corresponding sides in proportions. Write the similarity statement first, then set up ratios in the same order.
- Thinking SSA works for similarity. Valid criteria: AA, SSS~, SAS~ (with the included angle).

### Day 1 (Monday): AA criterion
*Student:* Use dilations to explain why two pairs of congruent angles give similar triangles.

*How to teach:* Explain: dilate one triangle so a side matches; with two equal angle pairs, the dilated copy is congruent to the other by ASA, so the triangles are similar. The student finds missing sides in AA-similar triangles.

- **Q:** Two triangles each have a 35° angle and a 90° angle. Are they similar?
  **A:** Yes, by AA.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): SSS~ and SAS~
*Student:* Decide which triangles are similar and write similarity statements.

*How to teach:* Model checking SSS~ (all side ratios equal) and SAS~ (two side ratios equal and included angles congruent). The student writes similarity statements in correct order.

- **Q:** Are triangles with sides 5, 7, 9 and 10, 14, 17 similar?
  **A:** No — 10/5 = 2 and 14/7 = 2, but 17/9 ≠ 2.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Indirect measurement lab
*Student:* Measure a tree's height with the mirror method and with shadows; compare results.

*How to teach:* Mirror method: place a mirror on the ground, step back until you see the treetop, measure eye height and both distances. Shadow method: measure your shadow and the tree's shadow at the same time. Set up proportions for each.

- **Q:** Eye height 1.6 m, you stand 2 m from the mirror, the mirror is 10 m from the tree. Tree height?
  **A:** h/10 = 1.6/2 → h = 8 m.
- **Q:** A 1.8 m person casts a 1.2 m shadow; a tree casts a 6 m shadow. Tree height?
  **A:** h/6 = 1.8/1.2 → h = 9 m.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Similarity proofs
*Student:* Write two similarity proofs.

*How to teach:* Model one similarity proof (e.g., parallel line creating AA through corresponding angles). The student writes a second proof independently.

- **Q:** In △ABC, DE ∥ BC with D on AB and E on AC. Why is △ADE ~ △ABC?
  **A:** ∠A is shared and ∠ADE ≅ ∠ABC (corresponding angles), so AA similarity.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on similarity criteria.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and collect the lab report; score both with the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** If two triangles are similar with scale factor 3, how do their perimeters compare?
  **A:** The larger perimeter is 3 times the smaller.

**Assessment:** Lab report and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) △ABC has angles 50° and 60°; △DEF has 60° and 70°. Similar? → Yes, AA (third angles 70° and 50°). 2) Sides 6, 8, 10 and 9, 12, 15 → similar SSS~, k = 1.5. 3) △ABC ~ △DEF, AB = 8, DE = 12, BC = 10; EF → 15. 4) Sides 4 and 6 with included 40°, and 6 and 9 with included 40° → similar by SAS~. Lab report rubric: 4 = diagram, measurements with units, proportions correctly set up, both methods computed, comparison and error sources discussed; 3 = minor calculation slips; 2 = only one method or missing diagram; 1 = incomplete.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Specific heat and the second law

**Prepare:** Gather foam cups with lids (nest two for insulation), a thermometer, metal washers (about 50 g), a pot of boiling water, tongs, a kitchen scale, dry sand, two identical containers and a lamp. Prepare a spreadsheet. Formula card: q = mcΔT; c(water) = 4.184 J/g·°C; heat lost by hot = heat gained by cold.

**Watch for:**
- Students average temperatures without weighting by mass (100 g at 80 °C + 200 g at 20 °C → 50 °C). The final temperature is mass-weighted: 40 °C.
- Students think materials with high specific heat get hot quickly. High specific heat means more energy is needed per degree, so water warms and cools slowly.
- Students forget that ΔT is final minus initial and get sign errors. Use magnitudes and the idea 'heat lost = heat gained'.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mixing water
*Student:* Mix known masses of hot and cold water in a foam cup and predict the final temperature.

*How to teach:* The student predicts the final temperature of 100 g of 70 °C water mixed with 100 g of 20 °C water, then tests it in a lidded foam cup. Repeat with unequal masses. Compare predictions and measurements.

- **Q:** Predict the final temperature when 100 g of water at 70 °C mixes with 100 g at 20 °C.
  **A:** 45 °C (equal masses, so the simple average).
- **Q:** Predict the final temperature for 100 g at 80 °C mixed with 200 g at 20 °C.
  **A:** (100 × 80 + 200 × 20) ÷ 300 = 40 °C.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Specific heat of metal
*Student:* Heat washers in boiling water, transfer to cool water and calculate the metal's specific heat.

*How to teach:* Weigh the washers, heat them in boiling water for 5 minutes (they reach ~100 °C), then transfer quickly with tongs into a cup with a known mass of room-temperature water. Record the highest temperature. Model the calculation: heat gained by water = heat lost by metal.

- **Q:** 50.0 g of metal at 100.0 °C goes into 100.0 g water at 20.0 °C; final 24.0 °C. Find the metal's specific heat.
  **A:** Water gains 100.0 × 4.184 × 4.0 = 1674 J. Metal: 1674 = 50.0 × c × 76.0 → c = 0.440 J/g·°C.
- **Q:** Why must the transfer be quick?
  **A:** The hot metal loses heat to the air while it travels, which would make the calculated specific heat too low.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Computational model
*Student:* Build a spreadsheet model that predicts final temperature for any two masses and temperatures.

*How to teach:* Build a spreadsheet with inputs m₁, T₁, m₂, T₂ (and c₁, c₂) and a formula for Tf. The student tests it against Day 1 data and then explores 'what if' cases.

- **Q:** What does the model predict for 150 g at 50 °C mixed with 150 g at 10 °C?
  **A:** 30 °C.
- **Q:** 50 g of iron (c = 0.45) at 100 °C is dropped into 50 g of water at 20 °C. Will the final temperature be near 60 °C, above it, or below it? Why?
  **A:** Well below 60 °C (about 27.7 °C): water's specific heat is about nine times larger, so the water's temperature changes much less.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Water vs. land
*Student:* Heat equal masses of sand and water under a lamp and compare temperature change; connect to coastal CA climate.

*How to teach:* Put equal masses of dry sand and water in identical containers under the same lamp for 15 minutes, recording temperatures every 3 minutes, then turn off the lamp and record cooling. Connect to coastal vs. inland California.

- **Q:** Which heated faster, sand or water, and why?
  **A:** Sand, because its specific heat (~0.8 J/g·°C) is much lower than water's (4.18), so less energy raises its temperature.
- **Q:** Why are summers milder in San Francisco than in Sacramento?
  **A:** The Pacific Ocean's high specific heat keeps coastal air cooler in summer (and milder in winter); inland land heats up much more.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Lab report on energy conservation.

*How to teach:* The student writes the lab report on energy conservation using this week's data. Review with the rubric.

- **Q:** State the second law of thermodynamics in your own words for this lab.
  **A:** Thermal energy spontaneously flows from hotter to colder objects until they reach the same temperature; the energy becomes more evenly spread out.

**Assessment:** Spreadsheet model and lab report.

**Answer key:** Spreadsheet model: full credit if Tf = (m₁c₁T₁ + m₂c₂T₂) ÷ (m₁c₁ + m₂c₂) is entered with cell references and gives 40.0 °C for 100 g at 80 °C + 200 g at 20 °C water, and 30.0 °C for 150 g at 50 °C + 150 g at 10 °C. Lab report rubric: 4 = predictions vs. measured values, percent difference, correct metal specific heat calculation (e.g., 50.0 g washers at 100.0 °C into 100.0 g water at 20.0 °C, final 24.0 °C: water gains 100 × 4.184 × 4.0 = 1674 J; c = 1674 ÷ (50.0 × 76.0) = 0.440 J/g·°C, close to iron/steel at 0.45), explanation of heat lost to cup and air, and a statement of energy conservation and the second law (heat flows hot → cold until equilibrium).

## Modern World History: Treaty of Versailles and the postwar world

**Prepare:** Wilson's Fourteen Points (January 8, 1918; archives.gov), Treaty of Versailles Article 231 and key terms, Keynes's Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919) excerpt, maps of Europe and the Middle East in 1914 and 1923, Dada images and Remarque's All Quiet on the Western Front quotes. Prepare the Unit 5 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Fourteen Points became the treaty. Most were dropped; the treaty punished Germany.
- Believing the U.S. joined the League of Nations. The Senate rejected the treaty and the League.

### Day 1 (Monday): Fourteen Points
*Student:* Analyze Wilson's Fourteen Points and rank them.

*How to teach:* Read the Fourteen Points together. The student summarizes each in a few words and ranks the five most important with reasons. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What was Wilson's fourteenth point?
  **A:** A general association of nations — the League of Nations — to guarantee independence and territorial integrity.
- **Q:** What does 'self-determination' mean?
  **A:** The right of national groups to choose their own government.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Versailles
*Student:* Role-play Wilson, Clemenceau, Lloyd George and Orlando negotiating terms.

*How to teach:* Assign roles: Wilson (just peace, League), Clemenceau (punish Germany, security for France), Lloyd George (punish but keep Germany as trading partner), Orlando (territory for Italy). Negotiate terms, then compare to the actual treaty (signed June 28, 1919).

- **Q:** What did Clemenceau want?
  **A:** Harsh terms — reparations, a weakened German military, and security for France.
- **Q:** What does Article 231 say?
  **A:** Germany accepts responsibility for causing all the loss and damage of the war (the 'war guilt clause').

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Maps
*Student:* Compare 1914 and 1923 maps of Europe and the Middle East; note mandates.

*How to teach:* Place 1914 and 1923 maps side by side. The student lists empires that disappeared, new nations in Eastern Europe, and League mandates in the Middle East, then predicts one problem new borders might cause.

- **Q:** Which empires collapsed after WWI?
  **A:** The German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian and Ottoman Empires.
- **Q:** What were mandates?
  **A:** Former Ottoman and German territories administered by Britain or France under the League of Nations, supposedly until ready for independence.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Disillusionment
*Student:* Analyze Dada art and Remarque quotes about the 'Lost Generation.'

*How to teach:* Show two Dada artworks (e.g., Duchamp's 'Fountain' or Hannah Höch's collage) and read Remarque quotes from All Quiet on the Western Front. The student explains in a paragraph why postwar artists and writers rejected old ideals.

- **Q:** What did Remarque mean by a generation 'destroyed by the war'?
  **A:** Even survivors were physically and psychologically damaged and lost faith in old ideals.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 test
*Student:* Unit 5 test with document analysis.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test from the assessment key, including the Keynes document analysis (about 50 minutes). Grade together and have the student correct missed items with notes. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What did Keynes predict about the treaty?
  **A:** Harsh reparations would ruin Germany's economy, hurt all of Europe, and could lead to future conflict.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test (sample answers): 1) MAIN causes. 2) Marne → led to trench stalemate. 3) Zimmermann Telegram → German offer to Mexico. 4) Armenian Genocide → 1915, Ottoman government, about 1–1.5 million killed. 5) Fourteen Points → open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, free trade, arms reduction, self-determination, League of Nations. 6) Article 231 → Germany accepts responsibility for causing all loss and damage (war guilt). 7) Treaty terms → reparations, army limited to 100,000, no air force, Rhineland demilitarized, Alsace-Lorraine to France, colonies lost. 8) New nations → Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Austria, Hungary, Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania. 9) Mandates → Syria and Lebanon (France); Iraq, Palestine, Transjordan (Britain). Document analysis: source and interpret Keynes (British economist warning harsh reparations would ruin Germany and Europe).

## Music & Media Arts: World music and music criticism

**Prepare:** Smithsonian Folkways recordings of Balinese gamelan (gamelan gong kebyar), a Hindustani raga performance (e.g., Ravi Shankar), mariachi (e.g., Mariachi Vargas de Tecalitlán) and son jarocho ('La Bamba' traditional versions); a professional music review (newspaper or Pitchfork/NPR).

## PE 10 (Course 2): Muscular strength and endurance training

**Prepare:** Gather resistance bands, dumbbells or water jugs (1 gal ≈ 8.3 lb), a sturdy chair, and a training log. Review correct form videos for squat, hip hinge (deadlift), push, pull (row) and carry.

**Watch for:**
- Students round the back in hinges. Keep a neutral spine: push hips back, chest proud, weight in heels.
- Students train only 'mirror muscles' (chest, biceps). Balance antagonist pairs (push/pull, quads/hamstrings) to prevent imbalance and injury.
- Students increase weight before form is solid. Progress when all reps of all sets are done with good form (e.g., 2 extra reps on the last set).

### Day 1 (Monday): Form
*Student:* Learn squat, hinge, push, pull and carry patterns.

*How to teach:* Teach the five patterns with bodyweight or light load; check form with the student watching a video of themselves.

- **Q:** What are the cues for a safe squat?
  **A:** Feet shoulder-width, chest up, hips back and down, knees tracking over toes, heels down, neutral spine.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Upper body
*Student:* Do an upper-body band workout: 3 sets of 10–12 reps.

*How to teach:* Upper-body band workout: rows, presses, face pulls, curls, triceps extensions; 3 × 10–12.

- **Q:** What are the antagonist muscles to the biceps?
  **A:** The triceps.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Lower body
*Student:* Do a lower-body workout with goblet squats and lunges.

*How to teach:* Lower-body workout: goblet squats, reverse lunges, Romanian deadlifts with jugs, glute bridges, calf raises.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Core
*Student:* Do a core circuit.

*How to teach:* Core circuit: dead bugs, side planks, bird dogs, Pallof press with band.

- **Q:** What is the main job of the core in lifting?
  **A:** Stabilizing the spine and transferring force between upper and lower body.

### Day 5 (Friday): Log
*Student:* Log weights and reps and plan progression.

*How to teach:* The student logs weights and reps and writes next week's progression.

- **Q:** What is progressive overload?
  **A:** Gradually increasing the demand (reps, load or sets) so the body keeps adapting.

**Assessment:** Training log.

**Answer key:** Training log: full credit for exercises, sets, reps, load and RPE recorded for each session, and a progression plan (e.g., add 1–2 reps per set or ~5% load when 3 × 12 is completed with good form). Key concepts: strength (heavier, 3–6 reps), hypertrophy/endurance (8–15 reps), progressive overload, rest 48 hours for the same muscle group, antagonist pairs (biceps/triceps, quads/hamstrings, chest/back).

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Distracted and drowsy driving

**Prepare:** Read the handbook section on distracted and drowsy driving. Find an online reaction-time test (e.g., humanbenchmark.com). Build the Unit 4 test.

**Watch for:**
- Students think hands-free calling is safe. Cognitive distraction remains; and drivers under 18 may not use any wireless device, even hands-free, except in emergencies.
- Students think they can multitask. The brain switches attention rather than doing both, increasing reaction time.
- Students think opening a window or loud music cures drowsiness. The only remedies are sleep or pulling over safely to rest (a short nap).

### Day 1 (Monday): Types
*Student:* Learn visual, manual and cognitive distraction.

*How to teach:* The student learns visual, manual and cognitive distractions and lists two examples of each.

- **Q:** Why is texting the most dangerous distraction?
  **A:** It involves all three types: eyes, hands and mind are off driving.
- **Q:** Give an example of cognitive distraction.
  **A:** An emotional phone call or daydreaming.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Phone laws
*Student:* Learn California phone laws (minors may not use any device, even hands-free).

*How to teach:* The student reads California phone laws and summarizes them.

- **Q:** Can a 17-year-old use a hands-free phone while driving?
  **A:** No — drivers under 18 may not use any wireless device, even hands-free, except for emergency calls.
- **Q:** How far does a car travel at 55 mph in 5 seconds?
  **A:** 55 × 1.467 × 5 ≈ 400 feet.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Reaction test
*Student:* Test reaction time with and without texting on an online reaction test.

*How to teach:* The student takes an online reaction-time test 5 times normally, then 5 times while texting a family member; compare averages.

- **Q:** What did texting do to your reaction time, and why?
  **A:** It increased it (typically by tenths of a second or more), because attention was split.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Drowsy driving
*Student:* Learn signs of drowsiness and prevention.

*How to teach:* The student learns signs of drowsy driving and prevention.

- **Q:** Name three warning signs of drowsy driving.
  **A:** Frequent yawning, heavy eyelids, drifting from the lane, missing exits or signs, not remembering the last few miles.
- **Q:** What's the best prevention?
  **A:** Get enough sleep (teens need 8–10 hours), avoid driving late at night, and pull over to rest when drowsy.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 test
*Student:* Unit 4 test.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 4 test. Grade together.

- **Q:** What should you do if your phone rings while driving?
  **A:** Ignore it; pull over safely first if you must respond.

**Assessment:** Unit 4 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 4 test key (sample): 1) Three distraction types — visual (eyes off road), manual (hands off wheel), cognitive (mind off driving). 2) Texting combines all three. 3) Drivers under 18 — no phone use, even hands-free (emergency calls allowed). 4) Adults — hands-free only; may touch a mounted phone once with one finger to activate a function. 5) At 55 mph, 5 seconds looking at a phone covers about the length of a football field (≈ 400 ft). 6) Signs of drowsiness — yawning, drifting, missing exits, heavy eyelids. 7) Remedy — pull over and rest or switch drivers. 8) Adult BAC — 0.08%. 9) Under-21 BAC — 0.01%. 10) Implied consent — must submit to testing. Score 90%+ mastery.

## Computer Science Principles: Loops

**Prepare:** Have the AP reference sheet section for REPEAT n TIMES and REPEAT UNTIL(condition). Prepare three programs with infinite loops for Day 4 (e.g. missing i++; condition that never becomes false; updating the wrong variable).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking REPEAT UNTIL works like while. REPEAT UNTIL(condition) runs until the condition becomes true; a JavaScript while loop runs while its condition is true.
- Off-by-one errors: for (var i = 0; i <= 10; i++) runs 11 times, not 10.
- Forgetting to update the loop variable, causing an infinite loop.

### Day 1 (Monday): While loops
*Student:* Write a while loop that simulates rolling a die until a 6 appears, counting rolls, and run it 20 times to find the average.

*How to teach:* Write: var rolls = 0; var die = 0; while (die != 6) { die = randomNumber(1,6); rolls++; } Run 20 times, record rolls, compute the average (expected about 6).

- **Q:** Why is a while loop the right choice for rolling until a 6?
  **A:** We don't know in advance how many rolls it will take; the loop must continue until a condition is met.
- **Q:** What is the theoretical average number of rolls to get a 6?
  **A:** 6 (probability 1/6 per roll, so expected rolls = 1 ÷ (1/6) = 6).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): For loops
*Student:* Write for loops that count, sum and repeat drawing commands.

*How to teach:* Model for (var i = 0; i < 5; i++) and its three parts (start, condition, update). Write loops that count 1–10, sum 1–100 and repeat drawing commands.

- **Q:** What is the sum of 1 to 100 using a for loop?
  **A:** 5,050.
- **Q:** How many times does for (var i = 2; i < 10; i += 2) run, and what values does i take?
  **A:** 4 times: i = 2, 4, 6, 8.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Choosing loops
*Student:* Compare while and for for three tasks and justify the choice for readability.

*How to teach:* For three tasks — print 10 stars, ask until correct password, process every item in a list — have the student choose while or for and justify for readability.

- **Q:** Which loop for 'print 10 stars'? Why?
  **A:** for — the number of repetitions is known, and the counter is built into the header.
- **Q:** Which loop for 'ask until the password is correct'? Why?
  **A:** while — the number of repetitions depends on user input and is not known in advance.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Infinite loops
*Student:* Find and fix the infinite loops in three provided programs and explain the missing update or wrong condition in each.

*How to teach:* The student finds and fixes the infinite loops and writes the cause of each (missing update, condition never false, wrong variable updated).

- **Q:** Why is this infinite: var i = 0; while (i < 5) { console.log(i); }
  **A:** i never changes, so i < 5 is always true; add i++ inside the loop.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Complete AP-style questions on REPEAT n TIMES and REPEAT UNTIL.

*How to teach:* Give the loop quiz in the assessment key in AP pseudocode.

- **Q:** x ← 1; REPEAT 3 TIMES { x ← x * 4 }. Final x?
  **A:** 64.

**Assessment:** Loop quiz.

**Answer key:** Loop quiz: 1) x ← 0; REPEAT 4 TIMES { x ← x + 3 } → 12. 2) n ← 1; REPEAT UNTIL(n > 20) { n ← n * 2 } → 32. 3) count ← 0; REPEAT UNTIL(count = 5) { count ← count + 2 } → infinite loop (count goes 0, 2, 4, 6… never equals 5). 4) sum ← 0; i ← 1; REPEAT 5 TIMES { sum ← sum + i; i ← i + 1 } → 15. 5) for (var i = 0; i < 10; i++) runs 10 times. 6) Choose a loop to keep asking for a password until correct → while/REPEAT UNTIL (unknown number of repetitions). Score 5–6 mastery.
