# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 4

## English 10: Narrative writing workshop

**Prepare:** Writing notebook; have the Unit 1 stories handy as mentor texts. Prepare the Unit 1 test (assessment key). Show the student MLA format on Purdue OWL.

**Watch for:**
- Starting the story with a long description or waking up. In medias res means starting in the middle of action.
- Punctuating dialogue incorrectly. Show: "I'm here," she said. — comma inside the quotation marks, new paragraph for each new speaker.

### Day 1 (Monday): Plan the story
*Student:* Brainstorm a 1,000–1,500 word suspense story and plan it on a plot mountain, choosing a narrator and one structural trick (flashback, twist, in medias res).

*How to teach:* Brainstorm suspense situations together. The student fills a plot mountain, chooses first or third person, and picks one structural trick. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Which mentor story used in medias res or flashback, and how?
  **A:** 'An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge' opens in the middle of the hanging, then flashes back in Part II to explain how Farquhar got there.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Draft opening
*Student:* Draft the opening scene starting in medias res, using at least three sensory details and one foreshadowing clue.

*How to teach:* Read Connell's opening for its sensory details. The student drafts the opening scene in medias res with three sensory details and one foreshadowing clue; underline each. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What makes an opening 'in medias res'?
  **A:** It drops the reader into action already happening, with background filled in later.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft middle & end
*Student:* Complete the draft, writing a climax scene where sentences shorten to speed pacing.

*How to teach:* Show how Connell's sentences shorten during the chase. The student finishes the draft and writes a climax with short, punchy sentences. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Why do short sentences speed up pacing?
  **A:** They are read quickly, mimicking rapid action and rising heartbeat.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revise & edit
*Student:* Revise using a checklist (dialogue punctuation, varied sentence openers, show-don't-tell), then peer/parent-read aloud for confusing spots.

*How to teach:* Read the checklist together: dialogue punctuation, varied sentence openers, show-don't-tell. The student revises with a colored pen, then reads the whole story aloud to you while you note any spot where you got confused or lost interest; they fix those spots before typing.

- **Q:** Correct the punctuation: "Run she shouted".
  **A:** "Run!" she shouted.

### Day 5 (Friday): Publish & unit test
*Student:* Type the final story in MLA format and take the Unit 1 test on literary terms and a cold-read short passage.

*How to teach:* The student formats the final story in MLA style (header, title, double spacing, 12-pt font) and submits it. Then give the Unit 1 test (about 40 minutes): terms section plus a short cold-read passage with written answers that must quote the text.

- **Q:** What is the difference between dramatic and situational irony?
  **A:** Dramatic irony: the audience knows something a character doesn't. Situational irony: what happens is the opposite of what's expected.

**Assessment:** Narrative rubric (W.9-10.3) and Unit 1 test.

**Answer key:** Narrative rubric (W.9-10.3): 4 = engaging in medias res opening, clear point of view, effective structural device (flashback/twist), sensory detail and pacing that build suspense, correctly punctuated dialogue, satisfying resolution; 3 = most elements with minor lapses; 2 = flat pacing or confusing sequence; 1 = incomplete. Unit 1 test (sample answers): foreshadowing — hints of future events; verbal irony — saying the opposite of what is meant; dramatic irony — audience knows more than a character; situational irony — outcome opposite of what's expected; unreliable narrator — narrator whose account can't be fully trusted (e.g., Montresor); in medias res — beginning in the middle of action; flashback — interrupting to show an earlier event (Owl Creek Part II). Cold read: score short answers 2 points each for a correct claim plus quoted evidence.

## Geometry: Translations and reflections

**Prepare:** Graph paper, patty paper, colored pencils, GeoGebra. Read through the reflection rules so you can check quickly: over x-axis (x, −y); y-axis (−x, y); y = x (y, x); y = −x (−y, −x).

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up reflections over the x-axis and y-axis. Reflecting over the x-axis keeps x the same and negates y — the figure flips up/down across the x-axis.
- Thinking a stretch is a rigid motion because the shape 'looks the same.' Measure: rigid motions preserve every length and angle.

### Day 1 (Monday): Transformations as functions
*Student:* Compare transformations that preserve distance and angle with those that don't (stretch) on a grid.

*How to teach:* Plot a triangle and apply (x, y) → (x + 2, y), then (x, y) → (2x, y). Have the student measure sides and angles in each image and decide which preserve distance and angle; introduce 'rigid motion' and the idea of a transformation as a function from points to points.

- **Q:** Does (x, y) → (x, 3y) preserve distance? Explain.
  **A:** No. Vertical distances triple, so lengths change (it is a stretch, not a rigid motion).
- **Q:** What two things does every rigid motion preserve?
  **A:** Distance (segment lengths) and angle measure.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Translations
*Student:* Translate polygons using rules like (x, y) → (x + 3, y – 5) and vector notation.

*How to teach:* Model translating a quadrilateral using a rule and using vector notation ⟨a, b⟩. Then the student translates figures and writes the rule from a pre-image and image pair.

- **Q:** Translate (−1, 4) by the vector ⟨3, −5⟩.
  **A:** (2, −1).
- **Q:** A(2, 3) maps to A'(−1, 7). Write the translation rule.
  **A:** (x, y) → (x − 3, y + 4).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Reflections
*Student:* Reflect figures over the axes, y = x and y = –x, and write coordinate rules.

*How to teach:* Reflect a triangle over each axis and over y = x and y = −x on graph paper, recording coordinates in a table so the student discovers each rule.

- **Q:** Reflect (4, −1) over y = x and over y = −x.
  **A:** Over y = x: (−1, 4). Over y = −x: (1, −4).
- **Q:** Reflect (−3, 5) over the y-axis.
  **A:** (3, 5).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Reflection definition
*Student:* Use patty paper to show the line of reflection is the perpendicular bisector of segments joining corresponding points.

*How to teach:* Trace a figure and its reflection on patty paper, connect each point to its image, and fold to show the mirror line meets every connecting segment at 90° at its midpoint. The student writes the definition of a reflection in their own words.

- **Q:** State the definition of a reflection across line ℓ.
  **A:** Each point P maps to P' so that ℓ is the perpendicular bisector of PP' (points on ℓ map to themselves).
- **Q:** A(2, 1) reflects to A'(2, 7). What is the line of reflection?
  **A:** y = 4, the perpendicular bisector of the segment from (2, 1) to (2, 7).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on translation and reflection rules.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key. Have the student check reflection answers by plotting. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Which reflection rule swaps the coordinates?
  **A:** Reflection over y = x: (x, y) → (y, x).

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Translate (2, 4) by (x, y) → (x + 3, y − 5) → (5, −1). 2) Write the rule that moves (1, 1) to (−2, 6) → (x, y) → (x − 3, y + 5), vector ⟨−3, 5⟩. 3) Reflect (3, −2) over the x-axis → (3, 2); y-axis → (−3, −2); y = x → (−2, 3); y = −x → (2, −3). 4) A(1, 2) maps to A'(5, 2) by a reflection; name the line → x = 3. 5) Is (x, y) → (2x, y) a rigid motion? → No, it changes horizontal lengths. Score out of 8 points (item 3 = 4 points).

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Combustion and the atmosphere

**Prepare:** Have a calculator, a periodic table, and the family's recent gas/utility bill and an estimate of weekly car miles and the car's mpg. Open the California Air Resources Board GHG inventory page and a spreadsheet. Print the Unit 1 test you make from the key below.

**Watch for:**
- Students assume 1 gallon of gasoline (about 2.8 kg) cannot produce more than 2.8 kg of CO₂. Show that each carbon atom picks up two oxygen atoms from the air, so the CO₂ mass is about three times the fuel mass.
- Students confuse criteria and constraints. Criteria are what a good solution should achieve (cut CO₂, affordable); constraints are hard limits it must work within (budget, existing cars, laws).
- Students think 'carbon footprint' counts only driving. Include home heating, electricity and goods.

### Day 1 (Monday): Carbon accounting
*Student:* Calculate kg of CO₂ produced by burning 1 gallon of gasoline using atom counts.

*How to teach:* Model the calculation: a gallon of gasoline is about 2.80 kg; treat gasoline as octane, C₈H₁₈ (114.2 g/mol). Moles of octane = 2800 ÷ 114.2 = 24.5 mol; each makes 8 CO₂, so 196 mol CO₂ × 44.0 g/mol ≈ 8.6 kg. The student repeats the reasoning for a 10-gallon tank.

- **Q:** How many kg of CO₂ are produced by burning a 10-gallon tank?
  **A:** About 86 kg (10 × 8.6 kg; EPA's figure of 8.89 kg/gal gives 89 kg).
- **Q:** Write the balanced equation for octane combustion.
  **A:** 2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Household audit
*Student:* Estimate your household's weekly combustion CO₂ from car miles and gas bills.

*How to teach:* Help the student gather the family's weekly miles, mpg and natural-gas use (therms). Weekly car CO₂ = miles ÷ mpg × 8.89 kg; natural gas ≈ 5.3 kg CO₂ per therm. The student builds a small spreadsheet and identifies the largest source.

- **Q:** A family drives 200 miles a week in a 25-mpg car. How much CO₂ is that?
  **A:** 200 ÷ 25 = 8 gallons × 8.89 kg ≈ 71 kg CO₂ per week.
- **Q:** A household uses 10 therms of natural gas in a week. Estimate the CO₂.
  **A:** 10 × 5.3 = 53 kg CO₂.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Criteria & constraints
*Student:* Define criteria and constraints for a cleaner transportation fuel in a table.

*How to teach:* Define criteria and constraints with examples. The student builds a table for a cleaner transportation fuel (criteria: lower CO₂ per mile, cost per mile, energy density, safety; constraints: existing infrastructure, budget, state law, vehicle range) and ranks the importance of each.

- **Q:** Is 'must work in existing gas stations' a criterion or a constraint?
  **A:** A constraint — it is a limit the solution must operate within.
- **Q:** Give one criterion for a cleaner fuel.
  **A:** For example: produces less CO₂ per mile than gasoline (or costs no more per mile).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revisit candle
*Student:* Revise your candle model with what you've learned and update the driving-question board.

*How to teach:* Pull out the Week 1 candle questions. The student redraws the candle model, now adding particle diagrams for wax + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O and energy release, and marks which driving questions are answered. Discuss what remains unknown.

- **Q:** Using what you know now, where does the energy of the candle flame come from?
  **A:** From the chemical potential energy stored in the bonds of wax and oxygen; forming the stronger bonds in CO₂ and H₂O releases more energy than is needed to break the reactant bonds.
- **Q:** Why does the flame need the wick to start?
  **A:** The wick holds melted wax so it can vaporize; wax burns as a vapor, and the wick's flame provides heat to melt and vaporize more wax.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 test
*Student:* Unit 1 test on combustion, energy and measurement.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 test in one sitting (allow 50 minutes). Grade with the key, then have the student redo missed problems and explain one in writing.

- **Q:** Balance: C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.
  **A:** 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test key (sample items): 1) Balance C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. 2) Products of complete hydrocarbon combustion: CO₂ and H₂O. 3) Steel wool gains mass when burned because oxygen atoms bond to iron. 4) 150 g water warmed by 12.0 °C absorbs q = 150 × 4.184 × 12.0 = 7530 J. 5) 300 Cal = 1255 kJ. 6) Sig figs in 0.0500 — 3. 7) 7.21 × 0.32 = 2.3. 8) Burning 1 gal gasoline (2.80 kg, modeled as C₈H₁₈) makes about 8.6 kg CO₂ (EPA uses 8.89 kg). 9) Define criteria vs. constraint (see misconception). 10) Short answer: why the candle went out sooner in a small jar — less oxygen available. Score 1 point each (item 10: 2 points); 90%+ mastery, 70–89% targeted review, below 70% reteach.

## Modern World History: The American Revolution and Latin American independence

**Prepare:** Declaration of Independence (archives.gov), a summary of the Haitian Revolution and an excerpt of the 1801 Haitian (Saint-Domingue) Constitution, Bolívar's Jamaica Letter (1815) excerpt. Chart template for Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the American Revolution created full equality. Enslaved people, women and Native Americans were excluded.
- Overlooking Haiti: it was the only successful revolution led by enslaved people and the first independent Black republic (1804).

### Day 1 (Monday): Declaration
*Student:* Close-read the Declaration and trace phrases to Locke.

*How to teach:* Read the preamble and the opening of the grievances aloud. The student highlights phrases that echo Locke and writes the Lockean idea beside each. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Which phrase echoes Locke's natural rights?
  **A:** 'Unalienable Rights, that among these are Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness' (Locke: life, liberty, property).
- **Q:** Which phrase echoes Locke's idea of consent and revolution?
  **A:** Governments derive 'their just powers from the consent of the governed,' and the people may 'alter or to abolish' a destructive government.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Unique character
*Student:* Discuss what made the American Revolution distinctive (federalism, written constitution).

*How to teach:* Discuss what made the American Revolution distinctive: a written constitution, federalism dividing power between national and state governments, and a Bill of Rights. The student writes a paragraph on one distinctive feature and its influence abroad.

- **Q:** What is federalism?
  **A:** A system dividing power between a national government and state governments.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Haiti
*Student:* Read about Toussaint L'Ouverture and the Haitian Revolution; analyze its 1801 constitution excerpt.

*How to teach:* Read about the Haitian Revolution (1791 uprising, Toussaint Louverture, independence in 1804). Analyze the 1801 constitution excerpt: what did it abolish, and what power did it give Toussaint? Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What did the 1801 constitution declare about slavery?
  **A:** Slavery was abolished forever — there could be no slaves in the territory.
- **Q:** Why was the Haitian Revolution so significant?
  **A:** It was the only successful slave revolt to create a nation — the first independent Black republic — and it alarmed slaveholding societies throughout the Americas.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Bolívar
*Student:* Analyze Bolívar's Jamaica Letter for Enlightenment ideas and Creole grievances.

*How to teach:* Source the Jamaica Letter (Bolívar, 1815, written in exile to a British audience). The student identifies Enlightenment ideas and Creole grievances. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What grievance does Bolívar raise for Creoles?
  **A:** Spain excluded American-born Creoles from high offices and trade, treating them as subordinates despite their wealth and education.
- **Q:** Why might Bolívar write to a British reader?
  **A:** To win British sympathy and support for Spanish American independence.

### Day 5 (Friday): Comparison
*Student:* Complete a chart comparing the American, Haitian and Latin American revolutions.

*How to teach:* The student completes the comparison chart for the three revolutions (leaders, causes, ideas, results, who was included/excluded). Score with the assessment key. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Which event in Europe helped trigger Latin American independence movements?
  **A:** Napoleon's invasion of Spain (1808), which weakened Spanish control over its colonies.

**Assessment:** Comparison chart.

**Answer key:** Comparison chart (expected content): American (1775–1783) — leaders: colonial elites (Washington, Jefferson); causes: taxation without representation, Enlightenment rights; result: independent republic, federal written Constitution, slavery persists. Haitian (1791–1804) — leaders: Toussaint Louverture, Jean-Jacques Dessalines; causes: brutal slavery, French Revolution ideals; result: slavery abolished, independence 1804, first Black republic. Latin American (1810–1820s) — leaders: Creoles like Simón Bolívar and José de San Martín; causes: Creole exclusion from power, Napoleon's invasion of Spain, Enlightenment ideas; result: independent republics often dominated by elites and caudillos. Score 4 = every cell accurate; 3 = 1–2 errors; 2 = several gaps; 1 = incomplete.

## Spanish III: El pretérito

**Prepare:** Make preterite charts: regular endings; irregulars ser/ir (fui), tener (tuve), estar (estuve), hacer (hice), decir (dije, dijeron), poder (pude), poner (puse); spelling changes -car/-gar/-zar (yo) and i→y (leyó, creyeron, oyó).

**Watch for:**
- Writing *dijieron or *trajieron: j-stem verbs drop the i — dijeron, trajeron.
- Accent errors: hablé/habló need accents; irregular stems do not (tuve, hizo).
- Missing i→y: leer → leyó/leyeron; oír → oyó/oyeron.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pretérito regular
*Student:* Review regular preterite forms and write about last weekend in 8 sentences.

*How to teach:* Review endings; your child writes 8 sentences about last weekend.

- **Q:** Translate: 'On Saturday I cleaned my room and watched a movie.'
  **A:** El sábado limpié mi cuarto y vi una película.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Irregulares
*Student:* Drill preterite irregulars (ser/ir, tener, estar, hacer, decir, poder, poner).

*How to teach:* Drill the irregular stems with quick oral prompts and a written chart.

- **Q:** Conjugate decir in the preterite for yo and ellos.
  **A:** dije; dijeron
- **Q:** Translate: 'We put the books on the table.'
  **A:** Pusimos los libros en la mesa.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cambios ortográficos
*Student:* Practice -car/-gar/-zar and i→y spelling changes.

*How to teach:* Practice spelling changes: busqué, pagué, empecé; leyó, oyeron, creyó, construyeron.

- **Q:** Give the yo preterite of sacar, jugar, and comenzar.
  **A:** saqué, jugué, comencé
- **Q:** Give the ellos preterite of leer.
  **A:** leyeron

### Day 4 (Thursday): Narración
*Student:* Write a 150-word narrative about a memorable trip using 15 preterite verbs.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a 150-word narrative about a memorable trip using 15 preterite verbs, then underlines each.

- **Q:** Translate: 'We arrived at the hotel and I fell asleep.'
  **A:** Llegamos al hotel y me dormí.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Quiz on preterite.

*How to teach:* Give the preterite quiz.

- **Q:** Correct: 'Ellos trajieron la comida y yo la servió.'
  **A:** Ellos trajeron la comida y yo la serví.

**Assessment:** Quiz and narrative.

**Answer key:** Preterite quiz sample key: llegar/yo — llegué; leer/ella — leyó; hacer/él — hizo; decir/ellos — dijeron; poner/yo — puse; poder/tú — pudiste; estar/nosotros — estuvimos; ir/ustedes — fueron; tocar/yo — toqué; creer/ellos — creyeron. Narrative rubric (150 words, 15 preterite verbs): 4 = clear sequence, 15+ correct preterite verbs including irregulars; 3 = 1–3 errors; 2 = many errors or under 120 words; 1 = fragments.

## Music & Media Arts: Sight-reading and performance

**Prepare:** Keyboard, voice, ukulele or recorder; three 8-bar sight-reading melodies (e.g., from sightreadingfactory.com free trial or a beginner method book); a simple piece (Ode to Joy or a folk song); practice log; performance rubric.

**Watch for:**
- Students practice pieces from the beginning every time. Isolate hard measures, play slowly, then connect.
- Students ignore dynamics until the end. Add expression from the start of practice.
- Students stop at every mistake when sight-reading. Keep the beat and keep going.

### Day 1 (Monday): Expressive markings
*Student:* Learn dynamics, tempo and articulation markings.

*How to teach:* Teach dynamics, tempo and articulation markings with examples.

- **Q:** What does mf mean?
  **A:** Mezzo forte — moderately loud.
- **Q:** What's the difference between staccato and legato?
  **A:** Staccato is short and detached; legato is smooth and connected.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sight-reading
*Student:* Sight-read three 8-bar melodies on keyboard or voice.

*How to teach:* The student sight-reads three 8-bar melodies, first scanning key, time signature and tricky spots.

- **Q:** What should you check before sight-reading?
  **A:** Key signature, time signature, tempo marking, and any tricky rhythms or accidentals.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Choose a piece
*Student:* Select a short piece (e.g., 'Ode to Joy' or a folk song) to perform.

*How to teach:* The student selects a short piece to perform and marks phrases and breaths.

- **Q:** What is a phrase in music?
  **A:** A musical 'sentence', often 4 bars, ending with a cadence or breathing point.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rehearse
*Student:* Use a practice log and slow-practice strategies on difficult measures.

*How to teach:* Use a practice log and slow practice on difficult measures.

- **Q:** Describe an effective slow-practice strategy.
  **A:** Play the hard measure slowly with a metronome until correct 3 times, then raise the tempo gradually.

### Day 5 (Friday): Perform
*Student:* Record a performance and self-evaluate with a rubric.

*How to teach:* Record a performance and self-evaluate with the rubric.

- **Q:** What's the Italian term for gradually slowing down?
  **A:** Ritardando (rit.).

**Assessment:** Performance rubric.

**Answer key:** Performance rubric (4 levels): pitch accuracy, rhythm/steady tempo, expression (dynamics and articulation as marked), tone and posture, and self-evaluation with two specific goals. Proficient = few errors, clear dynamic contrast, steady tempo. Markings key: p (piano) soft, f (forte) loud, mp/mf medium soft/loud, crescendo gets louder, decrescendo/diminuendo softer; allegro fast, andante walking, adagio slow, ritardando gradually slower; staccato short/detached, legato smooth, accent emphasized.

## PE 10 (Course 2): Hiking fundamentals and Leave No Trace

**Prepare:** Gather a daypack, water bottles, and the Ten Essentials (navigation, headlamp, sun protection, first aid, knife/tool, fire starter, emergency shelter, extra food, extra water, extra clothes). Choose a local 3–5 mile trail using a trail map app (AllTrails, CalTopo, or park maps). Check weather and trail conditions.

**Watch for:**
- Students think a phone is all the navigation they need. Batteries die and signal drops; carry a paper map and know your route.
- Students estimate hiking time by distance alone. Add time for elevation: Naismith's rule — about 1 hour per 3 miles plus 1 hour per 2,000 feet of gain.
- Students think biodegradable food scraps are fine to leave. Orange peels and apple cores take a long time to break down and attract wildlife; pack it out.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ten Essentials
*Student:* Pack the Ten Essentials and explain each item's purpose.

*How to teach:* The student packs the Ten Essentials and explains each item's purpose.

- **Q:** Why carry extra clothing on a sunny day hike?
  **A:** Weather can change, temperatures drop, and an injury or delay could mean being out after dark; layers prevent hypothermia.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Trail planning
*Student:* Use a trail map app to plan a local hike, calculating distance and elevation gain.

*How to teach:* The student plans a hike with a map app, calculating distance, elevation gain and estimated time.

- **Q:** Estimate time for a 4-mile hike with 1,000 feet of gain using Naismith's rule.
  **A:** 4 ÷ 3 = 1.33 h + 1,000 ÷ 2,000 = 0.5 h → about 1 hour 50 minutes (plus breaks).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Conditioning walk
*Student:* Do a 45-minute brisk walk with a loaded daypack, monitoring heart rate.

*How to teach:* The student does a 45-minute brisk walk with a loaded daypack, checking heart rate three times.

- **Q:** Why train with a loaded pack?
  **A:** To condition legs, core and shoulders for the actual load and check pack fit.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Leave No Trace
*Student:* Learn the seven LNT principles and apply them to scenarios.

*How to teach:* The student learns the seven LNT principles and applies them to scenarios.

- **Q:** You find an arrowhead on a trail. What should you do?
  **A:** Leave it where it is (Leave what you find); it may also be illegal to remove artifacts from public lands.

### Day 5 (Friday): Hike
*Student:* Complete a 3–5 mile hike with family, logging time, distance and heart rate.

*How to teach:* Complete a 3–5 mile family hike; the student logs time, distance and heart rate.

- **Q:** What's a turnaround time and why set one?
  **A:** A preset time to head back regardless of progress, so you return before dark or before weather or fatigue become dangerous.

**Assessment:** Hike log and plan.

**Answer key:** Hike log and plan: full credit for route map, distance, elevation gain, time estimate (Naismith), turnaround time, weather check, Ten Essentials checklist, who was told the plan, and log of time, distance and heart rate at 3+ points. Leave No Trace principles: 1) Plan ahead and prepare. 2) Travel and camp on durable surfaces. 3) Dispose of waste properly. 4) Leave what you find. 5) Minimize campfire impacts. 6) Respect wildlife. 7) Be considerate of other visitors.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Traffic signs

**Prepare:** Gather index cards and the handbook sign pages. Plan a ride for Day 4 where the student is a passenger with a phone or notebook.

**Watch for:**
- Students confuse the yield triangle and the warning diamond. Yield is a downward-pointing red-and-white triangle; most warnings are yellow diamonds.
- Students think a pennant means a school zone. The yellow pennant (on the left side of the road) means No Passing Zone; the five-sided pentagon is the school zone/crossing sign.
- Students mix up colors: red = stop/prohibit; yellow = general warning; fluorescent yellow-green = school, pedestrian and bicycle warnings; orange = construction/work zone; green = guidance/directions; blue = motorist services; brown = recreation.

### Day 1 (Monday): Shapes & colors
*Student:* Make flashcards for sign shapes and colors (octagon stop, triangle yield, pentagon school, round railroad, pennant no passing).

*How to teach:* The student makes flashcards for sign shapes and colors with the meaning on the back and drills with you until fast.

- **Q:** What shape is a stop sign, and why is that shape unique?
  **A:** An octagon; it is the only eight-sided sign so drivers can recognize it from behind or when covered with snow.
- **Q:** What does a round yellow sign with an X and 'RR' mean?
  **A:** Railroad crossing ahead.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Regulatory signs
*Student:* Study regulatory signs and sort 20 sign images into must-do and must-not-do.

*How to teach:* The student sorts 20 regulatory sign images into 'must do' (e.g., stop, yield, speed limit, keep right) and 'must not do' (no U-turn, do not enter, no left turn).

- **Q:** What should you do if you see a DO NOT ENTER sign?
  **A:** Do not enter that road or ramp; it is for traffic moving the other way.
- **Q:** What does a red circle with a diagonal slash mean?
  **A:** The pictured action is prohibited.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Warning & guide signs
*Student:* Study warning and guide signs and explain each to a family member.

*How to teach:* The student studies warning and guide signs and explains 10 of them to a family member.

- **Q:** What does a fluorescent yellow-green sign with a walking person mean?
  **A:** Pedestrian crossing ahead; watch for people.
- **Q:** What do blue signs indicate?
  **A:** Motorist services such as gas, food, lodging or hospitals.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sign hunt
*Student:* On a ride as a passenger, photograph or list 15 different signs and explain what a driver must do at each.

*How to teach:* On a car ride as a passenger, the student lists or photographs 15 different signs and writes what the driver must do at each.

- **Q:** You see a yellow sign with a curving arrow and '25 MPH' under it. What should you do?
  **A:** Slow to the advised 25 mph (or less) before entering the curve.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Sign identification quiz (40 signs, 90% goal).

*How to teach:* Give the 40-sign quiz. Goal 90%. Review missed signs.

- **Q:** What does an orange diamond sign indicate?
  **A:** A construction or work zone warning.

**Assessment:** Sign quiz.

**Answer key:** Sign quiz key: grade 40 signs; 36+ correct (90%) = mastery. ## Computer Science Principles: Compression: lossless and lossy

**Prepare:** Open the Code.org Text Compression widget. Find a photo and a free image editor (any phone editor, GIMP, or an online tool) that lets you export JPEG at different quality levels. Make three small grids (e.g. 6×6) of black/white pixels for run-length encoding practice.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking lossless compression can always shrink a file a lot. It works by removing redundancy; random or already-compressed data hardly shrinks.
- Thinking lossy compression is 'bad'. Lossy is the right choice when small, hard-to-notice quality loss is acceptable (streaming video, web photos); the original cannot be recovered, so never use it where exactness matters.
- Mixing up bits and bytes in download math. Internet speed is in megabits per second (Mb), file sizes are usually in megabytes (MB); 1 byte = 8 bits.

### Day 1 (Monday): Why compress?
*Student:* Calculate how long an uncompressed 4K video would take to download at 25 Mbps, then discuss why compression matters.

*How to teach:* Work the numbers together: one 4K frame is 3840 × 2160 pixels × 24 bits ≈ 199 million bits; at 30 frames per second that is about 6 billion bits (6,000 megabits) every second. A 1-minute clip is about 358,000 megabits; at 25 Mbps it takes about 14,300 seconds (≈ 4 hours) to download — while watching it takes 1 minute. Discuss why streaming services must compress video by 100× or more.

- **Q:** How long would a 100 GB file take to download at 25 Mbps?
  **A:** 100 GB × 8 = 800 Gb = 800,000 Mb; 800,000 ÷ 25 = 32,000 seconds ≈ 8.9 hours.
- **Q:** Why is an uncompressed video so large?
  **A:** Each frame is a full image (3840×2160 pixels × 24 bits ≈ 199 million bits ≈ 25 MB), and there are 24–60 frames every second.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Lossless compression
*Student:* Compress a song lyric with the Code.org Text Compression widget and record your compression ratio.

*How to teach:* Use the Text Compression widget: the student replaces repeated words/phrases in a song lyric with symbols (a dictionary) and records original size, compressed size and ratio. Point out that nothing is lost — the original can be rebuilt.

- **Q:** A 200-character lyric compresses to 120 characters (including the dictionary). What is the compression ratio?
  **A:** 120/200 = 60% of the original size (a 40% saving), or 200:120 ≈ 1.67:1.
- **Q:** Why is this compression called lossless?
  **A:** The original text can be rebuilt exactly by replacing each symbol with its dictionary entry; no information is lost.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Run-length encoding
*Student:* Write the run-length encoding for three pixel images and decode one back to the original grid.

*How to teach:* Explain run-length encoding (RLE): write each run as a count and a color, e.g. row WWWBBW → 3W 2B 1W. Do one grid together, have the student encode two more and decode one RLE back into a grid.

- **Q:** Encode the row BBBBWWBB with RLE.
  **A:** 4B 2W 2B.
- **Q:** Decode 2W 3B 1W into a row.
  **A:** WWBBBW.
- **Q:** When does RLE make data bigger?
  **A:** When colors change often (e.g. BWBWBW): each single pixel becomes a count plus a color, which takes more space than the original.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Lossy compression
*Student:* Save a photo as JPEG at quality 100, 50 and 10, compare file sizes and visible artifacts, and decide when lossy is acceptable.

*How to teach:* Have the student save the same photo as JPEG at quality 100, 50 and 10, record file sizes and zoom in to see blocky artifacts. Ask when lossy is acceptable (web photos, social media, streaming) and when not (medical images, legal documents).

- **Q:** What happens to file size and image quality as JPEG quality goes from 100 to 10?
  **A:** File size drops greatly, while visible artifacts (blockiness, blurry edges, color banding) increase.
- **Q:** Can you get the quality-100 image back from the quality-10 file?
  **A:** No — lossy compression permanently discards data.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a short comparison of lossless vs. lossy compression for three scenarios (medical scan, streaming video, text document).

*How to teach:* Have the student write the three-scenario comparison (medical scan, streaming video, text document). Score with the rubric in the assessment key and discuss any scenario they got wrong.

- **Q:** Which compression type would you use for a bank's transaction records, and why?
  **A:** Lossless — every digit must be exact; losing any data could change account balances.

**Assessment:** Compression comparison scored with rubric.

**Answer key:** Comparison rubric (4 levels) for the three scenarios: Proficient answers: medical scan → lossless, because doctors need every detail and lost data could hide a diagnosis; streaming video → lossy, because files must be small enough to stream and viewers don't notice small losses; text document → lossless, because every character must be exact (a lost letter changes meaning). 4 = all three correct with tradeoffs (file size vs. quality/recoverability) explained; 3 = all correct, brief reasons; 2 = one wrong or no reasons; 1 = confused terms.
