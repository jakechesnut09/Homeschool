# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 33

## English 10: A Doll's House Act 3 and poetry of identity

**Prepare:** A Doll's House Act 3; Tagore's 'Where the Mind is Without Fear'; Hughes's 'Let America Be America Again'; McKay's 'If We Must Die.'

**Watch for:**
- Reading Nora's departure as simply leaving her children. Discuss her argument that she must become a person first.
- Missing the irony of Hughes's title. The America he wants 'never has been yet.'

### Day 1 (Monday): Act 3
*Student:* Read Act 3 and analyze Nora's final speech line by line.

*How to teach:* Read Act 3. Analyze Nora's final speech line by line. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does Nora mean by 'I have been your doll-wife, just as at home I was papa's doll-child'?
  **A:** She has been treated as a plaything all her life, never as an independent person.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): The door slam
*Student:* Debate: Is Nora's leaving justified? Discuss the play's 1879 reception.

*How to teach:* Debate whether Nora's leaving is justified. Discuss the 1879 reception and the alternate German ending. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** How did audiences react in 1879?
  **A:** Many were shocked; a German production required an alternate ending where Nora stays for the children, which Ibsen called a 'barbaric outrage.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tagore
*Student:* Read Tagore's poem and connect its vision of freedom to Unit 6 speeches.

*How to teach:* Read Tagore's poem. Connect its freedom vision to Unit 6 speeches. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What freedom does Tagore envision?
  **A:** A world where the mind is without fear, knowledge is free, and people aren't divided by 'narrow domestic walls.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Hughes & McKay
*Student:* Read Hughes and McKay poems and analyze form and tone.

*How to teach:* Read Hughes and McKay. Analyze form and tone. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What form does McKay use?
  **A:** A Shakespearean sonnet.

### Day 5 (Friday): Response
*Student:* Write a comparative response: how do Nora and one poem's speaker claim identity?

*How to teach:* The student plans a thesis comparing Nora and one poem's speaker. They write the comparative response with two quotes from each text, then score it with the rubric together. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What does Nora say she is 'before all else'?
  **A:** 'A human being.'

**Assessment:** Comparative response.

**Answer key:** Comparative response rubric (4-point): thesis comparing how Nora and one poem's speaker claim identity; two quotes from each (Nora: 'I have been your doll-wife'; 'I believe that before all else I am a human being'; Hughes: 'America never was America to me'; McKay: 'Like men we'll face the murderous, cowardly pack'; Tagore: 'Where the mind is without fear and the head is held high'); analysis of form/tone; organization.

## Geometry: Two-way tables

**Prepare:** Spreadsheet software; plan two yes/no survey questions with the student (e.g., 'Do you have a pet?' and 'Do you live in a house?'). Arrange to survey 20 people (family, friends, co-op).

**Watch for:**
- Dividing by the grand total when a conditional probability needs the row or column total.
- Concluding cause and effect from an association in a two-way table.

### Day 1 (Monday): Two-way tables
*Student:* Build two-way tables from data and compute joint and marginal relative frequencies.

*How to teach:* Build the sample table from the assessment key with row and column totals. The student computes joint (cell/grand total) and marginal (total/grand total) relative frequencies.

- **Q:** Using the sports/music table, what is the joint relative frequency of 'no sports and no music'?
  **A:** 42/100 = 0.42.
- **Q:** What is the marginal relative frequency of music?
  **A:** 30/100 = 0.30.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Survey
*Student:* Survey 20 people on two categorical questions and organize results.

*How to teach:* The student surveys 20 people and organizes the results in a two-way table in a spreadsheet with totals.

- **Q:** Why should both questions be yes/no or categorical?
  **A:** Two-way tables organize categorical data; each person falls into exactly one cell.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Analyze
*Student:* Use the table to compute conditional probabilities and test independence.

*How to teach:* Compute conditional probabilities by row and column, then compare P(A|B) to P(A) to test independence.

- **Q:** From the sample table, P(sports | music)?
  **A:** 12/30 = 0.40.
- **Q:** Is P(sports | music) equal to P(sports)? What does that mean?
  **A:** Yes, both 0.40, so playing sports and liking music are independent in this data.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Report
*Student:* Write a one-page report with the table and conclusions.

*How to teach:* The student writes a one-page report: question, table, key probabilities, independence conclusion, and limitations.

- **Q:** Name a limitation of a 20-person survey of friends and family.
  **A:** Small, non-random sample; results may not represent a larger population.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on two-way tables.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key and score the report. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** What does a conditional relative frequency compare?
  **A:** The count in a cell to the total of its row (or column), i.e., the frequency within one group.

**Assessment:** Survey report and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz uses this table (100 students): Sports & Music 12, Sports & No music 28, No sports & Music 18, No sports & No music 42. Answers: 1) P(music) → 30/100 = 0.30. 2) P(sports and music) → 0.12. 3) P(music | sports) → 12/40 = 0.30. 4) P(sports | music) → 12/30 = 0.40. 5) Are sports and music independent? → yes, P(music | sports) = P(music) = 0.30. 6) Marginal relative frequency of sports → 0.40. Report rubric: 4 = clean table with totals, joint/marginal/conditional values, a correct independence test, a conclusion with limitations (small sample, not random); 3 = minor errors; 2 = table only with little analysis; 1 = incomplete.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Solutions, acids and bases

**Prepare:** Gather table salt, the kitchen scale, measuring cups, a 1-cup (≈ 250 mL) container, cabbage indicator, 10 household solutions (lemon juice, vinegar, soda, coffee, milk, tap water, baking soda solution, soap solution, diluted ammonia cleaner — never mix ammonia with bleach), droppers, and clear cups. Open PhET 'pH Scale'.

**Watch for:**
- Students think pH 6 is twice as acidic as pH 3. Each pH unit is a factor of 10: pH 3 has 1,000 times more H₃O⁺ than pH 6.
- Students think molarity uses liters of water added. Molarity = moles of solute per liter of total solution.
- Students think neutralization makes something disappear. Acid and base form water and a salt.

### Day 1 (Monday): Molarity
*Student:* Prepare salt solutions of known molarity and practice concentration calculations.

*How to teach:* Model M = mol ÷ L. The student makes 250 mL of a 0.50 M salt solution (7.31 g NaCl dissolved, then water added to 250 mL total) and solves concentration problems.

- **Q:** How many grams of NaCl are needed for 250 mL of 0.50 M solution?
  **A:** 0.250 L × 0.50 mol/L = 0.125 mol × 58.44 g/mol = 7.31 g.
- **Q:** What is the molarity of 10.0 g of NaOH (40.00 g/mol) in 500 mL of solution?
  **A:** 0.250 mol ÷ 0.500 L = 0.500 M.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): pH survey
*Student:* Test 10 household solutions with cabbage indicator and rank by pH.

*How to teach:* The student adds indicator to each household solution, records colors, and ranks them by pH using a cabbage color chart (red ~1–2, pink ~3–4, purple ~5–7, blue ~8, green ~9–11, yellow ~12+).

- **Q:** Which household items are acids?
  **A:** Lemon juice, vinegar, soda, coffee (and slightly, milk).
- **Q:** What color would you expect for ammonia cleaner?
  **A:** Green (strongly basic).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): PhET pH Scale
*Student:* Use PhET pH Scale to relate H₃O⁺ concentration to pH.

*How to teach:* In PhET pH Scale, the student tests items and relates [H₃O⁺] to pH using the logarithmic scale; record a table of pH vs. [H₃O⁺].

- **Q:** A solution has [H₃O⁺] = 1 × 10⁻⁵ M. What is its pH, and is it acidic?
  **A:** pH 5; acidic.
- **Q:** How does [H₃O⁺] change if you dilute an acid tenfold?
  **A:** It drops by a factor of 10, so the pH rises by 1.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Neutralization
*Student:* Titrate vinegar with baking soda solution using indicator to find the endpoint.

*How to teach:* Measure 10.0 mL of vinegar into a cup with indicator. Add 0.50 M baking-soda solution (2.1 g NaHCO₃ in 50 mL solution) a milliliter at a time, swirling, until the color turns purple-blue and stays. Calculate vinegar's molarity from the volume used.

- **Q:** It took 16.6 mL of 0.50 M NaHCO₃ to neutralize 10.0 mL of vinegar. What is the vinegar's molarity?
  **A:** 0.0166 L × 0.50 = 0.0083 mol NaHCO₃ = 0.0083 mol acetic acid (1:1); 0.0083 ÷ 0.0100 L = 0.83 M (about 5% acetic acid).
- **Q:** Why add base slowly near the endpoint?
  **A:** To avoid overshooting the exact point where the acid is just neutralized.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on solutions and pH.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz with a calculator; collect the pH chart and titration data.

- **Q:** How many times more H₃O⁺ is in lemon juice (pH 2) than in milk (pH 6.5)?
  **A:** About 10^4.5 ≈ 32,000 times.

**Assessment:** pH chart and titration data.

**Answer key:** pH chart: full credit for 10 solutions ranked with color observations and approximate pH (lemon ~2, vinegar ~2.5–3, soda ~3, coffee ~5, milk ~6.5, water ~7, baking soda ~8–8.5, soap ~9–10, ammonia ~11–12). Titration data: volume of baking-soda solution needed to reach the endpoint (purple/blue color change) in a measured vinegar sample, with mole calculation (e.g., 10.0 mL of 0.83 M vinegar contains 0.0083 mol acetic acid; with 0.50 M NaHCO₃ the endpoint is about 16.6 mL). Quiz key: 1) Molarity of 0.50 mol NaCl in 2.0 L — 0.25 M. 2) Grams of NaCl for 250 mL of 0.50 M — 7.31 g. 3) pH when [H₃O⁺] = 1 × 10⁻³ M — 3. 4) How many times more acidic is pH 3 than pH 5 — 100. 5) Products of HCl + NaOH — NaCl + H₂O. 6) Acidic, basic or neutral: pH 9 — basic. 7) Moles of HCl in 25.0 mL of 0.100 M — 0.00250 mol.

## Modern World History: Globalization and the information revolution

**Prepare:** World Bank or Our World in Data trade figures, 20 household products with country-of-origin labels, a blank world map. Readings on the WTO, IMF and EU; a short history of Silicon Valley.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking globalization benefits everyone equally. Consumers gain cheaper goods while some workers and regions lose jobs.
- Confusing the WTO (trade rules), IMF (financial stability and emergency loans) and World Bank (development loans).

### Day 1 (Monday): Label audit
*Student:* Inventory 20 household products by country of origin and map them.

*How to teach:* The student checks labels on 20 household products and records the country of origin, then plots them on a world map. Discuss patterns and why goods come from where they do.

- **Q:** Why are many electronics assembled in East Asia?
  **A:** Lower labor costs, large manufacturing capacity, skilled workforces and established supply chains.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Trade organizations
*Student:* Explain the roles of the WTO, IMF and EU.

*How to teach:* Read short descriptions of the WTO, IMF and EU. The student makes a three-column chart: purpose, year founded, one example of its role. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What is the difference between the IMF and the WTO?
  **A:** The IMF stabilizes the global financial system and lends to countries in crisis; the WTO sets rules for international trade and settles trade disputes.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Information revolution
*Student:* Trace television, satellites, computers and the internet; connect to Silicon Valley.

*How to teach:* The student builds a timeline of the information revolution (TV, satellites, computers, ARPANET, the Web, smartphones) and connects it to Silicon Valley companies in California. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Who created the World Wide Web?
  **A:** Tim Berners-Lee at CERN, 1989–1991.
- **Q:** Why is Silicon Valley important to the information revolution?
  **A:** Companies there (Intel, Apple, HP, Google) developed key chips, computers and internet services.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Winners & losers
*Student:* Analyze data on globalization's effects in two countries.

*How to teach:* Give the student data for two countries (e.g., China's poverty rate falling, and manufacturing job losses in a U.S. region). The student identifies winners and losers of globalization. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Name one group that has gained and one that has lost from globalization.
  **A:** Gained: consumers (cheaper goods) or workers in new export industries. Lost: workers in industries moved overseas.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on globalization.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key and check the map. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What is outsourcing?
  **A:** Moving production or services to another company or country, often to lower costs.

**Assessment:** Map and quiz.

**Answer key:** Map: full credit for 20 products correctly plotted with a legend and two observations (e.g., many electronics from China, produce from Mexico). Quiz (answers): 1) WTO → created 1995 (from GATT, 1947); sets trade rules and settles trade disputes. 2) IMF → created 1944 at Bretton Woods; stabilizes currencies and lends to countries in financial crisis. 3) EU → economic and political union of European nations with a single market; the euro adopted 1999 (coins/notes 2002). 4) Information revolution milestones → satellites (Sputnik 1957), personal computers (1970s–80s), ARPANET (1969), World Wide Web (Tim Berners-Lee, 1989–91). 5) Silicon Valley → Santa Clara Valley region of California; Hewlett-Packard, Intel, Apple, Google. 6) One benefit and one cost of globalization → cheaper goods / job losses in manufacturing regions.

## Spanish III: Cultura en los Estados Unidos

**Prepare:** Find Pew Research Center or U.S. Census data on Latinos in California (Latinos are about 40% of the state's population, the largest ethnic group). Arrange an interview with a Spanish speaker (in person or video). Locate a local Spanish-language radio station online. Write the Unit 9 test (festivals, heritage, Latino culture in the U.S., subjunctive review).

**Watch for:**
- Assuming all Latinos speak Spanish or share one culture: Latinos in the U.S. have roots in many countries, and many are English-dominant or bilingual.
- Treating Spanglish as 'bad Spanish': code-switching is a normal skill of bilingual speakers; formal writing still uses standard Spanish.

### Day 1 (Monday): Datos
*Student:* Analyze data about the Latino population in California.

*How to teach:* Your child analyzes data on the Latino population in California and writes 5 findings in Spanish.

- **Q:** Translate: 'About forty percent of Californians are Latino.'
  **A:** Aproximadamente el cuarenta por ciento de los californianos son latinos.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Entrevista
*Student:* Interview a Spanish speaker in your community (or via video).

*How to teach:* Your child interviews a Spanish speaker with 10 questions (origin, arrival, work, traditions, language) and records it with permission.

- **Q:** Ask: 'How long have you lived in California?'
  **A:** ¿Cuánto tiempo hace que vive en California? (¿Desde hace cuánto vive en California?)

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Medios locales
*Student:* Listen to a local Spanish-language radio station and summarize a segment.

*How to teach:* Listen to a local Spanish-language radio segment; your child summarizes the topic and 3 details.

- **Q:** What is 'el bilingüismo'?
  **A:** Bilingualism — speaking two languages.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Reportaje
*Student:* Write a report on the interview.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a report on the interview with quotations and reported speech.

- **Q:** Convert to reported speech: 'Me encanta mi comunidad,' dijo la señora.
  **A:** La señora dijo que le encantaba su comunidad.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Unit 9 test.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 9 test.

- **Q:** Fill in: Es importante que los jóvenes ___ (mantener) el español.
  **A:** mantengan

**Assessment:** Unit 9 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 9 test sample key: 1) Latinos ≈ 40% of Californians, the largest group 2) el bilingüismo = bilingualism 3) el spanglish = mixing Spanish and English 4) festival items (Día de Muertos, Fallas, Inti Raymi) 5) borrowed words: Nahuatl/Quechua/Arabic examples 6) subjunctive: Es importante que mantengamos el idioma; Si viviera en México, hablaría español todos los días. Report rubric: 4 = summarizes the interviewee's background, work, and traditions with accurate past tenses and direct quotations, plus a reflection; 3 = minor errors; 2 = thin; 1 = incomplete.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Healthy relationships and consent

**Prepare:** Parental preview required. Print a relationship continuum (healthy–unhealthy–abusive; loveisrespect.org has one). Read California's affirmative consent definition and loveisrespect.org resources. Discuss your family's values alongside the facts.

**Watch for:**
- Students think jealousy and constant texting show love. Monitoring, controlling and isolating are warning signs of an unhealthy relationship.
- Students think silence or not resisting means consent. Consent must be affirmative, conscious and voluntary; it can be withdrawn anytime and can't be given if someone is intoxicated, asleep or pressured.
- Students think sexting between teens is legal if both agree. Creating, sending or possessing sexual images of a minor can violate child pornography laws, even when minors share their own images.

### Day 1 (Monday): Healthy vs. unhealthy
*Student:* Sort relationship behaviors on a continuum from healthy to abusive.

*How to teach:* The student sorts behavior cards along the continuum and explains placements.

- **Q:** Where does 'checks your phone without asking' fall?
  **A:** Unhealthy (controlling); it may move toward abusive if combined with other control.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Consent
*Student:* Learn affirmative consent and practice clear communication of boundaries in scenarios.

*How to teach:* The student learns affirmative consent and practices stating and respecting boundaries in scenarios.

- **Q:** Is consent given if a person says yes to one thing but not another?
  **A:** No — consent to one activity does not mean consent to others; it must be given for each activity and can be withdrawn at any time.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Digital relationships
*Student:* Discuss online safety, sexting laws and digital dating abuse.

*How to teach:* Discuss online safety, sexting laws and digital dating abuse.

- **Q:** Someone pressures you to send a nude photo. What are the risks?
  **A:** It can be shared without consent and can never be fully deleted; for minors, creating or sending such images can violate laws.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Getting help
*Student:* Learn signs of dating violence and resources such as Love Is Respect.

*How to teach:* The student learns signs of dating violence and resources such as Love Is Respect.

- **Q:** How can a teen contact Love Is Respect?
  **A:** Text LOVEIS to 22522, call 1-866-331-9474, or chat at loveisrespect.org.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Quiz on healthy relationships and consent.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Review.

- **Q:** What makes consent 'affirmative'?
  **A:** It is an active, clear and voluntary 'yes' — not silence or lack of resistance.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Healthy signs — respect, trust, honesty, equality, good communication, independence. 2) Unhealthy/abusive signs — controlling, jealousy, isolating from friends, checking phone, name-calling, threats, physical harm, pressuring for sex. 3) Affirmative consent — clear, conscious, voluntary agreement; ongoing; can be withdrawn; not possible when incapacitated. 4) Boundaries — personal limits; stating them clearly is healthy. 5) Digital abuse — monitoring, demanding passwords, threatening to share images, constant texting. 6) Sexting laws — sharing sexual images of minors is illegal and can be prosecuted; images can't be taken back. 7) Resources — Love Is Respect (text LOVEIS to 22522 or call 1-866-331-9474), trusted adult, counselor, 911 in danger.

## Computer Science Principles: Create Performance Task: planning

**Prepare:** Read the current AP CSP Create performance task requirements and scoring guidelines (AP Central / AP Students site), since the exact format can change year to year. Print the checklist.

**Watch for:**
- Choosing a project too big to finish. Favor a simple program that clearly uses a list and a procedure.
- Using a list that isn't necessary (e.g. storing one value). The list must manage complexity — the program would be harder to write without it.
- Writing a procedure without a parameter that affects its behavior, or without sequencing, selection and iteration.

### Day 1 (Monday): Requirements
*Student:* Read the College Board Create task requirements and make a checklist: input, list, procedure with parameter, sequencing/selection/iteration, output.

*How to teach:* Read the Create task requirements together and build the checklist from them.

- **Q:** What must the student-developed procedure include?
  **A:** At least one parameter that affects its functionality, and an algorithm that includes sequencing, selection and iteration.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Brainstorm
*Student:* Brainstorm five program ideas for a real user and score each on purpose, feasibility and how naturally it uses a list.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm five program ideas for a real user; score each on purpose, feasibility and how naturally it uses a list (e.g. quiz app with a list of questions, workout planner, recipe filter).

- **Q:** Why is a 'quiz app with a list of questions' a good fit?
  **A:** The list naturally stores many questions, and a procedure can loop through it and check answers with conditionals.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Plan
*Student:* Write a program plan with the purpose, the list and what it manages, and the student-developed procedure and its algorithm in pseudocode.

*How to teach:* Write the plan: purpose, the list and what it manages, the procedure in pseudocode.

- **Q:** How would your program be different without the list?
  **A:** A strong answer: it would need many separate variables and repeated code, and couldn't easily grow — showing the list manages complexity.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Prototype
*Student:* Sketch the user interface screens and walk a potential user through them to collect feedback.

*How to teach:* Sketch UI screens and walk a user through them; record feedback.

- **Q:** What feedback is most useful at this stage?
  **A:** Where the user is confused about what to do next or what the output means.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Review the plan against the checklist with a parent and revise any missing requirement.

*How to teach:* Review the plan against the checklist in the assessment key; revise missing items.

- **Q:** Which three programming constructs must appear in the procedure's algorithm?
  **A:** Sequencing, selection (if) and iteration (loop).

**Assessment:** Plan checklist review.

**Answer key:** Plan checklist review — the plan passes when it includes: [ ] program purpose and intended user; [ ] input from the user (or other source); [ ] a list that stores data and is used to fulfill the purpose; [ ] a student-developed procedure with at least one parameter that affects its functionality; [ ] an algorithm inside the procedure that includes sequencing, selection and iteration; [ ] a call to the procedure; [ ] output based on input and program functionality; [ ] UI sketch with user feedback noted. Missing items must be revised before Week 34.
