# Teacher's Guide — 10th Grade, Week 24

## English 10: Seminal speeches of the 20th century

**Prepare:** Texts and audio of FDR's 'Four Freedoms' (1941), Churchill's 'We Shall Fight on the Beaches' (June 4, 1940), Gandhi's 'Quit India' (August 8, 1942), and Mandela's Rivonia statement 'I Am Prepared to Die' (April 20, 1964). Brief historical context for each.

**Watch for:**
- Treating every speech as the same kind of persuasion. Purpose and audience differ: Congress, Parliament, a political party, a courtroom.
- Assuming Mandela simply rejected nonviolence. He explains that peaceful protest was met with bans and violence, and the ANC chose sabotage designed to avoid loss of life.

### Day 1 (Monday): FDR
*Student:* Read and listen to FDR's Four Freedoms speech and identify his four claims and supporting reasons.

*How to teach:* Give one paragraph of context (January 1941, before the U.S. entered WWII). Listen to the key section; the student identifies the four freedoms and the reasons FDR gives. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What are the Four Freedoms?
  **A:** Freedom of speech and expression, freedom of every person to worship God in their own way, freedom from want, and freedom from fear.
- **Q:** Why did FDR give this speech in January 1941?
  **A:** To build support for aiding nations fighting the Axis (leading to Lend-Lease) while the U.S. was not yet at war.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Churchill
*Student:* Analyze Churchill's 'Beaches' speech for anaphora and tone.

*How to teach:* Explain Dunkirk (May–June 1940 evacuation). Listen to the ending; the student marks every repetition and describes the tone. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Identify the anaphora in Churchill's speech.
  **A:** 'We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields and in the streets, we shall fight in the hills; we shall never surrender.'
- **Q:** What is the tone, and how does the repetition create it?
  **A:** Defiant and resolute; the steady repetition sounds unshakable, like a drumbeat of determination.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Gandhi
*Student:* Read the 'Quit India' speech and evaluate how Gandhi frames nonviolence as strength.

*How to teach:* Explain the 1942 context (India under British rule during WWII). The student reads the excerpt and notes how Gandhi presents nonviolence as strength, not weakness. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What was the 'Quit India' demand, and what mantra did Gandhi give?
  **A:** Immediate end of British rule in India; 'Do or Die.'
- **Q:** How does Gandhi frame nonviolence as strength?
  **A:** He presents it as requiring courage and discipline — resisting without hatred or weapons takes more strength than violence.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mandela
*Student:* Read Mandela's 1964 Rivonia statement excerpt and identify his justification for armed resistance.

*How to teach:* Explain apartheid and the Rivonia Trial. The student reads the excerpt and lists Mandela's reasons for turning to armed resistance. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** What justification does Mandela give for armed resistance?
  **A:** Decades of peaceful protest were met with bans and violence (e.g., the 1960 Sharpeville massacre and the banning of the ANC), leaving no legal path; they chose sabotage designed to avoid loss of life.
- **Q:** What is Mandela's famous closing statement?
  **A:** He cherished the ideal of a democratic and free society — 'if needs be, it is an ideal for which I am prepared to die.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Synthesis chart
*Student:* Complete a chart comparing purpose, audience and key appeals across the four speeches.

*How to teach:* The student completes the comparison chart (purpose, audience, appeals, key quote) for all four speeches. Review using the assessment key. Close by asking the question(s) below; the student answers aloud and points to the exact line in the text (or their own draft) that supports the answer.

- **Q:** Which two speakers most rely on pathos, and how?
  **A:** Churchill (defiance and national pride) and Mandela (his willingness to die for freedom); FDR and Gandhi also use pathos, but each chart cell should name the specific technique.

**Assessment:** Rhetorical analysis chart.

**Answer key:** Rhetorical analysis chart (expected content): FDR — purpose: prepare Americans to support allies against Axis aggression; audience: Congress and the nation; appeals: logos (security), pathos (universal freedoms); key: freedom of speech, of worship, from want, from fear. Churchill — purpose: steel Britain after Dunkirk; audience: Parliament/nation; anaphora 'we shall fight...'; defiant, resolute tone; 'we shall never surrender.' Gandhi — purpose: demand immediate British withdrawal from India; audience: All-India Congress Committee; nonviolence framed as courage; 'Do or Die.' Mandela — purpose: explain his actions at trial; audience: court and world; ethos and logos (history of peaceful protest, Sharpeville, banning of the ANC); closing pathos: 'an ideal for which I am prepared to die.' Score 4 = all cells accurate with a quote per speech.

## Geometry: Inscribed angles and tangents

**Prepare:** GeoGebra, compass, straightedge.

**Watch for:**
- Using the full arc as the inscribed angle measure. Inscribed angle = half the intercepted arc; central angle = the arc.
- Forgetting that a tangent meets the radius at 90°, which creates right triangles for the Pythagorean theorem.

### Day 1 (Monday): Inscribed angle theorem
*Student:* Discover in GeoGebra that inscribed angles are half the intercepted arc, then prove one case.

*How to teach:* In GeoGebra, place an inscribed angle and a central angle on the same arc; drag and record. Then prove the case where one side of the inscribed angle is a diameter (isosceles triangle + exterior angle theorem).

- **Q:** An inscribed angle intercepts an arc of 140°. What is its measure?
  **A:** 70°.
- **Q:** Why is an angle inscribed in a semicircle a right angle?
  **A:** It intercepts a 180° arc, and half of 180° is 90°.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Inscribed quadrilaterals
*Student:* Prove opposite angles of an inscribed quadrilateral are supplementary.

*How to teach:* Show opposite angles of an inscribed quadrilateral intercept arcs that together make 360°, so the angles sum to 180°.

- **Q:** Quadrilateral ABCD is inscribed; m∠A = (2x)°, m∠C = (x + 30)°. Find x.
  **A:** 3x + 30 = 180 → x = 50.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tangents
*Student:* Prove a tangent is perpendicular to the radius and two tangents from a point are congruent.

*How to teach:* Prove the radius-tangent relationship (shortest distance argument) and the two-tangent theorem (HL on two right triangles). Practice problems.

- **Q:** Point P is 13 units from the center of a circle of radius 5. Find the length of a tangent from P.
  **A:** √(169 − 25) = 12.
- **Q:** Two tangent segments from P are 3x + 1 and 7. Find x.
  **A:** 3x + 1 = 7 → x = 2.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Constructions
*Student:* Construct the incircle and circumcircle of a triangle and a tangent from an outside point.

*How to teach:* Construct the incircle (angle bisectors) and circumcircle (perpendicular bisectors). For a tangent from an external point P: bisect OP to get M, draw a circle centered M through O; where it meets the circle are the tangent points.

- **Q:** Why does the tangent construction (circle on diameter OP) work?
  **A:** The tangent point T lies on a circle with diameter OP, so ∠OTP is inscribed in a semicircle and equals 90°, making PT ⊥ OT.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on inscribed angles and tangents.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and construction check in the assessment key. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** A central angle and an inscribed angle intercept the same 80° arc. Give each measure.
  **A:** Central 80°; inscribed 40°.

**Assessment:** Quiz and constructions.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Inscribed angle intercepts a 100° arc → 50°. 2) Inscribed angle 34° → arc 68°. 3) An inscribed angle intercepts a semicircle → 90°. 4) Quadrilateral inscribed in a circle, ∠A = 75°, ∠B = 110° → ∠C = 105°, ∠D = 70°. 5) External point P is 13 from center, radius 5 → tangent length 12. 6) Two tangents from P have lengths 2x + 3 and 5x − 9 → x = 4, length 11. Construction check: incircle (bisect two angles, perpendicular from incenter to a side for radius) and circumcircle (perpendicular bisectors of two sides, radius to a vertex), scored 3/2/1 as in Week 2.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Earth's energy budget

**Prepare:** Gather two identical jars, plastic wrap, a rubber band, two thermometers, a lamp with an incandescent or halogen bulb (or sunlight), and black and white paper. Bookmark NASA's Earth energy budget diagram and PhET 'Greenhouse Effect'. Make a data table.

**Watch for:**
- Students think the greenhouse effect is the ozone hole. They are different problems; ozone depletion lets in more UV, while greenhouse gases absorb outgoing infrared.
- Students think greenhouse gases trap sunlight coming in. Most incoming sunlight is visible light that passes through; greenhouse gases absorb the infrared Earth emits.
- Students think the greenhouse effect is entirely bad. Without it Earth's average temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about +15 °C; the problem is the enhanced effect from added gases.

### Day 1 (Monday): Energy budget
*Student:* Diagram Earth's energy budget from NASA data.

*How to teach:* Using NASA's diagram, the student draws Earth's energy budget: incoming sunlight (~340 W/m² average), ~30% reflected by clouds, air and surface, ~70% absorbed, and outgoing infrared, with the atmosphere re-radiating some back down. Discuss what 'balance' means.

- **Q:** What happens to Earth's temperature if more energy comes in than goes out?
  **A:** Earth warms until outgoing infrared increases enough to restore balance.
- **Q:** About what fraction of incoming sunlight is reflected?
  **A:** About 30%.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Albedo lab
*Student:* Measure temperature of thermometers under black vs. white paper in sunlight.

*How to teach:* Wrap one thermometer bulb in black paper and one in white; place both in sun for 15 minutes, reading every 3 minutes. The student graphs and explains in terms of albedo.

- **Q:** Which warmed more and why?
  **A:** Black paper — it has low albedo, absorbing more light and converting it to thermal energy.
- **Q:** Why does Arctic sea ice loss speed up warming?
  **A:** Dark ocean replaces reflective ice, lowering albedo so more sunlight is absorbed.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Greenhouse jar
*Student:* Compare temperature in a covered vs. open jar under a lamp for 30 minutes.

*How to teach:* Place thermometers in two jars under a lamp; cover one with plastic wrap. Record temperatures every 5 minutes for 30 minutes and graph. Discuss honestly what the model does and does not show.

- **Q:** Why did the covered jar get warmer?
  **A:** Mostly because the cover traps warm air (stops convection and mixing with room air); it is a model of energy trapping but not the same mechanism as CO₂ absorbing infrared.
- **Q:** How could you better model the real greenhouse effect?
  **A:** Compare two covered jars, one filled with extra CO₂ (from baking soda and vinegar), keeping everything else the same.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Molecular explanation
*Student:* Use PhET Greenhouse Effect to explain why CO₂ and H₂O absorb infrared but N₂ doesn't.

*How to teach:* In PhET Greenhouse Effect (photon absorption screen), the student shines infrared on N₂, O₂, CO₂, H₂O and CH₄ and records which absorb it and how they move. Explain using bond vibrations that change the molecule's charge distribution.

- **Q:** Why does CO₂ absorb infrared but N₂ does not?
  **A:** CO₂ can bend and stretch in ways that create a temporary dipole, letting it absorb IR; N₂ is a symmetric diatomic whose vibration produces no dipole change.
- **Q:** After a CO₂ molecule absorbs infrared, what happens to the energy?
  **A:** It is re-emitted in a random direction (some back toward Earth) or transferred to nearby molecules by collisions, warming the air.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on energy balance.

*How to teach:* Give the energy-balance quiz and grade together. Collect lab data.

- **Q:** Why is water vapor considered a feedback rather than a driver of climate change?
  **A:** Its amount depends on temperature: warmer air holds more water vapor, which amplifies warming started by other gases like CO₂.

**Assessment:** Lab data and quiz.

**Answer key:** Lab data: full credit for temperature tables every 5 minutes, graphs of both trials, and correct conclusions (black paper warmed more than white; covered jar warmed more than open). The write-up should note honestly that a sealed jar warms mainly because it blocks convection, which is different from how CO₂ works in the atmosphere. Quiz key: 1) Earth reflects about 30% of incoming sunlight (albedo ≈ 0.3). 2) Earth emits energy as infrared radiation. 3) Greenhouse gases include CO₂, H₂O, CH₄, N₂O. 4) Why N₂ and O₂ don't absorb IR — symmetric two-atom molecules; vibrating doesn't change their dipole. 5) Without greenhouse gases Earth would average about −18 °C. 6) Melting ice lowers albedo, so more sunlight is absorbed. 7) If incoming energy exceeds outgoing, Earth warms until balance returns. 8) Black surfaces have low albedo and absorb more.

## Spanish III: Subjuntivo con emociones y duda

**Prepare:** Chart WEIRDO triggers for this week: emotion (alegrarse de que, me sorprende que, temer que, es una lástima que) and doubt/denial (dudar que, no creer que, no estar seguro de que, es posible que) vs. certainty (creer que, pensar que, estar seguro de que, es cierto que + indicative). ### Day 1 (Monday): Emoción

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Contraste

*How to teach:* Your child completes a mixed 20-item indicative/subjunctive exercise and writes the trigger next to each answer.

- **Q:** Choose: 'Es posible que nosotros ___ (ir) a la playa.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Reacciones

*How to teach:* Your child reacts to 10 headlines using emotion and doubt expressions.

- **Q:** React to 'Suben los precios de la gasolina' with an emotion expression.
  **A:** Example: Es una lástima que suban los precios de la gasolina.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz.

- **Q:** Correct: 'Dudo que mi hermano está en casa.'
  **A:** Dudo que mi hermano esté en casa.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz sample key: 1) Dudo que él venga 2) Creo que él viene 3) No creo que llueva hoy 4) Es posible que tengamos examen 5) Me alegro de que estés mejor 6) Es cierto que ella sabe la respuesta 7) Temo que no haya boletos 8) Pienso que es fácil / No pienso que sea fácil 9) Es una lástima que no puedas venir 10) Estoy seguro de que ganaremos.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Understanding mental health

**Prepare:** Read KidsHealth (for teens) articles on anxiety and depression. Parental note: these weeks discuss mental illness and, next week, suicide. Check in privately with your teen about how they are doing; if you have concerns about their safety, call or text 988 or contact a mental health professional.

**Watch for:**
- Students think mental health means having or not having an illness. Mental health is a continuum; everyone moves along it (thriving, coping, struggling, in crisis).
- Students think depression is just sadness or weakness. It is a medical condition affecting mood, thinking, sleep and energy that lasts at least two weeks and is treatable.
- Students think therapy is only for severe problems. Therapy, such as cognitive behavioral therapy, helps many people with everyday stress as well as illness.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mental health continuum
*Student:* Learn the mental health continuum (thriving to struggling) and place everyday examples on it.

*How to teach:* Draw the continuum. The student places everyday examples (stressed before a test, can't sleep for weeks, feeling great after a hike) and explains the difference between normal stress and a problem that needs help.

- **Q:** When does sadness become a concern worth getting help for?
  **A:** When it lasts two weeks or more, or interferes with school, relationships, sleep, eating or daily life.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Conditions
*Student:* Research anxiety disorders and depression on KidsHealth and list symptoms, causes and treatments (therapy, medication, lifestyle).

*How to teach:* The student researches anxiety disorders and depression on KidsHealth and charts symptoms, causes and treatments.

- **Q:** Name two evidence-based treatments for depression.
  **A:** Psychotherapy such as cognitive behavioral therapy and medication such as SSRIs, often combined, plus healthy habits.
- **Q:** What can cause depression?
  **A:** A mix of genetics, brain chemistry, stressful life events, medical conditions and other factors; it is not a personal failing.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Brain & body
*Student:* Explain how sleep, exercise and social connection affect mood, using evidence from a reliable article.

*How to teach:* The student reads a reliable article and explains how sleep, exercise and social connection affect mood.

- **Q:** How does regular exercise help mood?
  **A:** It releases endorphins and other brain chemicals, reduces stress hormones, and improves sleep and self-esteem.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Stigma
*Student:* Analyze how media portray mental illness in two examples and rewrite one stigmatizing message.

*How to teach:* The student analyzes two media examples (news or film) for stigmatizing portrayals and rewrites one message using respectful, person-first language.

- **Q:** Rewrite 'He's a schizo' respectfully.
  **A:** 'He has schizophrenia' (or 'he's living with schizophrenia').

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Quiz on common conditions and treatments.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Review.

- **Q:** What's one thing you can do if a friend seems depressed?
  **A:** Listen without judgment, encourage them to talk to a trusted adult or professional, and tell an adult if you are worried about their safety.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Mental health continuum — thriving → reacting/coping → struggling → crisis/ill. 2) Signs of depression lasting 2+ weeks — persistent sadness or irritability, loss of interest, sleep or appetite changes, low energy, trouble concentrating, feelings of worthlessness, thoughts of death. 3) Anxiety disorder — excessive worry or fear that's hard to control and interferes with life (e.g., generalized anxiety, panic, social anxiety). 4) Treatments — therapy (e.g., CBT), medication (e.g., SSRIs prescribed by a doctor), lifestyle (sleep, exercise, social connection). 5) Teens need 8–10 hours of sleep. 6) Exercise reduces symptoms of depression and anxiety. 7) Stigma — negative stereotypes that discourage people from seeking help. 8) Where to get help — trusted adult, doctor, school counselor, therapist, 988.

## Computer Science Principles: Data visualization

**Prepare:** Prepare four small sample datasets (categories, values over time, two numeric variables, one numeric variable). Preview Google Sheets pivot tables (Insert → Pivot table). Pick a public CA data source (e.g. NOAA climate data or EPA AirNow/AirData).

**Watch for:**
- Using a line chart for unrelated categories. Line charts show change over time; bar charts compare categories.
- Thinking correlation proves causation. A scatter plot can show two variables move together without one causing the other.
- Unlabeled axes or truncated y-axes that exaggerate differences.

### Day 1 (Monday): Chart types
*Student:* Compare bar, line, scatter and histogram charts using four sample datasets and write which question each chart answers best.

*How to teach:* Show bar, line, scatter and histogram charts of the four datasets. Have the student match each to the question it best answers.

- **Q:** Which chart shows how a city's temperature changed over a year?
  **A:** Line chart (change over time).
- **Q:** Which chart shows whether hours studied relates to test score?
  **A:** Scatter plot (relationship between two numerical variables).
- **Q:** Which chart shows how many students fall into each range of sleep hours?
  **A:** Histogram (distribution of one numerical variable).

  **A:** Summarizes data by grouping rows into categories and calculating counts, sums or averages for each group.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check

- **Q:** Why should a data story include limitations?
  **A:** So the audience knows how far the conclusion can be trusted and doesn't overgeneralize from limited or biased data.
