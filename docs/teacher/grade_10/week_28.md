## English 10: Fahrenheit 451 Part 1: The Hearth and the Salamander

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mildred

Symbolism log: fire (destruction → later warmth), mirror (self-reflection), the Hearth and the Salamander (fireman's emblems), the hound (technology as threat).

## Geometry: Three-dimensional figures and cross sections

**Prepare:** Clay or play dough, dental floss, paper cutouts (rectangle, right triangle, semicircle), tape, pencil, calculator.

**Watch for:**
- Believing a cube's cross sections can only be squares. Slicing at angles gives rectangles, triangles, pentagons and hexagons.
- Forgetting the two bases when computing surface area of a prism or cylinder.

### Day 1 (Monday): Solids
*Student:* Classify solids and sketch nets.

*How to teach:* Sort solids (prism, pyramid, cylinder, cone, sphere) and count faces, edges, vertices. The student sketches a net for a cube, triangular prism, cylinder and square pyramid.

- **Q:** How many faces, edges and vertices does a triangular prism have?
  **A:** 5 faces, 9 edges, 6 vertices (F + V − E = 2).
- **Q:** What shapes make the net of a cylinder?
  **A:** Two circles and a rectangle.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cross sections lab
*Student:* Slice clay cubes, cylinders and cones with floss to find possible cross sections.

*How to teach:* Mold clay cube, cylinder and cone; slice with floss at different angles and sketch each cross section.

- **Q:** What cross sections can you get from a cone?
  **A:** Circle (parallel to base), triangle (through the apex vertically), ellipse, and parabola/hyperbola-shaped curves for other angles.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rotations
*Student:* Tape shapes to a pencil and spin them to identify solids of revolution.

*How to teach:* Tape paper shapes to a pencil and spin; the student predicts then names each solid of revolution.

- **Q:** What solid forms when a right triangle rotates about one leg?
  **A:** A cone.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Surface area
*Student:* Compute surface area of prisms, cylinders and pyramids from nets.

*How to teach:* Compute surface area from nets: sum the areas of all faces. Practice prisms, cylinders (2πr² + 2πrh) and pyramids (base + ½·perimeter·slant height).

- **Q:** Find the surface area of a cylinder with r = 3 and h = 10.
  **A:** 2π(9) + 2π(3)(10) = 78π ≈ 245.0.
- **Q:** Find the surface area of a 2 × 3 × 4 rectangular prism.
  **A:** 2(6 + 8 + 12) = 52.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on cross sections and surface area.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the assessment key and collect lab sketches. Allow no notes (formula sheet only if stated); about 30–50 minutes. Grade it together right away: the student reworks every missed item and explains the error aloud, and you note any skill to revisit next week.

- **Q:** Can a cube have a hexagonal cross section?
  **A:** Yes — a slice through the midpoints of six edges gives a regular hexagon.

**Assessment:** Lab sketches and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz with answers: 1) Possible cross sections of a cube → square, rectangle, triangle, pentagon, hexagon (and trapezoid/parallelogram). 2) Cylinder cross sections → circle (parallel to base), rectangle (perpendicular through axis), ellipse (slanted). 3) Rotating a rectangle about one side → cylinder; right triangle about a leg → cone; semicircle about its diameter → sphere. 4) SA of a 2 × 3 × 4 prism → 52. 5) SA of a cylinder r = 3, h = 10 → 78π ≈ 245.0. 6) SA of a square pyramid with base 6 and slant height 5 → 36 + 60 = 96. Lab sketches: full credit for labeled sketches of at least 3 cross sections per solid.

## Chemistry in the Earth System: Collision theory and reaction rates

**Prepare:** Gather Alka-Seltzer tablets (6+), clear cups, ice water, room-temperature water and hot tap water (~45–50 °C), a thermometer, a stopwatch, a spoon or mortar for crushing, 3% hydrogen peroxide and yeast. Open PhET 'Reactions & Rates'.

**Watch for:**
- Students think a catalyst is consumed. It speeds the reaction by providing a lower-activation-energy pathway and is recovered unchanged.
- Students say heat makes particles collide only more often. Higher temperature also makes a larger fraction of collisions energetic enough to exceed the activation energy — the bigger effect.
- Students think crushing a tablet changes how much gas forms. It changes the rate, not the total amount of product.

### Day 1 (Monday): Collision theory
*Student:* Use PhET Reactions & Rates to explore collisions and activation energy.

*How to teach:* In PhET Reactions & Rates, the student runs single collisions with low and high energy, then many-molecule runs at different temperatures, recording how often reactions happen. Introduce the four factors (temperature, concentration, surface area, catalyst).

- **Q:** According to collision theory, what two conditions must a collision meet to cause a reaction?
  **A:** Enough energy (at least the activation energy) and the correct orientation.
- **Q:** Why does increasing concentration speed a reaction?
  **A:** More particles per volume means more frequent collisions.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Temperature lab
*Student:* Time Alka-Seltzer dissolving in cold, room and hot water and graph results.

*How to teach:* Put equal volumes of ice water, room-temperature water and hot water in three cups, record temperatures, and time a whole tablet dissolving in each. Repeat once. The student graphs temperature vs. time.

- **Q:** Predict the effect of raising the temperature by 10 °C.
  **A:** The reaction speeds up — for many reactions roughly doubling the rate.
- **Q:** Explain the result with collision theory.
  **A:** Particles in hot water move faster, colliding more often and with more energy, so more collisions exceed the activation energy.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Surface area lab
*Student:* Compare whole vs. crushed tablets.

*How to teach:* Time a whole tablet and a crushed tablet (same mass) in equal room-temperature water. The student explains the difference and connects to real examples (flour-dust explosions, kindling vs. logs).

- **Q:** Which dissolved faster and why?
  **A:** The crushed tablet — more surface area exposes more particles to collisions with water.
- **Q:** Why do grain silos risk dust explosions?
  **A:** Fine dust has an enormous surface area, so it can burn extremely rapidly when ignited.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Catalysts
*Student:* Compare hydrogen peroxide decomposition with and without yeast (catalase).

*How to teach:* Pour peroxide into two cups; add yeast to one only. Compare bubbling. The student draws an energy diagram showing the catalyzed and uncatalyzed paths.

- **Q:** How does catalase speed the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide?
  **A:** It provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy, so more collisions succeed.
- **Q:** Is the yeast used up?
  **A:** No — the enzyme is a catalyst; it is not consumed (though it can eventually denature).

### Day 5 (Friday): Lab report
*Student:* Write a lab report explaining results with collision theory.

*How to teach:* The student writes the rate lab report. Score with the rubric and have the student revise the explanation section.

- **Q:** Why do we refrigerate food?
  **A:** Lower temperature slows the chemical reactions and bacterial growth that spoil food.

**Assessment:** Rate lab report.

**Answer key:** Lab report rubric: 4 = Question, hypothesis, controlled variables (same water volume, tablet size), data table with times for cold/room/hot and whole/crushed (with trials), graph of temperature vs. dissolving time showing time decreases as temperature rises; explanation using collision theory (faster particles → more frequent collisions and more collisions with energy ≥ activation energy; crushing increases surface area so more particles are exposed; yeast's catalase lowers activation energy); error analysis and improvement. 3 = correct data and partial collision-theory explanation. 2 = data with little explanation. 1 = incomplete.

## Modern World History: Cold War crises and Eastern Europe

**Prepare:** Nuclear stockpile data (e.g., Federation of American Scientists or Our World in Data), JFK's October 22, 1962 address (JFK Library), summaries of Khrushchev's October 26 and 27 letters, photographs of Budapest 1956 and Prague 1968, Solidarity's 21 demands (August 1980).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Cuban Missile Crisis ended with a one-sided Soviet retreat. The U.S. pledged not to invade Cuba and secretly agreed to remove missiles from Turkey.
- Assuming Eastern Europeans accepted Soviet control. Uprisings recurred in 1953, 1956, 1968 and 1980.

### Day 1 (Monday): Arms race
*Student:* Graph U.S. and Soviet nuclear stockpiles.

*How to teach:* The student graphs U.S. and Soviet stockpiles 1945–1990 and explains MAD (mutually assured destruction). Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What does 'mutually assured destruction' mean?
  **A:** Both sides had enough nuclear weapons to destroy each other, so neither could win a nuclear war — which deterred attack.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cuba
*Student:* Analyze JFK's address and the Khrushchev letters.

*How to teach:* Listen to JFK's address and read summaries of Khrushchev's letters. The student explains how the crisis was resolved. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What did JFK announce on October 22, 1962?
  **A:** Soviet missiles in Cuba and a naval 'quarantine' (blockade) of Cuba.
- **Q:** How was the crisis resolved?
  **A:** The USSR removed its missiles; the U.S. promised not to invade Cuba and secretly agreed to remove missiles from Turkey.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Uprisings
*Student:* Compare East Germany 1953, Hungary 1956 and Prague 1968.

*How to teach:* The student builds a chart comparing East Germany 1953, Hungary 1956 and Prague 1968: causes, demands, Soviet response. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What was the Prague Spring?
  **A:** Alexander Dubček's 1968 reforms ('socialism with a human face'), crushed by a Warsaw Pact invasion in August 1968.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Vietnam
*Student:* Summarize the Vietnam War as a Cold War conflict.

*How to teach:* Read a short summary of the Vietnam War. The student explains it as a Cold War conflict. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** What was the domino theory?
  **A:** The belief that if one country fell to communism, neighbors would follow.

### Day 5 (Friday): Solidarity
*Student:* Analyze Solidarity's 21 demands.

*How to teach:* Read Solidarity's 21 demands. The student explains which demands challenged communist rule. Check understanding with the question(s) below; on document days the student must name the source (author, date, purpose) in the answer.

- **Q:** Who led Solidarity?
  **A:** Lech Wałęsa, an electrician at the Gdańsk shipyard.

**Assessment:** Short constructed responses.

**Answer key:** Short constructed responses (answers): 1) Stockpiles → U.S. peaked around 31,000 warheads (1967); USSR peaked around 40,000 (mid-1980s). 2) Cuban Missile Crisis resolution → USSR removed missiles from Cuba; U.S. promised not to invade Cuba and secretly removed Jupiter missiles from Turkey. 3) Hungary 1956 → reform government under Imre Nagy crushed by Soviet tanks. 4) Prague Spring 1968 → Dubček's 'socialism with a human face' ended by Warsaw Pact invasion. 5) Vietnam as Cold War conflict → U.S. fought to contain communism (domino theory); North Vietnam supported by USSR and China; Saigon fell 1975. 6) Solidarity → independent trade union led by Lech Wałęsa; demands included free unions, the right to strike, freedom of speech, release of political prisoners. Score 2 points each for accuracy and explanation.

## Spanish III: El condicional

**Prepare:** Chart conditional endings added to the whole infinitive (-ía, -ías, -ía, -íamos, -íais, -ían) and the same irregular stems as the future (tendr-, saldr-, pondr-, vendr-, podr-, sabr-, querr-, habr-, har-, dir-). Write 10 '¿Qué harías?' scenario cards.

**Watch for:**
- Regularizing irregular verbs (*hacería, *decería, *podería): the conditional uses the future's irregular stems — haría, diría, podría.
- Translating every English 'would' as conditional: habitual past 'would' is imperfect (When I was little I would play outside = De pequeño jugaba afuera).
- Forgetting accents: all conditional forms have an accent on the í.

### Day 1 (Monday): Formación
*Student:* Learn conditional forms and irregulars.

*How to teach:* Teach formation and irregulars. Your child conjugates 15 verbs and says what they would do with a free day.

- **Q:** Conjugate tener and venir in the conditional for yo.
  **A:** tendría; vendría
- **Q:** Translate: 'We would travel to Peru.'
  **A:** Viajaríamos a Perú.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cortesía
*Student:* Use conditional for polite requests.

*How to teach:* Practice polite requests with the conditional (¿Podría...?, Me gustaría..., ¿Le importaría...?) in a restaurant and store role-play.

- **Q:** Make polite: '¡Dame agua!'
  **A:** ¿Me podrías dar agua, por favor? (formal: ¿Me podría dar agua, por favor?)

### Day 3 (Wednesday): ¿Qué harías?
*Student:* Answer 10 'What would you do?' scenarios.

*How to teach:* Your child answers 10 '¿Qué harías?' scenarios in full sentences.

- **Q:** Answer: '¿Qué harías si encontraras una billetera en la calle?'
  **A:** Example: La llevaría a la policía (Buscaría al dueño).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Invento
*Student:* Describe an invention you would create.

*How to teach:* Your child describes an invention they would create: what it would do, who would use it, how much it would cost.

- **Q:** Translate: 'My invention would clean the house by itself.'
  **A:** Mi invento limpiaría la casa solo.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the conditional quiz.

- **Q:** Correct: 'Yo hacería la tarea pero no podré.'
  **A:** Yo haría la tarea, pero no podría (or no puedo).

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz sample key: 1) hablar/yo — hablaría 2) comer/tú — comerías 3) tener/él — tendría 4) salir/nosotros — saldríamos 5) hacer/ellos — harían 6) decir/yo — diría 7) poder/ustedes — podrían 8) poner/tú — pondrías 9) 'Could you help me?' — ¿Podría ayudarme? / ¿Podrías ayudarme? 10) 'I would like a coffee' — Me gustaría (Quisiera) un café.

## Driver Education (S1) & First Aid/CPR + Health II (S2): Cannabis, opioids and fentanyl

**Prepare:** Read NIDA resources on cannabis, prescription drugs, opioids and fentanyl. Queue a naloxone (Narcan) nasal spray training video. Parental note: discuss safe storage and disposal of medications at home; consider keeping naloxone at home.

**Watch for:**
- Students think cannabis is harmless because it's legal for adults. For teens, regular use is linked to problems with memory, attention and learning, and higher risk of psychosis in some; it impairs driving.
- Students think pills from a friend or social media are pharmaceutical. Counterfeit pills often contain fentanyl, and a tiny amount (about 2 mg) can be lethal.
- Students think naloxone is dangerous to give if unsure. It's safe; it has no effect on someone who hasn't taken opioids, so give it when an overdose is suspected.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cannabis
*Student:* Learn short- and long-term effects of cannabis, including on memory, mental health and driving.

*How to teach:* The student researches short- and long-term effects of cannabis, including on driving.

- **Q:** Is it legal to drive after using cannabis in California?
  **A:** No — driving impaired by cannabis is a DUI.
- **Q:** Name one effect of regular teen cannabis use.
  **A:** Problems with memory, attention and learning; increased risk of addiction and, for some, psychosis.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Prescription misuse
*Student:* Learn risks of misusing prescription stimulants and opioids and how to dispose of medicines safely.

*How to teach:* The student learns risks of prescription misuse and safe disposal.

- **Q:** How should you dispose of unused medications?
  **A:** Take them to a pharmacy or police take-back kiosk, or use a take-back event; don't share them.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fentanyl
*Student:* Learn why counterfeit pills can contain lethal fentanyl and the signs of opioid overdose.

*How to teach:* The student learns why counterfeit pills are dangerous and the signs of opioid overdose.

- **Q:** Why can't you tell if a pill contains fentanyl by looking?
  **A:** Counterfeit pills are made to look identical to real medications; fentanyl is invisible and tasteless.
- **Q:** Name three signs of opioid overdose.
  **A:** Unresponsive, slow or stopped breathing, pinpoint pupils, blue/gray lips, gurgling sounds.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Naloxone
*Student:* Watch a naloxone (Narcan) nasal-spray training video and practice the steps; learn California pharmacies provide it without a prescription.

*How to teach:* Watch the naloxone video and practice the steps with a trainer device or by pantomime.

- **Q:** Describe the steps for giving nasal naloxone.
  **A:** Call 911, lay the person on their back, insert the nozzle in one nostril and press the plunger, give rescue breaths if trained, then place them in the recovery position; give a second dose after 2–3 minutes if no response.

### Day 5 (Friday): Quiz
*Student:* Quiz on cannabis, opioids and overdose response.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Review.

- **Q:** Will naloxone harm someone who isn't having an opioid overdose?
  **A:** No; it has no effect if no opioids are present.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Short-term cannabis effects — impaired memory, attention, coordination, reaction time; anxiety; altered judgment. 2) Long-term teen use — lower memory/learning, risk of cannabis use disorder, link to psychosis in vulnerable people. 3) Misusing prescription stimulants — heart problems, anxiety, addiction. 4) Safe disposal — pharmacy or police take-back bins or take-back days; never share prescriptions. 5) Fentanyl — synthetic opioid ~50× stronger than heroin; ~2 mg can be lethal; found in counterfeit pills. 6) Overdose signs — unresponsive, slow or stopped breathing, pinpoint pupils, blue or gray lips/fingertips, gurgling or snoring sounds, limp body. 7) Response — call 911, give naloxone (one spray in one nostril), rescue breaths/CPR if trained, recovery position, second dose in 2–3 minutes if no response, stay with the person. 8) Naloxone access — available without a prescription (OTC nasal spray; California pharmacies also furnish it). 9) California's Good Samaritan overdose law gives some protection from drug possession charges to people who seek help. 10) Fentanyl test strips can detect fentanyl but don't make drug use safe.

## Computer Science Principles: Cryptography

**Prepare:** Open the Code.org Caesar cipher, frequency analysis and Vigenère widgets (Unit 'Cybersecurity' lessons). Prepare an encrypted message for the student to crack. Watch a 'public key cryptography paint mixing' explainer (Diffie–Hellman analogy).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking encryption hides that a message exists. It hides the content; others can still see that a message was sent.
- Believing a public key must be kept secret. The public key is shared openly; only the private key is secret.
- Thinking strong encryption is unbreakable in principle. It's secure because breaking it requires solving a computationally hard problem (e.g. factoring huge numbers) that would take an unreasonable amount of time.

### Day 1 (Monday): Caesar cipher
*Student:* Encrypt a sentence with a Caesar cipher, then crack a partner's message by trying all 25 shifts with the Code.org widget.

*How to teach:* Encrypt a sentence with a shift (e.g. 3: A→D). Exchange with a partner and crack theirs using the widget by trying all 25 shifts until English appears.

- **Q:** Encrypt 'DOG' with a Caesar shift of 4.
  **A:** HSK.
- **Q:** Why is a Caesar cipher easy to crack?
  **A:** There are only 25 possible shifts, so you can try every one (brute force) in seconds.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Frequency analysis
*Student:* Use the Code.org frequency-analysis widget to crack a random-substitution cipher and explain why letter frequencies leak information.

*How to teach:* Use the frequency-analysis widget on a random-substitution cipher: match the most common cipher letters to E, T, A, O and look for short words like 'the' and 'a'.

- **Q:** A random substitution cipher has 26! possible keys. Why can it still be cracked?
  **A:** Letter frequencies stay the same after substitution, so the most common cipher letters likely stand for E, T, A, etc. — the pattern leaks information.
- **Q:** What is the most common letter in English text?
  **A:** E.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Vigenère cipher
*Student:* Encrypt with a Vigenère cipher using a keyword and explain why a longer key is harder to crack.

*How to teach:* Teach Vigenère: write the keyword repeated over the message; shift each letter by the key letter's value (A = 0, B = 1 …). Encrypt with a keyword and discuss why longer keys are harder.

- **Q:** Encrypt 'CAT' with key 'BAD' (B=1, A=0, D=3).
  **A:** C+1 = D, A+0 = A, T+3 = W → 'DAW'.
- **Q:** Why is a longer Vigenère key harder to crack?
  **A:** The pattern repeats less often, so frequency analysis has less repeated structure to work with; a random key as long as the message (a one-time pad) is unbreakable.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Public key
*Student:* Model public key exchange with the paint-mixing analogy, then explain how HTTPS uses public keys when you load a website.

*How to teach:* Model public-key exchange with paint mixing: both share a public color, each adds a secret color, they exchange mixes, then each adds their secret color again to get the same final color an eavesdropper can't un-mix. Connect to HTTPS (padlock icon).

- **Q:** In public-key encryption, which key do you share and which do you keep?
  **A:** Share the public key; keep the private key secret. Messages encrypted with your public key can only be decrypted with your private key.
- **Q:** What does the padlock / HTTPS mean when you load a site?
