## English 11 – American Literature: The Harlem Renaissance

**Prepare:** Print Hughes ('The Negro Speaks of Rivers,' 'Harlem,' 'I, Too,' 'The Weary Blues'), McKay ('If We Must Die'), Cullen ('Incident'), and Hurston ('How It Feels to Be Colored Me'). Have a classic blues recording ready (e.g., Bessie Smith). Parent note: 'Incident' contains a racial slur; discuss before reading.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking traditional forms can't carry protest. McKay's Shakespearean sonnet uses a formal, dignified structure to call for resistance after the violence of 1919's Red Summer.
- Reading Hurston's essay as bitter or angry. Her tone is confident and playful: 'I am not tragically colored.'

## Algebra II: Properties of logarithms

**Prepare:** Practice set. Have the student review exponent rules (b^m·b^n = b^(m+n), etc.).

**Watch for:**
- Writing log(M/N) = log M / log N. The quotient rule gives a difference: log M − log N.
- Applying the power rule to (log x)² as 2 log x. The power must be on the argument: log(x²) = 2 log x.

### Day 1 (Monday): Product & quotient
*Student:* Derive the product and quotient properties from exponent rules.

*How to teach:* Let M = b^m, N = b^n. Then MN = b^(m+n), so log_b(MN) = m + n = log_b M + log_b N. Do the same for the quotient. Today's task from the plan: Derive the product and quotient properties from exponent rules. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Use the product rule to evaluate log 4 + log 25.
  **A:** log 100 = 2.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Power property
*Student:* Derive and apply the power property.

*How to teach:* Derive log_b(M^p) = p·log_b M from (b^m)^p = b^(mp). Today's task from the plan: Derive and apply the power property. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Simplify log₂(8³).
  **A:** 3·log₂8 = 3·3 = 9.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Expand & condense
*Student:* Expand and condense log expressions (10 items).

*How to teach:* Model expanding and condensing; student completes 10 items. Today's task from the plan: Expand and condense log expressions (10 items). Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Expand log(x³y/z²).
  **A:** 3 log x + log y − 2 log z.
- **Q:** Condense 2 ln x − ln y + ½ ln z.
  **A:** ln(x²√z / y).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Estimating
*Student:* Estimate values like log 50 using known logs and properties.

*How to teach:* Given log 2 ≈ 0.301 and log 3 ≈ 0.477, estimate other logs using properties. Today's task from the plan: Estimate values like log 50 using known logs and properties. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Estimate log 50 and log 6.
  **A:** log 50 = log 100 − log 2 ≈ 2 − 0.301 = 1.699. log 6 = log 2 + log 3 ≈ 0.778.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz with a written proof of one property.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz with one written proof (about 35 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Quiz with a written proof of one property. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Condense log₃ 18 − log₃ 2.
  **A:** log₃ 9 = 2.

**Assessment:** Properties quiz with proof.

**Answer key:** Sample key: 1) log₂(4·8) = 5. 2) Expand ln(a²b) = 2 ln a + ln b. 3) Expand log(√x/y) = ½log x − log y. 4) Condense 3 log 2 + log 5 = log 40. 5) log 18 ≈ 0.301 + 2(0.477) = 1.255. Proof rubric: 4 = defines M=b^m, N=b^n, applies exponent rule, converts back correctly; 3 = small notation slip; 2 = idea present but steps missing; 1 = only numerical example.

## Physics of the Universe: Wave-particle duality

**Prepare:** Laser pointer (class 2, adult supervision; never point at eyes), a strand of hair, a CD, tape, a white wall; PhET Photoelectric Effect.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking light must be either a wave or a particle. Light shows both behaviors depending on the experiment.
- Thinking brighter light ejects electrons in the photoelectric effect. Only light above a threshold frequency ejects electrons, regardless of intensity.

### Day 1 (Monday): Diffraction
*Student:* Shine a laser past a hair and through a CD to observe diffraction patterns.

*How to teach:* Tape a hair across a card hole and shine the laser past it onto a wall; also reflect the laser off a CD. Today's task from the plan: Shine a laser past a hair and through a CD to observe diffraction patterns. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What does the pattern of bright and dark spots show?
  **A:** Diffraction and interference: light waves bend around the hair and overlap, adding in some places and canceling in others—wave behavior.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Double-slit
*Student:* Study Young's double-slit experiment as evidence for the wave model.

*How to teach:* Study Young's double-slit experiment (diagram or video). Today's task from the plan: Study Young's double-slit experiment as evidence for the wave model. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** Why does the double-slit pattern support the wave model?
  **A:** Only waves produce alternating bright and dark fringes through constructive and destructive interference.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Photoelectric effect
*Student:* Use PhET Photoelectric Effect to gather evidence for the particle model.

*How to teach:* In PhET, vary the light's frequency and intensity and note electron emission. Today's task from the plan: Use PhET Photoelectric Effect to gather evidence for the particle model. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What happens if dim UV light hits the metal vs bright red light?
  **A:** Dim UV ejects electrons (above threshold frequency); bright red ejects none (photon energy too low).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Argument
*Student:* Write a CER on why both models are needed.

*How to teach:* The student writes a CER: why both models are needed. Today's task from the plan: Write a CER on why both models are needed. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What did Einstein propose to explain the photoelectric effect?
  **A:** Light comes in packets (photons) with energy E = hf; one photon must have enough energy to free one electron.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on wave-particle duality.

*How to teach:* Wave-particle quiz. Today's task from the plan: Quiz on wave-particle duality. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Give one piece of evidence for each model of light.
  **A:** Wave: interference/diffraction patterns. Particle: photoelectric effect.

**Assessment:** CER and quiz.

**Answer key:** CER rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning). Claim: answers the question about why light needs both wave and particle models in one sentence. Evidence: specific data from the student's own lab/simulation with numbers and units. Reasoning: connects the evidence to the scientific principle. A proficient response: the diffraction pattern (wave evidence) and the PhET result that only frequencies above a threshold eject electrons regardless of intensity (particle evidence), with reasoning that neither model alone explains both. Quiz key: photon energy E = hf; higher frequency → more energy; interference → wave; photoelectric → particle; electrons also show diffraction (wave-particle duality applies to matter).

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Postwar society and the suburban boom

**Prepare:** Print the GI Bill (Servicemen's Readjustment Act, 1944) summary, Levittown advertisements and a restrictive covenant example, an HOLC redlining map of a California city (Mapping Inequality site), California population data (1940: 6.9 million; 1950: 10.6 million; 1960: 15.7 million; 1970: 20.0 million), and a summary of the 1960 California Master Plan for Higher Education.

**Watch for:**
- Students think the GI Bill benefited all veterans equally. Black veterans often were denied loans and admission because of segregation, discrimination and redlining.
- Students think covenants were illegal from the start. They were legal until Shelley v. Kraemer (1948) made them unenforceable; the Fair Housing Act (1968) banned housing discrimination.
- Students think the Master Plan created the UC system. UC existed since 1868; the Master Plan defined roles for UC, CSU and community colleges.

### Day 1 (Monday): GI Bill & suburbs
*Student:* Analyze the GI Bill and Levittown ads; examine who was excluded through redlining and covenants.

*How to teach:* Analyze the GI Bill and Levittown ads; examine exclusion through redlining and covenants.

- **Q:** How did redlining shape suburbs?
  **A:** Federal mortgage agencies rated minority neighborhoods as risky, so residents couldn't get loans, while new suburbs restricted sales to white buyers.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): California boom
*Student:* Graph California's population 1940–1970 and study aerospace, defense spending, and freeways.

*How to teach:* Graph California's population 1940–1970; study aerospace, defense spending and freeways.

- **Q:** Calculate California's percent population growth from 1940 to 1970.
  **A:** (20.0 − 6.9) ÷ 6.9 × 100 ≈ 190%.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Master Plan
*Student:* Read a summary of the 1960 California Master Plan for Higher Education and explain the UC/CSU/community college tiers.

*How to teach:* Read the Master Plan summary and explain the three tiers.

- **Q:** What share of high school graduates was UC to admit under the Master Plan?
  **A:** The top one-eighth (12.5%).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Popular culture
*Student:* Analyze rock and roll's origins, television, and the diffusion of California car and surf culture.

*How to teach:* Analyze rock and roll's origins, television and California car and surf culture.

- **Q:** Name a California band that spread surf culture nationally.
  **A:** The Beach Boys (from Hawthorne).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on postwar society.

*How to teach:* Give the postwar society quiz.

- **Q:** What did Shelley v. Kraemer (1948) decide?
  **A:** Courts could not enforce racially restrictive covenants.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) GI Bill — college tuition, low-interest home and business loans, unemployment pay for veterans. 2) Levittown (1947) — mass-produced suburb that barred Black buyers. 3) Redlining — HOLC/FHA maps rating minority neighborhoods hazardous, denying loans. 4) Baby boom — high birth rates 1946–1964. 5) Interstate Highway Act (1956). 6) California population grew from about 6.9 million (1940) to about 20 million (1970). 7) Aerospace/defense growth in Southern California. 8) Master Plan tiers — UC: top 12.5% of high school graduates, research, doctoral degrees; CSU: top one-third, bachelor's and master's; community colleges: open to all, transfer and vocational. 9) Rock and roll grew from rhythm and blues, gospel and country. 10) Television ownership soared in the 1950s.

**Watch for:**
- Retelling the plot. A critique evaluates choices with evidence.
- Only praising or only criticizing. Balance strengths and weaknesses.
- Vague vocabulary ('the lighting was good'). Use specific terms: 'warm, low-angle side light isolated the actor…'

*How to teach:* Read two reviews; identify structure (hook, context, evaluation of acting/design/direction, verdict) and criteria.

*How to teach:* Attend/watch; take notes on acting, design, direction with specific moments.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft

*How to teach:* Draft a 700-word critique evaluating three elements with evidence.

## Lifetime Fitness: Cycling and active transportation

**Prepare:** Check the bicycle (tires, brakes, chain) and helmet fit. Identify a low-traffic area or bike path for skills practice. Look up local bike lanes and a route to a frequent destination. Parent should ride along or supervise traffic practice.

**Watch for:**
- Riding against traffic. Cyclists ride in the same direction as traffic, as far right as practicable (California Vehicle Code).
- Wearing a helmet tilted back. It should sit level, about two finger-widths above the eyebrows.
- Assuming drivers see you. Make eye contact, use lights, and signal clearly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Bike safety
*Student:* Perform an ABC quick check (air, brakes, chain) and fit a helmet properly.

*How to teach:* ABC quick check: Air (squeeze tires/check PSI on sidewall), Brakes (squeeze, wheels lock), Chain/cranks (clean, spins smoothly); also quick releases. Helmet fit: level, 2 fingers above eyebrows, straps form a V under each ear, 1–2 fingers under chin strap.

- **Q:** What does ABC stand for in the quick check?
  **A:** Air, Brakes, Chain (cranks), followed by Quick releases and a Check ride.
- **Q:** Describe correct helmet fit.
  **A:** Level on the head, about two finger-widths above the eyebrows, side straps form a V just below each ear, chin strap snug with room for about one finger.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Traffic skills
*Student:* Practice signaling, scanning, and riding in bike lanes in a safe area.

*How to teach:* In a safe area, practice hand signals, looking over the shoulder without swerving, and straight-line riding as if in a bike lane.

- **Q:** Show the hand signals for left turn, right turn, and stop.
  **A:** Left: left arm straight out. Right: left arm bent up at 90° (or right arm straight out). Stop/slow: left arm bent down.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Endurance ride
*Student:* Complete a 45–60 minute ride at moderate intensity.

*How to teach:* Ride 45–60 minutes at moderate intensity (RPE 5–6); record distance and HR.

- **Q:** What cadence (pedal rpm) is efficient for most riders?
  **A:** About 70–90 rpm; use gears to keep cadence steady.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Active commute
*Student:* Map an active-transportation route to a frequent destination and ride or walk it.

*How to teach:* Map a route to a common destination using bike lanes/quiet streets; ride or walk it with a parent.

- **Q:** Why choose quiet streets or bike lanes for active commuting?
  **A:** Lower traffic speed and volume reduce crash risk.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a plan to replace two car trips per week with active transportation.

*How to teach:* Student writes a plan to replace two car trips weekly: routes, times, gear, safety, and weather plan.

- **Q:** Name two benefits of active transportation.
  **A:** Adds daily physical activity; reduces emissions and traffic; saves money; improves mood.

**Assessment:** Active commute plan.

**Answer key:** Active commute plan rubric. 4 – Two specific trips with mapped routes, distances and times, safety plan (helmet, lights, route choice, signals), gear and weather backup, and estimated weekly activity minutes added. 3 (proficient) – Two trips with routes, safety, and timing. 2 – Vague trips or missing safety. 1 – Incomplete.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Testing and iteration

**Prepare:** Gather the phone-stand prototype, a protractor, a kitchen scale, 300 g of weights (coins, a bag of rice) and a board you can tilt 10° (measure with the protractor). Prepare the Unit 6 test.

**Watch for:**
- Vague success criteria ('it works'). Each criterion needs a measurable target and tolerance (holds 300 g, 60° ± 5°).
- Changing several things at once in a redesign. Change the feature tied to the failure mode, then retest, so you know what fixed it.
- One trial only. Run at least five trials to account for variation.

### Day 1 (Monday): Test plan
*Student:* Write a test plan for the stand with measurable success criteria (holds 300 g, angle 60°±5°, no tipping at a 10° table tilt).

*How to teach:* Write a test plan with criteria: holds 300 g, viewing angle 60° ± 5°, doesn't tip on a 10° tilt. Specify procedure, equipment, trials and data table.

- **Q:** What is the independent variable in a load test?
  **A:** The load (mass) added — the thing you deliberately change.
- **Q:** Write a measurable criterion for stability.
  **A:** The stand with device does not tip when placed on a surface tilted 10° (5 out of 5 trials).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Testing
*Student:* Run five trials of each test and record results in a data table.

*How to teach:* Run five trials of each test and record results.

- **Q:** Angles measured: 58°, 61°, 59°, 62°, 60°. Does it meet 60° ± 5°?
  **A:** Yes — all are between 55° and 65°; mean = 60°.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Analysis
*Student:* Calculate mean and standard deviation for each test and identify the most likely failure mode.

*How to teach:* Calculate mean and SD for each test and identify the most likely failure mode (e.g. tipping backward, base flexing, joint cracking).

- **Q:** Find the mean and sample SD of 58, 61, 59, 62, 60.
  **A:** Mean 60; squared deviations 4, 1, 1, 4, 0 = 10; 10/4 = 2.5; SD ≈ 1.58°.
- **Q:** What is a failure mode?
  **A:** The specific way a design fails, e.g. tipping, cracking at a joint, or bending.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Iterate
*Student:* Modify the design to address the failure mode, rebuild, and retest.

*How to teach:* Modify the design to address the failure mode, rebuild and retest.

- **Q:** The stand tips backward with a tablet. Give two design fixes.
  **A:** Extend or widen the base behind the support, lower the center of gravity (heavier base), or reduce the angle.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 6 test on materials, manufacturing processes, and prototyping.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test in the key.

- **Q:** When would you choose CNC machining over 3D printing?
  **A:** When you need a strong metal part, tight tolerances or a smooth finish in small quantities.

**Assessment:** Test report and Unit 6 test.

**Answer key:** Test report rubric: 4 = test plan with measurable criteria, five trials per test in a table, mean and standard deviation, pass/fail for each criterion, failure mode identified with evidence, redesign tied to the failure mode and retest data; 3 = missing SD or weak link to failure; 2 = few trials or no retest; 1 = incomplete. Unit 6 test answers: 1) Material families. 2) Stiffness vs. strength. 3) Ductile vs. brittle. 4) Tensile strength definition. 5) Additive vs. subtractive. 6) Injection molding for high volume. 7) Draft angle purpose. 8) Low- vs. high-fidelity prototypes. 9) Independent variable = what you change on purpose. 10) Failure mode = the way a product fails. Mastery 8+/10.
