# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 4

## English 11 – American Literature: The Crucible, Acts III–IV and literary argument

**Prepare:** Finish The Crucible. Find the courtroom scene of the 1996 film (Daniel Day-Lewis, Winona Ryder). Print a literary argument rubric and a counterclaim template.

**Watch for:**
- Calling Proctor a tragic hero without defining the term. Use Aristotle's features: a person of stature, a tragic flaw, a reversal and recognition, and a downfall that brings insight.
- Ignoring the counterclaim or 'straw-manning' it. A strong counterclaim is a real objection (e.g., Proctor's lust and delay helped cause the tragedy) answered with evidence.

### Day 1 (Monday): Act III
*Student:* Read the courtroom act; trace how logic breaks down and cite the line where Hale turns.

*How to teach:* Read Act III. The student tracks how logic breaks down (Danforth: a witness is either for the court or against it) and finds where Hale turns. Today's task from the plan: Read the courtroom act; trace how logic breaks down and cite the line where Hale turns. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Where does Hale turn against the court?
  **A:** After Elizabeth denies the affair and Mary Warren accuses Proctor, Hale cries, 'I denounce these proceedings, I quit this court!'
- **Q:** Why does Elizabeth's lie in Act III backfire?
  **A:** Proctor had confessed the affair to discredit Abigail; Elizabeth, never known to lie, denies it to protect his name, which makes Proctor look like a liar.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Act IV
*Student:* Read Act IV; discuss why Proctor refuses to sign and what 'my name' means; draft a thesis on integrity.

*How to teach:* Read Act IV. Discuss why Proctor tears up his confession. The student drafts a one-sentence thesis on integrity. Today's task from the plan: Read Act IV; discuss why Proctor refuses to sign and what 'my name' means; draft a thesis on integrity. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why does Proctor refuse to let his signed confession be posted?
  **A:** 'Because it is my name! Because I cannot have another in my life!' A public lie would destroy his integrity and betray those who died honestly.
- **Q:** What does Elizabeth mean by 'He have his goodness now'?
  **A:** By choosing death over a lie, Proctor has finally regained his self-respect and goodness, which she will not take from him.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Two interpretations
*Student:* Watch the courtroom scene from the 1996 film and compare to the script; note choices about Abigail's power.

*How to teach:* Watch the film's courtroom scene with the script open. The student notes two staging choices about Abigail's power (camera, the girls' mimicry, her stare) and whether they match Miller's directions.

- **Q:** How does the 'yellow bird' scene show Abigail's power?
  **A:** Abigail claims to see Mary Warren's spirit as a bird; the other girls copy everything Mary says, so the court sees 'proof' and Mary breaks, showing Abigail controls the room.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Essay drafting
*Student:* Write a 700-word literary argument: Is John Proctor a tragic hero? Include a counterclaim; edit for subject-verb agreement.

*How to teach:* Review the rubric and a model counterclaim paragraph. The student writes the 700-word essay, then edits for subject-verb agreement (e.g., 'The girls, led by Abigail, are...').

- **Q:** Correct: 'Each of the accused girls were afraid.'
  **A:** 'Each of the accused girls was afraid.' (The subject 'each' is singular.)

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Revise essay with a checklist and take the Unit 1 test (passages from Edwards and Miller).

*How to teach:* The student revises with the checklist (thesis, counterclaim, citations), then takes the Unit 1 test. Today's task from the plan: Revise essay with a checklist and take the Unit 1 test (passages from Edwards and Miller). Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What is a tragic hero?
  **A:** A person of stature with a tragic flaw whose choices lead to downfall, often with recognition (insight) before the end.

**Assessment:** Literary argument essay (rubric) and Unit 1 test.

**Answer key:** Literary argument rubric (4 = exceeds, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning) on four rows: Claim/Thesis (arguable, specific, answers the prompt); Evidence (well-chosen quotations, introduced and cited); Analysis/Reasoning (explains HOW evidence supports the claim, not just what it says); Conventions & organization (logical paragraphs, transitions, few errors). A proficient (3) paper has a clear yes/no thesis about Proctor as tragic hero, uses at least three quotations (e.g., his confession in Act III, 'Because it is my name!' in Act IV), explains his flaw (pride/lust and his delay) and recognition, and answers a genuine counterclaim. Unit 1 test key: Edwards' purpose = frighten listeners into conversion; spider image = pathos/helplessness; providence = God's guidance; Miller's allegory = McCarthyism; dramatic irony = audience knows Abigail is lying; Hale's change = from confident expert to opponent of the court.

## Algebra II: Quadratic functions in three forms

**Prepare:** Desmos; draw a few square-and-rectangle area models (or use algebra tiles). Find a short video of a ball being thrown for Day 4.

**Watch for:**
- Reading the vertex of y = (x − 3)² + 2 as (−3, 2). The h value is the number that makes the inside zero: x = 3.
- When completing the square with a ≠ 1, forgetting to multiply the added constant by a. Factor a out first and track it: 2(x² − 4x + 4) adds 8, not 4.

### Day 1 (Monday): Vertex form
*Student:* Graph y = a(x−h)²+k using transformations; identify vertex, axis, and max/min.

*How to teach:* Model y = −2(x − 1)² + 5 as transformations of x². Student graphs four vertex-form equations by hand and labels vertex, axis, and max/min. Today's task from the plan: Graph y = a(x−h)²+k using transformations; identify vertex, axis, and max/min. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** State the vertex, axis of symmetry, and max/min of y = −2(x − 1)² + 5.
  **A:** Vertex (1, 5); axis x = 1; opens down, so maximum value 5.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Factored form
*Student:* Graph from zeros; find vertex via the midpoint of the zeros.

*How to teach:* Show that zeros come straight from factored form and the vertex x is their midpoint. Student graphs three factored-form quadratics. Today's task from the plan: Graph from zeros; find vertex via the midpoint of the zeros. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Find the zeros and vertex of y = (x − 2)(x + 6).
  **A:** Zeros 2 and −6; axis x = (2 + (−6))/2 = −2; y = (−4)(4) = −16; vertex (−2, −16).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Completing the square
*Student:* Use area models to complete the square and convert standard form to vertex form.

*How to teach:* Draw x² + 6x as a square plus two 3-by-x strips; the missing corner is 9. Model converting x² + 6x + 5, then 2x² − 8x + 3. Student converts five more.

- **Q:** Write x² + 6x + 5 in vertex form.
  **A:** (x + 3)² − 4, vertex (−3, −4).
- **Q:** Write 2x² − 8x + 3 in vertex form.
  **A:** 2(x² − 4x + 4) + 3 − 8 = 2(x − 2)² − 5.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing forms
*Student:* For one context (a launched ball), use each form to answer a different question.

*How to teach:* Use h = −16t² + 32t + 48 (feet, seconds). Student writes it in factored and vertex form and says which form answers which question. Today's task from the plan: For one context (a launched ball), use each form to answer a different question. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** When does the ball land, what is its maximum height, and from what height was it launched?
  **A:** Factored: −16(t − 3)(t + 1), lands at t = 3 s. Vertex: −16(t − 1)² + 64, max 64 ft at 1 s. Standard: launched from 48 ft (the constant).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on converting forms and graphing key features.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (about 30 minutes). Afterwards, check graphs in Desmos. Today's task from the plan: Quiz on converting forms and graphing key features. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Write y = x² − 4x − 12 in vertex and factored form.
  **A:** Vertex form (x − 2)² − 16; factored (x − 6)(x + 2).

**Assessment:** Quiz on quadratic forms.

**Answer key:** Sample key: 1) y=(x+1)²−9 → standard x²+2x−8, factored (x+4)(x−2). 2) y=x²−10x+21 → (x−5)²−4; zeros 3, 7. 3) y=3x²+12x+7 → 3(x+2)²−5. 4) y=−(x−4)(x+2): zeros 4, −2, vertex (1, 9). 5) Graph y=½(x−2)²−2: vertex (2,−2), zeros 0 and 4. 6) Which form shows the y-intercept? Standard form (c). Mastery = 5 of 6 with work shown.

## Physics of the Universe: Momentum and impulse

**Prepare:** Two toy cars of known mass (weigh on a kitchen scale), modeling clay, ruler, marbles; PhET Collision Lab. Eggs in zip-top bags, a towel, pillow, and hard floor protected with newspaper.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking momentum is 'lost' in an inelastic collision. Total momentum is conserved; kinetic energy is what is partly converted to heat and sound.
- Forgetting momentum is a vector. Objects moving in opposite directions have opposite signs.

### Day 1 (Monday): Momentum
*Student:* Calculate momentum for everyday objects; compare a truck and a baseball.

*How to teach:* Model p = mv with units kg·m/s. The student calculates momentum for a truck, a baseball, a person walking. Today's task from the plan: Calculate momentum for everyday objects; compare a truck and a baseball. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Compare a 2,000 kg truck at 10 m/s to a 0.145 kg baseball at 40 m/s.
  **A:** Truck: 20,000 kg·m/s. Baseball: 5.8 kg·m/s. The truck has about 3,400 times more momentum.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Collision lab
*Student:* Collide toy cars (with clay for inelastic) and measure velocities from video to test momentum conservation.

*How to teach:* Film one car hitting a stationary car (with clay to make them stick). Measure speeds before and after and compare total momentum. Today's task from the plan: Collide toy cars (with clay for inelastic) and measure velocities from video to test momentum conservation. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** A 2.0 kg cart at 3.0 m/s sticks to a 1.0 kg cart at rest. Find their speed after.
  **A:** Total p = 6.0 kg·m/s = 3.0 kg × v → v = 2.0 m/s.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Simulation
*Student:* Use PhET Collision Lab to verify momentum conservation in elastic and inelastic collisions.

*How to teach:* In PhET, run elastic and inelastic collisions; record total momentum and kinetic energy before and after. Today's task from the plan: Use PhET Collision Lab to verify momentum conservation in elastic and inelastic collisions. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** In a perfectly elastic collision, what is conserved?
  **A:** Both total momentum and total kinetic energy.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Impulse
*Student:* Drop an egg onto different surfaces (in a bag) and relate impulse = FΔt.

*How to teach:* Drop a bagged egg from 1 m onto floor, towel, and pillow. Discuss impulse J = FΔt = Δp. Today's task from the plan: Drop an egg onto different surfaces (in a bag) and relate impulse = FΔt. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why does the egg survive on the pillow?
  **A:** The egg's change in momentum is the same, but the pillow increases the stopping time, so the average force (Δp/Δt) is much smaller.
- **Q:** An egg (0.060 kg) hits at 4.4 m/s and stops in 0.010 s. Average force?
  **A:** Δp = 0.060 × 4.4 ≈ 0.26 kg·m/s; F = 0.26/0.010 ≈ 26 N (stopping in 0.10 s gives only 2.6 N).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Solve conservation of momentum problems in a quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the momentum quiz (30 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Solve conservation of momentum problems in a quiz. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** A 60 kg skater at rest pushes a 40 kg skater who moves off at 3 m/s. Velocity of the first skater?
  **A:** 0 = 40(3) + 60v → v = −2 m/s (2 m/s in the opposite direction).

**Assessment:** Momentum quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key (sample): 1) p of 0.5 kg ball at 8 m/s = 4 kg·m/s. 2) 1,000 kg car at 20 m/s hits and sticks to 1,500 kg car at rest → 8 m/s. 3) impulse to stop a 0.15 kg ball at 30 m/s = 4.5 N·s. 4) airbags increase stopping time, reducing force. 5) 3 kg cart at 4 m/s and 1 kg cart at −2 m/s stick → v = (12 − 2)/4 = 2.5 m/s. 6) gun recoil explained by conservation of momentum.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: The rise of industry and big business

**Prepare:** Print a map of railroads and natural resources c. 1890; Carnegie's 'The Gospel of Wealth' (1889) excerpt; William Graham Sumner and Walter Rauschenbusch excerpts; three trust cartoons from the Library of Congress (e.g., 'The Bosses of the Senate', 1889; Standard Oil octopus, 'Next!', 1904).

**Watch for:**
- Students think vertical and horizontal integration are the same. Vertical controls every stage of production (Carnegie: mines, railroads, mills); horizontal buys up competitors at the same stage (Rockefeller: refineries).
- Students see industrialists as all heroes or all villains. Use evidence for both innovation and philanthropy and for harsh labor practices and monopoly.
- Students think Social Darwinism is Darwin's idea. It was a misapplication of 'survival of the fittest' (Herbert Spencer's phrase) to society to justify inequality.

### Day 1 (Monday): Industrial growth
*Student:* Map railroads and resources; explain geographic advantages that fueled U.S. industry.

*How to teach:* Map railroads and resources; the student explains geographic advantages (coal, iron, oil, timber, rivers, transcontinental railroad 1869, immigrant labor).

- **Q:** Give two geographic advantages that fueled U.S. industry.
  **A:** Abundant natural resources (coal, iron ore, oil) and a large connected market linked by railroads and waterways.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Big business
*Student:* Compare Carnegie's vertical and Rockefeller's horizontal integration with diagrams.

*How to teach:* Diagram Carnegie's vertical and Rockefeller's horizontal integration.

- **Q:** Which strategy did Rockefeller use, and how?
  **A:** Horizontal integration — buying or driving out competing refineries until Standard Oil controlled about 90% of U.S. refining by 1880.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Gospel of Wealth
*Student:* Read Carnegie's essay and debate 'captains of industry or robber barons'.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Gospel of Wealth' and debate captains of industry vs. robber barons.

- **Q:** What did Carnegie argue the rich should do with their wealth?
  **A:** Use it during their lifetimes for the public good (e.g., libraries, universities) rather than leave it to heirs.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Social Darwinism vs. Social Gospel
*Student:* Compare Sumner and Rauschenbusch excerpts.

*How to teach:* Compare Sumner (Social Darwinism) and Rauschenbusch (Social Gospel).

- **Q:** How did the Social Gospel respond to industrial poverty?
  **A:** It argued Christians should apply ethics to society, reforming conditions like child labor and slums.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Analyze three trust cartoons in writing.

*How to teach:* The student analyzes three trust cartoons in writing.

- **Q:** What federal law in 1890 targeted trusts?
  **A:** The Sherman Antitrust Act.

**Assessment:** Cartoon analysis.

**Answer key:** Cartoon analysis rubric: for each of three cartoons — identifies symbols and labels, caption, artist/date, the message (e.g., 'The Bosses of the Senate' shows huge money-bag trusts looming over senators, arguing that monopolies control government), the point of view, and the historical context (Sherman Antitrust Act 1890, trust-busting). 4 = all elements with accurate context for all three.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Long-form improvisation and storytelling

**Prepare:** Print the story spine template: Once upon a time… Every day… But one day… Because of that… Because of that… Until finally… And ever since then… Find a short professional improv clip (e.g., a long-form set on a reputable channel); preview for content. Recruit 2–3 players for Day 4–5.

**Watch for:**
- Introducing new ideas instead of reusing earlier ones. Long-form rewards callbacks and connections.
- Skipping the 'until finally' climax. Stories need a turning point and resolution.
- Thinking long-form is just many short scenes. Scenes connect through themes, characters, and callbacks.

### Day 1 (Monday): Story spine
*Student:* Learn the story spine ('Once upon a time... until finally') and improvise five stories.

*How to teach:* Teach the story spine and improvise five stories from random titles; check each has a 'But one day' and 'Until finally.'

- **Q:** Recite the story spine.
  **A:** Once upon a time… Every day… But one day… Because of that… Because of that… Until finally… And ever since then…

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Callbacks
*Student:* Practice linking scenes with recurring characters and callbacks.

*How to teach:* Play scenes where a character or line from an earlier scene returns later (callback). Practice linking three scenes.

- **Q:** What is a callback?
  **A:** Bringing back a character, line, or idea from earlier in the performance, often for comic or thematic payoff.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Watch & respond
*Student:* Watch a professional improv clip and write a response identifying offers and callbacks.

*How to teach:* Watch the clip; student writes a response identifying three offers and one callback.

- **Q:** What is a 'Harold'?

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rehearse
*Student:* Rehearse a 10-minute structured long-form piece with family or friends.

*How to teach:* Rehearse a 10-minute structured piece: opening, three scene beats, a group game, and a returning beat. Agree on the structure, not the lines.

- **Q:** What is a 'beat' in improv or acting?

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check

*How to teach:* Perform the long-form piece and write a Unit 1 growth reflection.

**Assessment:** Long-form performance.

**Answer key:** Long-form performance rubric. 4 – Clear opening, scenes connected by theme and at least two callbacks, strong 'yes, and' support, satisfying ending, ~10 minutes; reflection identifies growth with examples. 3 (proficient) – Connected scenes with at least one callback and an ending; reflection. 2 – Disconnected scenes. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness: Heart rate and intensity

**Prepare:** Have a stopwatch or heart-rate app and a jump rope. Prepare a worked Karvonen example using the student's own age and resting heart rate from week 1. Have graph paper or a spreadsheet ready for Day 4.

**Watch for:**
- Using 220 − age as an exact number. It is only an estimate (±10–12 bpm); RPE and the talk test help cross-check.
- In the Karvonen formula, multiplying the percentage by maximum HR instead of heart rate reserve. Correct: THR = (HRmax − HRrest) × % + HRrest.
- Counting the first beat as 'one' when starting a 15-second count. Start counting at zero on the first beat (or just be consistent) and multiply by 4.

### Day 1 (Monday): Target zones
*Student:* Calculate maximum heart rate and target zones using the Karvonen formula.

*How to teach:* Walk through Karvonen: HRmax = 220 − age; HRR = HRmax − resting HR; THR = HRR × intensity% + resting HR. Work the example with the student's numbers, then they compute 50%, 60%, 70%, 85%. Check arithmetic.

- **Q:** A 16-year-old has resting HR 70. Find her 60% and 70% target heart rates using Karvonen.
  **A:** HRmax = 204; HRR = 134. 60%: 0.60 × 134 + 70 = 150.4 ≈ 150 bpm. 70%: 0.70 × 134 + 70 = 163.8 ≈ 164 bpm.
- **Q:** Using 220 − age only, what is 70% of max for a 17-year-old?
  **A:** HRmax = 203; 0.70 × 203 ≈ 142 bpm.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pulse checks
*Student:* Check pulse at rest, after walking, jogging, and sprinting; compare with RPE.

*How to teach:* Take pulse for 15 seconds × 4 at rest, after 3 min walking, after 3 min jogging, and after a 30-second sprint. The student records each with an RPE (1–10). Discuss how HR and RPE track together.

- **Q:** You count 38 beats in 15 seconds. What is your heart rate?
  **A:** 38 × 4 = 152 bpm.
- **Q:** What is the talk test?
  **A:** At moderate intensity you can talk but not sing; at vigorous intensity you can say only a few words before pausing for breath.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Zone training
*Student:* Complete a 25-minute run staying in 60–70% zone with pulse checks every 5 minutes.

*How to teach:* Student runs 25 minutes aiming to stay in the 60–70% zone calculated Monday; stop briefly every 5 minutes for a 15-second pulse check and adjust pace. Record the five readings.

- **Q:** Your check shows HR above your 70% zone. What do you do?
  **A:** Slow the pace (or walk briefly) until HR drops back into the 60–70% zone.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Intervals
*Student:* Perform jump-rope intervals (30 sec on/30 off ×10) and graph heart rate recovery.

*How to teach:* Do 10 rounds of jump rope 30 s on/30 s off. Take pulse at the end of a work bout and again 1 minute after finishing; graph HR vs. time. Explain heart rate recovery: a faster drop indicates better aerobic fitness.

- **Q:** HR is 180 at the end of exercise and 140 one minute later. What is the 1-minute recovery?
  **A:** 40 bpm drop — a larger drop generally indicates better cardiovascular fitness.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on heart rate calculation and submit the heart-rate graph.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz below and review the heart-rate graph for labeled axes, units, and a one-sentence interpretation.

- **Q:** Why might RPE be useful when a heart-rate monitor isn't available?
  **A:** RPE correlates with heart rate and intensity, so a person can judge effort by feel (on a 0–10 scale, about 5–6 is moderate and 7–8 is vigorous).

**Assessment:** Quiz and heart-rate graph.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) HRmax = 220 − age. 2) Karvonen THR = (HRmax − HRrest) × % + HRrest. 3) Age 15, rest 65, 70%: HRmax 205, HRR 140, 0.7 × 140 + 65 = 163 bpm. 4) 24 beats in 10 s = 144 bpm (×6). 5) On a 0–10 RPE scale, moderate ≈ 5–6 (can talk but not sing) and vigorous ≈ 7–8 (only a few words). Heart-rate graph rubric: proficient = time on x-axis, bpm on y-axis, labeled, all 10 intervals and the recovery point plotted, with a correct written interpretation of recovery.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Freehand sketching fundamentals

**Prepare:** Gather grid paper, soft pencils (HB/2H), and an eraser. Choose 3–4 household objects with simple shapes (mug, box, tape dispenser, stapler).

**Watch for:**
- Pressing hard on construction lines. Construction lines should be light so they can be left or erased; darken only final object lines.
- Drawing lines by moving only the wrist. Move the whole arm and use short, overlapping strokes for straighter lines.
- Guessing size instead of comparing. Use the pencil as a measuring stick to compare proportions (e.g. the mug is 1½ times as tall as wide).

### Day 1 (Monday): Line work
*Student:* Practice horizontal, vertical, and angled lines and construction lines.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate light construction lines versus dark object lines. Have the student fill a page with horizontal, vertical and angled lines, marking endpoints first and sketching between them.

- **Q:** What's the difference between a construction line and an object line?
  **A:** Construction lines are light guidelines used to lay out the sketch; object lines are dark, thick lines showing the visible edges of the object.
- **Q:** Tip for drawing a long straight line freehand?
  **A:** Mark both endpoints, keep your eye on the end point, and use your whole arm in light overlapping strokes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Circles & arcs
*Student:* Sketch circles and arcs using boxes and diagonals.

*How to teach:* Show the box method: draw a square, its diagonals and center lines, mark the radius on each line, then connect with arcs. Practice circles and arcs of different sizes.

- **Q:** How do you sketch a circle using a box?
  **A:** Draw a light square the size of the diameter, add diagonals and center lines, mark points at the radius along them, then connect the points with smooth arcs.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Proportion
*Student:* Sketch household objects using proportional estimation.

*How to teach:* Teach proportion: hold a pencil at arm's length to compare an object's height to its width. Sketch 3–4 household objects in proportion.

- **Q:** A mug is 10 cm tall and 8 cm wide. If your sketch is 5 cm tall, how wide should it be?
  **A:** 4 cm (scale factor 0.5).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Thumbnail sketches
*Student:* Create 10 thumbnail sketches of a single object from different views.

*How to teach:* Have the student make 10 small, quick 'thumbnail' sketches of one object from different angles (front, top, side, angled views).

- **Q:** What is the purpose of thumbnail sketches?
  **A:** Quickly explore views and ideas without detail, to decide which view or design is best to develop further.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Submit a sketch portfolio.

*How to teach:* Collect the sketch portfolio and score with the rubric in the assessment key; have the student redo the weakest sketch.

- **Q:** Which sketch in your portfolio best shows correct proportion, and how do you know?
  **A:** Answers vary; a strong answer cites a measured comparison (e.g. height-to-width ratio matches the object).

**Assessment:** Sketch portfolio.

**Answer key:** Sketch portfolio rubric (4 levels): 4 = clean line practice sheet (straight lines, light construction lines, dark object lines); circles/arcs built from boxes and diagonals; household objects in correct proportion (within about 10% by eye); 10 thumbnails showing different views; all labeled and dated; 3 = mostly accurate with some uneven lines; 2 = proportions noticeably off or missing parts; 1 = incomplete.
