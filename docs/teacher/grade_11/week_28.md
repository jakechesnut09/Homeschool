## Algebra II: Modeling periodic phenomena

**Prepare:** Daylight-hours table for your city (timeanddate.com 'sunrise sunset') and NOAA Tides & Currents data for a CA station (e.g., San Francisco 9414290). Desmos.

**Watch for:**
- Using the wrong period for monthly data (should be 12 months) or for tides (about 12.4 hours, two highs per day).
- Assuming the model is exact. Discuss residuals and why weather or geography make real data deviate.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ferris wheel
*Student:* Write a sinusoidal model for a rider's height on a Ferris wheel from given dimensions.

*How to teach:* Model a wheel with diameter 50 m, center 30 m high, one turn every 10 minutes, rider starting at the bottom. Today's task from the plan: Write a sinusoidal model for a rider's height on a Ferris wheel from given dimensions. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Write h(t) for the Ferris wheel.
  **A:** h(t) = −25cos(πt/5) + 30: amplitude 25, midline 30, period 10, starting at the minimum (5 m). At t = 5, h = 55 m (top).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Daylight data
*Student:* Collect monthly daylight hours for your California city and fit a sinusoidal model.

*How to teach:* Enter monthly daylight hours and fit y = a cos(b(x − h)) + d by reading max, min, and period, then refine with Desmos regression. Today's task from the plan: Collect monthly daylight hours for your California city and fit a sinusoidal model. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** If maximum daylight is 14.4 h (June) and minimum 9.9 h (December), find amplitude, midline, and period.
  **A:** Amplitude (14.4 − 9.9)/2 = 2.25 h; midline 12.15 h; period 12 months.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tides
*Student:* Use NOAA tide data for a California station to model water height.

*How to teach:* Plot a day or two of NOAA hourly heights and fit a sinusoid. Today's task from the plan: Use NOAA tide data for a California station to model water height. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** High tide is 5.5 ft, low tide 0.5 ft. Give amplitude, midline, and an approximate period.
  **A:** Amplitude 2.5 ft, midline 3 ft, period about 12.4 hours (two highs per day).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Interpreting
*Student:* Use models to predict values and compare to actual data; discuss model limits.

*How to teach:* Student predicts a value from each model and compares to actual data, then writes two limitations. Today's task from the plan: Use models to predict values and compare to actual data; discuss model limits. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** Name one limitation of a sinusoidal tide model.
  **A:** Real tides have unequal highs/lows (mixed tides), lunar-cycle changes, and weather effects, so a single sine with a fixed amplitude will drift from the data.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 7 test on radians, unit circle, identity, and trig graphs/models.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test (about 50 minutes) and collect the daylight report. Today's task from the plan: Unit 7 test on radians, unit circle, identity, and trig graphs/models. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Convert 225° to radians and evaluate its cosine.
  **A:** 5π/4; cos = −√2/2.

**Assessment:** Daylight model report and Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Daylight report rubric (4 levels): 4 = data table and graph, model with correct amplitude/midline/period/phase, prediction compared with actual, two limitations; 3 = one parameter off or weak discussion; 2 = model attempted but major parameter wrong; 1 = data only. Unit 7 sample answers: 240° = 4π/3; arc s for r=6, θ=π/3 is 2π; sin(3π/4)=√2/2; given cos θ=4/5 in QIV, sin θ=−3/5; period of y=4 sin(πx/6) is 12; y=tan x asymptotes at π/2+kπ.

## Physics of the Universe: Nuclear processes

**Prepare:** Pennies or M&Ms (100), cup, graph paper; PhET Nuclear Fission.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking half-life means the substance is gone after two half-lives. After two half-lives, 1/4 remains.
- Confusing fission and fusion.

### Day 1 (Monday): Isotopes
*Student:* Review isotopes and nuclear notation.

*How to teach:* Review atomic number (protons) and mass number (protons + neutrons) and write isotopes in nuclear notation (e.g., ¹⁴₆C). The student identifies protons and neutrons for five isotopes.

- **Q:** How many protons and neutrons are in carbon-14?
  **A:** 6 protons, 8 neutrons.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Decay
*Student:* Model alpha, beta, and gamma decay.

*How to teach:* Model each decay type by moving beads (protons and neutrons) on paper and balancing mass and atomic numbers. The student writes three balanced decay equations.

- **Q:** What happens to uranium-238 in alpha decay?
  **A:** It becomes thorium-234 (loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Half-life
*Student:* Simulate half-life with pennies and graph decay.

*How to teach:* Put 100 pennies in a cup, shake, dump, and remove all heads each round (one round = one half-life); repeat until none remain. Graph pennies remaining vs round and compare with 100 × (½)ⁿ.

- **Q:** After three half-lives, what fraction remains?
  **A:** 1/8.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fission & fusion
*Student:* Compare fission and fusion; use PhET Nuclear Fission.

*How to teach:* Use PhET Nuclear Fission to fire a neutron at uranium-235 and watch the chain reaction; then compare to fusion in the Sun. The student builds a T-chart: fuel, products, where it happens, waste, and uses.

- **Q:** Difference between fission and fusion?
  **A:** Fission splits heavy nuclei; fusion combines light nuclei.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on nuclear processes.

*How to teach:* Nuclear quiz. Today's task from the plan: Quiz on nuclear processes. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What happens in beta decay?
  **A:** A neutron changes into a proton, emitting an electron; atomic number increases by 1.

**Assessment:** Half-life lab and quiz.

**Answer key:** Half-life lab rubric (4-point): data table for every round; graph of pennies remaining vs round with a smooth decay curve; comparison with 100 × (½)ⁿ; explanation that decay is random for each nucleus but predictable for large numbers. Quiz key (sample): 1) ²³⁵₉₂U has 92 protons, 143 neutrons. 2) alpha decay of ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He. 3) beta decay of ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + electron. 4) gamma rays change neither mass number nor atomic number. 5) 80 g with 5-day half-life after 15 days → 10 g. 6) fission splits heavy nuclei (power plants); fusion joins light nuclei (stars). 7) both release energy because mass is converted (E = mc²).

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Civil rights DBQ and affirmative action

**Prepare:** Print Justice Powell's opinion excerpts in Regents of the University of California v. Bakke (1978), the 1996 Proposition 209 ballot text and arguments for and against (California Secretary of State voter guide), and eight DBQ documents for 'How successful was the civil rights movement in achieving its goals by 1975?' (e.g., Brown, Civil Rights Act, VRA registration data, Kerner Commission excerpt 1968, Black Panther Ten-Point Program 1966, income/poverty data, Swann busing case 1971, a UFW contract photo). Build the Unit 8 test.

**Watch for:**
- Students think Bakke banned affirmative action. It struck down UC Davis's racial quota (16 of 100 seats) but allowed race as one factor among many to pursue diversity.
- Students think Prop 209 is federal law. It amended California's constitution to ban preferences in public employment, education and contracting; a 2020 repeal attempt (Prop 16) failed. Nationally, SFFA v. Harvard (2023) later ended race-conscious college admissions.
- Students think 'success' must be all or nothing. Evaluate legal, political, economic and social goals separately.

### Day 1 (Monday): Bakke
*Student:* Read excerpts from Justice Powell's opinion in Regents of the University of California v. Bakke and chart what the Court allowed and prohibited.

*How to teach:* Read Powell's opinion and chart what the Court allowed and prohibited.

- **Q:** What did Bakke prohibit and allow?
  **A:** Prohibited fixed racial quotas (UC Davis set aside 16 of 100 seats); allowed race as one 'plus' factor among many to achieve a diverse student body.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Prop 209
*Student:* Read the 1996 Proposition 209 ballot arguments for and against and summarize each side's strongest evidence.

*How to teach:* Read the Prop 209 ballot arguments and summarize each side's strongest evidence.

- **Q:** What did Prop 209 ban?
  **A:** Preferential treatment based on race, sex, color, ethnicity or national origin in California public employment, education and contracting.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): DBQ planning
*Student:* Source and group eight documents for the DBQ 'How successful was the civil rights movement in achieving its goals by 1975?' and draft a thesis.

*How to teach:* Source and group eight documents and draft a thesis.

- **Q:** Suggest three groupings for the documents.
  **A:** Legal/political gains (Brown, CRA, VRA); economic and social limits (poverty data, Kerner Report); changing strategies (Black Power, busing).

### Day 4 (Thursday): DBQ writing
*Student:* Write the 60-minute DBQ essay using at least six documents plus outside evidence.

*How to teach:* Write the 60-minute DBQ essay.

- **Q:** What's the difference between a document summary and analysis?
  **A:** Summary repeats what it says; analysis explains how it supports your argument and considers its source.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 8 test on civil rights cases, leaders, legislation, and expanding movements.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test.

- **Q:** What did the Kerner Commission (1968) conclude?
  **A:** 'Our nation is moving toward two societies, one black, one white — separate and unequal,' blaming white racism for urban unrest.

**Assessment:** DBQ and Unit 8 test.

**Answer key:** DBQ rubric (7 points): thesis with a line of reasoning (e.g., highly successful in ending legal segregation and securing voting rights, less successful in economic equality and housing); contextualization (Jim Crow, WWII, Cold War); evidence from 6+ documents; outside evidence (e.g., Fair Housing Act 1968, Loving v. Virginia 1967, Shelley v. Kraemer); sourcing for 2+ documents; complexity. Unit 8 test key (sample): Plessy and Harlan; EO 9981; Mendez; Brown; Montgomery; sit-ins; Freedom Rides; Birmingham letter; CRA Titles II/VI/VII; VRA; 24th Amendment; Delano strike; East L.A. walkouts; TWLF; Alcatraz; Title IX; Bakke — quotas unconstitutional, race may be one factor; Prop 209 (1996).

## Spanish IV: Poesía: Martí, Darío y Mistral

**Prepare:** Print José Martí's Versos sencillos I ('Yo soy un hombre sincero') and XXXIX ('Cultivo una rosa blanca') and Rubén Darío's 'Sonatina' (Prosas profanas, 1896) — all public domain. Obtain Gabriela Mistral's 'Los que no danzan' (from Ternura) from a library or licensed anthology; do not copy it. Queue a recording of 'Guantanamera', whose verses come from Martí.

**Watch for:**
- Counting syllables by spelling: Spanish meter joins vowels between words (sinalefa) and adds a syllable when a line ends in a stressed word (aguda) or removes one when it ends in an esdrújula.
- Thinking modernismo is the same as English 'modernism': Hispanic modernismo (Darío, c. 1888–1910) emphasized musicality, exotic and refined imagery, and art for art's sake.
- Explicating by paraphrasing line by line: connect form (rhyme, meter, sound) to meaning and theme.

### Day 1 (Monday): Martí
*Student:* Read Versos sencillos I and 'Cultivo una rosa blanca'; identify rhyme and meter; listen to 'Guantanamera'.

*How to teach:* Read Versos sencillos I and XXXIX aloud; your child marks the rhyme scheme and counts syllables, then listens to 'Guantanamera'.

- **Q:** What is the meter and rhyme of 'Cultivo una rosa blanca'?
  **A:** Octosyllabic lines (8 syllables) in quatrains with abba rhyme (blanca/enero/sincero/franca).
- **Q:** What is the message of 'Cultivo una rosa blanca'?
  **A:** The speaker offers the same white rose (kindness, peace) to the sincere friend and to the cruel person who hurts him — he answers cruelty with generosity, not with thistles or nettles.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Darío
*Student:* Read 'Sonatina' and identify modernista imagery and musicality.

*How to teach:* Read 'Sonatina'; your child lists images of color, luxury, and sound and identifies the modernista features.

- **Q:** What is the situation in 'Sonatina'?
  **A:** A sad princess sits in her palace longing for something beyond her luxurious, confined life ('La princesa está triste... ¿qué tendrá la princesa?'); at the end her fairy godmother tells her a knight is coming on a winged horse to bring her love.
- **Q:** What meter does Darío use?
  **A:** Alexandrines — 14-syllable lines divided into two halves of 7 (hemistiquios), giving a musical rhythm.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mistral
*Student:* Read a Mistral poem and discuss its themes; learn about the first Latin American Nobel laureate in literature.

*How to teach:* Read a Mistral poem; learn that Gabriela Mistral (Chile) won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1945, the first Latin American to do so.

- **Q:** What is 'Los que no danzan' about?
  **A:** It is a children's poem in which a child who cannot dance (because of disability or illness) is told she can dance with her heart; it invites everyone to join the dance of life.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Recitación
*Student:* Memorize and recite one stanza of a poem with expression.

*How to teach:* Your child memorizes and recites one stanza with expression.

- **Q:** What should a good recitation do at the end of each line?
  **A:** Pause briefly only where punctuation or meaning requires, keeping the rhythm and linking vowels across words.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Write an explication of one poem in Spanish.

*How to teach:* Your child writes an explication of one poem in Spanish.

- **Q:** Write a thesis for an explication of 'Cultivo una rosa blanca'.
  **A:** Example: En 'Cultivo una rosa blanca', Martí usa la imagen repetida de la rosa blanca para mostrar que el perdón y la generosidad son más fuertes que el odio.

**Assessment:** Recitation and explication.

**Answer key:** Recitation rubric: 4 = memorized stanza, correct pronunciation and linking, expressive pauses at punctuation and line ends; 3 = minor slips; 2 = reads or hesitates; 1 = not prepared. Explication rubric (~250 words): 4 = identifies speaker, theme, and tone, analyzes 2–3 devices (rhyme, meter, imagery, metaphor, repetition) with quoted lines, explains how form supports meaning; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = mostly paraphrase; 1 = incomplete.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Writing a ten-minute play

**Prepare:** Play-format template, a recording app, and 2–4 volunteers for a table read. Read one or two published ten-minute plays as models if available.

**Watch for:**
- Too many characters or locations for ten minutes. Keep 2–3 characters and one setting.
- Late conflict. Start close to the conflict; the turning point should come around minute 7.
- Ignoring table-read reactions. Where listeners drift, the script needs cutting or sharpening.

### Day 1 (Monday): Idea
*Student:* Brainstorm 10 premises from personal, family, or community stories; pick one with a clear conflict and a single setting.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm 10 premises from personal, family, or community stories; choose one with clear conflict and one setting.

- **Q:** What makes a premise work for a ten-minute play?
  **A:** A single setting, two or three characters, an immediate conflict with stakes, and a possible change by the end.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Outline
*Student:* Outline the play in 6–8 beats with the turning point placed around minute 7.

*How to teach:* Outline in 6–8 beats with the turning point around minute 7 (about page 7; one page ≈ one minute).

- **Q:** About how long does one script page run in performance?
  **A:** About one minute.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft
*Student:* Write the full 8–10 page draft.

*How to teach:* Write the full 8–10 page draft.

- **Q:** What is a dramaturg?
  **A:** A theatre professional who researches context and helps develop scripts with the playwright and director.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Table read
*Student:* Hold a table read with family or friends, recording it and noting where listeners lost interest.

*How to teach:* Hold a recorded table read; mark where listeners lost interest.

- **Q:** What is a table read?
  **A:** A seated read-through of a script aloud by actors to hear the play and find problems.

### Day 5 (Friday): Revise
*Student:* Revise using the recording, tightening dialogue and clarifying the climax; submit the second draft.

*How to teach:* Revise using the recording: cut repetition, sharpen climax; submit draft 2.

- **Q:** Name two revision strategies for dialogue.
  **A:** Cut lines that repeat information, replace explanation with subtext, end scenes on a turn, read aloud for natural rhythm.

**Assessment:** Ten-minute play second draft.

**Answer key:** Ten-minute play second draft rubric. 4 – 8–10 pages in correct format, clear conflict from page 1, turning point near page 7, satisfying resolution, distinct voices, revision log shows meaningful changes from the table read. 3 (proficient) – Correct length/format, clear arc, revisions evident. 2 – Weak structure or little revision. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness: Ultimate and invasion-game strategy

**Prepare:** Get a flying disc (175 g ultimate disc), cones for end zones, and a field. Read USA Ultimate's Spirit of the Game summary. Look for local pickup games.

**Watch for:**
- Running with the disc. The thrower must establish a pivot foot and has 10 seconds (stall count) to throw.
- Contact defense. Ultimate is non-contact; marker must stay at least one disc-width away.
- Thinking a referee is needed. Players call their own fouls and resolve disputes respectfully.

### Day 1 (Monday): Throws
*Student:* Practice backhand and forehand disc throws for accuracy at 10 and 20 yards.

*How to teach:* Backhand (grip with thumb on top, step across, snap the wrist, flat release) and forehand/flick (two fingers under the rim, snap like skipping a stone). Throw at targets 10 and 20 yards.

- **Q:** What causes a disc to flutter or wobble?
  **A:** Too little spin — a strong wrist snap creates spin that stabilizes the flight.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cutting
*Student:* Learn cuts and creating space; practice give-and-go with a partner.

*How to teach:* Teach cuts: fake one way, explode the other; create space by clearing lanes. Practice give-and-go: pass, cut upfield, receive the return pass.

- **Q:** What is a give-and-go?
  **A:** A player passes to a teammate and immediately cuts to receive a quick return pass.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Defense
*Student:* Compare person-to-person and zone defenses and when to use each.

*How to teach:* Compare person-to-person (each defender guards one player) and zone (defenders guard areas — useful in wind or against weaker throwers).

- **Q:** When might a team choose zone defense?
  **A:** In windy conditions or against a team with poor long throws, to force many short passes and errors.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Game play
*Student:* Play ultimate with family or a community pickup game, self-officiating under Spirit of the Game.

*How to teach:* Play ultimate with self-officiating. Review: score by catching in the end zone; turnover on drop, out-of-bounds, interception, or stall.

- **Q:** Name three ways a turnover happens.
  **A:** Dropped or incomplete pass, interception/block, thrown out of bounds, or stall count reaches 10.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write an analysis of one offensive and one defensive strategy used in play.

*How to teach:* Student writes an analysis of one offensive and one defensive strategy, using examples from play.

- **Q:** What is Spirit of the Game?
  **A:** Ultimate's principle that players are responsible for fair play and rules adherence, respecting opponents and resolving calls without referees.

**Assessment:** Strategy analysis.

**Answer key:** Strategy analysis rubric. 4 – Explains an offensive strategy (e.g., vertical stack, give-and-go) and a defensive strategy (person or zone) with how and why they work, cites specific game moments, evaluates effectiveness, and suggests improvement. 3 (proficient) – Accurately explains both with examples. 2 – Vague or partly inaccurate. 1 – Missing a strategy.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Capstone: problem identification

**Prepare:** Review the student's engineering notebook for space. Help line up potential users to interview (family, neighbors, a community group). Have a decision matrix template and Gantt chart template (spreadsheet) ready.

**Watch for:**
- Picking a problem that is too big ('solve homelessness') or has no real user. Scope to something buildable in 8 weeks with a specific user.
- Choosing a solution before defining the problem. The problem statement must not contain the solution.
- Unrealistic schedules. Build in time for rework and testing; the prototype rarely works the first time.

### Day 1 (Monday): Problem hunting
*Student:* List 15 problems observed at home or in the community and select three candidates.

*How to teach:* The student lists 15 problems noticed at home or in the community, then picks three candidates based on interest and feasibility.

- **Q:** What makes a good capstone problem?
  **A:** A real user with a real need, a problem small enough to prototype and test in about 8 weeks with available tools and budget.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): User interviews
*Student:* Interview at least two potential users for each of the top candidates.

*How to teach:* Interview at least two potential users for each candidate; record quotes.

- **Q:** Why interview users before choosing a problem?
  **A:** To confirm the problem is real and important to them, and to learn needs that will shape criteria.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Selection
*Student:* Use a decision matrix to select the capstone problem based on impact and feasibility.

*How to teach:* Select the problem with a weighted decision matrix (impact, feasibility, user access, interest, cost).

- **Q:** Weights: impact 3, feasibility 2. Problem A scores 4 and 3; problem B scores 5 and 1. Which wins?
  **A:** A: 12 + 6 = 18; B: 15 + 2 = 17 → A.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Design brief
*Student:* Write a design brief with client, problem statement, criteria, and constraints.

*How to teach:* Write the design brief: client, problem statement, criteria, constraints.

- **Q:** Rewrite 'Design a phone app that reminds Grandma to take pills.' as a solution-neutral problem statement.
  **A:** 'Grandma sometimes forgets whether she has taken her daily medications, which risks missed or double doses.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Submit the design brief and a Gantt chart for weeks 28–36.

*How to teach:* Submit the design brief and a Gantt chart for weeks 28–36; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Why include buffer time in a Gantt chart?
  **A:** Problems and delays always happen; buffer keeps the final deadline achievable.

**Assessment:** Design brief and Gantt chart.

**Answer key:** Design brief rubric: 4 = names a real client/user; problem statement describes the need without implying a solution; at least four measurable criteria and three constraints (budget, time, materials/tools, size, safety); justification from user interviews; 3 = all parts, criteria partly unmeasurable; 2 = solution embedded or missing constraints; 1 = incomplete. Gantt chart: pass when it covers weeks 28–36 with research, ideation, CAD, build, testing, report and presentation tasks, durations, dependencies and at least one buffer.
