# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 20

## English 11 – American Literature: The Great Gatsby, Ch. 1–3

**Prepare:** The Great Gatsby (public domain in the U.S. since 2021; Gutenberg or library). A one-page overview of the Jazz Age and Prohibition. Make a symbol-tracker chart (symbol | chapter | quotation | meaning).

**Watch for:**
- Trusting Nick completely. He claims to reserve judgment but judges constantly, and in Ch. 3 calls himself 'one of the few honest people that I have ever known'; ask the student to question his reliability.
- Thinking East Egg and West Egg differ only in location. East Egg is inherited 'old money' (Tom and Daisy); West Egg is 'new money' (Gatsby), and the gap between them matters to the plot.

### Day 1 (Monday): Context
*Student:* Read a short Jazz Age/Prohibition overview; read Ch. 1 and analyze Nick's claim that he is 'inclined to reserve all judgments'.

*How to teach:* Read the overview, then Ch. 1 aloud for the first pages. Stop at Nick's claim that he is 'inclined to reserve all judgments' and ask the student to collect three judgments Nick makes in the same chapter.

- **Q:** What does Nick claim about judging, and is it true?
  **A:** He says he is 'inclined to reserve all judgments,' yet in Ch. 1 alone he judges Tom as brutal and arrogant and Daisy and Jordan as careless; he judges constantly, so the reader must be cautious.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ch. 1 symbols
*Student:* Analyze the green light and East/West Egg geography; start a symbol tracker.

*How to teach:* Reread the end of Ch. 1. Model the first row of the symbol tracker for the green light, then have the student map East Egg, West Egg, and New York on a sketch and add them to the tracker.

- **Q:** What is the green light and what does it suggest?
  **A:** The light at the end of Daisy's dock across the bay; Gatsby stretches his arms toward it, suggesting his longing for Daisy and, more broadly, a dream that is always just out of reach.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ch. 2
*Student:* Read the Valley of Ashes chapter; explain the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg.

*How to teach:* Read Ch. 2. Have the student describe the Valley of Ashes in their own words and explain why Fitzgerald places it between the Eggs and New York. Today's task from the plan: Read the Valley of Ashes chapter; explain the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What do the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg suggest?
  **A:** They are on a faded billboard above the Valley of Ashes, staring over a gray, morally empty world; they suggest an absent God or a watching conscience that no one heeds.
- **Q:** What happens at the end of Ch. 2 in the apartment?
  **A:** Tom breaks Myrtle's nose with his open hand when she keeps shouting Daisy's name, showing his violence and control.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Ch. 3
*Student:* Read Gatsby's party; collect rumors about Gatsby and evaluate Nick's reliability.

*How to teach:* Read Ch. 3, Gatsby's party. The student lists every rumor about Gatsby and notes how Nick first meets him. Today's task from the plan: Read Gatsby's party; collect rumors about Gatsby and evaluate Nick's reliability. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** List two rumors guests tell about Gatsby.
  **A:** That he once killed a man and that he was a German spy during the war (others say he went to Oxford).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Reading quiz Ch. 1–3 and a paragraph on setting as social commentary.

*How to teach:* Give a 10-question reading quiz, then the student writes a paragraph on how setting (the Eggs, the Valley of Ashes) comments on class. Today's task from the plan: Reading quiz Ch. 1–3 and a paragraph on setting as social commentary. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** How is Nick related to Daisy?
  **A:** She is his second cousin once removed (his cousin).

**Assessment:** Reading quiz and setting paragraph.

**Answer key:** Reading quiz key: 1) Nick Carraway, from the Midwest, sells bonds. 2) He rents in West Egg. 3) Daisy is his cousin. 4) Tom Buchanan is a wealthy former Yale football player. 5) Jordan Baker is a professional golfer. 6) Myrtle Wilson is Tom's mistress; George Wilson runs a garage in the Valley of Ashes. 7) Eckleburg's eyes are on a billboard. 8) Tom breaks Myrtle's nose. 9) Gatsby throws lavish parties. 10) Nick meets Gatsby without recognizing him. Setting paragraph rubric (4-point): claim about class; two quotations (e.g., the Valley of Ashes as 'a fantastic farm where ashes grow'); explanation of how place reflects class division; conventions.

## Algebra II: Compound interest and e

**Prepare:** Scientific calculator and a spreadsheet (Google Sheets is fine). Optional: real savings-account rates from two local banks.

**Watch for:**
- Using r = 4 instead of 0.04. Always convert percent to decimal.
- Mixing up n (compounds per year) with t (years). Label every variable before substituting.

### Day 1 (Monday): Compound interest
*Student:* Use A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) to compare savings accounts.

*How to teach:* Model A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) with $1,000 at 4% compounded monthly for 5 years. Student compares two accounts. Today's task from the plan: Use A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) to compare savings accounts. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Find the balance: $1,000 at 4% compounded monthly for 5 years.
  **A:** 1000(1 + 0.04/12)^60 ≈ $1,221.00.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Discovering e
*Student:* Increase n in a spreadsheet to see (1 + 1/n)^n approach e.

*How to teach:* Spreadsheet: column of n = 1, 10, 100, 1000, … and (1 + 1/n)^n. Watch it level off. Today's task from the plan: Increase n in a spreadsheet to see (1 + 1/n)^n approach e. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What are (1 + 1/n)^n for n = 1 and n = 100, and what does it approach?
  **A:** 2 and about 2.7048; it approaches e ≈ 2.71828.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Continuous growth
*Student:* Use A = Pe^(rt) for continuous compounding and bacterial growth.

*How to teach:* Model A = Pe^(rt) and compare to monthly compounding. Then use it for bacteria growth. Today's task from the plan: Use A = Pe^(rt) for continuous compounding and bacterial growth. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** $500 compounded continuously at 3% for 10 years?
  **A:** 500e^(0.3) ≈ $674.93.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rewriting expressions
*Student:* Rewrite 1.15^t as (1.15^(1/12))^(12t) to find monthly rates.

*How to teach:* Show that 1.15^t = (1.15^(1/12))^(12t) and compute 1.15^(1/12). Today's task from the plan: Rewrite 1.15^t as (1.15^(1/12))^(12t) to find monthly rates. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** A quantity grows 15% per year. What is the equivalent monthly growth rate?
  **A:** 1.15^(1/12) ≈ 1.0117, about 1.17% per month.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on interest problems and equivalent forms.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (about 30 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Quiz on interest problems and equivalent forms. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Which is better: 5% compounded annually or 4.9% compounded continuously?
  **A:** Annual: 1.05 effective. Continuous: e^0.049 ≈ 1.0502. The continuous account is very slightly better (5.02% vs 5%).

**Assessment:** Interest quiz.

**Answer key:** Sample key: 1) $2,000 at 6% quarterly for 8 years: 2000(1.015)^32 ≈ $3,220.65. 2) $2,000 at 6% continuously for 8 years: 2000e^0.48 ≈ $3,232.15. 3) Monthly rate equivalent to 12% annual growth: 1.12^(1/12) ≈ 1.0095 (≈0.95%). 4) Bacteria 200e^(0.4t) at t=5: ≈1,478. 5) Explain why more frequent compounding helps but levels off (approaches e^r).

## Physics of the Universe: Sound and wave interactions

**Prepare:** Glass bottles, water, a spoon; a free tone-generator app; two phones or a laptop with stereo speakers. Keep volume low to protect hearing.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking louder means higher pitch. Pitch depends on frequency; loudness depends on amplitude.
- Thinking two sound waves always make sound louder. They can cancel (destructive interference) where crest meets trough.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sound
*Student:* Use a phone tone generator and observe the effect of frequency on pitch.

*How to teach:* Play tones from 100 Hz to 10,000 Hz; the student notes the pitch and the highest frequency they can hear. Today's task from the plan: Use a phone tone generator and observe the effect of frequency on pitch. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What property of a sound wave determines pitch?
  **A:** Frequency: higher frequency → higher pitch. (Humans hear about 20–20,000 Hz.)

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Resonance
*Student:* Tune water bottles and explain standing waves.

*How to teach:* Fill bottles to different levels; blow across the tops and tap the sides; compare pitches. Today's task from the plan: Tune water bottles and explain standing waves. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Blowing across a bottle, does more water give a higher or lower pitch? Why?
  **A:** Higher: the air column is shorter, so the standing wave's wavelength is shorter and its frequency is higher.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Interference
*Student:* Use two phone speakers to find quiet spots of destructive interference.

*How to teach:* Play the same tone (e.g., 500 Hz) from two speakers 1 m apart; walk slowly across the room and listen for quiet spots. Today's task from the plan: Use two phone speakers to find quiet spots of destructive interference. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why are there quiet spots?
  **A:** At those points, waves from the two speakers arrive out of phase (crest meets trough) and cancel by destructive interference.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Doppler
*Student:* Explain Doppler effect and its use in radar and astronomy.

*How to teach:* Explain the Doppler effect with an ambulance siren; connect to radar guns and redshift. Today's task from the plan: Explain Doppler effect and its use in radar and astronomy. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What happens to the pitch of a siren as it approaches, then passes?
  **A:** Higher while approaching (waves compressed), lower after it passes (waves stretched).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain how noise-canceling headphones work.

*How to teach:* The student writes an explanation of how noise-canceling headphones work. Today's task from the plan: Explain how noise-canceling headphones work. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Summarize how noise-canceling headphones work.
  **A:** A microphone detects outside noise, and the electronics produce an inverted wave (opposite phase) that cancels it by destructive interference.

**Assessment:** Noise-canceling explanation.

**Answer key:** Explanation rubric (4-point): 4 = explains microphone sensing, inverted (180° out of phase) signal, destructive interference, and why it works best for steady low-frequency noise; includes a labeled wave diagram. 3 = correct with less detail. 2 = mentions 'canceling' without interference. 1 = incorrect.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Origins of the Cold War

**Prepare:** Print the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) excerpts, Churchill's 'Iron Curtain' speech (Fulton, Missouri, March 5, 1946), a summary of Kennan's Long Telegram (Feb. 1946), the Truman Doctrine speech (March 12, 1947), Marshall Plan aid by country, Berlin Airlift photos, and a map for the quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Students think the Cold War was a direct war between the U.S. and USSR. It was a rivalry fought through arms races, alliances, propaganda and proxy wars.
- Students think the Marshall Plan was purely charity. It rebuilt Western Europe to resist communism and to create markets for U.S. goods.
- Students think NATO and the Warsaw Pact formed at the same time. NATO formed in 1949; the Warsaw Pact in 1955.

### Day 1 (Monday): New world order
*Student:* Read the Universal Declaration of Human Rights; explain the purposes of the UN, IMF, and World Bank.

*How to teach:* Read the UDHR; explain the purposes of the UN, IMF and World Bank.

- **Q:** Who led the drafting of the UDHR?
  **A:** Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the UN Commission on Human Rights that drafted it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Iron curtain
*Student:* Analyze Churchill's speech and Kennan's 'Long Telegram' summary.

*How to teach:* Analyze Churchill's speech and Kennan's Long Telegram summary.

- **Q:** What did Churchill mean by an 'iron curtain'?
  **A:** A division across Europe from the Baltic to the Adriatic, with Eastern Europe under Soviet control.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Containment
*Student:* Read the Truman Doctrine; map Marshall Plan aid by country and explain why a rebuilt Europe mattered to the U.S. economy.

*How to teach:* Read the Truman Doctrine; map Marshall Plan aid and explain why a rebuilt Europe mattered.

- **Q:** Why did a rebuilt Europe matter to the U.S. economy?
  **A:** Prosperous European nations would buy American goods and be less likely to turn to communism.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Berlin & NATO
*Student:* Analyze Berlin Airlift photos and explain the formation of NATO and the Warsaw Pact.

*How to teach:* Analyze Berlin Airlift photos and explain NATO and the Warsaw Pact.

- **Q:** How did the U.S. respond to the Berlin blockade?
  **A:** With the Berlin Airlift — flying in food and fuel for about 11 months until the USSR lifted the blockade in May 1949.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on early Cold War policies with a map-identification section.

*How to teach:* Give the early Cold War quiz with a map section.

- **Q:** What is collective security in NATO?
  **A:** Article 5: an attack on one member is an attack on all.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) UN founded 1945 (Charter signed in San Francisco). 2) UDHR (1948) — Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the drafting commission. 3) IMF and World Bank — created at Bretton Woods (1944) to stabilize currencies and fund reconstruction/development. 4) 'Iron curtain' — Churchill's image of Soviet control dividing Europe. 5) Kennan — Soviet expansion must be contained. 6) Containment — prevent the spread of communism. 7) Truman Doctrine — aid to Greece and Turkey ($400 million) to resist communism. 8) Marshall Plan (1948–52) — about $13 billion to rebuild Western Europe; largest recipients Britain, France, West Germany, Italy. 9) Berlin blockade/airlift (1948–49). 10) NATO (1949) and Warsaw Pact (1955). Map section: identify Berlin, East/West Germany, Greece, Turkey, NATO members (U.S., Canada, UK, France, Italy, etc.), Warsaw Pact members (USSR, Poland, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Albania).

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Our Town and theatrical convention

**Prepare:** Borrow Thornton Wilder's Our Town (1938) from the library. Know: three acts (Daily Life, Love and Marriage, Death/Eternity), set in Grover's Corners, New Hampshire, beginning in 1901; the Stage Manager narrates; Emily Webb and George Gibbs. Parental note: Act III takes place in the town cemetery after Emily's death in childbirth.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a bare stage means low effort. Wilder's minimalism is deliberate: it makes the story universal and asks the audience to imagine.
- Mixing up presentational and representational. Presentational acknowledges the audience (Stage Manager); representational maintains the fourth wall.
- Pantomime with vague hands. Specific weight, size, and steps make invisible objects real.

### Day 1 (Monday): Act I
*Student:* Read Act I and discuss Wilder's use of the Stage Manager and bare stage.

*How to teach:* Read Act I; discuss the Stage Manager, who talks directly to the audience, controls time, and plays small roles; the stage uses only tables, chairs, and ladders.

- **Q:** What is the Stage Manager's role in Our Town?
  **A:** A narrator who addresses the audience directly, introduces characters, shifts time, and occasionally plays minor parts.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pantomime
*Student:* Practice pantomiming everyday tasks (making breakfast, stringing beans) with precise detail.

*How to teach:* Pantomime everyday tasks with precise detail (making breakfast, stringing beans as Mrs. Gibbs and Mrs. Webb do).

- **Q:** Why does Wilder use pantomime instead of real props?
  **A:** To focus attention on the universal everyday actions and people, not the objects, and to make the play timeless.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Act III
*Student:* Read Act III and discuss Emily's speech 'Do any human beings ever realize life?'

*How to teach:* Read Act III; discuss Emily's choice to relive her twelfth birthday and her question.

- **Q:** What does Emily ask the Stage Manager, and how does he answer?
  **A:** 'Do any human beings ever realize life while they live it?—every, every minute?' He answers, 'No.' Then: 'The saints and poets, maybe—they do some.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Staging
*Student:* Stage Emily's goodbye speech with no set and minimal props.

*How to teach:* Stage Emily's goodbye speech ('Good-by, Grover's Corners… Mama's sunflowers…') with no set and minimal props.

- **Q:** What is the theme of Emily's goodbye?
  **A:** People fail to appreciate the ordinary wonders of everyday life while they are living it.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Perform and compare Wilder's presentational style with Glaspell's realism in writing.

*How to teach:* Perform; write a comparison of Wilder's presentational style and Glaspell's realism.

- **Q:** How do Our Town and Trifles differ in style?
  **A:** Our Town is presentational: bare stage, narrator addresses the audience, time jumps. Trifles is realistic: detailed kitchen, fourth wall, continuous real time, meaning via subtext.

**Assessment:** Performance and comparison.

**Answer key:** Performance and comparison rubric. 4 – Emily's speech performed with clear specificity of each remembered item and emotional arc; essay compares staging, audience relationship, acting style, and theme with evidence from both plays. 3 (proficient) – Committed performance; essay accurately contrasts presentational vs. realistic styles with examples. 2 – General comparison. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness: Hiking and trail safety

**Prepare:** Gather a daypack, water, layers, sunscreen, first-aid kit, headlamp, map/app. Check California State Parks or trail apps for a 3–5 mile trail; check weather, closures, and fire conditions. Tell someone the plan. Parent should accompany the hike.

**Watch for:**
- Relying only on a phone for navigation. Batteries die and signal fails; carry a paper map and know the route.
- Underestimating time on hills. Add time for elevation gain (Naismith-style rule: roughly 1 extra hour per 2,000 ft of gain, on top of ~2 mph walking).
- Thinking biodegradable trash (orange peels) is fine to leave. Leave No Trace: pack it in, pack it out.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ten Essentials
*Student:* Learn the Ten Essentials and pack a daypack.

*How to teach:* List the Ten Essentials and pack a daypack together. Discuss why each is needed.

- **Q:** Name the Ten Essentials.
  **A:** Navigation (map/compass/GPS), headlamp, sun protection, first aid, knife/repair kit, fire (matches/lighter), emergency shelter, extra food, extra water, extra clothes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plan a hike
*Student:* Use a trail app or State Parks site to plan a 3–5 mile hike; calculate time and elevation.

*How to teach:* Student uses a trail app or State Parks site to choose a 3–5 mile route; record distance, elevation gain, and estimate time (~2 mph + 1 hour per 2,000 ft gain). Include a turnaround time.

- **Q:** Estimate hiking time for 4 miles with 1,000 ft gain.
  **A:** About 2 hours for distance (4 ÷ 2 mph) + 0.5 hour for gain = about 2.5 hours, plus breaks.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Conditioning
*Student:* Complete a 45-minute weighted-pack walk with hills or stairs.

*How to teach:* 45-minute walk with a pack weighted with water bottles, on hills or stairs.

- **Q:** Why train with a loaded pack?
  **A:** Specificity — it prepares the legs, back, and feet for carrying weight on hills.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Day hike
*Student:* Complete the hike, practicing Leave No Trace and trail etiquette.

*How to teach:* Do the hike. Practice the 7 Leave No Trace principles and trail etiquette (uphill hikers have right of way; yield to horses; stay on trail).

- **Q:** Who has the right-of-way on a trail: uphill or downhill hikers?
  **A:** Uphill hikers generally have the right-of-way; downhill hikers step aside.
- **Q:** Name three Leave No Trace principles.
  **A:** Plan ahead and prepare; travel and camp on durable surfaces; dispose of waste properly; leave what you find; minimize campfire impacts; respect wildlife; be considerate of other visitors.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Submit a trip report with route, distance, elevation, and heart-rate data.

*How to teach:* Student submits a trip report: route, distance, elevation, time, HR data, conditions, and reflections.

- **Q:** What is elevation gain?
  **A:** The total vertical distance climbed during the hike.

**Assessment:** Trip report.

**Answer key:** Trip report rubric. 4 – Route map, distance, elevation gain, total time, HR data with interpretation, Ten Essentials checklist, Leave No Trace practices described, reflection. 3 (proficient) – Route, distance, elevation, time, HR, reflection. 2 – Missing key data. 1 – Minimal.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Manufacturing processes

**Prepare:** Find short videos of injection molding, CNC milling, sand/die casting and 3D printing (e.g. 'How It's Made' segments or manufacturer videos). Install a free slicer (Cura or PrusaSlicer). Find a local library makerspace's 3D printing rules.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 3D printing will replace all manufacturing. Additive is great for prototypes and custom parts, but injection molding is far cheaper per part for large quantities.
- Forgetting draft angles. Molded parts need slightly tapered walls (often 1–2°) so they can be ejected from the mold.
- Mixing up additive and subtractive. Additive builds layer by layer; subtractive removes material from a solid block (milling, drilling, turning).

### Day 1 (Monday): Processes overview
*Student:* Watch videos of injection molding, CNC milling, and casting; take notes.

*How to teach:* Watch the videos; the student makes a table for each process: how it works, materials, typical products, cost for low vs. high volume.

- **Q:** Why is injection molding expensive to start but cheap per part?
  **A:** The steel mold (tooling) costs thousands to hundreds of thousands of dollars, but once made, each part takes seconds and little material.
- **Q:** What does CNC stand for?
  **A:** Computer numerical control.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Additive manufacturing
*Student:* Learn 3D printing and prepare an STL file for printing.

*How to teach:* Export an STL from Onshape, open in the slicer, set layer height and infill, and preview the toolpath and print time.

- **Q:** What does a slicer do?
  **A:** Converts a 3D model (STL) into layers and toolpaths (G-code) the printer follows.
- **Q:** How does infill percentage affect a print?
  **A:** Higher infill makes the part stronger and heavier but uses more material and time.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Design for manufacturing
*Student:* Identify draft angles and wall thickness in molded parts.

*How to teach:* Examine molded parts at home (bottle caps, containers) for draft angles, wall thickness, ribs and ejector-pin marks.

- **Q:** Why do molded parts have ribs instead of thick walls?
  **A:** Ribs add stiffness while keeping walls thin and uniform, saving material and avoiding sink marks.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Local manufacturing
*Student:* Research a California manufacturer and their processes.

*How to teach:* Research a California manufacturer (e.g. Tesla in Fremont, SpaceX in Hawthorne, a local machine shop or food packaging plant) and list their processes.

- **Q:** Name a California manufacturer and one process it uses.
  **A:** Examples: Tesla (Fremont) — stamping, casting (large single-piece castings), robotic assembly; SpaceX (Hawthorne) — CNC machining, welding, 3D printing of engine parts.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on manufacturing.

*How to teach:* Give the manufacturing quiz in the key.

- **Q:** Which process would you choose for 10 custom brackets? For 1 million bottle caps?
  **A:** 10 brackets: 3D printing or CNC machining. 1 million caps: injection molding.

**Assessment:** Manufacturing quiz.

**Answer key:** Manufacturing quiz: 1) Additive manufacturing = building parts layer by layer (3D printing). 2) Subtractive = removing material (CNC milling, lathe turning, drilling). 3) Injection molding = melted plastic injected into a steel mold; high tooling cost, very low per-part cost — best for high volume. 4) Casting = pouring molten metal into a mold (sand casting, die casting). 5) CNC = computer numerical control. 6) Draft angle = slight taper on molded walls so parts release from the mold. 7) Uniform wall thickness prevents warping and sink marks. 8) Best process for 5 custom prototypes → 3D printing; for 100,000 plastic phone cases → injection molding; for an aluminum engine block → casting then CNC machining. Score 7–8 = mastery.
