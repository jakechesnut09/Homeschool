# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 6

## English 11 – American Literature: Paine, Henry, and the Declaration

**Prepare:** Paine's Common Sense (excerpt) and The American Crisis No. 1; Patrick Henry's Speech to the Virginia Convention; the Declaration of Independence (archives.gov).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Declaration's grievances are all equally strong. Have the student evaluate which are specific and verifiable and which are exaggerated or blame the King for Parliament's acts.
- Missing who is excluded from 'all men are created equal.' Discuss enslaved people, women, and Native Americans (called 'merciless Indian Savages' in a grievance).

### Day 1 (Monday): Common Sense & The Crisis
*Student:* Read excerpts and label appeals and parallel structure; note how Paine addresses a mass audience.

*How to teach:* Read the opening of The Crisis aloud. The student labels appeals and parallel structure and notes plain diction aimed at ordinary readers. Today's task from the plan: Read excerpts and label appeals and parallel structure; note how Paine addresses a mass audience. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** Explain 'The summer soldier and the sunshine patriot will, in this crisis, shrink from the service of their country.'
  **A:** Paine shames those who support the cause only when it is easy; the alliteration and parallel images challenge readers to stay loyal in hard times.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Henry's speech
*Student:* Read and deliver the final paragraph aloud; chart rhetorical questions, allusions, and their effect.

*How to teach:* Read Henry's speech; the student delivers the final paragraph aloud. Chart rhetorical questions and allusions. Today's task from the plan: Read and deliver the final paragraph aloud; chart rhetorical questions, allusions, and their effect. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What does 'Suffer not yourselves to be betrayed with a kiss' allude to and why?
  **A:** Judas betraying Jesus with a kiss; Henry warns that Britain's friendly gestures hide betrayal.
- **Q:** What is the effect of the final rhetorical question 'Is life so dear, or peace so sweet, as to be purchased at the price of chains and slavery?'
  **A:** It forces listeners to admit that peace under tyranny is not worth it, building to 'give me liberty, or give me death!'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Declaration structure
*Student:* Outline the Declaration as a syllogism (major premise, minor premise/grievances, conclusion).

*How to teach:* Outline the Declaration as a syllogism on the board. Today's task from the plan: Outline the Declaration as a syllogism (major premise, minor premise/grievances, conclusion). Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** State the Declaration's argument as a syllogism.
  **A:** Major premise: when a government destroys people's rights, they may alter or abolish it. Minor premise: the King has repeatedly violated colonists' rights (the grievances). Conclusion: the colonies are free and independent states.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Evaluating reasoning
*Student:* Write a paragraph evaluating the strongest and weakest grievance as evidence; note who is excluded from 'all men'.

*How to teach:* The student picks the strongest and weakest grievance and writes a paragraph evaluating each as evidence, then a sentence on who is excluded. Today's task from the plan: Write a paragraph evaluating the strongest and weakest grievance as evidence; note who is excluded from 'all men'. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Give one strong grievance and explain why.
  **A:** 'For imposing Taxes on us without our Consent' is specific and connects directly to the principle of consent of the governed.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Compare two openings (Paine vs. Jefferson) in a timed 30-minute rhetorical paragraph.

*How to teach:* Timed 30-minute paragraph comparing how Paine and Jefferson open their texts. Today's task from the plan: Compare two openings (Paine vs. Jefferson) in a timed 30-minute rhetorical paragraph. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** How do the two openings differ in approach?
  **A:** Paine opens with emotional, urgent language ('These are the times that try men's souls'); Jefferson opens with calm, formal reasoning about the 'Laws of Nature' and a 'decent respect' for world opinion.

**Assessment:** Timed rhetorical analysis paragraph.

**Answer key:** Timed paragraph rubric (4-point): 4 = clear claim comparing purpose and tone; quotes both openings; analyzes specific choices (Paine's emotional diction and aphorism for soldiers and common readers vs. Jefferson's formal, logical diction for a world audience); explains effect on audience. 3 = accurate comparison with less precise analysis. 2 = summary of both. 1 = one text only.

## Algebra II: Complex numbers

**Prepare:** Graph paper for a complex plane (horizontal real axis, vertical imaginary axis). Preview Khan Academy 'Intro to i'.

**Watch for:**
- Writing √(−4)·√(−9) = √36 = 6. Convert first: 2i·3i = 6i² = −6.
- Treating i like a variable and leaving i² in the answer. Always replace i² with −1 and write the result as a + bi.

### Day 1 (Monday): Defining i
*Student:* Explore why x²+1 = 0 has no real solutions; define i and simplify powers of i.

*How to teach:* Ask: what number squared is −1? Define i, then build the cycle i, −1, −i, 1. Student simplifies powers of i by dividing the exponent by 4. Today's task from the plan: Explore why x²+1 = 0 has no real solutions; define i and simplify powers of i. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Simplify i²³ and √(−49).
  **A:** 23 = 4·5 + 3, so i²³ = i³ = −i. √(−49) = 7i.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Operations
*Student:* Add, subtract, and multiply complex numbers; plot sums on the complex plane.

*How to teach:* Model adding like combining like terms and multiplying with FOIL, replacing i² with −1. Student practices and plots one sum as a vector on the complex plane.

- **Q:** Compute (3 + 2i)(1 − 4i).
  **A:** 3 − 12i + 2i − 8i² = 3 − 10i + 8 = 11 − 10i.
- **Q:** Compute (5 − i) + (−2 + 3i).
  **A:** 3 + 2i.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Conjugates & division
*Student:* Use conjugates to divide complex numbers and rationalize denominators.

*How to teach:* Show that (a + bi)(a − bi) = a² + b² is real. Model dividing by multiplying top and bottom by the conjugate. Today's task from the plan: Use conjugates to divide complex numbers and rationalize denominators. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Divide (2 + 3i)/(1 − i).
  **A:** Multiply by (1 + i)/(1 + i): (2 + 2i + 3i + 3i²)/2 = (−1 + 5i)/2 = −1/2 + (5/2)i.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Complex solutions
*Student:* Solve quadratics with negative discriminants; factor x²+4 as (x+2i)(x−2i).

*How to teach:* Solve x² + 2x + 5 = 0 with the formula, simplifying √(−16) = 4i. Then factor sums of squares over the complex numbers. Today's task from the plan: Solve quadratics with negative discriminants; factor x²+4 as (x+2i)(x−2i). Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Solve x² + 2x + 5 = 0.
  **A:** x = (−2 ± √(4 − 20))/2 = (−2 ± 4i)/2 = −1 ± 2i.
- **Q:** Factor x² + 9.
  **A:** (x + 3i)(x − 3i).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on complex arithmetic and complex solutions.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (about 30 minutes) without a calculator. Today's task from the plan: Quiz on complex arithmetic and complex solutions. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Compute (4 − 3i)(4 + 3i).
  **A:** 16 + 9 = 25.

**Assessment:** Complex numbers quiz.

**Answer key:** Sample key: 1) i¹⁰ = −1. 2) √(−18) = 3i√2. 3) (2+5i)−(7−i) = −5+6i. 4) (1+i)² = 2i. 5) (3−2i)(2+i) = 8−i. 6) 5/(2−i) = 2+i. 7) x²+16=0 → ±4i. 8) x²−4x+13=0 → 2±3i. Mastery: 7/8 with a + bi form.

## Physics of the Universe: Newton's law of universal gravitation

**Prepare:** Objects of different mass (ball, book, crumpled paper), stopwatch or phone video, measuring tape; flashlight, graph paper; PhET Gravity Force Lab.

**Watch for:**
- Believing heavier objects fall faster. Without air resistance all objects fall with the same acceleration g; air resistance explains the paper.
- Thinking there's no gravity in space (on the ISS). Gravity there is about 90% of surface gravity; astronauts float because they are in free fall.

### Day 1 (Monday): Falling objects
*Student:* Drop objects of different mass; measure g from timed drops.

*How to teach:* Drop objects from a measured height (e.g., 2.0 m), time several trials, and compute g = 2d/t². Today's task from the plan: Drop objects of different mass; measure g from timed drops. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** An object falls 2.0 m in 0.64 s. Estimate g.
  **A:** g = 2(2.0)/0.64² ≈ 9.8 m/s².

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Inverse square law
*Student:* Model inverse square law by shining a flashlight on graph paper at increasing distances.

*How to teach:* Shine a flashlight through a square hole in a card at the graph paper; double the distance and count squares lit. Today's task from the plan: Model inverse square law by shining a flashlight on graph paper at increasing distances. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** At twice the distance, how many times as many squares are lit, and what happens to brightness per square?
  **A:** 4 times the area, so each square gets 1/4 the light: intensity ∝ 1/r².

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Universal gravitation
*Student:* Calculate gravitational force between Earth and Moon and between two people.

*How to teach:* Model F = Gm₁m₂/r² with scientific notation. Today's task from the plan: Calculate gravitational force between Earth and Moon and between two people. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Find the force between Earth (5.97 × 10²⁴ kg) and Moon (7.35 × 10²² kg) at 3.84 × 10⁸ m.
  **A:** F ≈ 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ × 7.35 × 10²² / (3.84 × 10⁸)² ≈ 2.0 × 10²⁰ N.
- **Q:** Force between two 70 kg people 1 m apart?
  **A:** ≈ 3.3 × 10⁻⁷ N, far too small to notice.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Simulation
*Student:* Use PhET Gravity Force Lab to explore F vs. mass and distance.

*How to teach:* In PhET, double one mass then double the distance; record the force each time. Today's task from the plan: Use PhET Gravity Force Lab to explore F vs. mass and distance. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** If the distance between two masses triples, how does the force change?
  **A:** It becomes 1/9 as large.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on gravitation calculations.

*How to teach:* Give the gravitation quiz. Today's task from the plan: Quiz on gravitation calculations. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** If both masses double and distance doubles, what happens to F?
  **A:** F × (2 × 2)/2² = F: unchanged.

**Assessment:** Gravitation quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key (sample): 1) g ≈ 9.8 m/s². 2) halving distance → 4× force. 3) doubling one mass → 2× force. 4) weight of 50 kg person = 490 N. 5) Earth–Sun force ≈ 3.5 × 10²² N (using M_sun 1.99 × 10³⁰ kg, r 1.5 × 10¹¹ m). 6) why astronauts float (free fall).

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Immigration and urbanization

**Prepare:** Print Angel Island poems (Calisphere or the Angel Island Immigration Station Foundation), Jacob Riis photos and text from How the Other Half Lives (1890), Plunkitt of Tammany Hall excerpt ('honest graft', 1905), Thomas Nast cartoons of Boss Tweed, and a Jane Addams/Hull House reading. Build the Unit 2 test with a DBQ on the immigrant experience.

**Watch for:**
- Students think Angel Island was 'the Ellis Island of the West' in the same way. Ellis Island processed most immigrants in hours; Angel Island, shaped by the Chinese Exclusion Act, detained many Chinese immigrants for weeks or months with interrogations.
- Students think political machines did only harm. They traded services (jobs, help for immigrants) for votes while engaging in corruption.
- Students think nativism targeted only one group. It targeted Chinese, Japanese, Southern and Eastern Europeans, Catholics and Jews in different ways.

### Day 1 (Monday): New immigrants
*Student:* Compare Ellis Island and Angel Island experiences using Angel Island wall poetry.

*How to teach:* Compare Ellis Island and Angel Island using Angel Island wall poetry.

- **Q:** Why were Chinese immigrants often detained at Angel Island?
  **A:** The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) barred most Chinese laborers; officials interrogated detainees to test claims of exempt status (e.g., 'paper sons').

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tenement life
*Student:* Analyze Jacob Riis photographs and text.

*How to teach:* Analyze Riis's photographs and text.

- **Q:** How did Riis use photography as reform?
  **A:** He used flash photography to show crowded, unsanitary tenements to middle-class readers, building pressure for housing laws like New York's 1901 Tenement House Act.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Machines
*Student:* Study Tammany Hall and Plunkitt's 'honest graft'.

*How to teach:* Study Tammany Hall and Plunkitt's 'honest graft'.

- **Q:** What did Plunkitt mean by 'honest graft'?
  **A:** Using insider knowledge for profit (e.g., buying land he knew the city would need) rather than outright theft — 'I seen my opportunities and I took 'em.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Americanization
*Student:* Read about settlement houses (Jane Addams) and Americanization programs.

*How to teach:* Read about Hull House and Americanization programs.

- **Q:** What services did settlement houses provide?
  **A:** English and citizenship classes, childcare, health care, job training and recreation for immigrants.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 2 test with a document-based short essay on the immigrant experience.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test.

- **Q:** What was the Chinese Exclusion Act?
  **A:** An 1882 federal law that banned Chinese laborers from immigrating; it was the first major U.S. law to bar a specific ethnic group.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test key (sample): 1) Vertical vs. horizontal integration. 2) Gospel of Wealth. 3) Social Darwinism vs. Social Gospel. 4) Sherman Antitrust Act (1890). 5) Homestead and Pullman strikes. 6) Omaha Platform. 7) Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) — first law banning immigration by a specific ethnic group; repealed 1943. 8) Ellis Island (1892) vs. Angel Island (1910). 9) Jacob Riis exposed tenement conditions. 10) Tammany Hall/Boss Tweed and political machines. 11) Settlement houses — Jane Addams's Hull House (Chicago, 1889). DBQ rubric: thesis about immigrant experience (opportunity and hardship; discrimination; community building; Americanization pressures); 4+ documents with sourcing; outside evidence.

## Spanish IV: Tradiciones y celebraciones

**Prepare:** Find short videos on Día de Muertos (Mexico, November 1–2) and Las Fallas (Valencia, March 15–19). Gather poster paper or ofrenda materials (photos, candles, paper for papel picado, optional marigolds). Chart adjective clauses: known antecedent → indicative (Tengo una amiga que baila); unknown or nonexistent → subjunctive (Busco a alguien que baile; No hay nadie que sepa).

**Watch for:**
- Using the indicative for a hypothetical antecedent (*Busco una fiesta que tiene música en vivo): Busco una fiesta que tenga música en vivo.
- Using personal 'a' with unknown people: it is usually dropped with an indefinite person (Busco un guía que hable inglés), but kept with alguien/nadie (No conozco a nadie que...).
- Calling Día de Muertos a sad or scary holiday: it is a joyful remembrance welcoming the spirits of loved ones.

### Day 1 (Monday): Celebraciones
*Student:* Watch short videos on Día de Muertos and Las Fallas and take notes.

*How to teach:* Watch the videos; your child takes notes on when, where, what people do, and why.

- **Q:** What are 'las fallas' and what happens to them?
  **A:** Huge papier-mâché and wood sculptures (often satirical) built in Valencia's streets; they are burned on the night of March 19 (la Cremà), the feast of San José.
- **Q:** Why do families build ofrendas for Día de Muertos?
  **A:** To welcome and honor the spirits of deceased loved ones, who are believed to return to visit; families place photos, favorite foods, candles, and marigolds.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cláusulas adjetivales
*Student:* Practice subjunctive in adjective clauses (Busco una fiesta que tenga...).

*How to teach:* Teach adjective clauses with the subjunctive; your child writes 10 sentences (Busco una fiesta que tenga..., No hay nadie que...).

- **Q:** Choose: 'Necesito un disfraz que ___ (ser) barato.'
  **A:** sea
- **Q:** Choose: 'Tengo un amigo que ___ (saber) bailar salsa.'
  **A:** sabe (known person → indicative)

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cultura
*Student:* Explain the practices, products, and perspectives of one celebration in a chart.

*How to teach:* Your child creates a practices–products–perspectives chart for one celebration.

- **Q:** Give one product and its perspective for Día de Muertos.
  **A:** Example: el pan de muerto (product) — it is an offering of food to share with the returning spirits (perspective: death is part of life and loved ones stay connected).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Proyecto
*Student:* Create an ofrenda or poster for a celebration with written explanations.

*How to teach:* Your child builds an ofrenda or poster with written explanations of each element.

- **Q:** Translate: 'The candles light the way for the spirits.'
  **A:** Las velas iluminan el camino de los espíritus (las almas).

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Present the project orally in 3 minutes.

*How to teach:* Your child presents the project for 3 minutes; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Translate: 'There is no one in my family who doesn't celebrate.'
  **A:** No hay nadie en mi familia que no celebre.

**Assessment:** Presentation rubric.

**Answer key:** Presentation rubric (3 minutes): 4 = explains practices (what people do), products (objects/foods), and perspectives (beliefs/values) of the celebration, uses adjective clauses and present/past tenses accurately, clear delivery with the ofrenda or poster; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = only describes products or many errors; 1 = incomplete.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Movement and physical theatre

**Prepare:** Prepare a music playlist with varied qualities. Learn Laban effort chart: Weight (strong/light), Time (sudden/sustained), Space (direct/indirect). Find a short clip from a physical theatre company (e.g., Frantic Assembly or Complicité); preview.

**Watch for:**
- Mime objects that change size or disappear. Keep the object consistent (fixed point).
- Physical theatre without a story. Movement must have a clear beginning, middle, and end.
- Confusing flow with speed. Flow is bound (controlled) vs. free (released), separate from time.

### Day 1 (Monday): Laban efforts
*Student:* Explore the eight Laban efforts (punch, float, glide, slash, etc.) through movement improvisation.

*How to teach:* Explore the eight efforts: punch/thrust (strong, sudden, direct), slash (strong, sudden, indirect), press (strong, sustained, direct), wring (strong, sustained, indirect), dab (light, sudden, direct), flick (light, sudden, indirect), glide (light, sustained, direct), float (light, sustained, indirect).

- **Q:** Which Laban effort is light, sustained, and indirect?
  **A:** Float.
- **Q:** What are the three factors combined in the eight efforts?
  **A:** Weight (strong/light), time (sudden/sustained), and space (direct/indirect).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mime
*Student:* Practice mime illusions (wall, rope pull, weight) and create a 1-minute mime scene.

*How to teach:* Teach mime illusions: wall (fixed point, flat hands), rope pull (hands stay in line, weight shifts), lifting weight (bend knees, show strain). Create a 1-minute mime scene.

- **Q:** What is a 'fixed point' in mime?
  **A:** Keeping a hand or body part still in space so an imaginary object seems solid.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Physical storytelling
*Student:* Watch a clip of a physical theatre company and identify techniques.

*How to teach:* Watch the clip; student lists techniques (lifts, repetition, slow motion, ensemble unison, objects as symbols).

- **Q:** What is physical theatre?
  **A:** Performance where the story is told mainly through movement and the body rather than spoken text.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Neutral mask
*Student:* Explore neutral movement and a character arc without words.

*How to teach:* Explore neutral movement then a wordless arc: a character enters, discovers something, and changes.

- **Q:** Why start from neutral?
  **A:** Neutral removes personal habits, so every movement choice becomes intentional and readable.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Perform a 2-minute wordless physical theatre piece with a clear beginning, middle, and end.

*How to teach:* Perform a 2-minute wordless piece with a clear beginning, middle, and end.

- **Q:** How can an audience see a character change without words?
  **A:** Through shifts in tempo, posture, Laban effort, focus, and use of space.

**Assessment:** Physical theatre piece.

**Answer key:** Physical theatre piece rubric. 4 – Clear wordless story with beginning, middle, end; at least three Laban efforts clearly contrasted; consistent mime objects; controlled, committed movement. 3 (proficient) – Readable story, varied efforts, mostly consistent mime. 2 – Story unclear or movement unvaried. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness: Interval and HIIT training

**Prepare:** Download an interval timer app; set cones 20 m apart for shuttles; find a moderate hill (30–60 seconds to climb). Review the student's target zones from week 4.

**Watch for:**
- Believing HIIT should be done every day. It is stressful; 2–3 sessions per week with recovery days is plenty.
- Thinking anaerobic means 'no oxygen in the body.' It means energy is produced without enough oxygen keeping up, so it can only be sustained briefly.
- Using too little rest so quality drops. Work-to-rest ratios (e.g., 1:1 to 1:3) keep the work intervals truly hard.

### Day 1 (Monday): HIIT science
*Student:* Learn aerobic vs. anaerobic energy systems; complete a 4-minute Tabata of squat jumps.

*How to teach:* Explain energy systems: aerobic (with oxygen, long duration, lower intensity) vs. anaerobic (short, very intense bursts; produces fatigue quickly). Do a Tabata of squat jumps: 20 s work/10 s rest × 8 = 4 minutes.

- **Q:** What is the structure of a Tabata?
  **A:** 8 rounds of 20 seconds all-out work and 10 seconds rest, totaling 4 minutes.
- **Q:** Which energy system powers a 30-minute easy jog vs. a 20-second sprint?
  **A:** The jog is mostly aerobic; the 20-second sprint relies mostly on anaerobic systems (phosphocreatine and anaerobic glycolysis).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Shuttle runs
*Student:* Complete 6×30-second shuttle runs with 90-second recovery.

*How to teach:* Set cones 20 m apart. Student does 6 × 30-second shuttle runs (touch line, turn) with 90 seconds walking recovery. Count lengths each round to check if pace holds.

- **Q:** What is the work-to-rest ratio of 30 s on/90 s off?
  **A:** 1:3.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hill repeats
*Student:* Run or walk 6 hill repeats, focusing on driving knees and arm swing.

*How to teach:* Six hill repeats: drive knees, pump arms, lean slightly into the hill, short quick steps; walk or jog down for recovery. Watch for upright posture.

- **Q:** Why are hill repeats useful?
  **A:** They build leg strength and power and raise intensity with less pounding than flat sprints.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Design HIIT
*Student:* Design a 20-minute HIIT workout with safe work-to-rest ratios and complete it.

*How to teach:* Student designs a 20-minute HIIT (warm-up, 4–6 exercises, work/rest ratio, cool-down) and completes it. Check that rest is adequate and exercises are safe.

- **Q:** A HIIT plan has 45 s work and 15 s rest for 20 minutes of burpees. What would you change?
  **A:** Rest is too short for all-out work and a single exercise risks form breakdown; use 1:1 or 1:2 ratios, vary exercises, and include warm-up and cool-down.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Lead a family member through your HIIT workout and write a comparison of HIIT vs. steady state.

*How to teach:* The student leads a family member through the workout, cueing form, then writes a comparison of HIIT vs. steady-state (time, intensity, benefits, risks).

- **Q:** Give one advantage of HIIT and one of steady-state training.
  **A:** HIIT: improves fitness in less time and builds anaerobic capacity. Steady-state: lower injury/fatigue risk, builds aerobic base, easier to sustain and recover from.

**Assessment:** HIIT design.

**Answer key:** HIIT design rubric. 4 – Includes warm-up, 4+ safe exercises, explicit work:rest ratio appropriate to intensity, cool-down, modifications, and correct HIIT vs. steady-state comparison. 3 (proficient) – Warm-up, exercises, safe ratio, cool-down, accurate comparison. 2 – Missing warm-up/cool-down or unsafe ratios. 1 – Incomplete plan.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Multiview (orthographic) projection

**Prepare:** Find a clear plastic box (or a shoebox you can draw on) and some blocks. Prepare 6 block models and 12 isometric-to-multiview matching problems (many free worksheets exist). Know the U.S. (third-angle) layout: top view above the front view, right-side view to the right.

**Watch for:**
- Placing views in the wrong positions. In third-angle projection (U.S. standard), the top view goes directly above the front, and the right side view directly to the right of the front.
- Leaving out hidden lines. Features you can't see from a view are shown with short dashed lines.
- Choosing a front view with lots of hidden lines. The front view should be the most descriptive view, showing the object's characteristic shape with the fewest hidden lines.

### Day 1 (Monday): Glass box
*Student:* Place a block inside a clear box, trace each face, and unfold it to see how standard views align.

*How to teach:* Place a block in the clear box; trace its outline on the front, top and right faces. Unfold the box flat to show how the top sits above the front and the right side sits to the right.

- **Q:** Which dimension do the front and top views share?
  **A:** Width.
- **Q:** Which dimension do the front and right-side views share?
  **A:** Height.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Three views
*Student:* Sketch front, top, and right-side views of 6 block models, choosing the most descriptive front view.

*How to teach:* Sketch the three views of 6 block models on grid paper, projecting light lines between views to keep them aligned. Discuss choosing the most descriptive front view.

- **Q:** How should you choose the front view?
  **A:** Pick the view that shows the most characteristic shape of the object, with the fewest hidden lines, usually its longest dimension horizontal.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hidden & center lines
*Student:* Add hidden lines and center lines to drawings of parts with holes and slots.

*How to teach:* Show hidden lines (short dashes) and center lines (long-short-long dash) on parts with holes and slots. Add them to drawings.

- **Q:** How is a hole that goes all the way through a block shown in the front view if you look at it from the side?
  **A:** As two parallel hidden (dashed) lines showing the hole's edges, with a center line between them.
- **Q:** What does a center line show?
  **A:** The axis of symmetry or center of a hole or cylinder.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Visualization
*Student:* Match 12 isometric pictorials to their multiview drawings and sketch the missing view in 6 problems.

*How to teach:* Do the 12 matching problems and sketch the missing view in 6 more, building blocks to check when stuck.

- **Q:** If the top view is a square and the front view is a triangle, what might the object be?
  **A:** A square-based pyramid (or a wedge, depending on the side view) — you need the third view to be sure.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Multiview quiz: draw three views of an unseen object from an isometric.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz: from an isometric pictorial the student hasn't seen, draw three aligned views. Score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Which dimension do the top and right-side views share?
  **A:** Depth.

**Assessment:** Multiview quiz.

**Answer key:** Multiview quiz rubric (4 levels): 4 = front, top and right-side views correctly aligned (top above front, right side to the right, widths and heights projected), all visible edges as solid lines, hidden features as dashed lines, center lines on holes, correct proportions; 3 = one minor missing line; 2 = views misaligned or major missing features; 1 = incorrect views. Written: front and top views share width; front and right-side share height; top and right-side share depth.
