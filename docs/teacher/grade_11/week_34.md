A proficient (3) paper takes a position, uses at least four sources (one literary) put in conversation, evaluates evidence, and addresses a counterclaim. Grammar test key: its/it's; compound modifiers (well-known author, twenty-year-old dream); semicolon between independent clauses; colon after a complete clause; comma splice corrections.

## Algebra II: Comparing treatments and statistics project

**Prepare:** Index cards for shuffling data. Materials for the chosen experiment (paper for airplanes, measuring tape) — let the student choose by Day 2.

**Watch for:**
- Concluding 'no effect' when a difference isn't significant. It means the data don't give convincing evidence, not proof of no difference.
- Running all trials of one treatment first (e.g., when tired). Randomize the order of trials.

### Day 1 (Monday): Randomization test
*Student:* Shuffle data cards from a two-treatment experiment to test whether a difference is significant.

*How to teach:* Write each result from a two-group experiment on a card; shuffle into two piles many times and record the difference in means. Compare to the actual difference.

- **Q:** If only 2 of 100 shuffles give a difference as large as observed, what do you conclude?
  **A:** About 2% chance by random assignment alone, so the difference is statistically significant (unlikely to be due to chance).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Project design
*Student:* Design a randomized experiment with two treatments (e.g., paper airplane wing design) and collect data.

*How to teach:* Student writes question, two treatments, response variable, randomization plan, and controlled variables. Today's task from the plan: Design a randomized experiment with two treatments (e.g., paper airplane wing design) and collect data. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** Name three variables to hold constant in a paper-airplane experiment.
  **A:** Paper type and size, launcher and launch force/angle, and location (indoors, no wind).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Data collection
*Student:* Run the experiment with at least 20 trials per treatment.

*How to teach:* Run at least 20 trials per treatment in random order; record carefully. Today's task from the plan: Run the experiment with at least 20 trials per treatment. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** Why at least 20 trials per treatment?
  **A:** Replication reduces the effect of chance variation so a real difference is easier to detect.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Analysis
*Student:* Analyze results with a randomization test and summary statistics.

*How to teach:* Compute means, medians, and SDs; run a randomization test (spreadsheet or by hand with many shuffles). Today's task from the plan: Analyze results with a randomization test and summary statistics. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What does the p-value from the randomization test mean in your project?
  **A:** The proportion of random re-shuffles giving a difference at least as large as observed: the chance of seeing this difference if the treatment had no effect.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Submit a statistics project report and take the Unit 9 test.

*How to teach:* Collect the report and give the Unit 9 test (about 45 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Submit a statistics project report and take the Unit 9 test. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Why can a well-designed randomized experiment show causation?
  **A:** Random assignment balances other variables, so a significant difference can be attributed to the treatment.

**Assessment:** Statistics project and Unit 9 test.

**Answer key:** Project rubric (4 levels): design (randomization, control, replication), data quality (≥20 per treatment), analysis (summary stats + randomization test), conclusion (answers question, states uncertainty and limitations). Proficient = clear design, correct statistics, and a conclusion that does not overclaim. Unit 9 test sample answers: experiment vs observational identification; z = (85−75)/5 = 2; 95% within 2 SD; MOE ≈ 1/√n; interpretation of a p-value of 0.30 → not significant.

## Physics of the Universe: Energy resources and trade-offs

**Prepare:** California Energy Commission electricity-mix data (energy.ca.gov 'Total System Electric Generation'); a home electric bill; appliance labels.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking any one energy source is perfect. Every source has trade-offs (cost, land use, emissions, reliability).
- Confusing kW (power) with kWh (energy).

### Day 1 (Monday): California's energy mix
*Student:* Graph the California Energy Commission's latest in-state electricity mix and identify trends since 2010.

*How to teach:* Download the California Energy Commission 'Total System Electric Generation' data. The student graphs the share of each source for at least three years since 2010 and describes the trends.

- **Q:** What trends are visible in California's electricity mix since 2010?
  **A:** Solar and wind have grown sharply, coal has nearly disappeared, and natural gas remains a large share.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Home audit
*Student:* Use a home electric bill and appliance labels to estimate household kWh use and carbon emissions.

*How to teach:* Use a home electric bill to find monthly kWh, then appliance labels to estimate which devices use the most. Multiply kWh by an emissions factor (use the utility's stated value, or about 0.2 kg CO₂/kWh) to estimate emissions.

- **Q:** A home uses 500 kWh/month; with an emissions factor of 0.2 kg CO₂/kWh, how much CO₂?
  **A:** 100 kg CO₂ per month.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cost-benefit matrix
*Student:* Compare solar, wind, natural gas, nuclear, and geothermal on cost per kWh, land use, emissions, and reliability.

*How to teach:* The student builds a matrix comparing solar, wind, natural gas, nuclear, and geothermal on cost per kWh, land use, CO₂ emissions, and reliability (can it run at night or on calm days?), citing a source for each row.

- **Q:** Why can't solar alone meet evening peak demand?
  **A:** Solar output drops after sunset when demand peaks, requiring storage or other sources.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Criteria & constraints
*Student:* Define criteria and constraints for meeting a town's evening peak demand, including storage.

*How to teach:* Pose the scenario: a town of 10,000 homes needs power during the 6–9 p.m. peak. The student defines criteria (reliability, low emissions) and constraints (budget, land, existing grid) and considers batteries or other storage.

- **Q:** Name one criterion and one constraint for a town energy plan.
  **A:** Criterion: meet peak demand reliably. Constraint: budget or land available.

### Day 5 (Friday): Recommendation
*Student:* Write and present a one-page recommendation for a community energy plan defending trade-offs.

*How to teach:* The student writes a one-page recommendation for the town and presents it in 5 minutes, defending trade-offs with data from the matrix. Today's task from the plan: Write and present a one-page recommendation for a community energy plan defending trade-offs. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What makes a strong energy recommendation?
  **A:** Clear criteria, evidence-based comparison, and honest trade-offs.

**Assessment:** Energy plan recommendation.

**Answer key:** Recommendation rubric (4-point): states criteria/constraints; compares at least three sources with data; addresses evening peak and storage; justifies trade-offs.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: History research project: question and sources

**Prepare:** Open the National History Day theme page (nhd.org), Calisphere, LOC and DocsTeach. Arrange library database access (public library card) for secondary sources. Annotated bibliography template (Chicago or MLA style per NHD rules).

**Watch for:**
- Students choose topics too broad ('World War II'). Narrow to a specific person, event or decision connected to California and the theme.
- Students treat any old source as primary. A primary source is created at the time by participants or witnesses; a later history book is secondary.
- Students write annotations that only summarize. NHD annotations explain how the source was used and why it is credible.

### Day 1 (Monday): Topic
*Student:* Choose a 20th-century topic connected to California and the annual National History Day theme; write a research question.

*How to teach:* Choose a 20th-century California topic tied to the NHD theme and write a research question.

- **Q:** Narrow 'immigration' into a researchable question.
  **A:** Example: 'How did detention at Angel Island shape Chinese immigrants' communities in San Francisco, 1910–1940?'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Primary sources
*Student:* Find at least six primary sources on Calisphere, LOC, or DocsTeach.

*How to teach:* Find at least six primary sources.

- **Q:** Is a 1936 Dorothea Lange photograph primary or secondary?
  **A:** Primary — created at the time by a witness.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Secondary sources
*Student:* Find three secondary sources from library databases and summarize historians' interpretations.

*How to teach:* Find three secondary sources and summarize historians' interpretations.

- **Q:** What is historiography?
  **A:** The study of how historians' interpretations of an event have changed over time.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Annotated bibliography
*Student:* Write an annotated bibliography separated into primary and secondary sources.

*How to teach:* Write the annotated bibliography.

- **Q:** What should an NHD annotation include?
  **A:** A brief description of the source, how you used it, and why it's credible.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Submit research question, working thesis, and bibliography for review.

*How to teach:* Submit research question, thesis and bibliography for review.

- **Q:** What makes a working thesis strong?
  **A:** It makes an arguable claim that answers the research question and can change as evidence is found.

**Assessment:** Bibliography rubric.

**Answer key:** Bibliography rubric: research question connected to the NHD theme and California; working thesis; at least 6 primary sources (Calisphere, LOC, DocsTeach) and 3 secondary sources (scholarly books or articles); correct citation format; annotations (2–3 sentences each) describing the source and how it was used; separated into primary and secondary. 4 = all criteria met; 3 = minor citation errors; 2 = too few sources or weak annotations; 1 = incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness: Implementing the plan

**Prepare:** Have the fitness journal and written plan ready; heart-rate app or stopwatch. Check access for the lifetime activity.

**Watch for:**
- Not logging because the session 'felt normal.' Self-monitoring keeps the plan honest.
- Changing many variables at once. Adjust one variable at a time to know what works.
- Ignoring enjoyment. Enjoyment strongly predicts long-term adherence.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cardio session
*Student:* Complete the cardio workout from your plan (e.g., 30-minute tempo run or bike) and log average heart rate and RPE.

*How to teach:* Complete the plan's cardio workout; log average HR and RPE.

- **Q:** Your average HR was 158 and your 60–80% zone is 150–177. Were you in zone?
  **A:** Yes, 158 falls within 150–177.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Strength session
*Student:* Complete the strength workout from your plan and log sets, reps, and loads for each exercise.

*How to teach:* Complete the strength workout; log sets, reps, loads.

- **Q:** Why log loads for each exercise?
  **A:** To track progressive overload and know when to increase.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Lifetime activity
*Student:* Spend 45 minutes on one of your chosen lifetime activities (pickleball, hiking, dance, swimming) and log duration and enjoyment rating.

*How to teach:* Spend 45 minutes on a chosen lifetime activity; log duration and enjoyment (1–10).

- **Q:** Why rate enjoyment?
  **A:** Enjoyable activities are more likely to be continued (adherence).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mobility session
*Student:* Complete your 20-minute flexibility and yoga routine and note any tight areas.

*How to teach:* 20-minute flexibility/yoga routine; note tight areas.

- **Q:** How often should flexibility work be done?
  **A:** At least 2–3 days per week; daily is beneficial.

### Day 5 (Friday): Plan review
*Student:* Compare the week's logs with your plan, identify one adjustment (intensity, time, or type), and revise the written plan.

*How to teach:* Compare logs to the plan, identify one adjustment, and revise.

- **Q:** Your RPE in every cardio session was 3. What adjustment fits the FITT principle?
  **A:** Increase intensity (pace or intervals) or time, applying overload gradually.

**Assessment:** Plan implementation log.

**Answer key:** Plan implementation log rubric. 4 – All 5 sessions logged with required data (HR, RPE, sets/reps/loads, duration, enjoyment, tight areas), honest comparison to plan, one well-justified adjustment written into the revised plan. 3 (proficient) – Complete log, comparison, and one adjustment. 2 – Missing sessions or adjustment. 1 – Incomplete.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Capstone: testing and iteration

**Prepare:** Have the prototype, measurement tools and a spreadsheet. Arrange time for the user to try the prototype. Print the specification list from Week 29.

**Watch for:**
- Testing only what works. Test every specification, especially the riskiest.
- Leading the user during user testing. Give the task, then observe silently.
- Claiming success without data. Each specification needs pass/fail evidence from repeated trials.

### Day 1 (Monday): Test plan
*Student:* Write a test procedure for each design specification with pass/fail criteria.

*How to teach:* Write a test procedure for each specification with pass/fail criteria.

- **Q:** Write pass/fail criteria for 'opens in under 10 seconds'.
  **A:** Pass if a first-time user opens it in less than 10 s in at least 4 of 5 trials (or mean < 10 s); fail otherwise.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Testing
*Student:* Run at least five trials per test and record data in a spreadsheet with mean and standard deviation.

*How to teach:* Run five or more trials per test; compute mean and SD in the spreadsheet.

- **Q:** Times: 8, 9, 7, 12, 9 s. Mean?
  **A:** 45 ÷ 5 = 9 s.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): User testing
*Student:* Have your user try the prototype while you observe; record quotes and pain points.

*How to teach:* The user tries the prototype while the student observes and records quotes and pain points.

- **Q:** Why stay silent while the user tests?
  **A:** Explaining hides real usability problems the user would face alone.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Iterate
*Student:* Make at least one evidence-based design improvement.

*How to teach:* Make at least one evidence-based improvement.

- **Q:** What makes an improvement 'evidence-based'?
  **A:** It addresses a specific failure seen in test data or user observations.

### Day 5 (Friday): Retest
*Student:* Retest the changed feature and summarize whether each specification is now met.

*How to teach:* Retest and summarize which specifications are met.

- **Q:** Why retest after changing the design?
  **A:** To confirm the change fixed the problem without creating new ones.

**Assessment:** Test report.

**Answer key:** Test report rubric: 4 = test procedure per specification with pass/fail criteria; at least five trials each in a spreadsheet with mean and standard deviation; user-test observations with quotes and pain points; at least one evidence-based improvement; retest data and a summary table showing each specification met or not; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = few trials or no retest; 1 = incomplete.
