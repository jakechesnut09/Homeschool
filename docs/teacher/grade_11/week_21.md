# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 21

## English 11 – American Literature: The Great Gatsby, Ch. 4–9

**Prepare:** Finish The Great Gatsby (Ch. 4–9). Find the party or ending scene from the 1974 (Robert Redford) and 2013 (Baz Luhrmann) films. Print an argument rubric.

**Watch for:**
- Saying Gatsby killed Myrtle. Daisy was driving Gatsby's car; Gatsby lets others believe he was, which leads Wilson to him.
- Treating Gatsby as purely heroic or purely fraudulent. Nick says he is 'worth the whole damn bunch put together' while also showing his ties to the criminal Wolfsheim; a strong essay holds both.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ch. 4–5
*Student:* Read; analyze Gatsby's invented biography and the reunion scene's use of weather.

*How to teach:* Read Ch. 4–5. The student compares Gatsby's story of himself (Ch. 4) with what Ch. 6 later reveals, and tracks the weather during the reunion at Nick's house.

- **Q:** How does the weather mirror the reunion in Ch. 5?
  **A:** It is pouring rain while the meeting is awkward and tense; as Gatsby and Daisy reconcile the rain stops and the sun comes out, matching Gatsby's joy.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ch. 6–7
*Student:* Read; trace the confrontation at the Plaza and how money divides 'old' and 'new' wealth.

*How to teach:* Read Ch. 6–7. Trace the Plaza Hotel confrontation and how Tom attacks Gatsby as new money. Today's task from the plan: Read; trace the confrontation at the Plaza and how money divides 'old' and 'new' wealth. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What does Gatsby mean when he says Daisy's voice is 'full of money'?
  **A:** Her charm is inseparable from her wealth and class; what he loves in her is tied to the status he wants.
- **Q:** Who is driving when Myrtle is killed?
  **A:** Daisy, driving Gatsby's yellow car; Gatsby plans to take the blame.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ch. 8–9
*Student:* Finish the novel; annotate the final page's 'boats against the current' passage.

*How to teach:* Read Ch. 8–9. Have the student annotate the last page and the 'careless people' paragraph. Today's task from the plan: Finish the novel; annotate the final page's 'boats against the current' passage. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Interpret 'So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.'
  **A:** People keep striving toward the future but are constantly pulled back by the past; Gatsby's dream of repeating the past was impossible.
- **Q:** Why does Nick call Tom and Daisy 'careless people'?
  **A:** They 'smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money,' letting others (Gatsby, Myrtle, Wilson) pay for their actions.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Interpretations
*Student:* Compare the 1974 and 2013 party or ending scenes; evaluate how each interprets Fitzgerald.

*How to teach:* Watch the same scene from both films. The student charts music, color, narration, and casting and decides which version better captures Fitzgerald's critique.

- **Q:** What should a strong film comparison evaluate?
  **A:** Specific choices (e.g., Luhrmann's modern hip-hop soundtrack and spectacle vs. the 1974 film's restraint) and whether each criticizes or glamorizes the excess the novel critiques.

### Day 5 (Friday): Essay & check
*Student:* Write a 900-word literary argument: Is The Great Gatsby a critique or a celebration of the American Dream?

*How to teach:* The student writes the 900-word literary argument (critique or celebration of the American Dream), including a counterclaim. Today's task from the plan: Write a 900-word literary argument: Is The Great Gatsby a critique or a celebration of the American Dream?. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Give a strong thesis for the critique side.
  **A:** Fitzgerald critiques the American Dream: Gatsby's self-made wealth cannot buy acceptance or Daisy, and the 'careless' rich escape all consequences while Gatsby dies alone.

**Assessment:** Literary argument essay on Gatsby.

**Answer key:** Literary argument on Gatsby rubric (4 = exceeds, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning) on four rows: Claim/Thesis (arguable, specific, answers the prompt); Evidence (well-chosen quotations, introduced and cited); Analysis/Reasoning (explains HOW evidence supports the claim, not just what it says); Conventions & organization (logical paragraphs, transitions, few errors). A proficient (3) paper takes a clear position, uses at least four quotations (e.g., the green light, 'careless people,' Daisy's voice 'full of money,' the final 'boats against the current'), and answers a counterclaim about Gatsby's 'extraordinary gift for hope.'

## Algebra II: Logarithms and their graphs

**Prepare:** Desmos. Make a small card: log_b(x) = y means b^y = x.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking log(x + 2) = log x + log 2. Logs turn products into sums, not sums into anything.
- Trying to take log of 0 or a negative number. The domain of log_b x is x > 0.

### Day 1 (Monday): Defining logs
*Student:* Convert between exponential and logarithmic form; evaluate log₂32 mentally.

*How to teach:* Teach 'a log is an exponent.' Student converts 10 forms each way and evaluates mentally. Today's task from the plan: Convert between exponential and logarithmic form; evaluate log₂32 mentally. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Evaluate log₂32 and log₃(1/9).
  **A:** 5 (2⁵ = 32) and −2 (3⁻² = 1/9).
- **Q:** Write 10³ = 1000 in log form.
  **A:** log 1000 = 3.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Common & natural logs
*Student:* Evaluate log and ln with a calculator; use the change-of-base formula.

*How to teach:* Use calculator log and ln; derive change of base as log_b x = ln x/ln b. Today's task from the plan: Evaluate log and ln with a calculator; use the change-of-base formula. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Evaluate log₅40.
  **A:** ln 40/ln 5 ≈ 2.292.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Graphs
*Student:* Graph y = log_b x as the inverse of y = b^x; identify asymptote and domain.

*How to teach:* Graph y = 2^x and y = log₂x and y = x; show the reflection. Today's task from the plan: Graph y = log_b x as the inverse of y = b^x; identify asymptote and domain. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** State the domain, asymptote, and two points of y = log₂x.
  **A:** Domain x > 0, vertical asymptote x = 0; points (1, 0), (2, 1), (8, 3).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Transformations
*Student:* Graph transformed log functions in Desmos and state features.

*How to teach:* Graph transformed logs in Desmos; student states features of four. Today's task from the plan: Graph transformed log functions in Desmos and state features. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Give the domain, asymptote, and x-intercept of y = log₃(x + 2) − 1.
  **A:** Domain x > −2; VA x = −2; x-intercept: log₃(x + 2) = 1 → x = 1.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on log conversions and graphs.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (about 30 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Quiz on log conversions and graphs. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Solve log_x 49 = 2.
  **A:** x² = 49 and the base must be positive: x = 7.

**Assessment:** Logarithm quiz.

**Answer key:** Sample key: 1) log₄64 = 3. 2) log 0.01 = −2. 3) ln e⁵ = 5. 4) 2⁻³ = 1/8 ↔ log₂(1/8) = −3. 5) log₇20 ≈ 1.540. 6) y = log(x−3): domain x>3, VA x=3, x-int (4,0). 7) Sketch y=log₂x reflecting y=2^x over y=x.

## Physics of the Universe: The electromagnetic spectrum

**Prepare:** UV-sensitive beads (online/craft stores), sunscreen of different SPF, plastic wrap, sunglasses, fabric samples. Access to credible sources (FDA, WHO, National Cancer Institute, FCC).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all 'radiation' is dangerous. Only high-frequency radiation (UV-C/X-ray/gamma, and high-energy UV) is ionizing; radio waves and visible light are not.
- Thinking a source is credible because it sounds scientific. Check author expertise, evidence, peer review, and conflicts of interest.

### Day 1 (Monday): EM spectrum
*Student:* Build a scale model of the spectrum.

*How to teach:* Build a scale model of the spectrum (radio → gamma) on a long strip with example uses and wavelengths. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** List the EM spectrum from lowest to highest frequency.
  **A:** Radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma ray.
- **Q:** What is the wavelength of 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi?
  **A:** λ = c/f = 3 × 10⁸ / 2.4 × 10⁹ = 0.125 m (12.5 cm).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): UV lab
*Student:* Test sunscreen and fabrics with UV beads.

*How to teach:* Test UV beads in sunlight under plastic wrap, sunscreen-coated wrap, sunglasses, and fabrics; record color intensity. Today's task from the plan: Test sunscreen and fabrics with UV beads. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What do UV beads that stay white under sunscreen-coated wrap show?
  **A:** The sunscreen absorbs or blocks UV radiation.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ionizing radiation
*Student:* Research ionizing vs. non-ionizing radiation.

*How to teach:* Research ionizing vs non-ionizing radiation. Today's task from the plan: Research ionizing vs. non-ionizing radiation. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why is UV more harmful than visible light?
  **A:** UV photons have higher frequency and therefore more energy (E = hf), enough to damage DNA in skin cells.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Evaluating claims
*Student:* Evaluate claims about cell phones and 5G health effects using credible sources.

*How to teach:* The student evaluates two online claims about cell phones/5G health effects using credible sources. Today's task from the plan: Evaluate claims about cell phones and 5G health effects using credible sources. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What do major health agencies conclude about 5G?
  **A:** 5G uses non-ionizing radio waves; at exposure levels within guidelines, agencies like the FCC and WHO have found no established health risk.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a claim evaluation report.

*How to teach:* Write the claim evaluation report. Today's task from the plan: Write a claim evaluation report. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What criteria should be used to evaluate a health claim?
  **A:** Source expertise and credibility, quality of evidence (peer-reviewed, replicated), whether it explains a physical mechanism, and possible bias.

**Assessment:** Claim evaluation report.

**Answer key:** Claim evaluation report rubric (4-point per row): states the claim and source; evaluates evidence with at least two credible sources; explains the physics (ionizing vs non-ionizing, photon energy); reaches a justified conclusion. Proficient = recognizes 5G as non-ionizing and cites agency evidence.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Hot wars and the Red Scare

**Prepare:** Print a Korean War phases map, Truman's statement on firing MacArthur (April 1951), McCarthy's Wheeling speech excerpt (Feb. 9, 1950), Margaret Chase Smith's 'Declaration of Conscience' (June 1, 1950), Hollywood Ten statements, and the film 'Duck and Cover' (1951).

**Watch for:**
- Students think the Korean War ended with a peace treaty. It ended with an armistice (July 27, 1953); technically the war never formally ended.
- Students think McCarthy found many spies. Some Soviet espionage was real (e.g., the Rosenbergs, revealed by Venona), but McCarthy's accusations were largely unsupported and ruined many innocent people.
- Students think 'Duck and Cover' was scientifically sound. It offered limited protection and also served to reduce panic and build support for civil defense.

### Day 1 (Monday): Korean War
*Student:* Map the phases of the Korean War and analyze Truman's firing of MacArthur.

*How to teach:* Map the phases of the Korean War and analyze Truman's firing of MacArthur.

- **Q:** Why did Truman fire MacArthur?
  **A:** MacArthur publicly challenged Truman's limited-war policy, urging attacks on China; Truman upheld civilian control of the military.
- **Q:** What were the main phases of the war?
  **A:** North Korean invasion to the Pusan Perimeter (1950), Inchon landing and UN advance to the Yalu, Chinese intervention and retreat, stalemate near the 38th parallel until the 1953 armistice.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Red Scare
*Student:* Read excerpts from McCarthy's Wheeling speech and Margaret Chase Smith's 'Declaration of Conscience'.

*How to teach:* Read excerpts from McCarthy's Wheeling speech and Smith's 'Declaration of Conscience'.

- **Q:** What did McCarthy claim at Wheeling?
  **A:** That he had a list of communists working in the State Department (he cited 205 names).
- **Q:** What was Margaret Chase Smith's main point?
  **A:** McCarthy's smear tactics threatened Americans' right to criticize, hold unpopular beliefs and protest — 'the right to independent thought'.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hollywood & HUAC
*Student:* Study the Hollywood Ten and the blacklist's effect on California's film industry.

*How to teach:* Study the Hollywood Ten and the blacklist.

- **Q:** Who were the Hollywood Ten?
  **A:** Screenwriters and directors who refused to answer HUAC's questions about communist ties in 1947, were cited for contempt, jailed and blacklisted.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Nuclear age
*Student:* Watch 'Duck and Cover' and evaluate it as propaganda; explain the arms race and SEATO.

*How to teach:* Watch 'Duck and Cover', evaluate it as propaganda; explain the arms race and SEATO.

- **Q:** What was brinkmanship?
  **A:** Secretary of State Dulles's policy of pushing to the edge of war to force the other side to back down.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a comparison of the first and second Red Scares.

*How to teach:* The student writes a comparison of the first and second Red Scares.

- **Q:** When did McCarthy's influence collapse?
  **A:** After the televised Army-McCarthy hearings (1954) and his Senate censure in December 1954.

**Assessment:** Comparison essay.

**Answer key:** Comparison essay rubric: 4 = compares the first Red Scare (1919–20: Palmer Raids, deportations of immigrant radicals, fear after the Russian Revolution) and second (late 1940s–1950s: HUAC, McCarthy, loyalty oaths, Hollywood blacklist, Rosenbergs); similarities (fear of communism, civil liberties violations, targeting of dissent) and differences (scale, Cold War nuclear context, role of television, federal loyalty programs); thesis and evidence. 3 = solid with fewer specifics. 2 = general. 1 = inaccurate.

## Spanish IV: El ocio y el bienestar

**Prepare:** Find a Spanish article on Spain's long workday and the siesta myth (Spain uses Central European Time since 1940 though it lies geographically in the GMT zone; meals and work hours run late; surveys show most Spaniards do not take a daily siesta). Chart hypothetical si clauses: si + imperfect subjunctive → conditional. Prepare a free-time survey.

**Watch for:**
- Using the conditional in the si clause (*Si tendría tiempo): Si tuviera tiempo, viajaría.
- Thinking all Spaniards nap every day: the siesta is more myth than daily habit; long workdays with a long lunch break are the real pattern.
- Translating 'If I was' with the indicative imperfect for hypotheticals (*Si era rico, viajaría): Si fuera rico, viajaría.

### Day 1 (Monday): Lectura
*Student:* Read an article on Spain's long workday and the myth of the siesta; answer 10 comprehension questions.

*How to teach:* Read the article; your child answers 10 comprehension questions.

- **Q:** Why are Spanish schedules late compared to the sun?
  **A:** Since 1940 Spain has used Central European Time even though it lies geographically in the GMT zone, so clocks run ahead of the sun, and daily routines (lunch around 2–3 p.m., dinner around 9–10 p.m.) shifted late.
- **Q:** Is the daily siesta common in Spain today?
  **A:** No; most Spaniards do not nap regularly — long workdays with a long lunch break are more typical.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Si hipotético
*Student:* Learn si + imperfect subjunctive → conditional (Si tuviera más tiempo, viajaría) and complete 15 transformations; compare with English 'if I were'.

*How to teach:* Teach hypothetical si clauses and compare with English 'If I were'; your child completes 15 transformations.

- **Q:** Transform to hypothetical: 'Si tengo más tiempo, viajaré.'
  **A:** Si tuviera más tiempo, viajaría.
- **Q:** Translate: 'If I were you, I would rest more.'
  **A:** Si yo fuera tú, descansaría más.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Encuesta
*Student:* Survey five people (in Spanish or translated) about free-time habits and graph the results with Spanish labels.

*How to teach:* Your child surveys five people about free-time habits and graphs results with Spanish labels.

- **Q:** Ask: 'What do you do in your free time?'
  **A:** ¿Qué haces en tu tiempo libre?

### Day 4 (Thursday): Conversación
*Student:* Discuss ¿Qué harías si no tuvieras que trabajar? for 10 minutes using at least eight si clauses.

*How to teach:* Discuss ¿Qué harías si no tuvieras que trabajar? for 10 minutes; your child uses at least eight si clauses.

- **Q:** Answer the question in one sentence.
  **A:** Example: Si no tuviera que trabajar, viajaría por Latinoamérica y aprendería a tocar la guitarra.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Write a 200-word cultural comparison of leisure in Spain and the U.S.; quiz on hypothetical si clauses.

*How to teach:* Your child writes the cultural comparison; give the si-clause quiz.

- **Q:** Correct: 'Si yo tendría dinero, compraría una casa.'
  **A:** Si yo tuviera dinero, compraría una casa.

**Assessment:** Cultural comparison and quiz.

**Answer key:** Cultural comparison rubric (200 words): 4 = compares work hours, meal times, and leisure in Spain and the U.S. with evidence from the article and survey, uses comparison phrases and 2+ si clauses; 3 = minor errors; 2 = one-sided; 1 = incomplete. Quiz sample key: 1) Si tuviera más tiempo, viajaría 2) Si fuéramos a España, comeríamos más tarde 3) Si pudiera, dormiría la siesta 4) ¿Qué harías si no tuvieras que trabajar? 5) Si gano, celebraré (real) vs. Si ganara, celebraría (hypothetical) 6) Si él supiera la verdad, se enojaría.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Diverse American voices: Hansberry, Valdez, and Wilson

**Prepare:** Borrow A Raisin in the Sun (Hansberry, 1959) and Fences (Wilson, 1985 premiere/1987 Broadway) for excerpts, and find Luis Valdez's acto Las Dos Caras del Patroncito (1965) — in it a farmworker and his masked boss trade places. Parental note: Fences contains strong language; select excerpts.

**Watch for:**
- Treating actos as polished plays. Actos were short, improvised-feeling sketches performed on flatbed trucks and in fields to educate and energize striking farmworkers.
- Reading these plays as only about race. They are also about family, dreams, dignity, and generational conflict.
- Researching a playwright without the community context.

### Day 1 (Monday): Teatro Campesino
*Student:* Learn about Luis Valdez and El Teatro Campesino's actos performed for striking farmworkers in Delano.

*How to teach:* Learn about El Teatro Campesino (founded 1965 by Luis Valdez during the Delano grape strike, with the United Farm Workers), using masks, placards, humor, and bilingual dialogue.

- **Q:** What was the purpose of the actos of El Teatro Campesino?
  **A:** To dramatize farmworkers' conditions, explain the strike and union, and inspire workers — theatre as social action.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Acto
*Student:* Read and rehearse Las Dos Caras del Patroncito with masks and placards.

*How to teach:* Rehearse Las Dos Caras del Patroncito with a pig-like mask for the boss and placards naming characters.

- **Q:** Why use placards and masks in an acto?
  **A:** They identify characters instantly, make types broad and clear for outdoor audiences, and add satire.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hansberry & Wilson
*Student:* Read excerpts from A Raisin in the Sun and Fences; compare family conflicts.

*How to teach:* Read excerpts: Raisin (the Younger family's $10,000 insurance check; Mama buys a house in all-white Clybourne Park; Walter loses money; refuses Lindner's buyout). Fences (Troy Maxson, former Negro Leagues player and Pittsburgh sanitation worker, blocks his son Cory's football scholarship). Compare the father/son and family conflicts.

- **Q:** What does Walter Lee ultimately decide when Karl Lindner offers to buy the family out?
  **A:** He refuses the offer and the family moves into the house in Clybourne Park, reclaiming his dignity.
- **Q:** Why does Troy oppose Cory's football recruitment?
  **A:** Troy was kept out of the major leagues by racism and believes Cory will be hurt the same way; he also resents and fears his son's opportunities — he insists Cory keep his job.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Research
*Student:* Research one playwright of color and the community their work speaks for.

*How to teach:* Student researches one playwright of color: biography, key works, community, and themes.

- **Q:** What is August Wilson's Century Cycle?
  **A:** Ten plays, each set in a different decade of the 20th century, depicting African American life (nine set in Pittsburgh's Hill District).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Present a 4-minute research talk with a short performed excerpt.

*How to teach:* 4-minute research talk with a performed excerpt.

- **Q:** Where does the title A Raisin in the Sun come from?
  **A:** Langston Hughes's poem 'Harlem': 'What happens to a dream deferred? / Does it dry up / like a raisin in the sun?'

**Assessment:** Research talk.

**Answer key:** Research talk rubric. 4 – Accurate biography, two works, community/historical context, themes explained, well-chosen performed excerpt connected to the research, sources cited, within 4 minutes. 3 (proficient) – Accurate facts, context, excerpt performed. 2 – Thin research or no connection. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness: Orienteering and navigation

**Prepare:** Get a baseplate compass and print a park map (topographic if possible). Measure a 100 m course (e.g., a track straightaway is ~100 m). Prepare 8 flags or markers and design a simple course with bearings and distances. Note that in California magnetic declination is roughly 11–13° east.

**Watch for:**
- Holding the compass near metal or a phone, which deflects the needle.
- Reading contour lines as trails. Contour lines connect points of equal elevation; close together = steep.
- Following the needle instead of the direction-of-travel arrow. 'Red in the shed': align the north needle with the orienting arrow, then walk where the direction-of-travel arrow points.

### Day 1 (Monday): Map reading
*Student:* Learn map symbols and contour lines.

*How to teach:* Teach map legend, scale, and contour lines. Have the student find the steepest slope and a hilltop on a topo map.

- **Q:** What does it mean when contour lines are close together?
  **A:** The terrain is steep.
- **Q:** A map scale is 1:24,000. How far is 1 inch on the map?
  **A:** 24,000 inches = 2,000 feet on the ground.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Compass bearings
*Student:* Learn to take and follow bearings.

*How to teach:* Teach compass parts; take a bearing to an object (point direction-of-travel arrow, rotate housing until orienting arrow aligns with the red needle, read the degrees). Then follow a given bearing.

- **Q:** What bearing is due east? Due south-west?
  **A:** East = 90°; southwest = 225°.
- **Q:** What is the back bearing of 70°?
  **A:** 250° (add 180°).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pace count
*Student:* Determine your pace count over 100 meters.

*How to teach:* Walk 100 m counting every time the same foot lands (double paces). Repeat 3 times and average. Practice estimating a 50 m distance.

- **Q:** Your pace count is 64 per 100 m. How many paces for 250 m?
  **A:** 64 × 2.5 = 160 paces.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Orienteering course
*Student:* Complete a family-designed orienteering course with 8 control points.

*How to teach:* Family sets 8 control points; student navigates using bearings and pace counts, recording control codes. Time the course.

- **Q:** What is a control point in orienteering?
  **A:** A marked checkpoint on the course that the navigator must find and record in order.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Navigation skills test.

*How to teach:* Navigation test: take/follow bearings, read map features, compute distances with scale and pace count.

- **Q:** Why adjust for declination?
  **A:** Magnetic north differs from true (map) north; in California the compass needle points about 11–13° east of true north, so bearings must be corrected.

**Assessment:** Navigation test.

**Answer key:** Navigation test key (sample): 1) North 0°/360°, east 90°, south 180°, west 270°. 2) Back bearing of 120° = 300°. 3) Close contour lines = steep slope; concentric rings = hilltop. 4) On a 1:24,000 map, 3 inches = 6,000 ft. 5) Pace count 60/100 m → 300 m = 180 paces. 6) Practical: follow a 3-leg course to within ~10 m of each target. Proficient = 80%+ written and all practical legs completed.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Prototyping

**Prepare:** Gather cardboard, foam board, hot glue gun, craft knife, cutting mat, metal ruler and safety glasses. Check whether a local library makerspace has a 3D printer and its booking process. Find an IDEO prototyping example video.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking prototypes must look finished. Low-fidelity prototypes are deliberately rough to test ideas quickly and cheaply.
- Cutting toward the hand or without a mat. Always cut away from yourself on a mat, with a sharp blade (dull blades slip).
- Skipping the mock-up and going straight to printing. Cheap mock-ups catch problems (angle, size) before spending hours printing.

### Day 1 (Monday): Prototype types
*Student:* Compare low- and high-fidelity prototypes using examples from IDEO and product teardown videos; review craft-knife and hot-glue safety.

*How to teach:* Compare low-fidelity (sketches, cardboard, foam) and high-fidelity (3D printed, machined, near-final materials) prototypes. Review craft-knife and hot-glue safety (hot glue can burn; keep the gun on its stand).

- **Q:** What's the purpose of a low-fidelity prototype?
  **A:** To test ideas quickly and cheaply early in the design process, before investing in detailed designs.
- **Q:** Name two hot-glue safety rules.
  **A:** Don't touch the nozzle or fresh glue (burns); set the gun on its stand and unplug when finished.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cardboard mock-up
*Student:* Build a full-size cardboard mock-up of a phone or tablet stand and test viewing angle with your device.

*How to teach:* Build a full-size cardboard phone/tablet stand; test with the real device for viewing angle, stability and cable access.

- **Q:** Your device slides off the stand. What change might fix it?
  **A:** Add a lip at the front edge or a higher-friction surface, or reduce the angle.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): CAD refinement
*Student:* Update the stand in Onshape based on what the mock-up revealed (angle, cable slot, stability).

*How to teach:* Update the stand in Onshape using the mock-up findings.

- **Q:** Why update the CAD model after a mock-up?
  **A:** So the digital design reflects what was learned and the next prototype is better.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Print or build
*Student:* Export an STL and print at a library makerspace, or build a refined version from foam board using the CAD drawing as a template.

*How to teach:* Export an STL and print at a makerspace, or build a refined foam-board version using the drawing as a template.

- **Q:** What's one advantage of a 3D-printed prototype over cardboard?
  **A:** It's more accurate and durable, closer to the final product, so tests of fit and strength are more realistic.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Photograph both prototypes and write a notebook entry comparing time, cost, and what each revealed.

*How to teach:* Write the comparison entry; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Which prototype gave more useful information for its cost?
  **A:** Answers vary; often the cardboard mock-up because it revealed major problems quickly and almost for free.

**Assessment:** Prototype comparison entry.

**Answer key:** Prototype comparison entry rubric: 4 = photos of both prototypes; table comparing time, material cost and what each revealed (e.g. cardboard showed the 70° angle was too steep; printed version tested cable slot fit); explains one change made in CAD because of the mock-up; 3 = all parts, less detail; 2 = only one prototype or no comparison; 1 = incomplete.
