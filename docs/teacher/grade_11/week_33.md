# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 33

## English 11 – American Literature: Contemporary poetry and Native American voices

**Prepare:** Poetry Foundation pages for Joy Harjo's 'Remember' and 'Perhaps the World Ends Here,' Li-Young Lee's 'Persimmons,' Rita Dove's 'Testimonial,' and Natalie Diaz's 'The Facts of Art.' Read the short poet biographies. Phone to record performance.

**Watch for:**
- Treating repetition as filler. In Harjo, repetition works like a chant or prayer, building rhythm and meaning.
- Ignoring the poet's background. Harjo (Muscogee Nation, former U.S. Poet Laureate) and Diaz (Mojave) write from Native histories that shape the poems' meanings.

### Day 1 (Monday): Harjo
*Student:* Read two Harjo poems; analyze repetition and the connection between land and identity.

*How to teach:* Read both Harjo poems aloud. The student marks repetition and connections between land and identity. Today's task from the plan: Read two Harjo poems; analyze repetition and the connection between land and identity. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What is repeated in 'Remember' and why?
  **A:** The command 'Remember' begins line after line (the sky, the moon, the earth, your mother); like a ritual, it ties personal identity to land, ancestors, and all living things ('you are all people and all people are you').
- **Q:** What does the kitchen table represent in 'Perhaps the World Ends Here'?
  **A:** The center of human life: 'The world begins at a kitchen table'; births, grief, celebration, and survival happen there, and perhaps the world will end there too.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Lee & Dove
*Student:* Read 'Persimmons' and 'Testimonial'; trace imagery of fruit, memory, and language.

*How to teach:* Read 'Persimmons' and 'Testimonial.' The student traces images of fruit, memory, and language. Today's task from the plan: Read 'Persimmons' and 'Testimonial'; trace imagery of fruit, memory, and language. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What happened in sixth grade in 'Persimmons'?
  **A:** Mrs. Walker slapped the speaker for not knowing the difference between 'persimmon' and 'precision'; the poem shows she didn't know persimmons as he and his family do.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Diaz
*Student:* Read 'The Facts of Art'; discuss how humor and history combine in the poem.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Facts of Art' with its epigraph. Discuss how humor and history combine. Today's task from the plan: Read 'The Facts of Art'; discuss how humor and history combine in the poem. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** How does Diaz combine humor and history?
  **A:** She uses irony and dark humor to retell a historical episode of government officials imposing their ideas on Native people, exposing condescension while making readers laugh uncomfortably.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Performance prep
*Student:* Choose a contemporary American poem and prepare an oral interpretation with a written introduction.

*How to teach:* The student chooses a contemporary American poem and prepares an oral interpretation and a written introduction (poet, context, why chosen). Today's task from the plan: Choose a contemporary American poem and prepare an oral interpretation with a written introduction. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What makes an oral interpretation strong?
  **A:** Accurate reading, pauses that follow punctuation and line breaks purposefully, tone that matches the poem's shifts, and eye contact.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Perform the poem (recorded) and submit a 300-word explication.

*How to teach:* Record the performance. Collect a 300-word explication. Today's task from the plan: Perform the poem (recorded) and submit a 300-word explication. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What does an explication do?
  **A:** It explains how a poem's devices (imagery, sound, form, line breaks) create its meaning, moving line by line or by section with quotations.

**Assessment:** Performance and explication rubric.

**Answer key:** Performance rubric (4-point): accuracy, pacing and pauses, expressive tone, introduction (poet and context). Explication rubric (4-point): central idea stated; at least three devices analyzed with quotations; explanation of how form supports meaning; conventions.

## Algebra II: Simulation and margin of error

**Prepare:** Coins, dice, and a spreadsheet with =RAND() or =RANDBETWEEN(). Find a current poll with a margin of error.

**Watch for:**
- Interpreting a margin of error as covering all possible errors. It accounts only for random sampling variability, not biased questions or samples.
- Thinking a single unusual result proves unfairness. Compare the result with the spread of many simulated results.

### Day 1 (Monday): Is it fair?
*Student:* Flip a coin 50 times; simulate 100 sets in a spreadsheet to judge whether results are consistent with fairness.

*How to teach:* Flip a coin 50 times and count heads. In a spreadsheet, simulate 100 sets of 50 fair flips and make a dot plot of head counts. Today's task from the plan: Flip a coin 50 times; simulate 100 sets in a spreadsheet to judge whether results are consistent with fairness. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** If you got 32 heads in 50 flips, is the coin likely unfair?
  **A:** With a fair coin, 32 or more heads happens only about 3% of the time, so 32 is unusual and gives some evidence the coin is unfair (but is not proof).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sampling distributions
*Student:* Simulate many samples of size 50 from a known population proportion and plot proportions.

*How to teach:* Simulate 200 samples of size 50 from a population with p = 0.40; plot the sample proportions. Today's task from the plan: Simulate many samples of size 50 from a known population proportion and plot proportions. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** Describe the distribution of sample proportions.
  **A:** Centered near 0.40, roughly bell-shaped, with most proportions within about ±0.14 (2 SD, since SD = √(0.4·0.6/50) ≈ 0.069).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Margin of error
*Student:* Estimate margin of error from simulation and compare to 1/√n.

*How to teach:* Estimate the margin of error as about 2 SD from the simulation and compare with 1/√n. Today's task from the plan: Estimate margin of error from simulation and compare to 1/√n. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** What is the approximate margin of error for n = 400? For n = 1,000?
  **A:** 1/√400 = 0.05 (±5%); 1/√1000 ≈ 0.032 (±3.2%).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fair decisions
*Student:* Use random number generators to make fair decisions and analyze strategies.

*How to teach:* Use random numbers to pick fairly (e.g., chores); analyze a game strategy with simulation. Today's task from the plan: Use random number generators to make fair decisions and analyze strategies. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** How would you use a die to choose fairly among three people?
  **A:** Assign 1–2, 3–4, 5–6 to each person; each has probability 1/3.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz interpreting a poll's margin of error.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz on interpreting margins of error (about 30 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Quiz interpreting a poll's margin of error. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** A poll says 52% support a measure, ±3%. Can you conclude a majority supports it?
  **A:** No. The plausible range is 49%–55%, which includes values below 50%.

**Assessment:** Simulation quiz.

**Answer key:** Sample key: 1) 45% ±4% → plausible 41%–49%. 2) Quadrupling n halves the margin of error. 3) n = 2,500 → MOE ≈ 2%. 4) Explain why a self-selected online poll's margin of error is meaningless (bias). 5) Simulation shows 18–32 heads covers about 95% of fair 50-flip results; decide whether 35 heads is unusual (yes).

## Physics of the Universe: Earth's formation and early history

**Prepare:** Two colors of candy or beans (100 of one color) in a bag; images of Moon craters; a roll of toilet paper or 4.6 m of string; NASA solar system formation overview.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Earth's oldest rocks date Earth's formation. Plate tectonics and erosion recycle crust; meteorites and Moon rocks preserve older ages.
- Thinking radiometric dating measures how much of an element is in a rock. It uses the ratio of parent to daughter isotopes and the known half-life.

### Day 1 (Monday): Accretion
*Student:* Read NASA's solar system formation overview and diagram the steps from nebula to differentiated Earth.

*How to teach:* Read NASA's overview of solar system formation. The student draws a 5-step diagram: collapsing nebula → spinning disk → planetesimals (accretion) → molten young Earth → differentiated layers (iron sinks to form the core).

- **Q:** What is accretion?
  **A:** Dust and rock clump together by collisions and gravity into larger bodies (planetesimals and planets).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Radiometric dating model
*Student:* Shake a bag of 100 'parent' candies, swap 'decayed' ones for a second color each round, and use the parent/daughter ratio to date mystery samples.

*How to teach:* Shake the bag; replace 'decayed' candies with the other color each round. Use ratios to date mystery samples. Today's task from the plan: Shake a bag of 100 'parent' candies, swap 'decayed' ones for a second color each round, and use the parent/daughter ratio to date mystery samples. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** A sample has 25% parent and 75% daughter. If the half-life is 1.3 billion years, how old is it?
  **A:** Two half-lives: 2.6 billion years.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ancient evidence
*Student:* Analyze data on 4.4-billion-year-old Jack Hills zircons, meteorite ages, and Moon rocks; explain why Earth's oldest rocks are younger than meteorites.

*How to teach:* Give data on ages: Jack Hills zircons (~4.4 billion years), oldest rocks (~4.0 billion), meteorites (~4.57 billion), Moon rocks (up to ~4.5 billion). The student explains why these differ and what they tell us about Earth's age.

- **Q:** Why are Earth's oldest rocks (~4.0 billion years) younger than meteorites (~4.57 billion years)?
  **A:** Earth's surface is constantly recycled by plate tectonics, erosion, and early melting; meteorites have been unchanged since the solar system formed.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Earth timeline
*Student:* Build a 4.6-meter toilet-paper or string timeline of Earth history (1 m = 1 billion years) marking key events.

*How to teach:* Unroll 4.6 m of string or toilet paper on the floor (1 m = 1 billion years). The student marks Earth's formation, oldest minerals, first life, oxygen rise, first animals, dinosaurs, and humans, then reflects on how recent humans are.

- **Q:** How far from 'now' is the dinosaurs' extinction (66 million years ago)?
  **A:** 6.6 cm.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 8 test on Big Bang evidence, stellar elements, and Earth's origin.

*How to teach:* Unit 8 test. Today's task from the plan: Unit 8 test on Big Bang evidence, stellar elements, and Earth's origin. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** How old is Earth?
  **A:** About 4.54 billion years.

**Assessment:** Timeline and Unit 8 test.

**Answer key:** Timeline rubric: key events placed at correct scale (formation 4.6 m, oldest zircons 4.4 m, first life ~3.5–3.8 m, oxygen rise ~2.4 m, Cambrian 0.54 m, dinosaurs extinct 6.6 cm, humans ~0.3 mm). Unit 8 test key: Big Bang evidence; dark matter/dark energy; stellar nucleosynthesis; radiometric dating; age of Earth.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Contemporary domestic issues

**Prepare:** Print a computing timeline (ENIAC 1945–46; transistor 1947; integrated circuit 1958–59; Intel 1968; Apple 1976; IBM PC 1981; World Wide Web 1989–91; iPhone 2007), Pew/BLS data on women's labor-force participation (about 34% in 1950, 43% in 1970, 58% in 1990, peak 60% in 1999, about 56% in 2020), local government data on homelessness and housing costs for a California city, and a summary of the California Environmental Quality Act (1970) and state climate laws (AB 32, 2006; SB 32, 2016). - Students think Silicon Valley began with personal computers. It grew from defense-funded electronics, Stanford, and semiconductor firms (Fairchild, Intel) in the 1950s–60s.

- Students think women's labor participation rose steadily without limits. It rose sharply from 1950 to the late 1990s, then plateaued.

### Day 1 (Monday): Technology

*How to teach:* Trace the computer revolution from ENIAC to the smartphone and analyze effects on work.

*How to teach:* Graph women's labor-force participation 1950–2020 and explain changing family structures.

- **Q:** Give two reasons women's labor participation rose after 1950.
  **A:** Expanded education, the women's movement and anti-discrimination laws, economic need, service-sector growth, access to contraception.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Urban issues
*Student:* Research homelessness, housing costs, and drug policy in a California city using local government data.

*How to teach:* Research homelessness, housing costs and drug policy in a California city using local data.

- **Q:** Why is housing so expensive in many California cities?

### Day 4 (Thursday): Environment
*Student:* Trace environmental laws from the EPA to California's climate policies and evaluate controversies.

*How to teach:* Trace environmental laws from the EPA to California's climate policies and evaluate controversies.

## Spanish IV: Comunidades hoy: entrevista y comunidad

**Prepare:** Find a Univision or Telemundo local news segment and La Opinión (Los Angeles) articles. Help your child identify an adult Spanish speaker to interview, get permission to record, and choose a safe public or family setting.

**Watch for:**
- Asking yes/no questions only: open questions (¿Cómo...? ¿Por qué...? Cuénteme sobre...) produce richer answers.
- Using tú with an older interviewee you don't know well: use usted unless invited to use tú.
- Transcribing word by word into English: transcribe in Spanish, then summarize.

### Day 1 (Monday): Medios locales
*Student:* Watch a Univision or Telemundo news segment and summarize one local story.

*How to teach:* Watch a local Spanish-language news segment; your child summarizes one local story (qué, quién, dónde, cuándo, por qué).

- **Q:** What does 'el reportaje' mean?
  **A:** A news report/feature story.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Preguntas
*Student:* Write 12 interview questions about immigration, work, and traditions.

*How to teach:* Your child writes 12 interview questions about immigration, work, and traditions in usted form.

- **Q:** Ask: 'When did you arrive in California?'
  **A:** ¿Cuándo llegó usted a California?
- **Q:** Ask: 'What traditions do you keep from your country?'
  **A:** ¿Qué tradiciones mantiene de su país?

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Entrevista
*Student:* Interview a Spanish speaker in your community (with permission) and record it.

*How to teach:* Your child conducts and records the interview with permission.

- **Q:** Ask permission to record.
  **A:** ¿Me permite grabar la entrevista? Solo la usaré para mi clase de español.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Transcripción
*Student:* Transcribe and summarize key points in Spanish.

*How to teach:* Your child transcribes and summarizes key points in Spanish.

- **Q:** Convert to reported speech: 'Llegué en 1995 y trabajé en un restaurante.'
  **A:** Dijo que había llegado en 1995 y que había trabajado en un restaurante.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Write a 300-word profile of the person interviewed.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a 300-word profile.

- **Q:** Write an engaging first sentence.
  **A:** Example: Cuando la señora Rosa llegó a Los Ángeles en 1995, solo tenía una maleta y muchos sueños.

**Assessment:** Interview profile.

**Answer key:** Interview profile rubric (300 words): 4 = engaging introduction, background, work, and traditions in accurate past and present tenses, 2–3 direct quotations and reported speech, respectful tone, conclusion with reflection; 3 = minor errors; 2 = list-like; 1 = incomplete.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Rehearsal process

**Prepare:** Calendar to performance week, prompt book, a line-notes form (line, error type: dropped, paraphrased, called for line).

**Watch for:**
- Vague notes ('make it better'). Notes should be specific and actionable ('pick up the cue on page 4').
- No off-book deadline. Set and enforce one.
- Working the whole play every day. Prioritize scenes that need the most work.

### Day 1 (Monday): Schedule
*Student:* Build a rehearsal schedule to performance week with an off-book deadline.

*How to teach:* Build a rehearsal schedule to performance week with an off-book deadline, tech, and dress dates.

- **Q:** What should a rehearsal schedule include?
  **A:** Dates/times, scenes and actors called, off-book deadline, tech rehearsal, dress rehearsals, performances.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Character work
*Student:* Lead an actor workshop on objectives for each character.

*How to teach:* Lead an actor workshop on objectives for each character.

- **Q:** Give an objective for Natalya in The Proposal.
  **A:** Example: 'To prove the Oxen Meadows belong to my family' / 'to win the argument with Lomov' (later, to get him back to propose).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Scene work
*Student:* Work two key scenes for pacing and stakes.

*How to teach:* Work two key scenes for pacing (picking up cues) and stakes.

- **Q:** What does 'pick up your cues' mean?
  **A:** Start your line promptly after the previous one ends, without dead air (unless a pause is intentional).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Off-book run
*Student:* Hold the off-book run and track line errors.

*How to teach:* Off-book run; track line errors on the notes form.

- **Q:** Why give actors line notes?
  **A:** So they correct paraphrases and dropped lines, honoring the playwright's text and giving partners accurate cues.

### Day 5 (Friday): Notes
*Student:* Practice giving specific, actionable notes and write a rehearsal report.

*How to teach:* Practice giving specific notes; write a rehearsal report (date, attendance, what was worked, notes, problems, next steps).

- **Q:** What goes in a rehearsal report?
  **A:** Date, who attended, scenes worked, notes for departments (props, costumes), issues, and next rehearsal plan.

**Assessment:** Rehearsal report.

**Answer key:** Rehearsal report rubric. 4 – Complete schedule; report includes attendance, work done, specific actor notes, department notes, line-error tracking, and next steps. 3 (proficient) – Schedule and report with specific notes. 2 – Vague report. 1 – Missing.

## Lifetime Fitness: Designing a personal fitness plan

**Prepare:** Gather all year's fitness data (baseline, midyear, logs) and a plan template with FITT sections for cardio, strength, flexibility, and lifetime activities.

**Watch for:**
- Making a plan too ambitious to follow. Adherence matters more than an ideal plan.
- Leaving out flexibility or strength. A complete plan addresses all components.
- Planning activities without considering access after high school (cost, location, schedule).

### Day 1 (Monday): Self-analysis
*Student:* Review all fitness data from the year and identify strengths and needs.

*How to teach:* Review data; student lists strengths and needs for each component.

- **Q:** Why base a plan on personal data?
  **A:** So it targets actual needs and allows measurable progress (specificity and individualization).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plan cardio
*Student:* Plan cardio component with FITT; complete a cardio session.

*How to teach:* Write the cardio component with FITT; complete a cardio session.

- **Q:** Give a FITT cardio plan meeting adult guidelines.
  **A:** Example: 4 days/week, moderate to vigorous (60–80% HRR), 30–45 minutes, running/cycling — at least 150 min moderate or 75 min vigorous weekly.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Plan strength & flexibility
*Student:* Plan strength and flexibility components; complete a strength session.

*How to teach:* Write strength and flexibility components; complete a strength session.

- **Q:** How often should adults do muscle-strengthening activity?
  **A:** At least 2 days per week, working all major muscle groups.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Lifetime activities
*Student:* Choose two lifetime activities and plan how to access them after high school.

*How to teach:* Choose two lifetime activities and plan access (location, cost, schedule, partners).

- **Q:** What makes an activity 'lifetime'?
  **A:** It can be done across the lifespan with few people, modest equipment, and is enjoyable (e.g., hiking, swimming, pickleball, cycling).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Submit a draft fitness plan.

*How to teach:* Review the draft against the rubric and give feedback.

- **Q:** What does 'adherence' mean in fitness?
  **A:** Sticking to the plan consistently over time.

**Assessment:** Fitness plan draft.

**Answer key:** Fitness plan draft rubric. 4 – Based on year data; FITT for cardio, strength, flexibility; meets guidelines; two lifetime activities with access plans; SMART goals; progression and self-monitoring method. 3 (proficient) – All components with FITT, lifetime activities, goals. 2 – Missing a component or FITT details. 1 – Incomplete.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Capstone: prototype build II

**Prepare:** Gather fasteners, adhesives (follow ventilation labels), sandpaper/files and calipers. Keep the CAD model open for updates.

**Watch for:**
- Forcing parts that don't fit. Measure the clearance, find which part is out of tolerance, and rework it.
- Forgetting to update CAD after changes. The as-built model and drawings must match the real prototype.
- Final assembly with permanent adhesive before testing. Dry-fit and use removable fasteners when possible until testing is done.

### Day 1 (Monday): Remaining parts
*Student:* Finish fabricating remaining parts and deburr or sand edges for safety.

*How to teach:* Finish remaining parts; deburr and sand edges.

- **Q:** Why deburr metal or plastic edges?
  **A:** Burrs are sharp and can cut users; removing them also improves fit.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Fit check
*Student:* Dry-fit all parts and measure clearances; record any parts that do not meet tolerance.

*How to teach:* Dry-fit all parts, measure clearances, and record any out of tolerance.

- **Q:** What does dry-fitting mean?
  **A:** Assembling parts temporarily without glue or permanent fasteners to check fit.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rework
*Student:* Rework or reprint out-of-tolerance parts and update the CAD model to match changes.

*How to teach:* Rework or reprint out-of-tolerance parts and update the CAD model.

- **Q:** A printed hole is 0.3 mm too small. Give two fixes.
  **A:** Drill or ream it to size, or enlarge the hole in CAD (compensate for printer shrinkage) and reprint.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Final assembly
*Student:* Assemble the full prototype and verify it operates as intended.

*How to teach:* Assemble the full prototype and verify it works.

- **Q:** What does 'integration' mean in a build?
  **A:** Bringing subsystems together and making sure they work properly as a whole.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Submit an as-built drawing update and a notebook entry describing build problems and solutions.

*How to teach:* Submit the as-built update and notebook entry.

- **Q:** What is an as-built drawing?
  **A:** A drawing updated to show the product exactly as it was actually built, including changes made during fabrication.

**Assessment:** Assembled prototype and as-built update.

**Answer key:** Assembled prototype and as-built update: 4 = all parts finished with safe edges; fit-check table of clearances vs. tolerances; reworked parts documented; CAD and drawings updated to match changes (revision noted in title block); prototype assembled and operates as intended; notebook entry explains problems and solutions; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = CAD not updated or prototype not functional; 1 = incomplete.
