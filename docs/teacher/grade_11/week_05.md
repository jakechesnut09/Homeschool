# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 5

## English 11 – American Literature: Franklin and the Enlightenment self

**Prepare:** Franklin's Autobiography, Part II ('arriving at moral perfection') and Poor Richard's Almanack sayings (Gutenberg); 'Remarks Concerning the Savages of North America' (Gutenberg/CommonLit).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Franklin's tone toward himself is entirely serious. Point out his self-deprecating humor, e.g., he admits he gained more of the 'appearance' of humility than the reality.
- Confusing verbal irony with sarcasm or coincidence. Verbal irony is saying the opposite of what one means; in 'Remarks' the 'Savages' are shown to be more civil than the English.

### Day 1 (Monday): Autobiography
*Student:* Read Franklin's 'project of arriving at moral perfection'; list his 13 virtues and mark his tone toward himself.

*How to teach:* Read the moral-perfection section. The student lists the 13 virtues and marks two places where Franklin laughs at himself. Today's task from the plan: Read Franklin's 'project of arriving at moral perfection'; list his 13 virtues and mark his tone toward himself. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** List Franklin's 13 virtues.
  **A:** Temperance, Silence, Order, Resolution, Frugality, Industry, Sincerity, Justice, Moderation, Cleanliness, Tranquility, Chastity, Humility.
- **Q:** Why did Franklin add Humility?
  **A:** A Quaker friend told him he was considered proud; he says he had little success with the reality of humility but a good deal with its appearance.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Aphorisms
*Student:* Read 20 Poor Richard sayings; rewrite five in modern English and explain the reasoning each assumes.

*How to teach:* Read 20 aphorisms. Model one ('Early to bed and early to rise, makes a man healthy, wealthy, and wise' assumes discipline produces success). The student rewrites five.

- **Q:** What assumption lies behind 'God helps them that help themselves'?
  **A:** That success comes from personal effort and that individuals are responsible for improving their own lives, an Enlightenment and self-made-man value.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Satire
*Student:* Read 'Remarks Concerning the Savages of North America'; identify verbal irony and its target.

*How to teach:* Read 'Remarks.' Model the first line's irony, then the student finds two more examples and names the target (English/colonial arrogance). Today's task from the plan: Read 'Remarks Concerning the Savages of North America'; identify verbal irony and its target. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What is ironic about the opening 'Savages we call them, because their manners differ from ours'?
  **A:** Franklin shows the label is based only on difference, not merit; he then presents Native customs as more polite and reasonable than colonial ones, so the 'civilized' are the real target.
- **Q:** How do the Native leaders respond to the offer to educate their young men at the college?
  **A:** They politely decline, saying their youths who were educated came back useless as hunters or warriors, and offer instead to educate a dozen Virginians' sons and 'make men of them.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Expository writing
*Student:* Track one personal virtue for three days Franklin-style and draft a 400-word reflective explanation.

*How to teach:* The student tracks one virtue for three days in a Franklin-style chart, then drafts a 400-word reflective explanation (what they tried, results, what it shows about self-improvement).

- **Q:** What should the reflection explain beyond 'I did it'?
  **A:** What specific behavior was tracked, the results (data from the chart), what was difficult, and what the experiment suggests about Franklin's method.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Short-answer quiz on Enlightenment ideas and irony; submit the virtue reflection.

*How to teach:* Give a short-answer quiz on Enlightenment ideas (reason, progress, self-improvement, deism) and irony. Collect the reflection. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Name two Enlightenment values Franklin's writing shows.
  **A:** Faith in reason/rational method (his chart system) and belief in self-improvement and progress through human effort.

**Assessment:** Quiz and reflective explanatory piece.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Enlightenment = 18th-century movement valuing reason, science, and progress. 2) Franklin's virtue chart = a rational, systematic method for moral improvement. 3) Aphorism = short, memorable statement of a general truth. 4) Verbal irony in 'Remarks' = calling Native people 'savages' while showing their superior courtesy. 5) Deism = belief in a creator who does not intervene, known through reason. Reflection rubric (4-point): specific virtue and tracking data; honest analysis of results; connection to Franklin's method; clear organization and correct conventions.

## Algebra II: Solving quadratic equations

**Prepare:** Index cards (one equation per card) for Day 5 sorting. Preview Khan Academy 'Quadratic formula proof'.

**Watch for:**
- Dividing both sides by x (e.g., in x² = 5x) and losing the solution x = 0. Move everything to one side and factor: x(x − 5) = 0.
- Mis-handling −b and the 2a in the quadratic formula (e.g., dividing only the radical by 2a). Write the formula with one long fraction bar and substitute b in parentheses.

### Day 1 (Monday): Factoring review
*Student:* Factor trinomials with a ≠ 1, difference of squares, and GCF; solve by zero-product property.

*How to teach:* Model factoring 6x² + x − 2 by the ac method (ac = −12, use 4 and −3). Student solves 8 equations by factoring, setting each factor to zero. Today's task from the plan: Factor trinomials with a ≠ 1, difference of squares, and GCF; solve by zero-product property. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Solve 6x² + x − 2 = 0.
  **A:** (3x + 2)(2x − 1) = 0, so x = −2/3 or x = 1/2.
- **Q:** Solve 4x² − 25 = 0.
  **A:** (2x − 5)(2x + 5) = 0, x = ±5/2.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Square roots & completing the square
*Student:* Solve equations by taking square roots and completing the square.

*How to teach:* Model (x − 3)² = 20 by square roots (remember ±), then x² + 8x − 1 = 0 by completing the square. Student does six of each. Today's task from the plan: Solve equations by taking square roots and completing the square. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Solve (x − 3)² = 20.
  **A:** x − 3 = ±√20 = ±2√5, so x = 3 ± 2√5.
- **Q:** Solve x² + 8x − 1 = 0 by completing the square.
  **A:** x² + 8x + 16 = 17, (x + 4)² = 17, x = −4 ± √17.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Quadratic formula
*Student:* Derive the quadratic formula by completing the square on ax²+bx+c = 0; solve 8 equations.

*How to teach:* Walk through completing the square on ax² + bx + c = 0 symbolically, line by line, with the student writing each step. Then solve 8 equations with the formula.

- **Q:** Solve 2x² − 3x − 5 = 0 with the quadratic formula.
  **A:** x = (3 ± √(9 + 40))/4 = (3 ± 7)/4 → x = 5/2 or x = −1.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Discriminant
*Student:* Predict number and type of solutions using b²−4ac; connect to x-intercepts in Desmos.

*How to teach:* Explain b² − 4ac: positive = two real roots (two x-intercepts), zero = one repeated root (vertex on axis), negative = two complex roots (no x-intercepts). Student predicts for 8 equations, then checks in Desmos.

- **Q:** Classify the solutions of x² + 4x + 7 = 0 and 9x² − 12x + 4 = 0.
  **A:** First: 16 − 28 = −12 < 0, two complex (non-real) solutions. Second: 144 − 144 = 0, one repeated real solution (x = 2/3).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Choose the most efficient method for 12 equations and justify each choice.

*How to teach:* Student sorts the 12 cards by best method, solves, and writes a one-sentence justification for each. Discuss choices together. Today's task from the plan: Choose the most efficient method for 12 equations and justify each choice. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Which method is best for x² = 49, x² + 5x + 6 = 0, and 3x² + 7x − 2 = 0?
  **A:** Square roots (x = ±7); factoring ((x + 2)(x + 3), x = −2, −3); quadratic formula (doesn't factor nicely: x = (−7 ± √73)/6).

**Assessment:** Method-choice assessment.

**Answer key:** Score each item 1 pt for a reasonable method and 1 pt for correct solutions. Sample answers: x²=49 → ±7 (roots); (x−4)²=9 → 1, 7 (roots); x²−7x+12=0 → 3, 4 (factor); 2x²+5x−3=0 → 1/2, −3 (factor); x²+6x=2 → −3±√11 (complete square); x²−2x−1=0 → 1±√2 (formula or complete square); 3x²+7x−2=0 → (−7±√73)/6 (formula); x²+2x+10=0 → −1±3i accepted later or 'no real solution' (formula). Proficient = 20/24 or better with justifications that mention structure (no linear term, nice factors, a=1 and even b, etc.).

## Physics of the Universe: Engineering challenge: egg crash protection

**Prepare:** Raw eggs (several), toy car or small cart, straws, paper, tape, rubber bands; a ramp (board on books) and a wall/book as a barrier. Kitchen scale. Put down newspaper.

**Watch for:**
- Making the device as stiff as possible. Crumple zones work by deforming to lengthen stopping time; rigid structures transfer force to the egg.
- Changing several variables between tests. Change one design feature at a time so you know what caused the improvement.

### Day 1 (Monday): Define the problem
*Student:* List criteria and constraints for an egg-in-cart crash device (limited materials, mass limit).

*How to teach:* Together list criteria (egg survives a crash from a set ramp height) and constraints (e.g., 20 straws, 1 m tape, mass limit 100 g, egg must be visible).

- **Q:** What is the difference between a criterion and a constraint?
  **A:** A criterion is what the design must achieve (egg doesn't crack); a constraint is a limit (materials, mass, time, cost).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Design
*Student:* Sketch three designs and choose one using a decision matrix.

*How to teach:* The student sketches three designs and scores them in a decision matrix (weighted criteria: protection, mass, buildability). Today's task from the plan: Sketch three designs and choose one using a decision matrix. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why use a decision matrix?
  **A:** It compares designs against the criteria objectively instead of choosing the favorite.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Build & test
*Student:* Build a prototype and test it in a ramp crash; record results.

*How to teach:* Build and test from the set ramp height. Film the crash to estimate stopping time. Today's task from the plan: Build a prototype and test it in a ramp crash; record results. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Which physics principle should guide the design?
  **A:** Impulse: increase the stopping time (crumple zones, padding) to reduce the force on the egg, since F = Δp/Δt.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Refine
*Student:* Redesign based on test data and retest; calculate impulse reductions.

*How to teach:* Redesign one feature based on test data and retest. Estimate the impulse and average force. Today's task from the plan: Redesign based on test data and retest; calculate impulse reductions. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** If the redesign doubles the stopping time, how does the average force change?
  **A:** It halves (same Δp).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Present the design and take the Unit 1 test.

*How to teach:* The student presents the design (5 minutes) and takes the Unit 1 test. Today's task from the plan: Present the design and take the Unit 1 test. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Why do modern cars have crumple zones but rigid passenger cages?
  **A:** Crumple zones deform to increase stopping time and absorb energy, while the rigid cage prevents intrusion into the passenger space.

**Assessment:** Design report and Unit 1 test.

**Answer key:** Design report rubric (4-point per row): problem definition with criteria/constraints; design selection with decision matrix; test data (at least two trials) and redesign based on evidence; explanation using impulse-momentum. Unit 1 test key (sample): kinematics (from rest a = 2 m/s² for 6 s → 12 m/s, 36 m), F = ma, free-body diagrams, third-law pairs, μ calculation, momentum conservation, impulse.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Labor and the Populists

**Prepare:** Print an excerpt of Upton Sinclair's The Jungle (1906) — preview, it's graphic about meatpacking — and Lewis Hine child labor photos (LOC); summaries of the Homestead (1892) and Pullman (1894) strikes; the Omaha Platform (1892); Bryan's 'Cross of Gold' speech (1896).

**Watch for:**
- Students think unions were widely accepted. Courts, employers and often government opposed them; strikes were frequently broken by force or injunctions.
- Students think 'free silver' means free money. It meant unlimited coinage of silver at 16:1 with gold, expanding the money supply to raise prices and ease farm debts.
- Students think the Populists failed completely. They lost elections but many demands later became law.

### Day 1 (Monday): Working conditions
*Student:* Read The Jungle excerpt and Lewis Hine child labor photos; record working conditions.

*How to teach:* Read The Jungle excerpt and study Hine photos; record working conditions.

- **Q:** What reforms did The Jungle help produce?
  **A:** The Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act (both 1906).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Unions & strikes
*Student:* Compare the Knights of Labor and AFL; study the Pullman and Homestead strikes.

*How to teach:* Compare the Knights of Labor and the AFL; study Homestead and Pullman.

- **Q:** How did the AFL differ from the Knights of Labor?
  **A:** The AFL (Gompers, 1886) organized skilled craft workers and focused on wages, hours and conditions; the Knights accepted nearly all workers and had broad reform goals.
- **Q:** How was the Pullman Strike ended?
  **A:** A federal injunction and federal troops sent by President Cleveland; Eugene Debs was jailed.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Farmers' grievances
*Student:* Analyze the Omaha Platform's demands.

*How to teach:* Analyze the Omaha Platform's demands.

- **Q:** List three Omaha Platform demands.
  **A:** Free coinage of silver, a graduated income tax, government ownership of railroads and telegraphs, direct election of senators, secret ballot, eight-hour day.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cross of Gold
*Student:* Read Bryan's speech and explain the silver vs. gold debate.

*How to teach:* Read Bryan's speech and explain the silver vs. gold debate.

- **Q:** Why did indebted farmers favor free silver?
  **A:** More money in circulation would cause inflation, raising crop prices and making debts easier to repay.
- **Q:** What is the famous line of the speech?
  **A:** 'You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on which Populist demands later became law.

*How to teach:* The student writes a paragraph on which Populist demands later became law.

- **Q:** Which amendment created the federal income tax?
  **A:** The 16th Amendment (1913).

**Assessment:** Paragraph.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric: 4 = claim identifies which demands became law with evidence — graduated income tax (16th Amendment, 1913), direct election of senators (17th Amendment, 1913), secret ballot (adopted by states in the 1880s–90s), eight-hour day (Adamson Act 1916 for railroads; Fair Labor Standards Act 1938), initiative and referendum (many states, including California 1911), regulation of railroads (ICC strengthened) — and notes those that did not (free silver, government ownership of railroads); explains why (Progressive reformers adopted them). 3 = two or three examples. 2 = general. 1 = inaccurate.

## Spanish IV: La familia en el mundo hispano

**Prepare:** Find a household-size chart (INE for Spain, INEGI for Mexico, U.S. Census) and a Spanish article on extended families. Make cards for 25 family terms (el/la bisabuelo/a, el/la suegro/a, el yerno, la nuera, el/la cuñado/a, el/la padrastro/madrastra, el/la hermanastro/a, el/la medio/a hermano/a, el padrino, la madrina, el compadre, la comadre, criar, convivir). Chart the present perfect (he/has/ha/hemos/habéis/han + participle; irregular participles).

**Watch for:**
- Confusing 'los padres' (parents) and 'los parientes' (relatives).
- Misunderstanding compadrazgo: compadres are the parents and godparents of a child in relation to each other — a lifelong bond of mutual support, not just friends.
- Making the participle agree after haber (*La familia ha cambiada): it never changes — ha cambiado.

### Day 1 (Monday): Vocabulario
*Student:* Learn 25 terms for family relationships and roles; build a family tree with descriptions.

*How to teach:* Teach 25 family terms; your child builds a family tree with a descriptive sentence for each person.

- **Q:** What are 'el suegro' and 'la nuera'?
  **A:** Father-in-law; daughter-in-law.
- **Q:** What is 'el compadrazgo'?
  **A:** The relationship between a child's parents and godparents (compadres), a lasting bond of mutual help.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pretérito perfecto
*Student:* Learn present perfect (he vivido, ha cambiado) and describe how your family has changed.

*How to teach:* Teach the present perfect; your child writes 8 sentences about how the family has changed (Mi familia se ha mudado dos veces...).

- **Q:** Translate: 'My family has lived in California for twenty years.'
  **A:** Mi familia ha vivido en California durante veinte años (Hace veinte años que mi familia vive en California).
- **Q:** Give the participles of volver, morir, and abrir.
  **A:** vuelto, muerto, abierto

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Datos
*Student:* Analyze a chart of household sizes in Spain, Mexico, and the U.S.

*How to teach:* Your child analyzes the household-size chart and writes 5 data statements.

- **Q:** Translate: 'In Mexico households are larger than in Spain.'
  **A:** En México los hogares son más grandes que en España.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Conversación
*Student:* Interview a family member in Spanish (or translate) about family traditions.

*How to teach:* Your child interviews a family member about traditions (in Spanish or translating) and notes answers.

- **Q:** Ask: 'What traditions has our family kept?'
  **A:** ¿Qué tradiciones ha mantenido nuestra familia?

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Write a comparison of family life in two cultures.

*How to teach:* Your child writes the comparison of family life in two cultures.

- **Q:** Write a comparison sentence with a diferencia de.
  **A:** Example: A diferencia de muchas familias estadounidenses, en muchas familias mexicanas los abuelos viven con sus hijos y nietos.

**Assessment:** Comparison writing.

**Answer key:** Comparison writing rubric (family life in two cultures, ~250 words): 4 = 3 specific points of comparison (household size, roles, traditions such as compadrazgo or Sunday meals) supported by data, comparison structures (al igual que, a diferencia de, mientras que, más/menos... que), present perfect used correctly, no stereotypes; 3 = minor errors; 2 = general or few comparisons; 1 = incomplete.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Voice: breath, resonance, and articulation

**Prepare:** Gather a mat, a hardback book, a mirror, and a recording app. Find (or record) the student's week 1 voice sample; choose a short poem (14–20 lines). Measure 5, 15, and 30 feet in a hall or yard.

**Watch for:**
- Projection means shouting. It means supported breath and resonance; shouting strains the throat.
- Breathing high in the chest with lifted shoulders. Breath should expand the belly and lower ribs.
- Mumbling final consonants. Crisp final consonants carry meaning to the back row.

### Day 1 (Monday): Breath
*Student:* Practice diaphragmatic breathing lying down with a book on the abdomen; sustain an 's' sound for time.

*How to teach:* Lie down with a book on the abdomen; inhale so the book rises, exhale slowly. Time a sustained 's' sound three times and record the best.

- **Q:** What is the diaphragm's role in breathing?
  **A:** It is a dome-shaped muscle under the lungs; it contracts and flattens to draw air in, and relaxes as you exhale.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Resonance
*Student:* Explore chest, mask, and head resonance with humming and sirens.

*How to teach:* Hum and feel vibration in the chest (low), mask/face (mid), and head (high). Do sirens up and down the range.

- **Q:** Where is 'mask' resonance felt?
  **A:** In the front of the face — lips, nose, cheekbones.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Articulation
*Student:* Drill 10 tongue twisters at increasing speeds and record before-and-after clips.

*How to teach:* Drill 10 tongue twisters slowly, then faster; record before and after.

- **Q:** Which articulators are used in speech?
  **A:** Lips, teeth, tongue, hard and soft palate, and jaw.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Projection
*Student:* Deliver a short poem to a partner at 5, 15, and 30 feet without shouting.

*How to teach:* Deliver the poem to a partner at 5, 15, and 30 feet without shouting; partner raises a hand if words are lost.

- **Q:** How do you project to 30 feet without shouting?
  **A:** Use full breath support, open resonance (especially mask), slightly slower pace, and crisp articulation, aiming the voice at the back listener.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Perform a vocal warm-up routine you designed and a 1-minute poem reading; compare to the week 1 recording.

*How to teach:* Student leads their designed 5-minute warm-up (breath, resonance, articulation) then reads the poem; compare with the week 1 recording.

- **Q:** Why warm up the voice before performing?
  **A:** To prepare breath, relax tension, and wake up resonance and articulators, preventing strain and improving clarity.

**Assessment:** Vocal recording comparison.

**Answer key:** Vocal recording comparison rubric. 4 – Designed warm-up covers breath, resonance, and articulation; final reading shows clear improvement in projection, articulation, and breath support; written comparison cites specific moments. 3 (proficient) – Complete warm-up, audible/clear reading, comparison notes improvements. 2 – Little improvement or missing comparison. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness: Building an aerobic base

**Prepare:** Ensure the student has supportive running shoes; set up a free route-mapping app (e.g., a GPS running app) and a training log with columns for date, distance, time, pace, RPE. Arrange access to a bike, pool, or rower for cross-training day.

**Watch for:**
- Running every run hard. Most base training should be easy, conversational pace; hard days need easy days around them.
- Overstriding (landing far in front with a heel) to go faster. Encourage quicker, shorter steps landing under the hips.
- Thinking cross-training 'doesn't count.' Cycling or swimming builds the aerobic base while reducing impact stress.

### Day 1 (Monday): Run form
*Student:* Learn running form (posture, arm swing, cadence ~170 steps/min) with drills.

*How to teach:* Teach run form: tall posture with slight forward lean from ankles, relaxed shoulders, arms swinging forward-back at ~90°, feet landing under the hips. Do drills (A-skips, butt kicks, strides). Count steps for 30 seconds and double to estimate cadence (aim ~160–180).

- **Q:** You count 82 steps in 30 seconds. What is your cadence?
  **A:** 164 steps per minute.
- **Q:** Name two cues for efficient running form.
  **A:** Run tall with a slight forward lean; land under the hips with quick light steps; relaxed shoulders; arms swing forward and back, not across the body.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Base run
*Student:* Complete a 30-minute easy run or brisk walk at conversational pace.

*How to teach:* Student completes 30 minutes at conversational pace (talk test). Record distance, time, and compute average pace.

- **Q:** You cover 3 miles in 33 minutes. What is your pace?
  **A:** 11:00 per mile (33 ÷ 3).
- **Q:** Why is 'long slow distance' useful?
  **A:** It builds the aerobic base — more capillaries and mitochondria and a stronger heart — with low injury and fatigue risk.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cross-training
*Student:* Cycle, swim, or row 30 minutes at moderate intensity.

*How to teach:* Do 30 minutes of cycling, swimming, or rowing at moderate intensity (RPE 5–6). Discuss how it trains the same heart and lungs with different muscles and less impact.

- **Q:** How does cross-training help a runner?
  **A:** It maintains or builds aerobic fitness while reducing repetitive impact stress, lowering overuse injury risk and adding variety.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Tempo
*Student:* Perform a 20-minute tempo workout (5 easy, 10 comfortably hard, 5 easy).

*How to teach:* Explain tempo pace: 'comfortably hard' — RPE about 7, can say only a few words. Workout: 5 min easy, 10 min tempo, 5 min easy. Check HR at the end of the tempo portion.

- **Q:** How should a tempo pace feel?
  **A:** Comfortably hard: sustained effort around RPE 7, breathing deep and rhythmic, only short phrases possible.

### Day 5 (Friday): Log
*Student:* Complete a 35-minute long easy session and update the training log with mileage and RPE.

*How to teach:* Student completes 35 minutes easy and updates the log with weekly totals. Review the log together for completeness and an easy/hard pattern.

- **Q:** What does the training log help you see?
  **A:** Weekly volume, pace trends, intensity (RPE), and whether you're progressing gradually or overdoing it.

**Assessment:** Training log review.

**Answer key:** Training log review rubric. 4 – All 5 sessions logged with date, type, time, distance, pace and RPE; weekly total calculated; a reflection notes trends and next-week adjustment. 3 (proficient) – All sessions logged with time, distance/type and RPE; total correct. 2 – Missing sessions or data columns. 1 – Log largely incomplete.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Pictorial sketching: isometric and oblique

**Prepare:** Get isometric grid paper (printable free online), wooden blocks or LEGO, and a coffee mug. Practice the four-center ellipse once yourself.

**Watch for:**
- Drawing isometric receding lines at 45°. Isometric axes are vertical plus two lines at 30° from horizontal (all three 120° apart).
- Drawing circles as circles on isometric faces. Circles on isometric faces appear as ellipses.
- Drawing full depth in cabinet oblique. Cabinet oblique uses half depth along the receding axis; cavalier uses full depth (which looks too deep).

### Day 1 (Monday): Isometric axes
*Student:* Learn the 30° isometric axes and sketch three stacked-block shapes on isometric grid paper.

*How to teach:* Show the three isometric axes (one vertical, two at 30° from horizontal). Sketch three stacked-block shapes on isometric paper, counting grid units along each axis.

- **Q:** At what angle from horizontal are isometric receding lines drawn?
  **A:** 30°.
- **Q:** How many degrees apart are the three isometric axes?
  **A:** 120°.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Isometric practice
*Student:* Sketch 8 objects (stapler, phone stand, LEGO builds) isometrically, starting from a bounding box.

*How to teach:* Model the bounding-box method: sketch an isometric box with the object's overall width, depth and height, then cut away material. Student sketches 8 objects.

- **Q:** Why start an isometric sketch with a bounding box?
  **A:** It sets correct overall proportions; details are then added or removed within it.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Isometric circles
*Student:* Use the four-center method to sketch isometric ellipses, then sketch a mug and a cylinder with a hole.

*How to teach:* Teach the four-center method for isometric ellipses: sketch the isometric square (rhombus), draw lines from the obtuse corners to the midpoints of the opposite sides, and use the intersections as centers for four arcs. Sketch a mug and a cylinder with a hole.

- **Q:** What shape does a circle become on an isometric face?
  **A:** An ellipse.
- **Q:** What shape do you box the circle in first on an isometric face?
  **A:** A rhombus (an isometric square) with sides equal to the circle's diameter.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Oblique sketching
*Student:* Create cavalier and cabinet oblique sketches of five objects and compare their distortion.

*How to teach:* Teach oblique: front face drawn true shape; receding lines at an angle (often 45°). Cavalier = full depth; cabinet = half depth. Sketch five objects both ways and compare which looks more realistic.

- **Q:** A box is 6 cm deep. How long is the receding edge in cabinet oblique? In cavalier?
  **A:** Cabinet: 3 cm (half). Cavalier: 6 cm (full).
- **Q:** When is oblique a good choice?
  **A:** When the object has circles or a complex shape on one face — that face can be drawn true shape as the front.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Sketch the same object in isometric and oblique for a scored quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz: sketch the same object in isometric and oblique; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Why does cavalier oblique look distorted?
  **A:** Full-length receding lines make the object look deeper than it really is.

**Assessment:** Pictorial sketch quiz.

**Answer key:** Pictorial sketch quiz rubric (each sketch scored 0–4): isometric — 4 = correct 30° axes, all parallel edges parallel, correct proportions, holes/curves drawn as ellipses, construction lines light and object lines dark; oblique — 4 = front face true shape, receding lines at a consistent angle (often 45°), depth scale stated (cavalier full, cabinet half). Proficient = 3+ on each sketch. Written items: 1) Isometric axes are 120° apart. 2) Cavalier = full depth; cabinet = half depth. 3) In oblique, the front face shows its true shape, so circles there can be drawn as true circles.
