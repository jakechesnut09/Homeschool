# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 10

## English 11 – American Literature: Dark Romanticism: Hawthorne and Melville

**Prepare:** Hawthorne's 'Young Goodman Brown' and 'The Minister's Black Veil' and Melville's 'Bartleby, the Scrivener' (Gutenberg).

**Watch for:**
- Demanding a single answer to whether Brown dreamed the meeting. Hawthorne leaves it ambiguous ('Be it so if you will'); the point is the effect on Brown.
- Seeing Bartleby as simply lazy. Focus on the narrator's reactions and the deadening Wall Street setting; the story asks what we owe each other.

### Day 1 (Monday): Young Goodman Brown
*Student:* Read and annotate symbols (Faith's ribbons, staff, forest); decide whether the journey was a dream.

*How to teach:* Read 'Young Goodman Brown.' The student annotates Faith's pink ribbons, the serpent-like staff, and the forest, then decides about the dream. Today's task from the plan: Read and annotate symbols (Faith's ribbons, staff, forest); decide whether the journey was a dream. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What do Faith's pink ribbons symbolize, and why does it matter when one blows down?
  **A:** They suggest innocence/faith; when a ribbon falls from the sky, Brown believes his wife has joined the devil and cries, 'My Faith is gone!'
- **Q:** How does the experience change Brown?
  **A:** He becomes stern, distrustful, and gloomy for life; 'his dying hour was gloom' because he sees sin in everyone.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Black Veil
*Student:* Read 'The Minister's Black Veil'; chart the community's reactions and explain the veil's meaning in a paragraph.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Minister's Black Veil.' The student charts reactions (congregation, Elizabeth, the dying) and writes a paragraph on the veil's meaning. Today's task from the plan: Read 'The Minister's Black Veil'; chart the community's reactions and explain the veil's meaning in a paragraph. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What does Hooper reveal on his deathbed?
  **A:** He says he sees 'on every visage a Black Veil' — everyone hides secret sin, and the veil only makes visible what all people conceal.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Bartleby I
*Student:* Read the first half of 'Bartleby'; analyze the narrator's attitude and the phrase 'I would prefer not to'.

*How to teach:* Read the first half of 'Bartleby.' Discuss the narrator's 'safe' personality and what 'I would prefer not to' does. Today's task from the plan: Read the first half of 'Bartleby'; analyze the narrator's attitude and the phrase 'I would prefer not to'. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why is 'I would prefer not to' so disruptive?
  **A:** It is a polite refusal, not a defiance the narrator can argue with; it leaves him confused and powerless.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Bartleby II
*Student:* Finish the story; compare Bartleby's refusal to Thoreau's civil disobedience in a T-chart.

*How to teach:* Finish the story. The student makes a T-chart comparing Bartleby's refusal with Thoreau's. Today's task from the plan: Finish the story; compare Bartleby's refusal to Thoreau's civil disobedience in a T-chart. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** How does Bartleby's refusal differ from Thoreau's?
  **A:** Thoreau's refusal is purposeful protest with a stated cause; Bartleby gives no reason and withdraws entirely, ending in death in the Tombs.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Timed essay (40 min): How do two Dark Romantic texts challenge Emerson's optimism?

*How to teach:* Timed essay (40 min): two Dark Romantic texts vs. Emerson's optimism. Today's task from the plan: Timed essay (40 min): How do two Dark Romantic texts challenge Emerson's optimism?. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What is the core difference between Dark Romantics and Transcendentalists?
  **A:** Transcendentalists believe in human goodness and divine nature; Dark Romantics emphasize sin, guilt, and the darker side of human nature.

**Assessment:** Timed comparative essay.

**Answer key:** Timed comparative essay rubric (4 = exceeds, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning) on four rows: Claim/Thesis (arguable, specific, answers the prompt); Evidence (well-chosen quotations, introduced and cited); Analysis/Reasoning (explains HOW evidence supports the claim, not just what it says); Conventions & organization (logical paragraphs, transitions, few errors). A proficient (3) paper states how two texts challenge Emerson's belief in human goodness and self-trust (e.g., Brown's discovery of universal sin; Hooper's veil; Bartleby's isolation), quotes Emerson and both stories, and explains contrast, not just summary.

## Algebra II: Factoring and zeros of polynomials

**Prepare:** Desmos for checking zeros. Make a reference card: a³ ± b³ = (a ± b)(a² ∓ ab + b²).

**Watch for:**
- Factoring x³ − 27 as (x − 3)³. Expand (x − 3)³ to show it is different; use the difference-of-cubes pattern.
- Listing only p/q with positive signs in the Rational Root Theorem. Every candidate needs ±.

### Day 1 (Monday): Special factoring
*Student:* Factor sums/differences of cubes and quadratic-form polynomials like x⁴−5x²+4.

*How to teach:* Model a³ − b³ and a³ + b³ (SOAP: Same, Opposite, Always Positive) and quadratic-form polynomials. Student factors eight. Today's task from the plan: Factor sums/differences of cubes and quadratic-form polynomials like x⁴−5x²+4. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Factor x³ − 27 and 8x³ + 1.
  **A:** (x − 3)(x² + 3x + 9); (2x + 1)(4x² − 2x + 1).
- **Q:** Factor x⁴ − 5x² + 4 completely.
  **A:** (x² − 1)(x² − 4) = (x − 1)(x + 1)(x − 2)(x + 2).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Grouping
*Student:* Factor four-term polynomials by grouping.

*How to teach:* Model grouping the first two and last two terms and pulling out the common binomial. Student factors six. Today's task from the plan: Factor four-term polynomials by grouping. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Factor x³ + 3x² − 4x − 12.
  **A:** x²(x + 3) − 4(x + 3) = (x + 3)(x² − 4) = (x + 3)(x − 2)(x + 2).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rational Root Theorem
*Student:* List possible rational roots and test with synthetic division.

*How to teach:* Model listing ±(factors of constant)/(factors of leading coefficient), testing with synthetic division, and factoring the quotient. Today's task from the plan: List possible rational roots and test with synthetic division. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Find the zeros of 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6.
  **A:** Candidates ±1, ±2, ±3, ±6, ±1/2, ±3/2. x = 3 works (54 − 27 − 33 + 6 = 0). Quotient 2x² + 3x − 2 = (2x − 1)(x + 2). Zeros 3, 1/2, −2.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fundamental Theorem of Algebra
*Student:* Find all real and complex zeros of degree-3 and degree-4 polynomials.

*How to teach:* State the Fundamental Theorem: a degree-n polynomial has exactly n complex zeros counting multiplicity. Model finding complex zeros after factoring out real ones.

- **Q:** Find all zeros of x³ − x² + 4x − 4.
  **A:** (x − 1)(x² + 4): zeros 1, 2i, −2i.
- **Q:** Find all zeros of x⁴ − 16.
  **A:** (x − 2)(x + 2)(x² + 4): ±2, ±2i.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: find all zeros of five polynomials and state multiplicities.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz: five polynomials, all zeros with multiplicities (about 35 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Quiz: find all zeros of five polynomials and state multiplicities. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** State the zeros and multiplicities of f(x) = (x − 1)²(x + 3).
  **A:** x = 1 with multiplicity 2; x = −3 with multiplicity 1.

**Assessment:** Zeros quiz.

**Answer key:** Sample key: 1) x³−4x = x(x−2)(x+2): 0, 2, −2 (each mult. 1). 2) x³+2x²+x = x(x+1)²: 0 (1), −1 (2). 3) x³−2x²−9x+18 = (x−2)(x−3)(x+3): 2, 3, −3. 4) x⁴−13x²+36 = (x−2)(x+2)(x−3)(x+3): ±2, ±3. 5) x³+8 = (x+2)(x²−2x+4): −2, 1±i√3. Full credit requires all zeros (including complex) and multiplicities.

## Physics of the Universe: Conservation of energy

**Prepare:** PhET Energy Skate Park; foam pipe insulation (cut in half lengthwise) and marbles; spreadsheet; string and a mass for a pendulum; phone for slow-motion video.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking energy disappears due to friction. It is transformed into thermal energy; total energy is conserved.
- Using mass in v = √(2gh). Mass cancels.

### Day 1 (Monday): Skate park
*Student:* Use PhET Energy Skate Park to track KE, PE, and thermal energy.

*How to teach:* In PhET, observe energy bars with and without friction. Today's task from the plan: Use PhET Energy Skate Park to track KE, PE, and thermal energy. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Where does energy go when friction is turned on?
  **A:** Into thermal energy; total energy stays constant.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Marble coaster
*Student:* Build a foam pipe-insulation marble roller coaster and predict speeds at points.

*How to teach:* Build the marble coaster; predict speed at the bottom of each hill from drop heights. Today's task from the plan: Build a foam pipe-insulation marble roller coaster and predict speeds at points. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** A marble drops 0.50 m. Predict its speed at the bottom (no friction).
  **A:** v = √(2 × 9.8 × 0.50) ≈ 3.1 m/s.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Computational model
*Student:* Build a spreadsheet model predicting the speed of a pendulum from drop height.

*How to teach:* Build a spreadsheet: drop height → predicted speed. Today's task from the plan: Build a spreadsheet model predicting the speed of a pendulum from drop height. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Predict speed for a 0.20 m drop.
  **A:** ≈ 2.0 m/s.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Pendulum test
*Student:* Test the model with a real pendulum and compare predicted vs. measured speed.

*How to teach:* Release the pendulum from measured heights; film and measure speed at the bottom. Today's task from the plan: Test the model with a real pendulum and compare predicted vs. measured speed. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Why is measured speed usually a bit less than predicted?
  **A:** Air resistance and friction at the pivot convert some energy to thermal energy.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a CER on energy conservation with friction.

*How to teach:* CER on energy conservation with friction. Today's task from the plan: Write a CER on energy conservation with friction. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What evidence shows energy conservation even with friction?
  **A:** Measured speed slightly lower than predicted; the missing kinetic energy equals thermal energy produced.

**Assessment:** Spreadsheet model and CER.

**Answer key:** CER rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning). Claim: answers the question about energy conservation with friction in one sentence. Evidence: specific data from the student's own lab/simulation with numbers and units. Reasoning: connects the evidence to the scientific principle. A proficient response: predicted vs measured speeds from the pendulum or coaster (e.g., predicted 2.0 m/s, measured 1.9 m/s) and reasoning that the small loss in kinetic energy became thermal energy, so total energy is conserved.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: World War I

**Prepare:** Print the Zimmermann Telegram (DocsTeach) with its decoded translation, four WWI propaganda posters (e.g., 'I Want You', 'Destroy This Mad Brute', 'Food Will Win the War', a Liberty Bond poster), Schenck v. United States (1919) excerpt, and a Fourteen Points summary.

**Watch for:**
- Students think the U.S. entered WWI right after the Lusitania (1915). The U.S. entered in April 1917, after Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram was revealed.
- Students think 'clear and present danger' is today's free-speech standard. It was later replaced; since Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), speech can be punished only if it incites imminent lawless action.
- Students think the Senate rejected the Treaty because of the war's outcome. Opposition centered on the League of Nations (Article X) and fears of entangling commitments.

### Day 1 (Monday): Road to war
*Student:* Analyze the Zimmermann Telegram and unrestricted submarine warfare.

*How to teach:* Analyze the Zimmermann Telegram and unrestricted submarine warfare.

- **Q:** What did the Zimmermann Telegram propose?
  **A:** A German-Mexican alliance if the U.S. entered the war, with Mexico regaining Texas, New Mexico and Arizona.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Propaganda
*Student:* Analyze four WWI posters for persuasion techniques.

*How to teach:* Analyze four WWI posters for persuasion techniques.

- **Q:** Name two propaganda techniques in WWI posters.
  **A:** Appeals to patriotism, fear (dehumanizing the enemy as a brute), guilt, and calls to action like buying Liberty Bonds.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Civil liberties
*Student:* Read Schenck v. U.S. and debate 'clear and present danger'.

*How to teach:* Read Schenck v. U.S. and debate 'clear and present danger'.

- **Q:** What analogy did Holmes use in Schenck?
  **A:** Free speech would not protect someone 'falsely shouting fire in a theatre and causing a panic.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Home front
*Student:* Study the Great Migration, women's work, and the 1918 flu.

*How to teach:* Study the Great Migration, women's war work and the 1918 flu.

- **Q:** Why did many Black Southerners move North during WWI?
  **A:** Factory jobs opened as immigration fell and men went to war; they also sought to escape Jim Crow segregation and violence.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz and paragraph on the Fourteen Points and Treaty of Versailles debate.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and a paragraph on the Fourteen Points and Versailles debate.

- **Q:** What was the main objection to the League of Nations in the Senate?
  **A:** Article X's collective-security pledge might commit the U.S. to war without Congress's approval.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Zimmermann Telegram (Jan. 1917) — Germany proposed a military alliance with Mexico, promising Texas, New Mexico and Arizona. 2) Unrestricted submarine warfare resumed Feb. 1917. 3) U.S. declared war April 1917. 4) Espionage Act (1917) and Sedition Act (1918) limited speech. 5) Schenck (1919) — Justice Holmes: 'clear and present danger'; distributing anti-draft leaflets not protected in wartime. 6) Great Migration — Black Southerners moved North for war jobs and to escape Jim Crow. 7) 1918 influenza killed about 675,000 Americans. 8) Fourteen Points — open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, free trade, arms reduction, self-determination, League of Nations. Paragraph rubric: explains why the Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles (Lodge's reservations, Article X collective security, Wilson's refusal to compromise) with evidence.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up the two Ochoas: Severo Ochoa was a Spanish biochemist (Nobel Prize, 1959); Ellen Ochoa is a Mexican-American engineer and astronaut — they are not related.
- Regularizing conditional stems (*tenería, *podería): tendría, podría.

### Day 1 (Monday): Lectura
*Student:* Read about three Hispanic scientists and inventors.

*How to teach:* Read about the three scientists; your child fills a chart: nationality, field, discovery or invention, impact.

- **Q:** What did Guillermo González Camarena invent?
  **A:** An early color television system (patented in the 1940s), making him a pioneer of color TV in Mexico.
- **Q:** Who is Ellen Ochoa?

## Lifetime Fitness: Upper body and core strength

**Prepare:** Gather dumbbells or jugs, resistance bands (anchor point such as a door anchor), and a mat. Preview the dead bug and bird dog movements so you can demonstrate.

**Watch for:**
- Arching the lower back when pressing overhead. Brace the core and squeeze glutes; reduce the load if needed.
- Thinking 'core' means just sit-ups. Core training emphasizes stability — resisting movement (anti-extension, anti-rotation).
- Holding the breath for long sets. Exhale on the effort phase; brief bracing is fine.

### Day 1 (Monday): Overhead press
*Student:* Learn overhead press and lateral raises with bands or light dumbbells.

*How to teach:* Teach overhead press (feet hip-width, brace core, press straight up, biceps by ears) and lateral raises (slight elbow bend, raise to shoulder height). 3×10 each with light load.

- **Q:** Which muscle does a lateral raise mainly target?
  **A:** The deltoids (especially the middle/lateral deltoid).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Core stability
*Student:* Learn planks, side planks, dead bugs, and bird dogs.

*How to teach:* Teach plank, side plank, dead bug, and bird dog. Cue neutral spine and slow movement. Hold or do 3 rounds.

- **Q:** What is the main job of the core in a plank?
  **A:** Anti-extension — preventing the lower back from sagging, keeping a neutral spine.
- **Q:** In a dead bug, what should the low back do?
  **A:** Stay gently pressed toward the floor while opposite arm and leg extend.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Anti-rotation
*Student:* Perform Pallof press with bands.

*How to teach:* Anchor a band at chest height; stand sideways, press hands straight out and resist the band's pull to rotate. 3×10 each side.

- **Q:** Why is the Pallof press called 'anti-rotation'?
  **A:** The band pulls the body to rotate, and the core works to resist that rotation and keep the torso square.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Upper body workout
*Student:* Complete a full upper-body and core workout.

*How to teach:* Upper body and core workout: overhead press, band row or inverted row, push-up, lateral raise, plank, Pallof press. 3 sets; log.

- **Q:** Name the large back muscle worked in rows and pulldowns.
  **A:** The latissimus dorsi ('lats').

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Log and reflect on progress.

*How to teach:* Review the week's log; student writes what improved and one goal for next week.

- **Q:** If all your sets of 10 felt easy (RPE 5), what should you do next week?
  **A:** Apply progressive overload — add load or reps (e.g., 12 reps or a slightly heavier weight).

**Assessment:** Workout log.

**Answer key:** Workout log rubric. 4 – All sessions logged with exercises, sets, reps, loads, RPE, and a reflection that names progress and a specific progression for next week. 3 (proficient) – Complete exercise/set/rep/load data and a short reflection. 2 – Partial log. 1 – Minimal data.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Applied geometry and unit review

**Prepare:** Gather a kitchen scale (0.1 g or 1 g resolution), a measuring cup marked in mL, water, and small solid samples of three materials (e.g. a steel bolt, an aluminum part, a piece of wood or plastic). Prepare the Unit 3 test from the key below.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing units: 1 mL = 1 cm³. Keep volume in cm³ and mass in g so density comes out in g/cm³.
- Forgetting to subtract the starting water level in displacement. Volume of object = final level − initial level.
- Adding surface area of hidden faces in composite parts. Only exterior surfaces count; subtract any removed hole and add its inside wall.

### Day 1 (Monday): Area & volume
*Student:* Calculate volume and surface area of prisms, cylinders, and composite parts from drawings.

*How to teach:* Review formulas: rectangular prism V = l·w·h, SA = 2(lw + lh + wh); cylinder V = πr²h, SA = 2πr² + 2πrh. Work composite parts by adding and subtracting volumes.

- **Q:** Find the volume and surface area of a 5 × 4 × 3 cm block.
  **A:** V = 60 cm³; SA = 2(20 + 15 + 12) = 94 cm².
- **Q:** Find the volume of a cylinder with r = 2 cm and h = 5 cm.
  **A:** π × 4 × 5 = 20π ≈ 62.8 cm³.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Density lab
*Student:* Find the density of three materials using water displacement and a kitchen scale.

*How to teach:* Weigh each sample. Fill the cup partway, read the level, submerge the sample, read again; volume = difference. Density = mass ÷ volume. Compare with reference values.

- **Q:** A bolt has mass 78.5 g and displaces 10 mL of water. What is its density and likely material?
  **A:** 7.85 g/cm³ — steel.
- **Q:** Why does a piece of pine float?
  **A:** Its density (about 0.4–0.5 g/cm³) is less than water's 1.0 g/cm³.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mass estimates
*Student:* Predict part mass from volume and material density and verify by weighing.

*How to teach:* Pick a part, compute its volume from measurements, predict mass = volume × density, then weigh and compute percent error.

- **Q:** A 2 × 2 × 10 cm aluminum bar: predicted mass?
  **A:** V = 40 cm³; mass = 40 × 2.70 = 108 g.
- **Q:** Predicted 108 g, measured 104 g. Percent error?
  **A:** |104 − 108| ÷ 108 × 100 ≈ 3.7%.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Review
*Student:* Mixed review of measurement, statistics, and geometry problems.

*How to teach:* Mixed review set covering measurement, statistics and geometry; check and correct each error together.

- **Q:** Convert 75 mm to inches.
  **A:** 75 ÷ 25.4 ≈ 2.95 in.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 3 test.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 test in the assessment key.

- **Q:** What is the density of an object with mass 54 g and volume 20 cm³?
  **A:** 2.7 g/cm³ (likely aluminum).

**Assessment:** Density lab and Unit 3 test.

**Answer key:** Density lab: accept densities within about 10% of reference values — aluminum 2.70 g/cm³, steel about 7.85 g/cm³, brass about 8.5 g/cm³, pine about 0.35–0.50 g/cm³, PLA plastic 1.24 g/cm³, ABS about 1.04 g/cm³ (wood floats; push it under with a pin for volume). Unit 3 test: 1) Accuracy vs. precision definitions. 2) Convert 2.5 in to mm → 63.5 mm. 3) Read a caliper value (check against the item). 4) Mean/median/mode/range of 8, 9, 9, 10, 14 → 10, 9, 9, 6. 5) Sample SD of 2, 4, 6 → 2. 6) ~68% of data within 1 SD. 7) Volume of a cylinder r = 2 cm, h = 5 cm → 62.8 cm³. 8) Surface area of that cylinder → 2π(2²) + 2π(2)(5) = 28π ≈ 88.0 cm². 9) Rectangular block 4 × 3 × 2 cm with a Ø1 cm through-hole along the 2 cm direction → 24 − π(0.5²)(2) = 24 − 1.57 ≈ 22.4 cm³. 10) Mass of that block in aluminum → 22.43 × 2.70 ≈ 60.6 g. Score 8+ mastery.
