# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 25

## English 11 – American Literature: Research: notes, outline, and drafting

**Prepare:** Research notes and sources; an outline template. Notecards or a digital notes document with columns for source, page, note type.

**Watch for:**
- Patchwriting (swapping a few words while keeping the source's sentence structure). Teach read–cover–write–compare and always cite paraphrases.
- 'Dropping' quotations into paragraphs with no introduction. Every quotation needs a signal phrase and analysis after it.

### Day 1 (Monday): Note-taking
*Student:* Take notes using quotation, paraphrase, and summary, labeling each with source and page.

*How to teach:* Model one note of each type (quotation, paraphrase, summary), each labeled with source and page. The student takes notes from three sources. Today's task from the plan: Take notes using quotation, paraphrase, and summary, labeling each with source and page. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a paraphrase and a summary?
  **A:** A paraphrase restates a specific passage in your own words at about the same length; a summary condenses the main ideas of a longer section. Both must be cited.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Paraphrase practice
*Student:* Practice paraphrasing three tricky passages and compare with originals to avoid patchwriting.

*How to teach:* Give three tricky passages. The student paraphrases from memory, then compares with the original to find borrowed phrasing. Today's task from the plan: Practice paraphrasing three tricky passages and compare with originals to avoid patchwriting. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Is changing a few words of a source sentence an acceptable paraphrase?
  **A:** No. That is patchwriting; a true paraphrase changes both wording and sentence structure, and it still needs a citation.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Outline
*Student:* Build a detailed outline with thesis, sub-claims, and assigned evidence.

*How to teach:* Model one outline section (sub-claim, evidence A and B, analysis). The student completes a full outline. Today's task from the plan: Build a detailed outline with thesis, sub-claims, and assigned evidence. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What belongs in each body section of the outline?
  **A:** A sub-claim supporting the thesis, the specific evidence assigned to it (with sources), and a note on the analysis connecting evidence to claim.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Draft I
*Student:* Draft introduction and first two body sections (about 1,000 words) with signal phrases and in-text citations.

*How to teach:* The student drafts the introduction and first two sections (about 1,000 words) with signal phrases and in-text citations. Today's task from the plan: Draft introduction and first two body sections (about 1,000 words) with signal phrases and in-text citations. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Write a model signal phrase with MLA citation.
  **A:** As historian Lizabeth Cohen argues, '...' (Cohen 45). Or: Fitzgerald describes the valley as 'a fantastic farm where ashes grow' (Fitzgerald 23).

### Day 5 (Friday): Draft II & check
*Student:* Draft remaining sections and conclusion; submit full rough draft (1,800–2,200 words).

*How to teach:* The student drafts the remaining sections and conclusion and submits the full 1,800–2,200 word rough draft. Today's task from the plan: Draft remaining sections and conclusion; submit full rough draft (1,800–2,200 words). Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What should a research paper's conclusion do?
  **A:** Restate the thesis in fresh words, synthesize the main points, and explain the significance or a broader implication; no new evidence.

**Assessment:** Rough draft checkpoint.

**Answer key:** Rough draft checkpoint (complete/incomplete with comments): 1,800–2,200 words; clear thesis in the introduction; every body section has a sub-claim, cited evidence, and analysis; signal phrases and MLA in-text citations; Works Cited draft. Flag any uncited paraphrase immediately.

## Algebra II: The Pythagorean identity and other trig values

**Prepare:** The completed unit circle from last week.

**Watch for:**
- Ignoring the quadrant when taking a square root: from cos²θ = 16/25, cos θ could be ±4/5; the quadrant decides the sign.
- Thinking sec θ = 1/sin θ. 'S' goes with 'C': sec = 1/cos, csc = 1/sin.

### Day 1 (Monday): Proof
*Student:* Prove sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 from the unit circle and the Pythagorean theorem.

*How to teach:* Point (cos θ, sin θ) on the unit circle forms a right triangle with legs |cos θ|, |sin θ| and hypotenuse 1; Pythagoras gives the identity. Today's task from the plan: Prove sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 from the unit circle and the Pythagorean theorem. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Use the identity to check sin(π/6) and cos(π/6).
  **A:** (1/2)² + (√3/2)² = 1/4 + 3/4 = 1 ✓.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Using the identity
*Student:* Given sin θ = 3/5 in Quadrant II, find cos θ and tan θ.

*How to teach:* Model finding cos θ from sin θ with the identity, then the sign by quadrant. Today's task from the plan: Given sin θ = 3/5 in Quadrant II, find cos θ and tan θ. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** sin θ = 3/5 with θ in Quadrant II. Find cos θ and tan θ.
  **A:** cos²θ = 1 − 9/25 = 16/25; QII so cos θ = −4/5; tan θ = (3/5)/(−4/5) = −3/4.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Reciprocal functions
*Student:* Define sec, csc, cot and evaluate them on the unit circle.

*How to teach:* Define sec, csc, cot as reciprocals; evaluate at special angles. Today's task from the plan: Define sec, csc, cot and evaluate them on the unit circle. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Evaluate sec(π/3), csc(π/6), cot(π/4), and sec(π/2).
  **A:** 2, 2, 1, and undefined (cos(π/2) = 0).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mixed practice
*Student:* Solve 12 problems finding all six trig values from one value.

*How to teach:* Student finds all six values from one given value for 12 problems. Today's task from the plan: Solve 12 problems finding all six trig values from one value. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** cos θ = −5/13 in Quadrant III. Find the other five.
  **A:** sin θ = −12/13, tan θ = 12/5, csc θ = −13/12, sec θ = −13/5, cot θ = 5/12.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on the identity and reciprocal functions.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (about 30 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Quiz on the identity and reciprocal functions. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** If tan θ = 1 and θ is in Quadrant III, find sin θ.
  **A:** θ = 5π/4, sin θ = −√2/2.

**Assessment:** Identity quiz.

**Answer key:** Sample key: 1) proof of sin²+cos²=1 from x²+y²=1 (rubric: diagram, coordinates, Pythagorean step). 2) cos θ = 0.6 in QIV → sin θ = −0.8, tan θ = −4/3. 3) csc(3π/2) = −1. 4) sec(π/4) = √2. 5) cot(π/6) = √3. 6) sin θ = −1/3 in QIII: cos θ = −2√2/3.

## Physics of the Universe: Plate tectonics and crustal ages

**Prepare:** Seafloor age map (NOAA), world map for cutting continent shapes, fossil distribution map (Mesosaurus, Glossopteris).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking continents plow through the ocean. Plates include both continental and oceanic lithosphere moving together.
- Thinking the oldest rocks are on the seafloor. Oceanic crust is young (under about 200 million years); the oldest rocks are on continents (about 4 billion years).

### Day 1 (Monday): Continental drift
*Student:* Fit continent cutouts using fossil and rock evidence.

*How to teach:* Print a world map, cut out the continents along the continental shelf edges, and fit them together. The student then adds fossil locations (Mesosaurus, Glossopteris, Lystrosaurus) and matching rock belts as evidence for Wegener's continental drift.

- **Q:** What fossil evidence supports continental drift?
  **A:** Fossils of the same land reptile (Mesosaurus) and plants (Glossopteris) are found on continents now separated by oceans.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Seafloor spreading
*Student:* Analyze seafloor age maps and magnetic striping.

*How to teach:* Show the NOAA seafloor-age map and a diagram of magnetic stripes at a ridge. The student measures how far rock of a given age is from the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and computes a spreading rate (distance ÷ age).

- **Q:** What pattern shows seafloor spreading?
  **A:** Rock is youngest at mid-ocean ridges and gets older with distance, with symmetric magnetic stripes on both sides.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Boundary types
*Student:* Map divergent, convergent, and transform boundaries.

*How to teach:* On a world map, the student colors divergent, convergent, and transform boundaries using a USGS plate map, then adds one landform example for each (ridge/rift, trench/mountain range, fault).

- **Q:** Give an example of each boundary.
  **A:** Divergent: Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Convergent: Cascadia subduction zone or Himalayas. Transform: San Andreas Fault.

### Day 4 (Thursday): California
*Student:* Study the San Andreas Fault as a transform boundary and Cascadia subduction.

*How to teach:* Look at a map of California's faults. The student explains how the San Andreas (transform) and the Cascadia subduction zone (convergent, northern California to British Columbia) produce different kinds of earthquakes and hazards.

- **Q:** What type of boundary is the San Andreas Fault?
  **A:** Transform: the Pacific Plate slides northwest past the North American Plate.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a CER on the ages of crustal rocks.

*How to teach:* The student writes a CER answering 'Why are seafloor rocks so much younger than continental rocks?' using seafloor-age map data and the idea of subduction.

- **Q:** Why is oceanic crust younger than continental crust?
  **A:** Oceanic crust is constantly created at ridges and destroyed by subduction; continental crust is less dense and isn't subducted.

**Assessment:** Plate tectonics CER.

**Answer key:** CER rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning). Claim: answers the question about why oceanic crust is younger than continental crust in one sentence. Evidence: specific data from the student's own lab/simulation with numbers and units. Reasoning: connects the evidence to the scientific principle. A proficient response: seafloor age map data (youngest at ridges, oldest about 180–200 million years) vs continental rocks up to about 4 billion years, and reasoning about seafloor spreading, subduction, and density.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Nonviolent direct action

**Prepare:** Print Rosa Parks's arrest record and the Montgomery Improvement Association leaflet (National Archives), Freedom Riders' mugshots (Mississippi Department of Archives), excerpts of 'Letter from Birmingham Jail' (April 16, 1963), Birmingham campaign photos, 'I Have a Dream' (Aug. 28, 1963) and a Malcolm X excerpt ('The Ballot or the Bullet', April 1964).

**Watch for:**
- Students think Rosa Parks was simply tired. She was an experienced NAACP activist and secretary of the Montgomery branch who made a deliberate choice.
- Students think the movement was led only by King. Local organizers, students (SNCC), women (Ella Baker, Fannie Lou Hamer, Diane Nash) and many others led.
- Students think King and Malcolm X were simple opposites. They differed on integration and nonviolence, but both fought racism, and their views evolved.

### Day 1 (Monday): Montgomery
*Student:* Analyze Rosa Parks' arrest record and the Montgomery Improvement Association leaflet.

*How to teach:* Analyze Parks's arrest record and the MIA leaflet.

- **Q:** Why was the bus boycott effective?
  **A:** Black riders were the majority of Montgomery's bus customers; a sustained boycott with car pools cost the company revenue and drew national attention.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sit-ins & Freedom Rides
*Student:* Study Greensboro, SNCC, and the Freedom Rides; analyze Freedom Riders' mugshots.

*How to teach:* Study Greensboro, SNCC and the Freedom Rides; analyze mugshots.

- **Q:** What did the Freedom Riders test?
  **A:** Whether Southern states would obey Supreme Court rulings desegregating interstate buses and terminals.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Birmingham
*Student:* Read excerpts of 'Letter from Birmingham Jail' and connect to the Birmingham campaign photos.

*How to teach:* Read 'Letter from Birmingham Jail' excerpts and connect to campaign photos.

- **Q:** How did King distinguish just and unjust laws?
  **A:** A just law squares with moral law; an unjust law degrades human personality, and segregation laws are unjust.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Different visions
*Student:* Compare 'I Have a Dream' with a Malcolm X speech excerpt.

*How to teach:* Compare 'I Have a Dream' with a Malcolm X excerpt.

- **Q:** How did Malcolm X's approach differ from King's in 1964?
  **A:** He emphasized Black nationalism, self-defense and the ballot 'or the bullet', rather than nonviolent integration.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on civil rights strategies and leaders.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz on civil rights strategies and leaders.

- **Q:** What is a sit-in?
  **A:** A nonviolent protest where people occupy seats in a segregated place and refuse to leave.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Montgomery Bus Boycott (Dec. 1955–Dec. 1956, 381 days); ended by Browder v. Gayle. 2) Montgomery Improvement Association — led by Martin Luther King Jr. 3) Greensboro sit-in (Feb. 1, 1960) — four NC A&T students at Woolworth's. 4) SNCC founded 1960 with Ella Baker's help. 5) Freedom Rides (1961) — CORE tested desegregation of interstate bus terminals (Boynton v. Virginia); riders attacked in Anniston and Birmingham. 6) Birmingham campaign (1963) — Bull Connor's dogs and fire hoses on children. 7) 'Letter from Birmingham Jail' — 'Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere'; just vs. unjust laws; criticism of the white moderate. 8) March on Washington (Aug. 28, 1963). 9) Malcolm X — Black nationalism, self-defense, 'by any means necessary'. 10) SCLC — founded 1957, King president.

## Spanish IV: La salud pública

**Prepare:** Find a PAHO/OPS (Organización Panamericana de la Salud) Spanish infographic on teen health and prepare a por/para chart. Gather information on the health systems of Cuba (universal, state-run), Spain (universal public Sistema Nacional de Salud funded by taxes), and the U.S. (mix of private insurance, employer plans, Medicare, Medicaid). Preview: topics may include mental health.

**Watch for:**
- Using por for purpose/destination (*Estudio por ser médico): para (purpose, goal, recipient, deadline, destination) — Estudio para ser médico.
- Using para for cause, duration, exchange, or through/by (*Gracias para la ayuda): Gracias por la ayuda; Caminamos por el parque; Lo compré por diez dólares.

### Day 1 (Monday): Infografía
*Student:* Analyze a PAHO infographic on teen health and summarize findings.

*How to teach:* Your child analyzes the PAHO infographic and summarizes findings in 5 sentences.

- **Q:** What is the OPS (PAHO)?
  **A:** The Pan American Health Organization, the World Health Organization's regional office for the Americas.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Por y para
*Student:* Review por vs. para uses and complete 25 contextual sentences.

*How to teach:* Review por vs. para; your child completes 25 contextual sentences with reasons.

- **Q:** Fill in: 'Hago ejercicio ___ estar sano y camino ___ el parque.'
  **A:** para; por
- **Q:** Fill in: 'Gracias ___ la información; es muy útil ___ mi proyecto.'
  **A:** por; para

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sistemas de salud
*Student:* Compare public health systems in Cuba, Spain, and the U.S. in a chart.

*How to teach:* Your child compares health systems in Cuba, Spain, and the U.S. in a chart.

- **Q:** How is Spain's health system funded?
  **A:** Mainly through taxes; the national public system provides universal coverage, with optional private insurance.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Anuncio
*Student:* Create a public service announcement script on a teen health issue.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a PSA script on a teen health issue (sleep, nutrition, mental health, vaping).

- **Q:** Write a PSA line with a command and por/para.
  **A:** Example: Duerme ocho horas para tener energía; tu cuerpo te lo agradecerá por la mañana.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Record the PSA and take a por/para quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child records the PSA; give the por/para quiz.

- **Q:** Correct: 'Salimos por Los Ángeles mañana para la mañana.'
  **A:** Salimos para Los Ángeles mañana por la mañana.

**Assessment:** PSA and quiz.

**Answer key:** PSA rubric: 4 = 1 minute, clear teen health message with facts from the infographic, commands and subjunctive (Es importante que...), por/para used correctly; 3 = minor errors; 2 = unclear; 1 = incomplete. Por/para quiz sample key: 1) Salgo para Madrid (destination) 2) Viajamos por Chile (through) 3) Este regalo es para ti (recipient) 4) Gracias por todo (cause) 5) Estudio para ser enfermera (goal) 6) Lo vendí por veinte dólares (exchange) 7) La tarea es para el lunes (deadline) 8) Estuve enfermo por una semana (duration) 9) Para un adolescente, habla muy bien (comparison) 10) Fue escrito por la OPS (agent in passive).

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Costume and makeup design

**Prepare:** Gather colored pencils, paper, thrift/closet pieces, and basic makeup (neutral foundation, brown/gray contour, highlight, brushes). Choose a play with a clear period (e.g., Trifles, 1916 rural Iowa; Our Town, 1901–1913 New Hampshire). Check for skin sensitivities.

**Watch for:**
- Designing costumes to look fashionable instead of revealing character and period.
- Using too much contour so aging looks like dirt. Blend and follow natural lines.
- Forgetting quick changes and movement needs in the costume plot.

### Day 1 (Monday): Research
*Student:* Research the historical period of a chosen play.

*How to teach:* Research the period: silhouettes, fabrics, social class indicators.

- **Q:** What clothing might Mrs. Hale wear in Trifles (1916 rural farm)?
  **A:** A long, plain cotton or wool dress (ankle length), apron, sturdy shoes, a coat, hat, and scarf for the cold.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Renderings
*Student:* Draw costume renderings for three characters.

*How to teach:* Draw renderings for three characters with fabric notes and swatches.

- **Q:** What is a costume rendering?
  **A:** A drawing of a character in costume showing color, silhouette, and details, used to communicate the design.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Costume plot
*Student:* Create a costume plot by scene.

*How to teach:* Create a costume plot: chart of each character's costume per scene.

- **Q:** Why make a costume plot?
  **A:** To track which costume each actor wears in each scene and plan changes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Makeup
*Student:* Practice basic stage makeup (contour, aging) on yourself.

*How to teach:* Practice aging makeup: find natural creases, add shadow with contour, highlight above each line, blend.

- **Q:** How do highlight and shadow create aging?
  **A:** Shadow makes areas recede (hollows, wrinkle lines) and highlight makes areas come forward, imitating sagging and creases under stage light.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Present a costume design portfolio.

*How to teach:* Present the portfolio: research, renderings, plot, makeup photos.

- **Q:** What does 'silhouette' mean in costume design?
  **A:** The overall outline or shape of the costume on the body.

**Assessment:** Costume portfolio.

**Answer key:** Costume portfolio rubric. 4 – Period research with sources, three detailed renderings reflecting character and period, complete costume plot, makeup demonstration photos, written rationale. 3 (proficient) – Research, three renderings, plot, makeup practice. 2 – Missing elements. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness: Group fitness: circuits and boot camp

**Prepare:** Set up 10 stations (cones, jump rope, bands, mats) with signs; interval timer app. Prepare a feedback survey (3–5 questions). Invite family or friends for Day 4.

**Watch for:**
- Leading by talking constantly. Good instructors cue briefly, early, and specifically.
- Offering only one level. Always give easier and harder modifications.
- Thinking 40/20 means 'rest is optional.' Rest maintains quality and safety.

### Day 1 (Monday): Circuit
*Student:* Complete a 10-station, 40-second-on/20-second-off circuit and rate each station's intensity.

*How to teach:* Student completes a 10-station circuit, 40 s on/20 s off, and rates each station's RPE.

- **Q:** How long is one round of a 10-station 40/20 circuit?
  **A:** 10 minutes (60 s per station × 10).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cueing
*Student:* Learn verbal, visual, and tactile cueing and practice cueing a squat and push-up to a family member.

*How to teach:* Teach cueing: verbal ('chest up'), visual (demonstrate), tactile (only with permission, e.g., touch shoulder blade). Student cues a squat and a push-up.

- **Q:** Give one verbal cue each for a squat and a push-up.
  **A:** Squat: 'Sit back, chest proud, knees out.' Push-up: 'Body like a plank, lower chest to fist height.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Design
*Student:* Design a 30-minute boot-camp class with warm-up, two circuits, cool-down, and easier/harder modifications.

*How to teach:* Student designs a 30-minute boot camp: 5-min warm-up, two circuits, cool-down, with easier/harder modifications for each exercise.

- **Q:** Give an easier and harder modification for a push-up.
  **A:** Easier: incline or knee push-up. Harder: feet elevated or tempo/plyometric push-up.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Lead
*Student:* Lead family or friends through the boot camp, adjusting for different fitness levels.

*How to teach:* Student leads the class, adjusting for participants. Observe using the leadership rubric.

- **Q:** A participant reports knee pain during lunges. What should the leader do?
  **A:** Stop the exercise for them and substitute a pain-free option (e.g., glute bridge or partial-range squat); recommend checking with a professional if pain persists.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Collect participant feedback with a short survey and write a self-evaluation of your leadership.

*How to teach:* Distribute the survey; student summarizes results and writes a self-evaluation.

- **Q:** Why collect participant feedback?
  **A:** To learn what worked and what to improve — leadership requires adapting to the group.

**Assessment:** Leadership rubric.

**Answer key:** Leadership rubric. 4 – Clear plan with warm-up, 2 circuits, cool-down, modifications for every exercise; concise cues; adjusts in real time; safe environment; thoughtful self-evaluation using survey data. 3 (proficient) – Complete plan, clear cues, offers modifications, reflection. 2 – Missing modifications or unclear cues. 1 – Unsafe or unprepared.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Design communication and pitching

**Prepare:** Learn Onshape's appearance and render options (or take clean screenshots). Have slide software ready. Prepare a short Unit 7 quiz from the key.

**Watch for:**
- Pitches that describe features instead of the problem. Start with the user's problem; features matter only as they solve it.
- Going over time. An elevator pitch is about 60 seconds — rehearse with a timer.
- Storyboards that show only the product. Show the user, the problem, and how the product fits into their experience.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rendering
*Student:* Create appearance-styled renderings of your phone stand in Onshape.

*How to teach:* Apply appearances (colors, materials) to the phone-stand model and create clean renderings or screenshots from several angles.

- **Q:** Why do engineers create renderings?
  **A:** To communicate what a product will look like to clients, users or investors before it's made.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Storyboard
*Student:* Create a 6-panel storyboard showing a user's experience with the product.

*How to teach:* Draw a 6-panel storyboard: user has a problem, tries current options, finds the product, uses it, outcome, how they feel.

- **Q:** What should a storyboard show besides the product?
  **A:** The user, their problem and context, and how their experience changes.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pitch writing
*Student:* Write a 60-second elevator pitch covering problem, user, solution, and evidence.

*How to teach:* Write a 60-second pitch: problem, user, solution, evidence, ask.

- **Q:** About how many words is a 60-second pitch?
  **A:** About 130–160 words at a normal speaking pace.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Practice
*Student:* Record and revise the pitch twice.

*How to teach:* Record the pitch, watch it, revise twice.

- **Q:** What should you look for when reviewing your recording?
  **A:** Time, clarity of the problem, filler words, pace, and whether evidence is included.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Deliver the pitch and take a short Unit 7 quiz.

*How to teach:* Deliver the pitch and give the Unit 7 quiz.

- **Q:** What is anthropometry?
  **A:** The measurement of the size and proportions of the human body.

**Assessment:** Pitch rubric and quiz.

**Answer key:** Pitch rubric (4 levels): 4 = within 60 seconds; states the problem and the specific user, the solution and how it works, evidence (test results or user feedback), and a clear ask/closing; confident voice and eye contact; 3 = all content, delivery uneven; 2 = missing problem or evidence; 1 = unprepared. Unit 7 quiz: 1) Ergonomics definition. 2) 5th–95th percentile design range. 3) Anthropometry = measurement of human body dimensions. 4) Universal design example. 5) Open vs. leading interview questions. 6) Persona definition. 7) Journey map definition. 8) 'How might we' statements frame needs without dictating solutions. Mastery 7+/8.
