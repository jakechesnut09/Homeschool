## Algebra II: Other trig graphs

**Prepare:** Desmos in radian mode.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking tan x has a maximum of 1 like sine. Tangent's range is all real numbers.
- Placing secant asymptotes where cosine is 1. Secant is undefined where cosine is 0.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tangent
*Student:* Graph y = tan x from sin/cos ratios and identify asymptotes and period π.

*How to teach:* Build tan x = sin x/cos x: zeros where sin = 0, asymptotes where cos = 0. Today's task from the plan: Graph y = tan x from sin/cos ratios and identify asymptotes and period π. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Give the period, zeros, and asymptotes of y = tan x.
  **A:** Period π; zeros at x = kπ; vertical asymptotes at x = π/2 + kπ.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cosecant & secant
*Student:* Graph csc and sec from their reciprocal sine and cosine graphs.

*How to teach:* Graph sin x, then csc x = 1/sin x on top: U-shapes touching the sine peaks. Today's task from the plan: Graph csc and sec from their reciprocal sine and cosine graphs. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Where are the asymptotes of y = csc x and what is its range?
  **A:** At x = kπ (where sin x = 0). Range y ≤ −1 or y ≥ 1.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cotangent
*Student:* Graph cot x and compare with tan x.

*How to teach:* Graph cot x = cos x/sin x and compare with tan x. Today's task from the plan: Graph cot x and compare with tan x. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** How does cot x differ from tan x?
  **A:** Asymptotes at x = kπ (instead of π/2 + kπ), zeros at π/2 + kπ, and it decreases on each interval while tan increases. Same period π.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Transformations
*Student:* Apply transformations to tangent and secant graphs.

*How to teach:* Apply a, b, h, k to tangent and secant in Desmos. Today's task from the plan: Apply transformations to tangent and secant graphs. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Find the period and asymptotes of y = 2 tan(x/2).
  **A:** Period π/(1/2) = 2π; asymptotes at x = π + 2kπ.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz matching six trig function graphs and equations.

*How to teach:* Quiz: match six graphs to equations (about 25 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Quiz matching six trig function graphs and equations. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Which graph has U-shaped pieces opening up at x = 0 and asymptotes at ±π/2?
  **A:** y = sec x.

**Assessment:** Trig graphs quiz.

**Answer key:** Matching key: y = tan x ↔ increasing branches, asymptotes ±π/2, through origin; y = cot x ↔ decreasing branches, asymptotes 0 and π; y = sec x ↔ U at (0,1), asymptotes ±π/2; y = csc x ↔ U at (π/2,1), asymptotes 0, π; y = tan 2x ↔ period π/2; y = −sec x ↔ downward U at (0,−1). 1 pt each.

## Physics of the Universe: The Sun

**Prepare:** Materials for a pinhole projector (two pieces of cardboard, foil, pin); NASA SDO images. NEVER look directly at the Sun.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Sun burns like fire. It shines from nuclear fusion.
- Thinking the Sun will explode as a supernova. It's not massive enough.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sun structure
*Student:* Study the Sun's layers using NASA images.

*How to teach:* Look at NASA Solar Dynamics Observatory images in different wavelengths. The student draws and labels the Sun's layers and notes the temperature of the core (about 15 million °C) versus the photosphere (about 5,500 °C).

- **Q:** List the Sun's layers from the center out.
  **A:** Core, radiative zone, convective zone, photosphere, chromosphere, corona.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sunspots
*Student:* Build a pinhole projector and safely observe the Sun's disk.

*How to teach:* Make a pinhole in foil taped over a hole in cardboard; stand with your back to the Sun and project its image onto a second card. Look for sunspots (larger pinhole images or binocular projection work better) and record their positions. Never look at the Sun through the pinhole or any optics.

- **Q:** Why must you never look at the Sun directly?
  **A:** Intense light (including UV and IR) can permanently damage the retina.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fusion
*Student:* Model hydrogen fusion and calculate energy release with E = mc².

*How to teach:* Show that four hydrogen-1 nuclei (total mass 4.032 u) fuse into one helium-4 (4.003 u), losing about 0.7% of the mass. The student uses E = mc² to compute energy released per kilogram of hydrogen fused (≈ 0.007 × 9 × 10¹⁶ ≈ 6 × 10¹⁴ J).

- **Q:** How much energy is released if 1 kg of mass is converted?
  **A:** E = 1 × (3 × 10⁸)² = 9 × 10¹⁶ J.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Life span
*Student:* Model the Sun's life span from fuel and energy output.

*How to teach:* Using the Sun's mass, the fraction of hydrogen it can fuse in its core (about 10%), and its luminosity (3.8 × 10²⁶ W), the student estimates its main-sequence lifetime and compares it with the accepted ~10 billion years.

- **Q:** How long will the Sun remain on the main sequence?
  **A:** About 10 billion years total; it is about 4.6 billion years old.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on the Sun.

*How to teach:* Sun quiz. Today's task from the plan: Quiz on the Sun. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What are sunspots?
  **A:** Cooler, darker regions on the photosphere caused by intense magnetic activity.

**Assessment:** Sun quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key (sample): layers; fusion of hydrogen into helium; E = mc²; sunspots cooler regions; Sun's age 4.6 billion years; future red giant → white dwarf.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: The movement expands

**Prepare:** Calisphere Delano grape strike documents (photos, UFW flyers), sources on the 1968 East L.A. walkouts, Third World Liberation Front strike materials (SF State 1968–69), the Alcatraz proclamation (1969), the NOW Statement of Purpose (1966), and summaries of Title IX (1972), the ADA (1990) and Stonewall (1969). *How to teach:* Analyze the 1968 East L.A. walkouts.

## Spanish IV: El cuento hispanoamericano: Quiroga y Borges

**Prepare:** Print Horacio Quiroga's 'El almohadón de plumas' (1907, public domain) with a glossary. Obtain Jorge Luis Borges's 'Los dos reyes y los dos laberintos' (in El Aleph, 1949) from a library or licensed anthology — it is under copyright, so do not copy it. Preview: Quiroga's story is a horror tale about a slow, mysterious death.

**Watch for:**
- Summarizing instead of analyzing: an analysis explains how the author creates an effect (suspense, irony, symbolism) with evidence.
- Calling every strange story 'realismo mágico': Quiroga's tale is horror with a naturalistic explanation; Borges writes fantastic, philosophical fiction; magical realism (García Márquez) treats the supernatural as ordinary.
- Writing about literature in the past only: use the literary present (Quiroga describe..., el rey abandona...).

### Day 1 (Monday): Quiroga
*Student:* Read 'El almohadón de plumas' with a vocabulary guide and summarize the plot.

*How to teach:* Read 'El almohadón de plumas' with the glossary; your child summarizes the plot in 8 sentences.

- **Q:** Summarize the plot.
  **A:** Newlywed Alicia lives in a cold, silent house with her stern husband Jordán; she grows weak and anemic, has hallucinations, and dies; the maid notices stains on her feather pillow, and Jordán cuts it open to find a swollen parasite that had sucked her blood nightly.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Análisis
*Student:* Analyze how Quiroga builds suspense; discuss in Spanish using literary vocabulary.

*How to teach:* Analyze how Quiroga builds suspense (setting, gradual decline, doctors' failure, delayed revelation).

- **Q:** How does the setting contribute to the mood?
  **A:** The white, silent, cold house (like an enchanted palace in autumn) mirrors the cold marriage and creates a chilling, isolated atmosphere.
- **Q:** What is the effect of the final explanation about bird parasites?
  **A:** The clinical, matter-of-fact ending makes the horror seem real and possible, increasing its impact.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Borges
*Student:* Read 'Los dos reyes y los dos laberintos' and discuss its symbolism.

*How to teach:* Read Borges's story; discuss its symbolism.

- **Q:** Summarize 'Los dos reyes y los dos laberintos'.
  **A:** A king of Babylon builds a complex labyrinth and traps a visiting Arab king in it to mock him; the Arab king escapes by praying to God, later conquers Babylon, and leaves the Babylonian king in the desert — a labyrinth with no stairs, doors, or walls — where he dies.
- **Q:** What does the desert symbolize?
  **A:** A labyrinth made by God/nature, vaster than any human design; it suggests human pride is small before the divine and that cruelty is repaid.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparación
*Student:* Compare the two stories' endings in a Venn diagram.

*How to teach:* Your child compares the two endings in a Venn diagram.

- **Q:** Give one similarity between the endings.
  **A:** Both end in death caused by something hidden or vast that the victim could not escape, and both reveal a final ironic twist.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Write a 250-word literary analysis paragraph on one story.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a 250-word literary analysis paragraph on one story.

- **Q:** Write a thesis for an analysis of Quiroga's story.
  **A:** Example: En 'El almohadón de plumas', Quiroga crea suspenso al retrasar la causa de la enfermedad de Alicia hasta el desenlace.

**Assessment:** Literary analysis paragraph.

**Answer key:** Literary analysis paragraph rubric (250 words): 4 = arguable thesis about technique or theme, 2–3 pieces of textual evidence (brief quotations or specific references) explained, literary vocabulary (el ambiente, el desenlace, el narrador, el símbolo), literary present, accurate; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = mostly summary; 1 = incomplete.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Elements of playwriting

**Prepare:** Have Trifles and a play-format template (character names in caps centered or flush left, stage directions in italics/parentheses). Gather paper for character design.

**Watch for:**
- Characters explaining feelings directly ('I am angry because…'). Good dialogue uses subtext.
- Scenes without conflict. Two characters must want incompatible things.
- Writing novel-style description in stage directions. Keep them brief, present tense, playable.

### Day 1 (Monday): Structure
*Student:* Map Freytag's pyramid onto Trifles, labeling the inciting incident, turning points, and climax.

*How to teach:* Map Freytag's pyramid onto Trifles: exposition (men arrive; Hale's account of finding Mr. Wright), rising action (the women find preserves, quilt, birdcage), climax (discovery of the strangled canary), falling action (they decide what to do as men return), resolution (they hide the box; 'knot it').

- **Q:** What is the climax of Trifles?
  **A:** The discovery of the dead canary with its neck wrung — revealing the motive and forcing the women's choice.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dialogue only
*Student:* Write a 1-page scene using only dialogue in which two characters want the same object; no stage directions allowed.

*How to teach:* Write a 1-page scene with only dialogue: two characters want the same object.

- **Q:** How can dialogue show action without stage directions?
  **A:** Characters reference what they do or see ('Put that down!'), and conflict is revealed through what they say and withhold.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Play format
*Student:* Learn standard play format (character names, parentheticals, stage directions) and reformat your scene.

*How to teach:* Teach play format; reformat the scene.

- **Q:** Where do stage directions go in play format and what tense are they in?
  **A:** Set off (often in italics or parentheses), indented, written in present tense.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Character design
*Student:* Create two original characters with a want, a fear, and a secret; write a 1-paragraph bio for each.

*How to teach:* Create two characters with want, fear, and secret; write bios.

- **Q:** Why give a character a secret?
  **A:** It creates subtext and potential for revelation and conflict.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Submit a 3-page scene draft putting the two characters in conflict.

*How to teach:* Submit a 3-page scene draft with the two characters in conflict.

- **Q:** What is an inciting incident?
  **A:** The event that sets the main conflict in motion.

**Assessment:** Scene draft.

**Answer key:** Scene draft rubric. 4 – Correct play format; two distinct characters with clear competing wants; conflict escalates to a turning point; subtext in dialogue; concise stage directions; 3 pages. 3 (proficient) – Correct format, clear conflict, distinct characters. 2 – Format errors or weak conflict. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness: Volleyball: skills and strategy

**Prepare:** Get a volleyball and a net or a rope tied between two posts at about 7'4"–7'11" (or lower for recreation). Find a smooth wall. Look up a 6-player rotation diagram.

**Watch for:**
- Swinging arms in a forearm pass. Keep arms straight and together, angle the platform, and use the legs.
- Setting with palms. Contact the ball with the finger pads in a triangle/diamond shape above the forehead.
- Rotating counterclockwise. Teams rotate clockwise when they win back the serve.

### Day 1 (Monday): Passing
*Student:* Practice forearm passing against a wall (target 30 consecutive) and with a partner.

*How to teach:* Teach the forearm pass: ready stance, hands joined, thumbs parallel, flat platform, contact on forearms, lift with legs. Wall pass goal: 30 in a row.

- **Q:** Where on the arms should the ball contact in a forearm pass?
  **A:** On the flat part of the forearms just above the wrists, not the hands.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Setting
*Student:* Practice overhead setting with proper hand shape; 50 self-sets.

*How to teach:* Teach setting: hands form a window above the forehead, contact with finger pads, extend arms and legs. 50 self-sets.

- **Q:** Why set with the fingertips instead of palms?
  **A:** It gives control and avoids a 'lift/carry' violation; palms cause slapping and poor accuracy.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Serving
*Student:* Practice underhand and overhand serves to target zones.

*How to teach:* Practice underhand serve (step, swing from shoulder, contact the ball below center with heel of hand) and overhand float serve (toss, high elbow, firm contact). Serve to target zones.

- **Q:** What makes an overhand float serve 'float'?
  **A:** Hitting it firmly with little or no spin, so it moves unpredictably in the air.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Strategy
*Student:* Learn 6-player rotation and the pass-set-hit sequence; play modified games with friends or at a park.

*How to teach:* Teach 6-player rotation (clockwise when gaining serve), pass-set-hit, three contacts maximum. Play modified games.

- **Q:** How many contacts may a team use to return the ball?
  **A:** Three (a block does not count as one in indoor rules).
- **Q:** When does a team rotate?
  **A:** When it wins the rally on the opponent's serve (side-out); players rotate one position clockwise.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Skills test and a diagram of a serve-receive formation.

*How to teach:* Skills test: 10 forearm passes and 10 sets to a target; 10 serves. Student diagrams a W serve-receive formation.

- **Q:** What is a free ball?
  **A:** An easy, non-attacking return (usually a high pass) that the receiving team can easily set up for an attack.

**Assessment:** Skills test and diagram.

**Answer key:** Skills test: proficient = 6/10 passes and 6/10 sets to target zone, 7/10 legal serves. Diagram key: a W serve-receive shows 5 passers in a W shape (three across the middle/back, two front corners) with the setter near the net between right-front and middle, positions numbered 1–6 (1 = right back server, rotating clockwise).

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Sustainability and intellectual property

**Prepare:** Have a plastic water bottle and a disposable product (e.g. a pen, a plastic toy) for redesign. Bookmark Google Patents (patents.google.com) and the USPTO's 'Trademark, Patent, or Copyright?' page. Prepare the Unit 8 quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking you can patent an idea. Patents protect specific inventions that are new, useful and non-obvious, described in detail — not general ideas.
- Mixing up IP types. Patent = invention; trademark = brand name/logo; copyright = creative works; trade secret = confidential business information.
- Thinking recycling solves all waste. Reduce and reuse come first; many plastics are not actually recycled.

### Day 1 (Monday): Life cycle
*Student:* Map the life cycle (materials, manufacturing, use, disposal) of a plastic water bottle and estimate its impact.

*How to teach:* Map a water bottle's life cycle: raw materials (petroleum → PET), manufacturing, transportation, use, disposal (landfill, recycling, litter). Estimate impacts at each stage.

- **Q:** What are the main stages of a product's life cycle?
  **A:** Raw material extraction, manufacturing, distribution/transport, use, and end of life (disposal or recycling).
- **Q:** Which stage of a single-use water bottle likely has the biggest impact?
  **A:** Material production/manufacturing and end of life (plastic waste) — use has almost no impact since it's used briefly.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Design for sustainability
*Student:* Redesign a disposable product for disassembly and recycling.

*How to teach:* Redesign a disposable product for disassembly and recycling: single material, snap fits instead of glue, labeled plastics, refillable parts.

- **Q:** Why does using a single type of plastic help recycling?
  **A:** Mixed materials are hard or impossible to separate; a single labeled material can be recycled together.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Intellectual property
*Student:* Learn the differences among patents, trademarks, copyrights, and trade secrets.

*How to teach:* Compare patents, trademarks, copyrights and trade secrets with examples.

- **Q:** Which IP protects the Coca-Cola recipe? The Nike swoosh? A new type of hinge? A song?
  **A:** Trade secret; trademark; patent; copyright.
- **Q:** How long does a U.S. utility patent last?
  **A:** 20 years from the filing date (with maintenance fees).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Patent search
*Student:* Search Google Patents for prior art related to a product idea and read one patent's claims.

*How to teach:* Search Google Patents for prior art on a product idea; read one patent's abstract and first claim.

- **Q:** What are a patent's 'claims'?
  **A:** The numbered statements that define exactly what the patent legally protects.
- **Q:** What is prior art?
  **A:** Any public evidence (patents, products, publications) that an invention already exists, which can prevent a new patent.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 8 quiz on ethics, sustainability, and IP.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 quiz in the key.

- **Q:** What is 'cradle to cradle' design?
  **A:** Designing products so that at end of life their materials become inputs for new products, rather than waste.

**Assessment:** Unit 8 quiz.

**Answer key:** Unit 8 quiz: 1) First NSPE canon: hold paramount public safety, health and welfare. 2) Tacoma Narrows: aeroelastic flutter in a slender, flexible deck. 3) Challenger: O-ring failure in cold; managers overrode engineers. 4) FOS = failure load ÷ working load; 1,500 N ÷ 500 N = 3. 5) Life cycle stages: raw materials, manufacturing, distribution/transportation, use, end of life (disposal/recycling). 6) Cradle to cradle = designing products so materials can be fully reused/recycled at end of life. 7) Utility patent lasts 20 years from filing. 8) Trademark protects brand names/logos and can last indefinitely if used and renewed. 9) Copyright protects creative works (generally the author's life + 70 years). 10) Prior art = existing evidence that an invention is already known. Mastery 8+/10.
