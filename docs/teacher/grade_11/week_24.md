# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 24

## English 11 – American Literature: Research: question, sources, and evaluation

**Prepare:** A public library card for database access (Gale, JSTOR free articles, EBSCO). Purdue OWL MLA 9 guide. A research folder or document.

**Watch for:**
- Choosing too broad a topic ('the 1920s'). Narrow to a specific, arguable question linking one author to one context.
- Trusting a source because it is first in search results or looks professional. Evaluate every source with the CRAAP test.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose a topic
*Student:* Brainstorm topics linking an American author to historical context; narrow to an arguable research question.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm 10 author-plus-context pairs from the year (e.g., Miller and McCarthyism, Hansberry and redlining). Model narrowing one into a question that can be argued, then let the student narrow their own.

- **Q:** Narrow 'Fitzgerald and the 1920s' into a research question.
  **A:** Example: 'How did Prohibition-era bootlegging shape Fitzgerald's portrayal of new money in The Great Gatsby?'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Library databases
*Student:* Use public library databases to locate at least six sources, including two scholarly articles.

*How to teach:* Show how to log in to the library databases and use keywords with AND/OR. The student finds six sources, including two scholarly articles. Today's task from the plan: Use public library databases to locate at least six sources, including two scholarly articles. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** What makes a source scholarly?
  **A:** It is written by experts, usually peer-reviewed, published in an academic journal or press, and cites its own sources.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Evaluate sources
*Student:* Apply the CRAAP test to each source and discard weak ones.

*How to teach:* Model the CRAAP test on one source. The student rates all six and discards weak ones. Today's task from the plan: Apply the CRAAP test to each source and discard weak ones. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What does CRAAP stand for?
  **A:** Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Annotated bibliography
*Student:* Write MLA 9 entries with 3-sentence annotations for five sources.

*How to teach:* Model one MLA 9 entry and annotation. The student writes five. Today's task from the plan: Write MLA 9 entries with 3-sentence annotations for five sources. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What should a three-sentence annotation do?
  **A:** Summarize the source's main point, evaluate its credibility, and explain how it will be used in the paper.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Submit research question, working thesis, and annotated bibliography for feedback.

*How to teach:* The student submits question, working thesis, and annotated bibliography; give written feedback within two days. Today's task from the plan: Submit research question, working thesis, and annotated bibliography for feedback. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What is a working thesis?
  **A:** A tentative, arguable answer to the research question that will guide (and may change during) research.

**Assessment:** Annotated bibliography rubric.

**Answer key:** Annotated bibliography rubric (4-point): 4 = five correct MLA 9 entries (author, title, container, publisher/date, URL or DOI), alphabetized with hanging indents, at least two scholarly sources, annotations that summarize, evaluate, and state use; 3 = minor format errors; 2 = annotations summary-only or fewer scholarly sources; 1 = incomplete.

## Algebra II: Radian measure and the unit circle

**Prepare:** A paper plate, string, scissors, protractor, marker. Optional printed blank unit circle.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking π = 180. π radians corresponds to 180°; π itself is about 3.14.
- Mixing calculator modes. Check DEG vs RAD before every calculation (sin 30 should be 0.5 in degree mode).

### Day 1 (Monday): Radians
*Student:* Wrap string around a paper plate to construct one radian; convert between degrees and radians.

*How to teach:* Cut string equal to the plate's radius and wrap it along the rim: that arc subtends 1 radian. Count about 6.28 radius-lengths around the circle. Today's task from the plan: Wrap string around a paper plate to construct one radian; convert between degrees and radians. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Convert 150° to radians and 3π/4 to degrees.
  **A:** 150 · π/180 = 5π/6; 3π/4 · 180/π = 135°.
- **Q:** About how many degrees is 1 radian?
  **A:** 180/π ≈ 57.3°.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Arc length
*Student:* Use s = rθ to solve arc length and angular speed problems.

*How to teach:* Model s = rθ (θ in radians) and angular speed ω = θ/t, v = rω. Today's task from the plan: Use s = rθ to solve arc length and angular speed problems. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Find the arc length for r = 10 cm and θ = 2π/5.
  **A:** s = 10·2π/5 = 4π ≈ 12.57 cm.
- **Q:** A wheel of radius 0.3 m turns 10 revolutions per second. Linear speed?
  **A:** ω = 20π rad/s, v = 0.3·20π = 6π ≈ 18.85 m/s.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Unit circle
*Student:* Build a unit circle with special angles from 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles.

*How to teach:* Draw 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles with hypotenuse 1 to find coordinates; fill the first quadrant then reflect. Today's task from the plan: Build a unit circle with special angles from 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Give cos(π/3) and sin(π/4).
  **A:** 1/2 and √2/2.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Extending trig
*Student:* Evaluate sine, cosine, and tangent for any angle using reference angles.

*How to teach:* Model finding the reference angle, then the sign from the quadrant (All Students Take Calculus). Today's task from the plan: Evaluate sine, cosine, and tangent for any angle using reference angles. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Evaluate sin(5π/6), cos(4π/3), tan(7π/4).
  **A:** 1/2, −1/2, −1.
- **Q:** Evaluate sin 210°.
  **A:** −1/2 (reference 30°, Quadrant III).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: fill in a blank unit circle and evaluate 10 expressions.

*How to teach:* Quiz: fill in a blank unit circle and evaluate 10 expressions (about 30 minutes). Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What are the coordinates of 2π/3 on the unit circle?
  **A:** (−1/2, √3/2).

**Assessment:** Unit circle quiz.

**Answer key:** Unit circle key: points at 0, π/6, π/4, π/3, π/2 are (1,0), (√3/2,1/2), (√2/2,√2/2), (1/2,√3/2), (0,1), reflected with signs into other quadrants. Sample 10: sin π/2 = 1; cos π = −1; tan π/4 = 1; sin 7π/6 = −1/2; cos 5π/3 = 1/2; tan 2π/3 = −√3; sin 3π/2 = −1; cos 3π/4 = −√2/2; sin 11π/6 = −1/2; tan π/6 = √3/3.

## Physics of the Universe: Earth's interior and convection

**Prepare:** A hard-boiled egg and a knife; P- and S-wave shadow-zone diagram (USGS or textbook); a pot, water, oatmeal flakes or rice, stove (adult supervision).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the mantle is liquid magma. The mantle is solid rock that flows very slowly over millions of years.
- Thinking we have drilled to the core. All evidence for the interior is indirect (seismic waves, meteorites, gravity).

### Day 1 (Monday): Earth layers
*Student:* Model Earth's layers with a hard-boiled egg; compare to seismic data.

*How to teach:* Cut the egg in half: shell = crust, white = mantle, yolk = core. Compare proportions to Earth's actual layers. Today's task from the plan: Model Earth's layers with a hard-boiled egg; compare to seismic data. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** Name Earth's layers from outside in.
  **A:** Crust, mantle, outer core (liquid), inner core (solid).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Seismic evidence
*Student:* Analyze P- and S-wave data showing the liquid outer core.

*How to teach:* Show a diagram of P- and S-wave paths through Earth with the shadow zones marked. The student traces where each wave type arrives and explains why S-waves vanish beyond about 104° from the epicenter while P-waves bend (refract) and leave a 104°–140° gap.

- **Q:** What evidence shows the outer core is liquid?
  **A:** S-waves cannot travel through liquids, and they disappear on the far side of Earth (S-wave shadow zone beyond about 104°).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Convection model
*Student:* Heat water with oatmeal flakes to model mantle convection.

*How to teach:* Heat water with oatmeal flakes on low; watch flakes rise in the middle and sink at edges. Today's task from the plan: Heat water with oatmeal flakes to model mantle convection. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What drives mantle convection?
  **A:** Hotter, less dense rock rises and cooler, denser rock sinks, forming slow circulating currents.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Heat sources
*Student:* Explain radioactive decay and primordial heat as energy sources for convection.

*How to teach:* Explain the two heat sources with a simple model: a warm potato cooling (leftover heat of formation) plus a hand warmer inside it (radioactive decay adding heat). The student lists the radioactive elements involved and explains why the interior is still hot after 4.5 billion years.

- **Q:** Where does Earth's internal heat come from?
  **A:** Leftover (primordial) heat from Earth's formation and heat from radioactive decay of elements like uranium, thorium, and potassium.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draw and explain a model of mantle convection.

*How to teach:* The student draws a cross-section of Earth showing a convection cell: hot rock rising under a ridge, spreading, cooling, and sinking at a subduction zone. Check that arrows, labels, and an explanation of density differences are all present.

- **Q:** How does mantle convection relate to plate movement?
  **A:** Convection currents and the pull of sinking slabs move the lithospheric plates above.

**Assessment:** Convection model rubric.

**Answer key:** Convection model rubric (4-point): labeled layers; arrows for rising hot and sinking cool material; explanation of density differences; energy sources (radioactive decay, primordial heat); connection to plate motion.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Roots of the civil rights movement

**Prepare:** Print Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) majority and Justice Harlan's dissent excerpts, Jim Crow photographs (LOC), Executive Order 9981 (1948), summaries of Murray v. Pearson (1936), Missouri ex rel. Gaines (1938), Sweatt v. Painter (1950), Mendez v. Westminster (1946–47) and Brown v. Board of Education (1954).

**Watch for:**
- Students think Brown immediately desegregated schools. Brown II (1955) ordered desegregation 'with all deliberate speed', and Southern resistance delayed real integration for years.
- Students think Mendez was a Supreme Court case. It was decided by a federal district court (1946) and affirmed by the Ninth Circuit (1947); it led California to repeal school segregation laws in 1947.
- Students think the NAACP attacked school segregation all at once. It strategically began with graduate and law schools where inequality was easiest to prove.

### Day 1 (Monday): Jim Crow
*Student:* Analyze Plessy v. Ferguson and Harlan's dissent alongside Jim Crow photographs.

*How to teach:* Analyze Plessy and Harlan's dissent alongside Jim Crow photos.

- **Q:** What did Plessy v. Ferguson decide?
  **A:** Racial segregation laws were constitutional as long as facilities were 'separate but equal'.
- **Q:** What did Harlan argue in dissent?
  **A:** 'Our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Desegregating the military
*Student:* Read Executive Order 9981 and explain its significance.

*How to teach:* Read Executive Order 9981 and explain its significance.

- **Q:** What did EO 9981 (1948) do?
  **A:** Ordered equality of treatment and opportunity in the armed forces, leading to desegregation of the military.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Legal strategy
*Student:* Trace the NAACP's strategy from Murray and Sweatt v. Painter to Brown.

*How to teach:* Trace the NAACP strategy from Murray and Gaines to Sweatt and Brown.

- **Q:** Why did Sweatt v. Painter matter?
  **A:** The Court ruled Texas's hastily created Black law school was not equal to UT Law in faculty, reputation and opportunities — intangible factors counted.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mendez & Brown
*Student:* Compare California's Mendez v. Westminster with Brown v. Board of Education.

*How to teach:* Compare Mendez v. Westminster and Brown v. Board.

- **Q:** How was Mendez connected to Brown?
  **A:** Mendez struck down segregation of Mexican American students in California; Thurgood Marshall filed a brief in the appeal, and Governor Earl Warren signed the repeal of California's segregation law — Warren later wrote the Brown decision.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining how Brown overturned Plessy.

*How to teach:* The student writes a paragraph explaining how Brown overturned Plessy.

- **Q:** What was the key holding of Brown?
  **A:** 'In the field of public education the doctrine of separate but equal has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.'

**Assessment:** Paragraph.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric: 4 = explains that Plessy (1896) upheld 'separate but equal'; the NAACP Legal Defense Fund (Houston, Marshall) chipped away with graduate-school cases (Gaines, Sweatt, McLaurin) showing separate facilities were not equal; Brown (1954, unanimous, Chief Justice Warren) held that 'separate educational facilities are inherently unequal' and violate the Equal Protection Clause, citing psychological harm (doll studies); notes Mendez as precedent and Harlan's dissent ('Our Constitution is color-blind'). 3 = accurate but fewer details. 2 = general. 1 = inaccurate.

## Spanish IV: Agua y sequía: California y América Latina

**Prepare:** Gather graphs of California reservoir levels (California Department of Water Resources) and information on Mexico City's water supply (aquifer overuse, land subsidence, the Cutzamala system), plus an article on Chile's megadrought (central Chile, since about 2010). Prepare stakeholder role cards (farmer, city resident, environmentalist, mayor, water company).

**Watch for:**
- Assuming drought is only about rainfall: overuse of groundwater, agriculture, and infrastructure matter (e.g., Mexico City is sinking because of aquifer overextraction).
- Confusing 'el embalse' (reservoir) with 'la presa' (dam) and 'el acuífero' (underground aquifer).

### Day 1 (Monday): Datos
*Student:* Interpret graphs of California reservoir levels and Mexico City water supply with Spanish questions.

*How to teach:* Your child interprets reservoir graphs and Mexico City supply data, answering questions in Spanish.

- **Q:** Translate: 'The reservoir level dropped 20 percent compared to last year.'
  **A:** El nivel del embalse bajó un veinte por ciento en comparación con el año pasado.
- **Q:** Why is Mexico City sinking?
  **A:** Because so much water is pumped from the aquifer beneath it, the ground compacts and subsides.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Lectura
*Student:* Read an article on Chile's megadrought and identify the author's point of view.

*How to teach:* Read about Chile's megadrought; your child identifies the author's point of view and evidence.

- **Q:** What is the 'megasequía' in Chile?
  **A:** An unusually long drought affecting central Chile since about 2010, linked to climate change and worsened by heavy water use (agriculture, mining).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Comparación
*Student:* Prepare a 2-minute oral comparison of water use in California and Chile.

*How to teach:* Your child prepares a 2-minute oral comparison of water use in California and Chile.

- **Q:** Write a comparison sentence.
  **A:** Example: Tanto en California como en Chile, la agricultura usa la mayor parte del agua, pero en Chile los derechos de agua son privados.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Conversación
*Student:* Role-play a town meeting about water restrictions, each person representing a stakeholder.

*How to teach:* Role-play a town meeting on water restrictions; each person represents a stakeholder.

- **Q:** As a farmer, object politely.
  **A:** Example: Entiendo la situación, pero si reducen el riego, perderemos nuestras cosechas.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Record the cultural comparison and self-assess with the rubric.

*How to teach:* Your child records the cultural comparison and self-assesses with the rubric.

- **Q:** What does 'escasear' mean?
  **A:** To be scarce, to run short.

**Assessment:** Recorded cultural comparison.

**Answer key:** Recorded cultural comparison rubric (2 minutes): 4 = compares water use and policy in California and Chile with specific examples (agriculture, conservation rules, reservoirs), clear organization, comparison phrases, mostly accurate grammar; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = describes one place mainly; 1 = incomplete.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Lighting and sound design

**Prepare:** Gather flashlights, colored cellophane (warm and cool), desk lamps, and an audio editor/free sound libraries (e.g., freesound.org with proper licensing). Choose a short scene (e.g., from Trifles or Our Town).

**Watch for:**
- Lighting only the face from the front. Front light alone flattens; side and back light add depth.
- Sound cues that are too loud. Underscore supports, it shouldn't compete with dialogue.
- Calling cues without numbers. Every cue is numbered and placed exactly in the script.

### Day 1 (Monday): Light qualities
*Student:* Experiment with intensity, color, and angle using flashlights and cellophane on a face.

*How to teach:* In a dim room, light a face from front, side, below, above, and back; add warm (amber) and cool (blue) gels. Note emotional effects.

- **Q:** What mood does light from below create?
  **A:** Eerie, unnatural, or frightening — it reverses normal shadows.
- **Q:** Which has a warmer color temperature: 2700 K or 5600 K?
  **A:** 2700 K (warmer/amber); 5600 K is cooler, like daylight.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Light plot
*Student:* Create a simple cue sheet with 8 lighting cues for a short scene.

*How to teach:* Write a cue sheet with 8 lighting cues (cue number, page/line, description, timing).

- **Q:** What information goes on a lighting cue sheet?
  **A:** Cue number, the exact line or action where it happens, what changes, and the fade time.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sound
*Student:* Find or record sound effects and underscore for a scene.

*How to teach:* Find or record sound effects and underscore for the scene; check licensing.

- **Q:** What is underscore?
  **A:** Music played softly beneath dialogue or action to support mood.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sound cue sheet
*Student:* Write a sound cue sheet with timings.

*How to teach:* Write the sound cue sheet with timings and levels.

- **Q:** What is a 'cue' in theatre?
  **A:** A signal for a technical change (light, sound) or an actor's entrance/line, triggered at a set moment.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Run a scene with your light and sound cues for family.

*How to teach:* Run the scene with light and sound cues for family; adjust.

- **Q:** What are the four controllable qualities of stage light?
  **A:** Intensity, color, distribution (angle/shape/focus), and movement (changes over time).

**Assessment:** Cue run.

**Answer key:** Cue run rubric. 4 – 8 numbered light cues and complete sound cue sheet, all executed on time, levels and colors purposeful for mood, explanation of choices. 3 (proficient) – Cue sheets complete, most cues on time, mood supported. 2 – Missed cues or incomplete sheets. 1 – Not run.

## Lifetime Fitness: Dance fitness

**Prepare:** Build a playlist with songs at different tempos (about 100–130 BPM). Find free dance-fitness videos (salsa/merengue basics, the Cupid Shuffle or Electric Slide). Clear floor space.

**Watch for:**
- Counting music without finding the downbeat. Listen for the strong beat ('1'), often where a new phrase starts.
- Thinking dance isn't real exercise. Heart-rate checks will show moderate-to-vigorous intensity.
- Watching feet. Keep eyes up and posture tall; learn in slow motion first.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rhythm
*Student:* Count music in eights at different tempos and learn grapevine, step-touch, box step, and V-step.

*How to teach:* Count songs in eights; clap the beat at different tempos. Teach grapevine, step-touch, box step, and V-step to 8-counts.

- **Q:** How many counts does a grapevine to the right take?
  **A:** 4 counts: step right, cross left behind, step right, tap/touch left (then repeat left to complete 8).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Latin dance
*Student:* Learn basic salsa and merengue steps and dance continuously for 20 minutes with heart-rate checks.

*How to teach:* Teach basic salsa (forward-back basic, step on 1-2-3, pause 4, 5-6-7, pause 8) and merengue (march in place, step-together with hip action). Dance 20 minutes; check HR.

- **Q:** In the salsa basic, which counts are paused?
  **A:** Counts 4 and 8 (steps on 1-2-3 and 5-6-7).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Line dance
*Student:* Learn a full line dance (e.g., the Cupid Shuffle or Electric Slide) and teach it to a family member.

*How to teach:* Learn a full line dance from video; student teaches it to a family member using counts.

- **Q:** How does a line dance repeat?
  **A:** It repeats the same sequence, usually turning to face a new wall each time.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Choreography
*Student:* Choreograph a 2-minute cardio routine to a song using at least six steps and two formations.

*How to teach:* Student choreographs a 2-minute routine with at least six steps and two formations (e.g., line, circle) mapped to 8-counts on paper.

- **Q:** What is choreography?
  **A:** The planned sequence of movements arranged to music.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Perform the routine for an audience and record heart rate before and after.

*How to teach:* Perform for family; record HR before and after; reflect.

- **Q:** HR rose from 80 to 150 after the routine. About what percent of estimated max is that for a 17-year-old?
  **A:** 150 ÷ 203 ≈ 74% of max — the upper end of moderate, bordering vigorous (CDC: moderate ≈ 64–76%, vigorous ≈ 77–93% of max). Compare with their Karvonen zones.

**Assessment:** Choreography performance.

**Answer key:** Choreography performance rubric. 4 – On-beat throughout, 6+ distinct steps, 2+ formations, smooth transitions, energetic, written 8-count map, HR data with reflection. 3 (proficient) – Mostly on-beat, 6 steps, 2 formations, HR recorded. 2 – Frequently off-beat or fewer steps. 1 – Incomplete.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): User research and empathy

**Prepare:** Write or adapt an interview guide template and buy sticky notes. Line up three people to interview. Find a design-thinking empathy video (e.g. IDEO or Stanford d.school examples).

**Watch for:**
- Asking leading or yes/no questions. Ask open-ended questions about specific past experiences ('Tell me about the last time…').
- Jumping to solutions during interviews. The goal is to understand needs, not pitch an idea.
- Writing a needs statement that includes the solution ('How might we build an app…'). Keep 'How might we…' focused on the user's need.

### Day 1 (Monday): Empathy
*Student:* Learn the design thinking empathize stage and watch a user-research example video.

*How to teach:* Explain empathize: observe, engage and immerse to understand users' needs. Watch an example video and note techniques.

- **Q:** Why is empathy the first stage of design thinking?
  **A:** Designers must understand users' real needs and frustrations before defining the problem, or they may solve the wrong problem.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Interview guide
*Student:* Write 10 open-ended interview questions about a daily frustration (e.g., organizing a backpack or kitchen).

*How to teach:* Write 10 open-ended interview questions about a daily frustration.

- **Q:** Rewrite 'Do you find organizing your backpack hard?' as an open question.
  **A:** 'Walk me through how you packed your backpack this morning. What was frustrating?'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Interviews
*Student:* Interview three people and record notes and direct quotes.

*How to teach:* Interview three people; record notes and direct quotes.

- **Q:** Why record direct quotes?
  **A:** They capture the user's real words and feelings, which give stronger evidence than paraphrases.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Synthesis
*Student:* Cluster findings on sticky notes and create a persona and journey map.

*How to teach:* Write each finding on a sticky note, cluster into themes, and build a persona and journey map.

- **Q:** What is a journey map?
  **A:** A diagram of the steps a user goes through to complete a task, with their actions, thoughts, feelings and pain points at each step.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write three 'How might we...' needs statements.

*How to teach:* Write three 'How might we…' needs statements; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Improve 'How might we design a better kitchen?'
  **A:** Make it specific and user-focused: 'How might we help a family cook find spices quickly while cooking in a small kitchen?'

**Assessment:** Persona and needs statements.

**Answer key:** Persona and needs statements rubric: 4 = persona built from interview evidence (name, background, goals, frustrations, direct quotes); journey map with steps, actions, feelings and pain points; three 'How might we…' statements that are specific, user-focused, solution-neutral and neither too broad nor too narrow (e.g. 'How might we help a busy parent find school forms in the morning without unpacking the whole backpack?'); 3 = mostly evidence-based; 2 = persona invented, weak HMWs; 1 = incomplete.
