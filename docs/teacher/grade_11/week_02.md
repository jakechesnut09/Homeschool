# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 2

## English 11 – American Literature: Puritan poetry and sermon

**Prepare:** Print Bradstreet's 'To My Dear and Loving Husband' and 'Upon the Burning of Our House' and Edwards' 'Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God' (CommonLit/Gutenberg). Make a SOAPSTone chart (Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject, Tone). Have a phone ready to record Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Reading Edwards as a hateful man rather than analyzing his purpose. Ask: what did he want listeners to DO? (Seek salvation now.) Analysis means explaining choices, not just reacting.
- Labeling every vivid image 'pathos' without explaining the emotion. Have the student name the specific emotion (terror, urgency) and why it moves this audience.

### Day 1 (Monday): Bradstreet
*Student:* Read two Bradstreet poems aloud; annotate the extended metaphors and identify the conflict between earthly and heavenly love.

*How to teach:* Read both poems aloud twice. Model annotating the metaphor of love as wealth ('more than whole mines of gold'), then have the student find the conflict in the house poem: grief for lost belongings versus the lesson that true treasure is in heaven.

- **Q:** In 'Upon the Burning of Our House,' how does Bradstreet resolve her grief?
  **A:** She reminds herself that earthly goods are 'mould'ring dust' and that she has a better house in heaven: 'My hope and treasure lies above.'
- **Q:** What metaphor dominates 'To My Dear and Loving Husband'?
  **A:** Love as riches/debt: she prizes his love 'more than whole mines of gold' and says she can never repay it, so she prays the heavens will reward him.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Edwards' sermon
*Student:* Read the first half of 'Sinners'; highlight imagery of spiders, fire, and flood and label each as an appeal.

*How to teach:* Read the first half aloud with emphasis. The student highlights images (spider, fire, flood/dammed waters, bent bow) and labels each with the appeal and emotion.

- **Q:** What is the effect of the spider image?
  **A:** God holds the sinner 'much as one holds a spider, or some loathsome insect, over the fire'; it makes listeners feel small, helpless, and in immediate danger, which creates terror (pathos).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rhetorical appeals
*Student:* Finish 'Sinners'; complete a SOAPSTone chart and explain why fear was an effective strategy for his audience.

*How to teach:* Finish the sermon. Fill SOAPSTone together for Speaker and Occasion (Edwards, Enfield, Connecticut, 1741, during the Great Awakening), then let the student finish and explain why fear worked.

- **Q:** Why was fear effective for Edwards' 1741 audience?
  **A:** They already believed in hell and God's judgment; Edwards made that belief feel immediate and personal, so terror pushed them to seek conversion right away, during the revival of the Great Awakening.
- **Q:** What hope does Edwards offer at the end?
  **A:** That Christ 'has thrown the door of mercy wide open' now; listeners can still be saved if they turn to God immediately.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: sentence variety
*Student:* Analyze Edwards' long periodic sentences; rewrite three of your own sentences as periodic and cumulative sentences.

*How to teach:* Explain periodic sentences (main point delayed to the end) and cumulative sentences (main clause first, details added). Find one long Edwards sentence and mark where the main clause lands; the student rewrites three of their own sentences both ways.

- **Q:** Turn this into a periodic sentence: 'I finished the race even though I was exhausted and my shoe was untied.'
  **A:** Example: 'Exhausted, shoe untied, gasping for every breath, I finished the race.' (The main clause comes last.)

### Day 5 (Friday): Seminar & check
*Student:* Hold a recorded 15-minute discussion with a parent on 'Is fear a legitimate persuasive tool?'; submit a SOAPSTone analysis.

*How to teach:* Record a 15-minute discussion on 'Is fear a legitimate persuasive tool?' The parent should push back once; the student must cite Edwards at least twice. Collect the SOAPSTone chart.

- **Q:** Give one argument that fear can be legitimate persuasion.
  **A:** When the danger is real (e.g., public-health warnings), fear can motivate protective action; it becomes manipulation when it exaggerates or replaces reasons.

**Assessment:** SOAPSTone chart and discussion rubric (evidence, building on ideas).

**Answer key:** SOAPSTone key: Speaker = Jonathan Edwards, Puritan minister; Occasion = sermon at Enfield, CT, 1741, Great Awakening revival; Audience = a congregation he believed complacent about salvation; Purpose = to frighten them into seeking conversion now; Subject = sinners' precarious position before an angry God; Tone = urgent, threatening, then pleading. Discussion rubric (4-point): 4 = cites the text accurately at least twice, responds directly to the other speaker's points, extends or challenges ideas with reasons; 3 = cites once or twice and responds; 2 = opinions with little evidence; 1 = minimal participation.

## Algebra II: Piecewise and absolute value functions

**Prepare:** Find a current USPS price list or make a simple parking-garage table (e.g., $5 first hour, $3 each extra hour or part). Have Desmos ready (it graphs piecewise with braces).

**Watch for:**
- Using a closed dot on both pieces at a boundary. A function has one output per input: check which piece's condition includes the boundary (≤ or ≥) and put the closed dot only there.
- Solving |x + 3| = −4 and getting answers. An absolute value is never negative, so there is no solution; always isolate the absolute value first and check its sign.

### Day 1 (Monday): Piecewise graphs
*Student:* Graph piecewise functions by hand with open/closed endpoints; evaluate at boundary values.

*How to teach:* Model f(x) = {x + 2 if x < 1; 3x if x ≥ 1}: graph each piece only on its interval, open dot at (1, 3) on the first piece, closed dot at (1, 3) on the second. Student graphs three piecewise functions and evaluates at boundaries.

- **Q:** For f(x) above, find f(1), f(0), and f(−3).
  **A:** f(1) = 3(1) = 3 (second piece since 1 ≥ 1); f(0) = 0 + 2 = 2; f(−3) = −3 + 2 = −1.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Step functions
*Student:* Model USPS postage or parking-garage prices with a step function; graph and interpret.

*How to teach:* Use the parking table: $5 for the first hour or part, $3 for each additional hour or part. Student builds the table, graphs the step function with open/closed dots, and explains what a jump means.

- **Q:** Using that rate, what does 2.5 hours cost? What about exactly 2 hours?
  **A:** 2.5 hours: 5 + 3 + 3 = $11 (any part of the third hour counts). Exactly 2 hours: 5 + 3 = $8.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Absolute value equations
*Student:* Solve |2x−5| = 9 type equations and check for extraneous answers.

*How to teach:* Model: isolate the absolute value, then split into two equations (= +value and = −value). Student solves 6–8 equations and substitutes each answer back to check.

- **Q:** Solve |2x − 5| = 9.
  **A:** 2x − 5 = 9 gives x = 7; 2x − 5 = −9 gives x = −2. Both check.
- **Q:** Solve 2|x − 1| + 4 = 2.
  **A:** |x − 1| = −1, which is impossible: no solution.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Absolute value inequalities
*Student:* Solve and graph compound inequalities; model a manufacturing tolerance problem.

*How to teach:* Teach 'less than = AND (between)', 'greater than = OR (outside)'. Model |x − 4| < 3, then the tolerance problem. Student graphs each solution on a number line.

- **Q:** Solve |x − 4| < 3 and |2x + 1| ≥ 5.
  **A:** −3 < x − 4 < 3 → 1 < x < 7. 2x + 1 ≥ 5 or 2x + 1 ≤ −5 → x ≥ 2 or x ≤ −3.
- **Q:** A bolt must be 10 mm long with tolerance 0.05 mm. Write and solve the inequality.
  **A:** |L − 10| ≤ 0.05 → 9.95 ≤ L ≤ 10.05 mm.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on piecewise evaluation, graphing, and absolute value solutions; average rate of change on each piece.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (about 30 minutes). Review the average rate of change as (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a) on one piece before starting. Today's task from the plan: Quiz on piecewise evaluation, graphing, and absolute value solutions; average rate of change on each piece. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** For f(x) = 3x on [1, 3], what is the average rate of change?
  **A:** (9 − 3)/(3 − 1) = 3.

**Assessment:** Quiz on piecewise and absolute value.

**Answer key:** Sample key: 1) For g(x) = {x² if x ≤ 0; 2x+1 if x > 0}: g(−2)=4, g(0)=0, g(3)=7. 2) Graph: parabola left of 0 with closed dot at (0,0), line from open dot (0,1). 3) |x+2|=6 → x=4 or x=−8. 4) |3x|−4=5 → x=±3. 5) |x−1|≤4 → −3≤x≤5. 6) |x+5|>2 → x>−3 or x<−7. 7) Average rate of change of x² on [−3,−1]: (1−9)/2 = −4. 8) Parking 3.2 h at $5 + $3/extra hour: $14. Full credit requires checked solutions and correctly drawn endpoints.

## Physics of the Universe: Newton's first and second laws

**Prepare:** Toy car, rubber bands, washers, a coin, an index card, a cup; a smooth plastic tablecloth and unbreakable dishes for the tablecloth trick. Open PhET 'Forces and Motion: Basics.'

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a moving object needs a net force to keep moving. Newton's first law: with no net force it keeps moving at constant velocity; friction is what usually slows things down.
- Labeling 'the force of motion' on free-body diagrams. Only real forces with an agent (gravity, normal, friction, tension, applied) appear.

### Day 1 (Monday): Inertia
*Student:* Perform coin-card-cup and tablecloth inertia demos; explain using Newton's first law.

*How to teach:* Do the coin-card-cup demo (flick the card; the coin drops into the cup) and the tablecloth pull. The student explains each with Newton's first law. Today's task from the plan: Perform coin-card-cup and tablecloth inertia demos; explain using Newton's first law. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why does the coin fall into the cup instead of flying off with the card?
  **A:** The flick acts on the card for a very short time with little friction on the coin; the coin's inertia keeps it nearly at rest, then gravity pulls it into the cup.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Free-body diagrams
*Student:* Draw free-body diagrams for 8 scenarios including a car braking and a book on a table.

*How to teach:* Model a free-body diagram for a book on a table (weight down, normal up, equal). The student draws eight, including a car braking. Today's task from the plan: Draw free-body diagrams for 8 scenarios including a car braking and a book on a table. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Draw the forces on a car braking on a level road.
  **A:** Weight down, normal force up (equal), and friction from the road pointing backward (opposite motion); net force is backward, so it decelerates.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): F = ma lab
*Student:* Pull a cart with stretched rubber bands (constant force), varying added washers; graph acceleration vs. mass.

*How to teach:* Pull the car with a rubber band stretched the same length each time (constant force) while adding washers. Film and measure acceleration; graph a vs. mass.

- **Q:** If the force stays the same and mass doubles, what happens to acceleration?
  **A:** It halves (a = F/m): an inverse relationship.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Simulation
*Student:* Use PhET Forces and Motion to test acceleration vs. net force; analyze the linear relationship.

*How to teach:* In PhET, hold mass constant and vary applied force; record acceleration and graph a vs. F_net. Today's task from the plan: Use PhET Forces and Motion to test acceleration vs. net force; analyze the linear relationship. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** A 2.0 kg cart has a net force of 10 N. What is its acceleration?
  **A:** a = F/m = 10/2.0 = 5.0 m/s².
- **Q:** What does the slope of the a vs. F graph represent?
  **A:** 1/m (the inverse of the mass).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a CER explaining your lab data using Newton's second law.

*How to teach:* The student writes a CER explaining the lab data with Newton's second law. Today's task from the plan: Write a CER explaining your lab data using Newton's second law. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** State Newton's second law in words.
  **A:** An object's acceleration equals the net force on it divided by its mass, in the direction of the net force.

**Assessment:** CER lab report.

**Answer key:** CER rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning). Claim: answers the question about how mass affects acceleration with a constant force in one sentence. Evidence: specific data from the student's own lab/simulation with numbers and units. Reasoning: connects the evidence to the scientific principle. A proficient response: 'Adding mass decreased the cart's acceleration' with data such as 'with 0 washers a ≈ 0.60 m/s², with 4 washers a ≈ 0.30 m/s²', and reasoning that with constant force a = F/m, so acceleration is inversely proportional to mass (graph curves downward, a vs 1/m is linear).

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Religion, rights, and democratization

**Prepare:** Print Jefferson's letter to the Danbury Baptists (1802) and the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom (drafted 1777, enacted 1786). Choose case summaries: Engel v. Vitale (1962, Establishment) and Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972, Free Exercise) from Oyez or the U.S. Courts site. Seneca Falls Declaration of Sentiments (1848).

**Watch for:**
- Students think the First Amendment originally applied to states. It originally limited only Congress; the Fourteenth Amendment's incorporation later applied most protections to states.
- Students think 'separation of church and state' is in the Constitution's text. The phrase comes from Jefferson's Danbury letter; the text says 'Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof'.
- Students think suffrage expanded steadily to everyone. By 1860 most states had removed property requirements for white men, but women, most Black men and Native Americans remained excluded.

### Day 1 (Monday): Great Awakenings
*Student:* Read about the First and Second Great Awakenings and their ties to abolition and reform.

*How to teach:* Read about the First and Second Great Awakenings; the student charts leaders, dates, beliefs and effects on reform.

- **Q:** How did the Second Great Awakening encourage reform?
  **A:** It stressed that individuals could choose salvation and improve society, inspiring abolition, temperance, prison and education reform.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Religious liberty
*Student:* Analyze Jefferson's Danbury letter and the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom.

*How to teach:* Analyze the Danbury letter and the Virginia Statute.

- **Q:** What did Jefferson mean by a 'wall of separation between Church & State'?
  **A:** The government should neither establish a religion nor interfere with the free exercise of religion; religious belief is a matter between a person and God.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): First Amendment
*Student:* Examine one Establishment Clause and one Free Exercise case summary and explain the reasoning.

*How to teach:* The student reads one Establishment and one Free Exercise case summary and explains the reasoning.

- **Q:** Why did the Court strike down school prayer in Engel v. Vitale?
  **A:** A state-composed prayer recited in public schools was a government establishment of religion, even if voluntary and nondenominational.
- **Q:** Why did the Amish win in Wisconsin v. Yoder?
  **A:** Compulsory schooling beyond eighth grade substantially burdened their religious practice, and the state's interest wasn't strong enough to override it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Expanding democracy
*Student:* Trace expansion of suffrage from 1789 to 1860 and the Seneca Falls Declaration.

*How to teach:* Trace suffrage expansion 1789–1860 on a timeline and read excerpts of the Declaration of Sentiments.

- **Q:** How did the Declaration of Sentiments use the Declaration of Independence?
  **A:** It copied its structure and language — 'all men and women are created equal' — and listed grievances against men's denial of women's rights, including the vote.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on founding principles and religious liberty.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz on founding principles and religious liberty.

- **Q:** Quote the two religion clauses of the First Amendment.
  **A:** 'Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof.'

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Founding principles — natural rights, consent of the governed, limited government, separation of powers, federalism. 2) The two religion clauses — Establishment and Free Exercise. 3) 'Wall of separation' — Jefferson's 1802 letter to the Danbury Baptists. 4) Virginia Statute — no one compelled to support or attend any church; religious opinions don't affect civil rights; authored by Jefferson, passed with Madison's leadership in 1786. 5) Engel v. Vitale — state-sponsored school prayer violates the Establishment Clause. 6) Wisconsin v. Yoder — Amish families could not be compelled to send children to school past eighth grade (Free Exercise). 7) First Great Awakening (1730s–40s) — Edwards, Whitefield; emotional revivals, challenged authority. 8) Second Great Awakening (c. 1790s–1840s) — Finney; inspired abolition, temperance and other reforms. 9) Jacksonian democracy — expansion of white male suffrage, end of property requirements, rise of party politics. 10) Seneca Falls (1848) — Stanton and Mott; Declaration of Sentiments: 'all men and women are created equal'; demanded voting rights.

## Spanish IV: Lengua e identidad: el bilingüismo

**Prepare:** Choose a TED en Español (or TEDx in Spanish) talk on bilingualism, 10–15 minutes; preview it for appropriateness and turn on Spanish subtitles for a second viewing. Optionally read a short excerpt about Gloria Anzaldúa's ideas on language and identity (Borderlands/La Frontera, 1987). Print a present-subjunctive chart with emotion triggers (me alegra que, es triste que, me molesta que, me sorprende que, ojalá que).

**Watch for:**
- Using the indicative after emotion (*Me alegra que hablas español): Me alegra que hables español.
- Using que + subjunctive when there is no change of subject: 'Me alegra hablar dos idiomas' (one subject) vs. 'Me alegra que mis hermanos hablen dos idiomas'.
- Thinking Spanglish means someone cannot speak either language: code-switching is a normal, rule-governed bilingual practice; registers still matter in formal writing.

### Day 1 (Monday): Video
*Student:* Watch a TED en Español talk on bilingualism and take notes on three main ideas.

*How to teach:* Watch the talk once for gist; your child writes three main ideas in Spanish, then rewatches with subtitles to confirm.

- **Q:** Translate: 'The speaker says that being bilingual opens doors.'
  **A:** El/la conferenciante dice que ser bilingüe abre puertas.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Subjuntivo presente
*Student:* Review present subjunctive formation and use with emotion (me alegra que, es triste que).

*How to teach:* Review present-subjunctive formation (yo form, drop -o, opposite vowel; DISHES irregulars) and emotion triggers. Your child writes 10 sentences.

- **Q:** Translate: 'It's sad that some children lose their mother tongue.'
  **A:** Es triste que algunos niños pierdan su lengua materna.
- **Q:** Give the subjunctive of ser (ellos), ir (nosotros), and saber (tú).
  **A:** sean, vayamos, sepas

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Conversación
*Student:* Hold a 10-minute conversation with a partner/tutor on the advantages of speaking two languages.

*How to teach:* Hold a 10-minute conversation on the advantages of speaking two languages; push for follow-up questions and reasons.

- **Q:** Ask: 'Do you think it is important to be bilingual? Why?'
  **A:** ¿Crees que es importante ser bilingüe? ¿Por qué?

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparación
*Student:* Compare attitudes toward Spanglish in the U.S. with attitudes in Latin America in a Venn diagram.

*How to teach:* Your child compares attitudes toward Spanglish in the U.S. and in Latin America in a Venn diagram, using examples (parquear, lonche, textear).

- **Q:** What do 'parquear' and 'lonche' mean, and where do they come from?
  **A:** To park and lunch — Spanglish adaptations of English words common in U.S. Spanish (standard: estacionar, almuerzo).

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Write an opinion paragraph using five subjunctive expressions.

*How to teach:* Your child writes an opinion paragraph with five subjunctive expressions.

- **Q:** Correct: 'Me sorprende que mucha gente piensa que el spanglish es malo.'
  **A:** Me sorprende que mucha gente piense que el spanglish es malo.

**Assessment:** Opinion paragraph rubric.

**Answer key:** Opinion paragraph rubric (5 subjunctive expressions): 4 = clear opinion on bilingualism with 2–3 reasons and an example, five correct emotion/impersonal + subjunctive structures, transitions; 3 = 3–4 correct structures or minor errors; 2 = few subjunctives or unclear opinion; 1 = incomplete. Model sentence: 'Me alegra que cada vez más jóvenes hablen español, pero es triste que algunos pierdan su lengua materna.'

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Improvisation fundamentals

**Prepare:** Make suggestion cards (locations, relationships, occupations, quirks). Get a timer. Learn the rules of Freeze, Three-Line Scene, and Party Quirks (any improv games website).

**Watch for:**
- Asking questions instead of making offers ('Who are you?'). Make statements that give information ('Grandpa, the barn's on fire!').
- Saying 'yes, but' and blocking partner ideas. Accept the offer and add to it.
- Trying to be funny. Truthful reactions to clear situations create comedy naturally.

### Day 1 (Monday): Yes, and
*Student:* Practice 'yes, and' vs. 'yes, but' dialogues and identify blocking offers.

*How to teach:* Do two-person dialogues: first with 'yes, but' (scene stalls), then 'yes, and' (scene grows). Identify blocks in examples.

- **Q:** Partner says, 'Let's go fishing.' Give a 'yes, and' response and a blocking response.
  **A:** 'Yes, and I brought the giant net for the shark!' (accepts and adds). Block: 'There's no lake here' or 'I don't want to.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): CROW
*Student:* Improvise 2-minute scenes establishing character, relationship, objective, and where within the first 30 seconds.

*How to teach:* Teach CROW: Character, Relationship, Objective, Where. Improvise 2-minute scenes; the observer checks whether all four were clear within 30 seconds.

- **Q:** What does CROW stand for?
  **A:** Character, Relationship, Objective, Where.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Status
*Student:* Explore high- and low-status physical and vocal choices in short scenes.

*How to teach:* Explore status: high status (still, open, eye contact, measured speech) vs. low status (fidgeting, small, avoiding eye contact, hesitant). Play short scenes swapping status.

- **Q:** Give two physical signs of high status.
  **A:** Stillness, upright open posture, steady eye contact, slow deliberate movement and speech.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Short-form games
*Student:* Play Freeze, Three-Line Scene, and Party Quirks; journal which choices worked.

*How to teach:* Play Freeze, Three-Line Scene, and Party Quirks; journal which choices worked.

- **Q:** In a Three-Line Scene, what must the three lines accomplish?
  **A:** Establish who the characters are, their relationship, and where they are (the scene's basic CROW) in only three lines.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Perform three improvised scenes from audience suggestions and self-evaluate with a CROW rubric.

*How to teach:* Perform three scenes from audience suggestions; self-evaluate with the CROW rubric.

- **Q:** What is an 'offer' in improv?
  **A:** Any line or action that gives information or direction to the scene.

**Assessment:** Improvisation rubric.

**Answer key:** Improvisation rubric (each scene scored 1–4 on C, R, O, W plus acceptance). 4 – All CROW elements clear within 30 s, consistent acceptance and building of offers, committed characters, a clear ending. 3 (proficient) – CROW mostly clear early, accepts offers, stays in scene. 2 – Missing elements, frequent questions or blocks. 1 – Breaks character or stalls repeatedly.

## Lifetime Fitness: SMART goals and the FITT principle

**Prepare:** Print or draw a goal sheet (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) and a FITT planning grid. Have a jump rope, fitness journal, and a safe walking/jogging route. Review the student's week 1 baseline data so goals can be built from it.

**Watch for:**
- Writing vague goals like 'get fit.' Push for a number and date: 'Improve my mile from 10:30 to 9:45 by December 1 by running 3x/week.'
- Confusing overload with doing as much as possible. Overload means slightly more than the body is used to, increased gradually (progression).
- Believing gains are permanent. Reversibility: fitness declines when training stops, so consistency matters.

### Day 1 (Monday): SMART goals
*Student:* Write two SMART goals based on baseline data; do a 20-minute circuit (jump rope, squats, planks).

*How to teach:* Explain each letter of SMART with an example. The student writes two goals based on baseline weaknesses, then completes a 20-minute circuit (e.g., 1 min jump rope, 15 squats, 30-sec plank, repeat). Check that each goal has a number and a deadline.

- **Q:** Rewrite 'I want to be stronger' as a SMART goal.
  **A:** Example: 'I will increase my 90° push-ups from 12 to 20 by March 1 by doing push-up workouts 3 days a week.' (Must be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and dated.)

### Day 2 (Tuesday): FITT
*Student:* Learn frequency, intensity, time, type; design a FITT plan for one goal and walk/jog 25 minutes.

*How to teach:* Teach FITT: Frequency (how often), Intensity (how hard), Time (how long), Type (what activity). The student fills in a FITT grid for one goal, then walks/jogs 25 minutes. Check that intensity is measurable (heart rate zone or RPE).

- **Q:** Write a FITT plan for improving aerobic endurance.
  **A:** Example: F = 3–5 days/week; I = 60–80% heart rate reserve or RPE 5–7; T = 20–40 min; T = jogging, cycling, or swimming.
- **Q:** Which FITT letter changes if you go from walking to jogging at the same time and frequency?
  **A:** Intensity (and arguably type) — jogging is a higher intensity.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Training principles
*Student:* Learn overload, progression, specificity, and reversibility; complete an interval walk/jog.

*How to teach:* Define overload, progression, specificity, and reversibility with examples. Do an interval walk/jog (e.g., 2 min jog/1 min walk ×7). Afterwards ask the student to name which principle the interval session applies.

- **Q:** A swimmer wants to run faster but only trains in the pool. Which principle is violated?
  **A:** Specificity — training should match the desired outcome; to improve running, you must run.
- **Q:** What does reversibility mean?
  **A:** Fitness gains are lost when training stops ('use it or lose it').

### Day 4 (Thursday): Barriers
*Student:* Identify personal barriers to activity and strategies to overcome them; complete a 30-minute bike ride or hike.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm barriers (time, weather, motivation, cost, safety) and match each with a strategy (schedule it, indoor workout, workout partner, free resources). Then complete a 30-minute bike ride or hike and log it.

- **Q:** Give one barrier to activity and a practical strategy to overcome it.
  **A:** Example: 'No time' → schedule three 20-minute sessions in the calendar before school; 'bad weather' → indoor bodyweight circuit or online workout video.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Submit the goal sheet and FITT plan; complete a 30-minute activity of choice and log it.

*How to teach:* Review the goal sheet and FITT plan with the student against the rubric; ask them to explain how they'll measure progress. They complete and log a 30-minute activity of their choice.

- **Q:** How will you know if your SMART goal is met?
  **A:** By retesting the measurable outcome (e.g., push-up count or mile time) by the stated deadline and comparing to baseline.

**Assessment:** Goal sheet and FITT plan.

**Answer key:** Goal sheet and FITT plan rubric. 4 – Two SMART goals with all five elements based on baseline data; FITT plan specifies measurable frequency, intensity (HR zone/RPE), time, and type, and applies overload/progression. 3 (proficient) – Two SMART goals with numbers and dates; complete FITT plan. 2 – Goals missing measurable or time element or FITT incomplete. 1 – Vague goals, no FITT plan.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): The design process

**Prepare:** Gather printer paper (e.g. 10 sheets), tape (limit to 30 cm), scissors, about 200 pennies and a small cup. Set two books 20 cm apart as bridge supports. Copy a decision matrix template into the notebook.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the design process is a straight line. Real design loops back — testing often sends you back to research or ideation.
- Mixing up criteria and constraints. Criteria are goals used to judge success (holds the most pennies); constraints are limits (span 20 cm, only 10 sheets).
- Picking a favorite idea before evaluating. Use the decision matrix honestly; it's a tool to compare against the criteria, not to justify a choice already made.

### Day 1 (Monday): Design process model
*Student:* Learn the steps (define, research, ideate, select, prototype, test, iterate, communicate) and diagram them in the notebook.

*How to teach:* Teach the steps: define the problem, research, ideate (brainstorm), select, prototype, test, iterate, communicate. Have the student draw it as a cycle with arrows back from 'test' to earlier steps.

- **Q:** Why is the design process drawn as a cycle?
  **A:** Testing reveals problems, so engineers loop back to redesign and improve (iterate) rather than finishing after one try.
- **Q:** What happens in the 'define' step?
  **A:** The problem is clearly stated, including who it's for, the criteria for success, and constraints.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Design brief
*Student:* Write a design brief for a paper bridge spanning 20 cm with limited materials.

*How to teach:* Model a design brief: client, problem statement, criteria (e.g. holds as many pennies as possible at midspan; uses fewest sheets), constraints (spans a 20 cm gap, only paper and 30 cm of tape, built in 30 minutes). The student writes their own.

- **Q:** Is 'must span 20 cm' a criterion or a constraint?
  **A:** A constraint — it's a required limit.
- **Q:** Write one criterion for the bridge.
  **A:** Example: holds the most pennies in a cup at the center before collapsing (or uses the least paper).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Brainstorm
*Student:* Sketch five bridge ideas in 15 minutes and select one with a decision matrix.

*How to teach:* Set a 15-minute timer for five quick sketches (folded beams, tubes, arches, trusses). Then build a decision matrix: criteria in rows (strength, material use, ease of building), score 1–5, multiply by weights, total.

- **Q:** Idea A scores 4 strength (weight 3) and 2 ease (weight 1); Idea B scores 3 strength and 5 ease. Which wins?
  **A:** A: 4×3 + 2×1 = 14; B: 3×3 + 5×1 = 14 — a tie; break it with another criterion or reconsider weights.
- **Q:** Why does folding paper into a channel or tube make it stronger?
  **A:** Folding moves material away from the center (neutral axis), increasing stiffness in bending — the same reason I-beams work.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Build & test
*Student:* Build the bridge and test load with pennies; record results.

*How to teach:* Build, place the cup at midspan, and add pennies one at a time until failure. Record the count and where/how it failed.

- **Q:** Why record how the bridge failed, not just how many pennies it held?
  **A:** The failure mode (buckling, tearing, slipping off supports) tells you what to improve in the redesign.

### Day 5 (Friday): Iterate & check
*Student:* Redesign once, retest, and write a reflection on what changed.

*How to teach:* The student redesigns once to fix the failure, retests and writes a reflection; score with the rubric in the assessment key.

- **Q:** Your bridge sagged in the middle and folded. What redesign might help?
  **A:** Increase stiffness at midspan — e.g. deeper folds, a tube or truss shape, or an arch — so it resists bending.

**Assessment:** Paper bridge challenge.

**Answer key:** Paper bridge challenge rubric (4 levels): 4 = design brief states problem, criteria and constraints; five sketches; decision matrix with weighted criteria; build meets all constraints; load test recorded in a data table (pennies held before failure, failure location); redesign based on observed failure; reflection explains what changed and why the result improved or not; 3 = all parts, reflection thin; 2 = missing matrix or redesign; 1 = incomplete. Load results are not graded on size — process documentation is.
