# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 8

## English 11 – American Literature: Romantic fiction: Irving and Poe

**Prepare:** Irving's 'The Devil and Tom Walker'; Poe's 'The Tell-Tale Heart' and 'The Fall of the House of Usher' (Gutenberg). Notes page on Romantic traits: emotion over reason, imagination, nature, the supernatural, the individual.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing the narrator with the author. Poe was not a murderer; the unreliable narrator is a constructed character.
- Thinking 'Romantic' means love stories. Literary Romanticism means valuing emotion, imagination, nature, and the individual.

### Day 1 (Monday): Romantic traits
*Student:* Take notes on Romantic traits; read 'The Devil and Tom Walker' and mark satire of greed.

*How to teach:* Give notes on Romanticism, then read Irving's story. The student marks satire of greed (Tom's usury, the 'devil take me' oath). Today's task from the plan: Take notes on Romantic traits; read 'The Devil and Tom Walker' and mark satire of greed. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** How does Tom Walker's end satirize greed?
  **A:** After swearing 'The devil take me if I have made a farthing!' he is carried off by the devil on a black horse, and his wealth turns to cinders and shavings — greed literally damns him and leaves nothing.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Poe's narrator
*Student:* Read 'The Tell-Tale Heart'; annotate evidence the narrator is unreliable and chart sentence length vs. tension.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Tell-Tale Heart' aloud with expression. The student annotates evidence of unreliability and notes how short, exclamatory sentences build tension.

- **Q:** How does the narrator's opening undercut his claim of sanity?
  **A:** He insists 'why will you say that I am mad?' while describing a 'very, very dreadfully nervous' state and a murder motivated by an old man's 'vulture eye'; his frantic protests prove the opposite.
- **Q:** Why does the narrator confess?
  **A:** He hears what he thinks is the dead man's heart beating louder under the floor and, believing the police are mocking him, shrieks, 'I admit the deed!'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): House of Usher
*Student:* Read 'The Fall of the House of Usher'; trace setting as a symbol of Roderick's mind.

*How to teach:* Read 'Usher' (it is long; split with breaks). The student traces how the house (fissure, decay, reflection in the tarn) mirrors Roderick's mind. Today's task from the plan: Read 'The Fall of the House of Usher'; trace setting as a symbol of Roderick's mind. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** How does the house symbolize Roderick?
  **A:** Its decay and nearly invisible crack match his mental deterioration; when Madeline dies and Roderick collapses, the house splits along the fissure and sinks into the tarn.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Structure
*Student:* Write an analytical paragraph on how Poe's choice of ending structure produces 'unity of effect'.

*How to teach:* Explain Poe's 'unity of effect' (every detail builds toward one emotional impression). The student writes a paragraph on how one ending achieves it. Today's task from the plan: Write an analytical paragraph on how Poe's choice of ending structure produces 'unity of effect'. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What is 'unity of effect'?
  **A:** Poe's idea that a short story should be read in one sitting and every element should build a single intended effect, such as terror.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on Romantic/Gothic conventions; one-paragraph comparison of Irving's and Poe's narrators.

*How to teach:* Give a 10-item quiz on Romantic/Gothic conventions, then a comparison paragraph of Irving's and Poe's narrators. Today's task from the plan: Quiz on Romantic/Gothic conventions; one-paragraph comparison of Irving's and Poe's narrators. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Compare the narrators of 'Devil and Tom Walker' and 'Tell-Tale Heart.'
  **A:** Irving uses a humorous third-person storyteller who passes on local legend; Poe uses an unreliable first-person narrator whose voice creates horror.

**Assessment:** Quiz and analytical paragraph.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: Gothic conventions = gloomy settings, decay, madness, the supernatural, suspense, death; Romantic = emotion, imagination, nature, individual; unreliable narrator = one whose account cannot be trusted; satire = humor exposing folly; unity of effect = single emotional impression. Analytical paragraph rubric (4-point): claim about how the ending creates the effect; two quotations; analysis of structure (e.g., the rapid, repeated 'louder! louder!' leading to confession); conventions.

## Algebra II: Polynomial operations

**Prepare:** Algebra tiles or area-model templates. Have the student review exponent rules from Algebra I.

**Watch for:**
- Writing (x + 2)² = x² + 4 (forgetting the middle term). Draw the area model: the two 2x rectangles are part of the square.
- Subtracting only the first term of the second polynomial. Rewrite subtraction as adding the opposite and distribute the negative to every term.

### Day 1 (Monday): Polynomial vocabulary
*Student:* Classify polynomials by degree and terms; write in standard form.

*How to teach:* Model naming polynomials by degree and number of terms. Student classifies 10 polynomials and rewrites each in standard form. Today's task from the plan: Classify polynomials by degree and terms; write in standard form. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Classify 4x³ − 2x + 7 and write 5 − 3x² + x⁴ in standard form.
  **A:** 4x³ − 2x + 7: cubic trinomial (degree 3, three terms). 5 − 3x² + x⁴ = x⁴ − 3x² + 5, a quartic trinomial.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Add & subtract
*Student:* Combine polynomials and discuss closure compared with integers.

*How to teach:* Model combining like terms vertically. Discuss closure: integers + integers stay integers; polynomials ± and × stay polynomials. Today's task from the plan: Combine polynomials and discuss closure compared with integers. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Simplify (3x² − 2x + 5) − (x² + 4x − 1).
  **A:** 2x² − 6x + 6.
- **Q:** Are polynomials closed under division? Give an example.
  **A:** No. 1 ÷ x = 1/x (x⁻¹) is not a polynomial.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Multiplication
*Student:* Multiply polynomials with area models and distribution, including (x+2)³.

*How to teach:* Use an area grid to multiply (2x − 1)(x² + 3x − 4), then expand (x + 2)³ as (x + 2)(x + 2)². Today's task from the plan: Multiply polynomials with area models and distribution, including (x+2)³. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Expand (x + 2)³.
  **A:** (x + 2)(x² + 4x + 4) = x³ + 6x² + 12x + 8.
- **Q:** Multiply (2x − 1)(x² + 3x − 4).
  **A:** 2x³ + 6x² − 8x − x² − 3x + 4 = 2x³ + 5x² − 11x + 4.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Polynomial identities
*Student:* Prove (a+b)² and a³−b³ identities; use x²+y² identity to generate Pythagorean triples.

*How to teach:* Prove an identity by expanding one side until it matches the other. Use (x² − y²)² + (2xy)² = (x² + y²)² to make Pythagorean triples. Today's task from the plan: Prove (a+b)² and a³−b³ identities; use x²+y² identity to generate Pythagorean triples. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Use x = 3, y = 2 in the identity to make a Pythagorean triple.
  **A:** x² − y² = 5, 2xy = 12, x² + y² = 13: 5-12-13 (25 + 144 = 169).
- **Q:** Verify a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²).
  **A:** (a − b)(a² + ab + b²) = a³ + a²b + ab² − a²b − ab² − b³ = a³ − b³.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on operations and a written proof of one identity.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (about 30 minutes) including one written proof. Today's task from the plan: Quiz on operations and a written proof of one identity. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Expand (x − 3)².
  **A:** x² − 6x + 9.

**Assessment:** Operations quiz with proof.

**Answer key:** Sample key: 1) (4x³+x−2)+(x³−5x+9) = 5x³−4x+7. 2) (2x²−x)−(5x²+3x−4) = −3x²−4x+4. 3) (x−4)(x+4) = x²−16. 4) (3x+2)(x²−x+1) = 3x³−x²+x+2. 5) (2x−1)³ = 8x³−12x²+6x−1. Proof rubric (4 levels): 4 = expands one side completely, each step justified, ends with the other side; 3 = correct with a skipped step; 2 = partial expansion; 1 = substitutes numbers only (numbers check but do not prove).

## Physics of the Universe: Coulomb's law and the two forces at a distance

**Prepare:** Balloons, wool cloth, clear tape, aluminum foil, a glass jar, paper clip, cardboard lid. PhET Coulomb's Law. Dry days work best.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking charging creates charge. Charging transfers electrons from one object to another; total charge is conserved.
- Thinking only charged objects are attracted. A charged balloon attracts neutral paper bits by polarizing them (induced charge separation).

### Day 1 (Monday): Static electricity
*Student:* Charge balloons and tape strips; observe attraction and repulsion.

*How to teach:* Rub balloons on wool; test balloon-balloon (repel) and balloon-wall (attract). Make tape strips (top 'T' and bottom 'B') and test. Today's task from the plan: Charge balloons and tape strips; observe attraction and repulsion. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why do two balloons rubbed on wool repel each other?
  **A:** Both gain extra electrons (negative charge); like charges repel.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Coulomb's law
*Student:* Use PhET Coulomb's Law to explore force vs. charge and distance.

*How to teach:* In PhET, vary charge and distance; record force. Today's task from the plan: Use PhET Coulomb's Law to explore force vs. charge and distance. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** If the distance between two charges is halved, what happens to the force?
  **A:** It becomes 4 times larger (inverse square).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Comparison
*Student:* Compare gravitational and electrostatic forces between protons.

*How to teach:* Compare F_e and F_g for two protons using the formulas. Today's task from the plan: Compare gravitational and electrostatic forces between protons. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** How many times stronger is the electric force than gravity between two protons?
  **A:** About 1.2 × 10³⁶ times (distance cancels since both follow 1/r²).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Electroscope
*Student:* Build a foil-leaf electroscope in a jar and test charging by conduction and induction.

*How to teach:* Build the foil-leaf electroscope; test conduction (touch) and induction (bring near). Today's task from the plan: Build a foil-leaf electroscope in a jar and test charging by conduction and induction. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Why do the foil leaves spread apart when a charged balloon touches the top?
  **A:** Charge spreads to both leaves; like charges repel.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 2 test on gravitation, orbits, and Coulomb's law.

*How to teach:* Unit 2 test. Today's task from the plan: Unit 2 test on gravitation, orbits, and Coulomb's law. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Compare Newton's law of gravitation and Coulomb's law.
  **A:** Both are inverse-square laws; gravity is only attractive and acts between masses, while electric forces can attract or repel and act between charges.

**Assessment:** Electroscope and Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Electroscope rubric: built correctly, tested both methods, explanation of electron movement. Unit 2 test key (sample): F_g calculations, Kepler's third law, orbital speed, inverse-square effects, Coulomb's law (two 1 μC charges 0.1 m apart: 0.90 N), conduction vs induction.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Imperialism and the Spanish-American War

**Prepare:** Print excerpts: Alfred Thayer Mahan, The Influence of Sea Power upon History (1890); Queen Liliuokalani's protest (1893 or 1897); Hearst's New York Journal and Pulitzer's New York World headlines on the Maine (Feb. 1898); McKinley's reported rationale for the Philippines (1899, published 1903); the Anti-Imperialist League platform (1899).

**Watch for:**
- Students think the Maine was proven to be sunk by Spain. The cause was never proven; a 1976 U.S. Navy study concluded an internal coal-bunker fire likely caused it.
- Students think Hawaii's annexation was requested by Hawaiians. American planters and businessmen overthrew Queen Liliuokalani in 1893 with U.S. Marines present; many Native Hawaiians petitioned against annexation (1897).
- Students think the war ended in 1898 for the Philippines. Filipinos fought U.S. rule in the Philippine-American War (1899–1902, with fighting continuing later).

### Day 1 (Monday): Motives
*Student:* Analyze Mahan's argument for sea power and motives for expansion.

*How to teach:* Analyze Mahan's argument and list motives for expansion (economic markets, naval bases, Social Darwinism/racial ideas, missionary goals, closing of the frontier).

- **Q:** What did Mahan argue?
  **A:** Great nations need a strong navy, overseas coaling stations and bases, and access to trade; sea power builds national greatness.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Hawaii
*Student:* Read Queen Liliuokalani's protest and the annexation debate.

*How to teach:* Read Liliuokalani's protest and the annexation debate.

- **Q:** How was Hawaii's monarchy overthrown?
  **A:** In 1893 American and European planters and businessmen, backed by U.S. Marines from the USS Boston, forced Queen Liliuokalani from power; the U.S. annexed Hawaii in 1898.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Spanish-American War
*Student:* Analyze yellow journalism headlines about the Maine.

*How to teach:* Analyze yellow journalism headlines about the Maine.

- **Q:** What is yellow journalism?
  **A:** Sensational, exaggerated news to attract readers — used by Hearst and Pulitzer to inflame opinion against Spain.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Philippines debate
*Student:* Compare McKinley's rationale and the Anti-Imperialist League platform.

*How to teach:* Compare McKinley's rationale and the Anti-Imperialist League platform.

- **Q:** What territories did the U.S. gain from the 1898 Treaty of Paris?
  **A:** Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines (paying Spain $20 million); Cuba became nominally independent.
- **Q:** How did the Anti-Imperialist League use the Declaration of Independence?
  **A:** It argued governing Filipinos without their consent violated the principle that governments derive power from the consent of the governed.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a short argument on whether U.S. expansion was consistent with founding ideals.

*How to teach:* The student writes a short argument on whether U.S. expansion was consistent with founding ideals.

- **Q:** What was the Open Door Policy (1899)?
  **A:** Secretary of State John Hay's call for equal trading rights for all nations in China.

**Assessment:** Argument.

**Answer key:** Argument rubric: 4 = clear claim on whether expansion was consistent with founding ideals (consent of the governed, self-determination); uses at least three pieces of evidence (Liliuokalani's protest, annexation without a Hawaiian vote, Philippine-American War, Anti-Imperialist League's quoting of the Declaration, McKinley's 'uplift' rationale, Puerto Ricans' limited rights under the Insular Cases, Cuba's Platt Amendment); addresses a counterargument (spreading democracy, strategic or economic needs); clear organization. 3 = claim with two pieces of evidence. 2 = general. 1 = unsupported.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Stanislavski and given circumstances

**Prepare:** Find an overview of Stanislavski's system (e.g., a reputable theatre education site) and a public-domain copy of Chekhov's The Proposal (Lomov comes to propose to Natalya; they quarrel over Oxen Meadows and their dogs). Pick a 1-page scene for beat work.

**Watch for:**
- Playing an emotion ('be angry') instead of an action. Actors pursue objectives with tactics; emotion results.
- Writing objectives as states ('to be happy'). Use active, playable verbs aimed at the partner ('to win her approval').
- Thinking the 'magic if' means pretending you are the character. It means asking 'What would I do if I were in these circumstances?'

### Day 1 (Monday): Stanislavski
*Student:* Read an overview of Stanislavski's system and the 'magic if'.

*How to teach:* Read the overview: Constantin Stanislavski co-founded the Moscow Art Theatre (1898) and developed a system for truthful acting: magic if, given circumstances, objectives, super-objective.

- **Q:** What is the 'magic if'?
  **A:** The question 'What would I do if I were in this character's situation?' — it engages imagination to produce truthful behavior.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Objectives & tactics
*Student:* Play an improvised scene where you must get a partner to open a door using five different tactics.

*How to teach:* Improvise: one actor must get a partner to open a door using five tactics (beg, charm, threaten, bribe, trick). Debrief which worked.

- **Q:** Define objective, obstacle, and tactic.
  **A:** Objective: what the character wants. Obstacle: what stands in the way. Tactic: the action used to overcome the obstacle and get the objective.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Given circumstances
*Student:* Analyze who, what, where, when, why for a short scene from Chekhov's The Proposal.

*How to teach:* For a short scene of The Proposal, list who, what, where, when, why.

- **Q:** What are Lomov's given circumstances when he arrives at Chubukov's house?
  **A:** He is a nervous, hypochondriac landowner in 19th-century rural Russia who has come in formal dress to propose to his neighbor's daughter, Natalya Stepanovna.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Beats
*Student:* Break a 1-page scene into beats and label each with an active verb.

*How to teach:* Divide a 1-page scene into beats (new beat when the topic or tactic changes); title each with an active verb (to flatter, to accuse).

- **Q:** When does a new beat begin?
  **A:** When the character's objective, tactic, or the subject changes — something shifts.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Quiz on Stanislavski terms and a scored scene analysis.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and score the scene analysis.

- **Q:** What is a super-objective?
  **A:** The character's overarching goal for the whole play, which all scene objectives serve.

**Assessment:** Scene analysis.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Stanislavski co-founded the Moscow Art Theatre (1898). 2) Magic if: 'What would I do if…'. 3) Given circumstances: who/what/where/when/why. 4) Objective = want; obstacle = what blocks it; tactic = action to get it. 5) Super-objective = overarching goal for the play. 6) Beat = unit of action with one objective/tactic. Scene analysis (proficient): accurate given circumstances, playable objective per character, beats marked with active verbs.

## Lifetime Fitness: Resistance training fundamentals

**Prepare:** Gather an exercise mat and resistance bands; set up a phone to record form video. Find a sturdy table for inverted rows (test that it won't tip) or use a band row instead. Preview a muscle diagram.

**Watch for:**
- Believing lifting makes teens 'bulky' or stunts growth. Supervised, technique-focused resistance training is safe and beneficial for teens.
- Letting knees cave in during squats. Cue 'knees track over toes' and push the floor apart.
- Sagging hips in push-ups. The body should be a straight plank from head to heels; regress to incline push-ups if form breaks.

### Day 1 (Monday): Muscle physiology
*Student:* Learn major muscle groups and how muscles adapt; perform a bodyweight circuit.

*How to teach:* Show a muscle diagram and name major groups: chest (pectorals), back (latissimus dorsi, trapezius), shoulders (deltoids), arms (biceps, triceps), core (abdominals, obliques), legs (quadriceps, hamstrings, glutes, calves). Explain adaptation: neural gains first, then hypertrophy. Do a bodyweight circuit (squats, push-ups, glute bridges, plank) ×3.

- **Q:** In a biceps curl, what are the concentric and eccentric phases?
  **A:** Concentric: lifting the weight as the biceps shortens. Eccentric: lowering it as the biceps lengthens under control.
- **Q:** Why do beginners gain strength quickly in the first few weeks?
  **A:** Mostly neural adaptations — better recruitment and coordination of muscle fibers — before significant muscle growth.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Squat pattern
*Student:* Learn bodyweight squat and goblet squat technique with video feedback.

*How to teach:* Teach the bodyweight squat (feet shoulder-width, hips back and down, chest up, knees over toes, depth to at least parallel if comfortable), then goblet squat holding a jug at the chest. Record a side and front video and review.

- **Q:** Name three squat form cues.
  **A:** Chest up, sit hips back and down, knees track over toes, weight across the whole foot, neutral spine.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Push pattern
*Student:* Learn push-up progressions (incline to full) and band chest press.

*How to teach:* Progress push-ups from wall → incline (counter) → full; student finds the level they can do 8–12 good reps. Then band chest press (band behind back). Check straight body line.

- **Q:** What muscles does a push-up mainly work?
  **A:** Pectorals (chest), anterior deltoids (front shoulders), triceps, with the core stabilizing.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Pull pattern
*Student:* Learn band rows and inverted rows using a sturdy table.

*How to teach:* Teach band rows (squeeze shoulder blades, elbows close) and inverted rows under a sturdy table (body straight, pull chest to table). Test table safety first.

- **Q:** What muscles do rows mainly work?
  **A:** Back muscles — latissimus dorsi, rhomboids, middle trapezius — plus biceps and rear deltoids.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Demonstrate squat, push, and pull patterns with correct form.

*How to teach:* Use a form checklist to score squat, push, and pull (3 reps each). Give specific feedback and a regression/progression for each.

- **Q:** Define a set and a repetition.
  **A:** A repetition is one complete movement; a set is a group of consecutive repetitions (e.g., 3 sets of 10 reps).

**Assessment:** Form checklist.

**Answer key:** Form checklist (1 point each, proficient ≥ 8/10). Squat: chest up; hips back; knees track toes; heels down; depth to parallel or comfortable. Push-up: straight body line; elbows ~45° from body; chest to fist height/full range. Row: shoulder blades squeeze; torso stable/no swinging.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Precision measurement

**Prepare:** Get a ruler with 1/16-inch and mm markings and a low-cost digital or dial caliper. Gather 10 coins, some bolts and a LEGO 2×4 brick. Preview how to zero and read your caliper.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up accuracy and precision. Accuracy = close to the true value; precision = close to each other (repeatable).
- Not zeroing the caliper first. Close the jaws, clean them, and zero before every measuring session.
- Reporting more digits than the tool can measure. Record to the tool's resolution (e.g. 0.01 mm for most digital calipers).

### Day 1 (Monday): Accuracy vs. precision
*Student:* Use target diagrams and a measurement game to distinguish accuracy and precision.

*How to teach:* Draw four targets: accurate & precise (tight cluster at center), precise not accurate (tight cluster off center), accurate not precise (scattered around center), neither. Play a quick game: toss coins at a target to illustrate.

- **Q:** A scale reads 2.51, 2.50, 2.52 kg for a 3.00 kg weight. Accurate, precise, both or neither?
  **A:** Precise (readings close together) but not accurate (far from 3.00).
- **Q:** Define accuracy and precision.
  **A:** Accuracy: how close a measurement is to the true value. Precision: how close repeated measurements are to each other.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ruler reading
*Student:* Measure 10 objects to 1/16 inch and 1 mm, converting between units.

*How to teach:* Show how to read a ruler to 1/16 inch (count marks between inches) and to 1 mm. Measure 10 objects in both units and convert (1 in = 25.4 mm).

- **Q:** Convert 3 5/16 in to mm.
  **A:** 3.3125 × 25.4 = 84.1 mm.
- **Q:** Convert 50 mm to inches.
  **A:** 50 ÷ 25.4 ≈ 1.97 in.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Calipers
*Student:* Learn to zero and read a caliper for outside, inside, and depth measurements; measure 10 coins.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate the caliper: outside jaws (outer size), inside jaws (hole diameter), depth rod (depth), zero first. Measure 10 coins.

- **Q:** Which part of the caliper measures a hole's diameter?
  **A:** The inside (upper) jaws.
- **Q:** What is the diameter of a U.S. quarter?
  **A:** 24.26 mm (0.955 in).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Measure a part
*Student:* Measure all features of a LEGO brick and sketch it fully dimensioned.

*How to teach:* Measure every feature of a LEGO brick and sketch it fully dimensioned.

- **Q:** What are typical measurements of a LEGO 2×4 brick?
  **A:** About 31.8 mm × 15.8 mm, 9.6 mm tall without studs; studs 4.8 mm in diameter on 8.0 mm centers.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Measurement practical with a scored data sheet.

*How to teach:* Give the measurement practical with stations (coins, bolt length and diameter, LEGO brick, hole diameter) and score with the reference values in the key.

- **Q:** What does the resolution of a measuring tool mean?
