## English 11 – American Literature: Rhetorical analysis essay: voices excluded and included

**Prepare:** Print Wheatley's 'On Being Brought from Africa to America' and her 1774 letter to Samson Occom; Abigail Adams' March 31, 1776 letter ('Remember the Ladies'); an excerpt of Washington's Farewell Address for the test.

**Watch for:**
- Reading Wheatley's poem as simple gratitude for slavery. Show the turn in the last lines that commands 'Christians' to remember that Black people 'may be refin'd, and join th' angelic train' — she uses their religion to claim equality.
- Summarizing instead of analyzing in a rhetorical analysis essay. Every body paragraph must name a choice, give an example, and explain its effect on the audience.

### Day 1 (Monday): Wheatley
*Student:* Read Wheatley's poem and Occom letter; analyze how she uses her audience's own beliefs to argue for freedom.

*How to teach:* Read the poem twice and the letter. Model analyzing how she addresses Christian readers on their own terms. Today's task from the plan: Read Wheatley's poem and Occom letter; analyze how she uses her audience's own beliefs to argue for freedom. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** How does Wheatley's poem use her audience's beliefs?
  **A:** She appeals to Christian teaching that all souls can be saved, warning readers that 'Negros, black as Cain, / May be refin'd, and join th' angelic train,' so they must accept Black people as spiritual equals.
- **Q:** What contradiction does the Occom letter point out?
  **A:** That colonists cry for liberty while holding others in slavery; she says it does not take a philosopher to see these attitudes don't agree.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Abigail Adams
*Student:* Read 'Remember the Ladies'; identify tone shifts and the argument beneath the playful diction.

*How to teach:* Read the letter aloud. The student marks tone shifts (affectionate, playful, serious) and paraphrases the argument underneath. Today's task from the plan: Read 'Remember the Ladies'; identify tone shifts and the argument beneath the playful diction. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What is Abigail Adams' argument beneath the playful threat to 'foment a Rebelion'?
  **A:** Women should have legal protections and a voice; she applies the Revolution's own principle (no laws without representation) to women and warns that 'all Men would be tyrants if they could.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Essay plan
*Student:* Choose one founding-era text and outline a rhetorical analysis essay (situation, choices, effect).

*How to teach:* The student chooses one founding-era text and outlines: situation, three rhetorical choices, effect of each. Today's task from the plan: Choose one founding-era text and outline a rhetorical analysis essay (situation, choices, effect). Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What three parts should each body paragraph plan contain?
  **A:** The rhetorical choice (e.g., allusion), a quoted example, and an explanation of its effect on the specific audience and purpose.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Draft & edit
*Student:* Write a 750-word rhetorical analysis; punctuation focus: semicolons and colons with quoted evidence.

*How to teach:* The student drafts 750 words. Teach punctuation: a colon introduces a quotation after a complete sentence; a semicolon joins two related independent clauses.

- **Q:** Punctuate: Henry ends with a choice Give me liberty or give me death
  **A:** Henry ends with a stark choice: 'Give me liberty, or give me death!'

Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What does Washington warn against in his Farewell Address?
  **A:** The dangers of political parties/factions and permanent alliances with foreign nations.

**Assessment:** Rhetorical analysis essay and Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Rhetorical analysis rubric (4 = exceeds, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning) on four rows: Claim/Thesis (arguable, specific, answers the prompt); Evidence (well-chosen quotations, introduced and cited); Analysis/Reasoning (explains HOW evidence supports the claim, not just what it says); Conventions & organization (logical paragraphs, transitions, few errors). A proficient (3) paper identifies the speaker's situation and audience, analyzes at least three choices (e.g., Wheatley's biblical allusion to Cain, Adams' ironic threat of rebellion) with quotations, and explains how each serves the purpose. Unit 2 test key: ethos/pathos/logos definitions; parallelism; rhetorical question; allusion; syllogism. Unseen Farewell excerpt: purpose = to advise national unity; warns against faction and foreign entanglement; tone = paternal, earnest.

## Algebra II: Quadratic modeling and linear-quadratic systems

**Prepare:** Tennis ball, phone that records video (slow-motion helps), a wall or doorframe with tape marks every foot (or meter), stopwatch. Desmos regression: type a table and y1 ~ ax1² + bx1 + c.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the parabola is the path in the air across the room. In the lab the graph is height vs TIME; a ball tossed straight up still produces a parabola in time.
- Accepting a negative time or a negative length as an answer. Always state the domain that makes sense in context and reject nonviable solutions.

### Day 1 (Monday): Projectile lab
*Student:* Toss a ball, video it, record height vs. time points, and fit a quadratic regression in Desmos.

*How to teach:* Toss the ball straight up next to the taped wall while the phone records. Step through the video to record height every 0.1 s (or every few frames). Enter points in Desmos and fit y ~ ax² + bx + c.

- **Q:** Why should the data be quadratic, and what should a be close to?
  **A:** Gravity gives constant downward acceleration, so height vs time is a parabola. In feet a ≈ −16 (half of −32 ft/s²); in meters a ≈ −4.9.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Interpreting the model
*Student:* Use the regression to find max height and landing time; compare to h = −16t² + v₀t + h₀.

*How to teach:* Student uses the regression to find the vertex (max height) and positive zero (landing time) and compares the a-value to −16 (or −4.9). Today's task from the plan: Use the regression to find max height and landing time; compare to h = −16t² + v₀t + h₀. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** For h = −16t² + 48t + 6, find the maximum height and when it lands.
  **A:** Vertex at t = −48/(2·−16) = 1.5 s; h = −36 + 72 + 6 = 42 ft. Landing: −16t² + 48t + 6 = 0 → t = (48 + √2688)/32 ≈ 3.12 s (reject the negative root).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Linear-quadratic systems
*Student:* Solve algebraically and graphically (e.g., a line crossing a parabola).

*How to teach:* Model solving y = x², y = x + 2 by substitution and showing the two crossing points in Desmos. Discuss 0, 1, or 2 intersections. Today's task from the plan: Solve algebraically and graphically (e.g., a line crossing a parabola). Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Solve y = x² and y = x + 2.
  **A:** x² − x − 2 = 0 → (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0 → (2, 4) and (−1, 1).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Optimization
*Student:* Solve a fencing/area and a revenue (price × quantity) maximization problem.

*How to teach:* Model the fencing problem: 40 ft of fence, A = x(20 − x). Then revenue R = p(200 − 10p). Student finds each vertex and interprets. Today's task from the plan: Solve a fencing/area and a revenue (price × quantity) maximization problem. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What dimensions maximize the area with 40 ft of fence?
  **A:** A = x(20 − x) has vertex at x = 10: a 10 ft × 10 ft square, 100 sq ft.
- **Q:** If quantity sold is 200 − 10p, what price maximizes revenue?
  **A:** R = 200p − 10p², vertex p = 10: price $10, revenue $1,000.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 2 test: forms, solving methods, complex numbers, and modeling.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test (about 50 minutes) and collect the lab report. Today's task from the plan: Unit 2 test: forms, solving methods, complex numbers, and modeling. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Solve x² − 6x + 13 = 0.
  **A:** x = (6 ± √(36 − 52))/2 = (6 ± 4i)/2 = 3 ± 2i.

**Assessment:** Projectile lab report and Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Lab report rubric (4 pts each): data table and graph; regression equation stated; max height and landing time found and interpreted; comparison of a to −16 or −4.9 with a sentence on error sources. Unit 2 test sample answers: (x−1)²−9 → zeros −2, 4; 2x²+x−6=0 → 3/2, −2; x²+2x+3=0 → −1±i√2; (2−i)/(2+i) = (3−4i)/5; max of −5t²+20t+1 is 21 at t=2; y=2x+3 and y=x² meet at (3, 9) and (−1, 1).

## Physics of the Universe: Orbits and Kepler's laws

**Prepare:** String, two pushpins, cardboard; planet data table (semi-major axis in AU and period in years); PhET Gravity and Orbits.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking seasons come from Earth's distance to the Sun. Earth's orbit is nearly circular (e ≈ 0.017); seasons are from axial tilt.
- Thinking a satellite needs fuel to stay in orbit. It is continuously falling around Earth; its sideways speed keeps it from hitting the ground.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ellipses
*Student:* Draw ellipses with string and pins; calculate eccentricity.

*How to teach:* Draw ellipses with pins at different spacings. e = c/a (focus distance ÷ semi-major axis). Today's task from the plan: Draw ellipses with string and pins; calculate eccentricity. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** An ellipse has a = 10 cm and foci 4 cm from the center. What is e?
  **A:** e = 4/10 = 0.4.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Kepler's laws
*Student:* Analyze planet data to verify Kepler's third law (T² ∝ a³).

*How to teach:* The student computes T² and a³ for each planet (AU, years) and graphs. Today's task from the plan: Analyze planet data to verify Kepler's third law (T² ∝ a³). Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** Mars has a = 1.52 AU. Predict its period.
  **A:** T² = a³ = 3.51 → T ≈ 1.87 years.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Simulation
*Student:* Use PhET Gravity and Orbits to change the Sun's mass and observe orbit changes.

*How to teach:* In PhET, increase the Sun's mass and observe Earth's orbit. Today's task from the plan: Use PhET Gravity and Orbits to change the Sun's mass and observe orbit changes. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** What happens to Earth's orbit if the Sun's mass doubles (at the same speed)?
  **A:** Stronger gravity pulls Earth into a smaller, faster, more elliptical orbit.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Satellites
*Student:* Calculate orbital speed and period for the ISS and GPS satellites.

*How to teach:* Model v = √(GM/r) and T = 2πr/v for the ISS (about 400 km up). Today's task from the plan: Calculate orbital speed and period for the ISS and GPS satellites. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Find the ISS's orbital speed and period (r ≈ 6.77 × 10⁶ m, GM = 3.99 × 10¹⁴).
  **A:** v ≈ 7.7 km/s; T ≈ 92 minutes.
- **Q:** Why do GPS satellites (about 20,200 km up) have longer periods?
  **A:** Larger r means weaker gravity and slower speed over a longer path; period ≈ 12 hours.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write an explanation of how orbital speed changes in an elliptical orbit.

*How to teach:* The student writes an explanation of speed changes in an elliptical orbit. Today's task from the plan: Write an explanation of how orbital speed changes in an elliptical orbit. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Where is a planet fastest?
  **A:** At perihelion (closest to the Sun), as Kepler's second law requires equal areas in equal times.

**Assessment:** Kepler data analysis.

**Answer key:** Data analysis key: T²/a³ ≈ 1 for all planets in years and AU (Mercury 0.387 AU, 0.241 yr; Jupiter 5.20 AU, 11.86 yr). Rubric (4-point): table complete, graph of T² vs a³ linear through origin, slope ≈ 1, explanation of Kepler's second law with perihelion/aphelion speeds.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: The Progressive Era

**Prepare:** Print Ida Tarbell's History of the Standard Oil Company (1904) or Lincoln Steffens's The Shame of the Cities excerpt; Meat Inspection Act summary; a reform chart template; Calisphere sources on Hiram Johnson, the 1911 special election and suffrage posters.

**Watch for:**
- Students think Progressives were one party. Progressivism was a movement with Republicans (TR), Democrats (Wilson) and the 1912 Progressive ('Bull Moose') Party.
- Students confuse initiative, referendum and recall. Initiative: citizens propose laws; referendum: voters approve or reject laws; recall: voters remove officials.
- Students think all women got the vote in 1920. California women voted from 1911; the 19th Amendment (1920) barred sex discrimination in voting nationally, but many women of color still faced barriers.

### Day 1 (Monday): Muckrakers
*Student:* Read Tarbell or Steffens excerpts and identify targets.

*How to teach:* Read Tarbell or Steffens excerpts and identify targets.

- **Q:** What did Ida Tarbell expose?
  **A:** Standard Oil's ruthless and unfair business practices (rebates, crushing competitors).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Federal reform
*Student:* Chart reforms of Roosevelt, Taft, and Wilson (Pure Food and Drug Act, Federal Reserve, 16th–17th Amendments).

*How to teach:* Chart reforms of TR, Taft and Wilson.

- **Q:** What did the Federal Reserve Act (1913) create?
  **A:** A central banking system of 12 regional Federal Reserve Banks to manage the money supply and stabilize banks.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): California Progressivism
*Student:* Study Hiram Johnson and California's 1911 initiative, referendum, and recall.

*How to teach:* Study Hiram Johnson and California's 1911 reforms.

- **Q:** Why did California reformers want the initiative and referendum?

### Day 4 (Thursday): Women's suffrage
*Student:* Analyze suffrage posters and California's 1911 suffrage vote.

*How to teach:* Analyze suffrage posters and California's 1911 vote.

- **Q:** How close was California's 1911 suffrage vote?

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on Progressive reforms.

*How to teach:* Give the Progressive reforms quiz.

  **A:** A vote by citizens to remove an elected official from office before the term ends.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Muckrakers — journalists exposing corruption (Tarbell: Standard Oil; Steffens: city corruption; Sinclair: meatpacking). 2) TR — Square Deal, trust-busting (Northern Securities, 1904), Meat Inspection and Pure Food and Drug Acts (1906), conservation. 3) Taft — more antitrust suits (Standard Oil broken up 1911). 4) Wilson — Federal Reserve Act (1913), Clayton Antitrust Act and FTC (1914), Underwood Tariff with income tax. 5) 16th Amendment — income tax (1913). 6) 17th — direct election of senators (1913). 7) 18th — Prohibition (1919). 8) 19th — women's suffrage (1920). 9) Hiram Johnson — elected governor 1910, broke Southern Pacific Railroad's political power. 10) California 1911 — initiative, referendum, recall adopted; women's suffrage (Proposition 4) passed narrowly, making California the sixth state with full women's suffrage.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Voice and body together: dialects and choral speaking

**Prepare:** Find the International Dialects of English Archive (IDEA) website. Choose a choral text (Whitman 'I Hear America Singing' or a Greek chorus ode, e.g., the 'Ode to Man' from Antigone in a public-domain translation). Recording app ready; recruit voices.

**Watch for:**
- Treating dialect as a joke or stereotype. Dialects are real people's speech; use them respectfully and only when the character requires it.
- Choral speaking with everyone speaking the whole time. Contrast (solo, small group, full chorus) creates interest.
- Inflection only at the end of lines. Pitch variety should follow meaning and operative words.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pitch & inflection
*Student:* Read a neutral sentence ten ways to convey different subtexts.

*How to teach:* Read 'I didn't say she took the money' ten ways, stressing a different word or using different pitch/inflection; partner guesses the subtext.

- **Q:** How does stressing 'she' change 'I didn't say SHE took the money'?
  **A:** It implies someone else took the money.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dialect awareness
*Student:* Listen to the International Dialects of English Archive samples and discuss respectful use of dialect.

*How to teach:* Listen to 3–4 IDEA samples; discuss features (vowels, rhythm, melody) and respectful use: research, specificity, avoid caricature.

- **Q:** What is a dialect?
  **A:** A variety of a language with distinctive pronunciation, vocabulary, and grammar, associated with a region or group.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Choral speaking
*Student:* Arrange a Greek chorus excerpt or Whitman poem for multiple voices with movement.

*How to teach:* Divide the text into solo, small-group, and unison lines; add simple movement or gestures.

- **Q:** What is choral speaking?
  **A:** A group speaking a text together with planned variation in voices, dynamics, and pacing.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rehearse
*Student:* Rehearse the choral piece focusing on unison, contrast, and staging.

*How to teach:* Rehearse for unison (breathe together), contrast (loud/soft, fast/slow), and staging.

- **Q:** How do groups stay in unison?
  **A:** Breathe together, follow a leader's cue, agree on pace and pauses, and listen to each other.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Perform the choral piece and complete a voice and movement self-assessment.

*How to teach:* Perform the choral piece; student completes a voice-and-movement self-assessment.

- **Q:** Name two vocal elements you varied.
  **A:** Pitch, pace, volume (dynamics), inflection, and tone.

**Assessment:** Choral performance.

**Answer key:** Choral performance rubric. 4 – Precise unison, purposeful contrasts in volume, pace and grouping, clear articulation, staging supports meaning; thoughtful self-assessment. 3 (proficient) – Mostly unified, clear contrasts, audible, complete self-assessment. 2 – Ragged unison or little variation. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness: Cardio assessment: 5K challenge

**Prepare:** Measure a 5K (3.1-mile) route or register for a local fun run. Gather the student's training log and week 1 mile time. Have foam roller (optional) for recovery day.

**Watch for:**
- Starting the race too fast. Even or negative splits (second half faster) usually produce better times.
- Training hard the day before a race. Taper: reduce volume, keep a little intensity (strides).
- Skipping recovery after a race. Gentle activity aids recovery more than total rest.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pacing
*Student:* Plan a pacing strategy for a 5K based on training data.

*How to teach:* Use the student's recent paces to set a goal 5K pace. Divide the 3.1 miles into splits and plan a strategy (start controlled, settle, push the last mile).

- **Q:** A runner's goal 5K time is 27:54. What pace per mile is that?
  **A:** 27:54 ÷ 3.1 ≈ 9:00 per mile.
- **Q:** What is a negative split?
  **A:** Running the second half of a race faster than the first half.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Easy run
*Student:* Do a 20-minute shake-out run plus strides.

*How to teach:* 20-minute easy shake-out run plus 4–6 strides (20-second relaxed accelerations). Keep it easy.

- **Q:** Why do a shake-out run before a race instead of a hard workout?
  **A:** To stay loose and keep legs fresh (taper) while not adding fatigue.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): 5K
*Student:* Run, walk, or bike a timed 5K (or join a community fun run).

*How to teach:* Timed 5K (run, walk, or bike). Record total time and mile splits if possible; check that the student hydrates and warms up first.

- **Q:** How many kilometers and miles is a 5K?
  **A:** 5 kilometers ≈ 3.1 miles.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Recovery
*Student:* Complete active recovery (walk, foam rolling, stretching).

*How to teach:* Easy walk 20–30 min, foam rolling of calves/quads, and static stretching. Discuss soreness vs. pain.

- **Q:** How can you tell normal muscle soreness from injury?
  **A:** Soreness is dull, in both legs or muscles broadly, and improves within 1–3 days; injury pain is sharp, localized, worsens with activity, or causes limping — stop and seek care.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Compare 5K results to week 1 mile time and update goals.

*How to teach:* Student compares 5K average pace to week 1 mile pace and writes a reflection; update SMART goals if needed.

- **Q:** Why might 5K pace per mile be slower than a single mile's pace?
  **A:** Longer distances must be run at a lower intensity to be sustained, so pace per mile is usually slower.

**Assessment:** 5K time and reflection.

**Answer key:** 5K reflection rubric. 4 – Records time and splits, calculates average pace correctly, compares to week 1 mile pace with explanation, evaluates pacing strategy and sets a revised goal. 3 (proficient) – Time, correct pace, comparison, and updated goal. 2 – Time recorded but comparison/goal missing. 1 – Incomplete. (No time standard is required; effort and accurate reflection are graded.)

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Dimensioning technical drawings

**Prepare:** Gather a ruler, grid paper and a sample part (LEGO brick or a small bracket). Preview ASME Y14.5 basics (a free summary or a tutorial video on dimensioning rules).

**Watch for:**
- Dimensioning the same feature twice (double dimensioning). Each feature is dimensioned once, in its most descriptive view.
- Dimensioning to hidden lines. Place dimensions on visible features in the view where the shape is clearest.
- Thinking tolerance means the part may be 'wrong'. Tolerance is the allowed variation — no part is made exactly to size, so the designer states how much variation still works.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dimensioning rules
*Student:* Learn line types and ASME basics (no double dimensions, dimension features in their most descriptive view).

*How to teach:* Teach line types: object, hidden, center, extension, dimension, leader. Rules: dimension each feature once, in the most descriptive view, place dimensions between views, smaller dimensions inside larger ones.

- **Q:** What is double dimensioning and why avoid it?
  **A:** Dimensioning the same feature more than once; it can create conflicting values and confusion about which to follow.
- **Q:** Should extension lines touch the object?
  **A:** No — leave a small visible gap between the object and the extension line.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Practice
*Student:* Fully dimension multiview drawings of three block parts.

*How to teach:* Fully dimension three block parts. Check every feature has size and location dimensions and none are duplicated.

- **Q:** What two kinds of dimensions does every feature need?
  **A:** Size dimensions (how big) and location dimensions (where it is).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Holes & arcs
*Student:* Dimension holes, radii, and counterbores using leaders and notes.

*How to teach:* Teach leaders for holes and radii: Ø for diameter, R for radius, and counterbore notes (⌴). Dimension example holes.

- **Q:** How is a 12 mm diameter hole labeled? A 5 mm radius fillet?
  **A:** Ø12 with a leader pointing to the circle; R5 with a leader to the arc.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Tolerances
*Student:* Learn limit and plus/minus tolerances and calculate fits between a peg and a hole.

*How to teach:* Teach plus/minus and limit tolerances. Work: hole 0.500 ± 0.005 in; peg 0.490 ± 0.003 in. Hole ranges 0.495–0.505; peg 0.487–0.493. Tightest clearance = 0.495 − 0.493 = 0.002; loosest = 0.505 − 0.487 = 0.018. Always a clearance fit.

- **Q:** A peg is 20.0 ± 0.1 mm and the hole is 20.3 ± 0.1 mm. What are the minimum and maximum clearances?
  **A:** Min = 20.2 − 20.1 = 0.1 mm; max = 20.4 − 19.9 = 0.5 mm. It always fits (clearance fit).
- **Q:** Write 25 ± 0.2 as limit dimensions.
  **A:** 24.8 / 25.2.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 2 test: produce a fully dimensioned multiview drawing of a household part.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test: fully dimension a multiview drawing of a household part. Score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Why do engineers use baseline instead of chain dimensioning for critical parts?
  **A:** Baseline measures each feature from a common datum, so tolerances don't add up (stack) from feature to feature.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test rubric for the dimensioned multiview drawing (4 levels): 4 = three correctly aligned views; overall width, height and depth dimensioned; every feature size and location dimensioned exactly once in its most descriptive view; extension lines with a small gap from the object; dimension lines with arrowheads; holes dimensioned with a leader and diameter symbol (e.g. Ø6) and located by their centers; units noted; a tolerance note; 3 = one or two missing/duplicated dimensions; 2 = several missing dimensions or views misaligned; 1 = incomplete. Short items: 1) Hole Ø10.0 ± 0.1 → limits 9.9 to 10.1. 2) Baseline dimensioning measures every feature from one datum, preventing tolerance stack-up; chain dimensioning measures feature to feature and lets errors add up.
