## English 11 – American Literature: Hemingway, Faulkner, and the Modern short story

**Prepare:** Hemingway's 'Hills Like White Elephants' and Faulkner's 'A Rose for Emily' (library anthology). Parent note: Hemingway's story centers on an implied abortion; Faulkner's ending implies Emily slept beside a corpse. Preview both.

**Watch for:**
- Expecting Hemingway to state the conflict. His 'iceberg' method leaves most meaning beneath the surface; the reader infers from dialogue.
- Reading 'A Rose for Emily' as if events were in order. Faulkner scrambles chronology to delay the revelation and mimic town memory.

### Day 1 (Monday): Hemingway
*Student:* Read 'Hills Like White Elephants'; list what is never stated and infer the conflict from dialogue alone.

*How to teach:* Read 'Hills' aloud as a dialogue (you read the man, the student reads Jig). The student lists everything never stated and infers the conflict. Today's task from the plan: Read 'Hills Like White Elephants'; list what is never stated and infer the conflict from dialogue alone. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What is the 'awfully simple operation' the man keeps mentioning?
  **A:** It is implied to be an abortion; the man pressures Jig while she seems to want the baby and a different life.
- **Q:** Why does Jig say 'Would you please please please please please please please stop talking?'
  **A:** The repetition shows her frustration with his insistent reassurances; she sees through him.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Faulkner
*Student:* Read 'A Rose for Emily'; reconstruct the chronology on a timeline and explain the effect of the nonlinear order.

*How to teach:* Read 'A Rose for Emily.' The student reconstructs the true chronology on a timeline and compares it to the story's order. Today's task from the plan: Read 'A Rose for Emily'; reconstruct the chronology on a timeline and explain the effect of the nonlinear order. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What is found in Emily's house after her funeral?
  **A:** Homer Barron's decayed body in the bridal room, and a long strand of iron-gray hair on the pillow beside it, revealing she poisoned him and lay beside him for years.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Narrator 'we'
*Student:* Analyze the communal narrator in 'Emily' and how it builds suspense.

*How to teach:* Discuss the narrator 'we.' The student finds three places where 'we' reveals the town's attitudes. Today's task from the plan: Analyze the communal narrator in 'Emily' and how it builds suspense. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why does Faulkner use the communal narrator 'we'?
  **A:** It represents the town's gossiping collective voice, which both pities and spies on Emily; its partial knowledge builds suspense until the end.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Style imitation
*Student:* Write a 500-word scene in Hemingway's minimalist style using only dialogue and gesture.

*How to teach:* Review Hemingway's style rules. The student writes a 500-word scene using only dialogue and gesture, implying (not stating) the conflict. Today's task from the plan: Write a 500-word scene in Hemingway's minimalist style using only dialogue and gesture. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** List three features of Hemingway's minimalist style.
  **A:** Short declarative sentences, dialogue carrying the meaning, and no direct statement of characters' emotions.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 6 test: unseen Modernist passage analysis plus terms.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test: an unseen Modernist passage plus terms. Today's task from the plan: Unit 6 test: unseen Modernist passage analysis plus terms. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What is Hemingway's 'iceberg theory'?
  **A:** Like an iceberg, most of a story's meaning lies beneath the surface; the writer omits what readers can feel without being told.

**Assessment:** Style imitation and Unit 6 test.

**Answer key:** Style imitation rubric (4-point): conflict clearly implied but never named; mostly dialogue and gesture; short, plain sentences; an ending that resonates. ## Algebra II: Exponential and logarithmic equations and modeling

**Prepare:** A mug of hot water, a kitchen or digital thermometer, timer. Desmos. Look up the pH and decibel definitions.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting to check log-equation solutions; arguments must be positive.
- Treating log scales as linear: pH 3 is not 'a bit' more acidic than pH 5; it is 100 times more acidic.

### Day 1 (Monday): Solving exponential equations
*Student:* Solve ab^(ct) = d using logarithms (doubling time, half-life).

*How to teach:* Model isolating the exponential, then taking ln of both sides. Doubling time and half-life examples. Today's task from the plan: Solve ab^(ct) = d using logarithms (doubling time, half-life). Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** $500 grows at 6% per year. When does it double?
  **A:** 1.06^t = 2 → t = ln 2/ln 1.06 ≈ 11.9 years.
- **Q:** Carbon-14 half-life is 5,730 years. How old is a sample with 25% remaining?
  **A:** Two half-lives: 11,460 years.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Solving log equations
*Student:* Solve log equations and check extraneous solutions.

*How to teach:* Model condensing to one log, rewriting in exponential form, solving, and checking. Today's task from the plan: Solve log equations and check extraneous solutions. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Solve log₂x + log₂(x − 2) = 3.
  **A:** log₂(x(x − 2)) = 3 → x² − 2x − 8 = 0 → x = 4 or −2. Reject −2 (log of negative). x = 4.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Newton's cooling lab
*Student:* Record a cooling cup's temperature every 2 minutes and fit an exponential model.

*How to teach:* Record room temp, then the mug's temperature every 2 minutes for 30 minutes. Subtract room temp and fit an exponential to (T − T_room). Today's task from the plan: Record a cooling cup's temperature every 2 minutes and fit an exponential model. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** Why subtract room temperature before fitting the exponential?
  **A:** Newton's law says the DIFFERENCE from room temperature decays exponentially: T = T_room + (T₀ − T_room)e^(−kt); the water approaches room temp, not 0.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Log scales
*Student:* Compute pH, decibel, and earthquake magnitude comparisons.

*How to teach:* Model pH = −log[H⁺], dB = 10 log(I/I₀), and magnitude comparisons with powers of 10. Today's task from the plan: Compute pH, decibel, and earthquake magnitude comparisons. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Compare pH 3 to pH 5.
  **A:** pH 3 has 10² = 100 times the hydrogen-ion concentration.
- **Q:** How much more intense is 90 dB than 60 dB?
  **A:** 30 dB difference = 10³ = 1,000 times the intensity.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 6 test on exponential and logarithmic functions.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test (about 50 minutes) and collect the cooling lab. Today's task from the plan: Unit 6 test on exponential and logarithmic functions. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Solve 3^(x+1) = 20.
  **A:** x + 1 = ln 20/ln 3 ≈ 2.727, x ≈ 1.727.

**Assessment:** Cooling lab and Unit 6 test.

**Answer key:** Cooling lab rubric: table (1), graph of T − T_room with exponential fit (1), model written in T form (1), prediction for when water reaches a chosen temperature with comment on accuracy (1). Unit 6 test sample answers: 2^x = 50 → x ≈ 5.644; e^(2x) = 7 → x ≈ 0.973; log(x+1) = 2 → x = 99; ln x + ln 3 = ln 12 → x = 4; earthquake 6.0 vs 4.0: 100 times the amplitude; half-life 8 days, 100 g after 24 days = 12.5 g.

## Physics of the Universe: Digital information and wave technology

**Prepare:** Flashlight; laser pointer and a clear plastic bottle of water with a hole near the bottom (do over a sink); a dark room.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking digital is always 'better quality.' Digital is more resistant to noise and easy to copy and compress, but it samples the analog signal.
- Thinking light can't bend in a fiber. Total internal reflection keeps light inside as long as the angle is greater than the critical angle.

### Day 1 (Monday): Analog vs. digital
*Student:* Compare analog and digital signals; encode a message in binary.

*How to teach:* Compare analog (continuous) and digital (discrete) signals. The student encodes their initials in 8-bit binary ASCII. Today's task from the plan: Compare analog and digital signals; encode a message in binary. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Encode 'H' (ASCII 72) in 8-bit binary.
  **A:** 72 = 64 + 8 → 01001000.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Signal noise
*Student:* Send a message by flashlight Morse code with noise and discuss error correction.

*How to teach:* Send a message by flashlight Morse code while someone waves a hand to add 'noise'; discuss error correction (repeats, check bits). Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** How can digital signals correct errors?
  **A:** By adding redundant information like parity/check bits or repeating the message, the receiver can detect and fix flipped bits.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fiber optics
*Student:* Demonstrate total internal reflection in a stream of water with a laser.

*How to teach:* Shine the laser through the bottle into the water stream in a dark room. Today's task from the plan: Demonstrate total internal reflection in a stream of water with a laser. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why does the light follow the curved water stream?
  **A:** Total internal reflection: light hits the water–air boundary at an angle greater than the critical angle (about 49° for water), so it reflects instead of leaving.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Technology report
*Student:* Research how a device (MRI, solar cell, Wi-Fi) uses waves.

*How to teach:* The student researches one technology (MRI, solar cell, Wi-Fi) and how it uses waves. Today's task from the plan: Research how a device (MRI, solar cell, Wi-Fi) uses waves. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** How does a solar cell use light?
  **A:** Photons with enough energy free electrons in a semiconductor (photoelectric effect), creating current.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 5 test on waves and EM radiation.

*How to teach:* Unit 5 test. Today's task from the plan: Unit 5 test on waves and EM radiation. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Why is fiber optic cable better than copper for long distances?
  **A:** Light signals lose less energy, carry more data, and are not affected by electrical interference.

**Assessment:** Technology report and Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Technology report rubric: explains which waves are used, the physics principle, and a benefit/limitation; sources cited. Unit 5 test key (sample): v = fλ; sound faster in solids; Doppler; interference; EM order; ionizing vs non-ionizing; E = hf; total internal reflection; binary encoding.

## Lifetime Fitness: Aquatics or outdoor challenge

**Prepare:** Arrange pool access with a lifeguard (or plan the trail alternative). Review reach-throw-don't-go and rip-current escape. If the student is a weak swimmer, keep all water work in shallow water with an adult present.

**Watch for:**
- Jumping in to save someone. Untrained rescuers often drown; reach with an object or throw a flotation device, and call for help.
- Swimming straight toward shore against a rip current. Swim parallel to shore until out of the current, or float and signal for help.
- Lifting the head forward to breathe in freestyle. Rotate the head to the side with one goggle in the water.

### Day 1 (Monday): Water safety
*Student:* Learn water safety, rip currents, and reach-throw-don't-go rescues.

*How to teach:* Teach water safety: swim near lifeguards, never swim alone, reach-throw-don't-go, and rip currents (narrow channel flowing away from shore — swim parallel to escape).

- **Q:** You're caught in a rip current. What do you do?
  **A:** Stay calm, don't fight it; swim parallel to the shore until out of the current, then angle back. If you can't, float and wave/yell for help.
- **Q:** What does reach-throw-don't-go mean?
  **A:** Help a struggling swimmer by reaching with a pole/towel or throwing a flotation device from safety, rather than entering the water.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Strokes
*Student:* Practice freestyle and backstroke technique (or hike stair intervals if no pool).

*How to teach:* Freestyle: horizontal body, flutter kick from hips, side breathing with body rotation. Backstroke: face up, ears in water, straight-arm recovery. (Alternative: stair intervals.)

- **Q:** How should you breathe in freestyle?
  **A:** Exhale underwater, then rotate the head with the body to the side to inhale, keeping one ear in the water.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Endurance
*Student:* Swim 20 minutes continuously or complete a long hike.

*How to teach:* Swim 20 minutes continuously (rest at wall as needed) or hike 60+ minutes. Record distance/HR.

- **Q:** Why is swimming a good lifetime activity?
  **A:** It's low-impact, works the whole body, builds aerobic fitness, and is a survival skill.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Survival skills
*Student:* Practice treading water and survival floating for time.

*How to teach:* Practice treading water (eggbeater or scissor kick, sculling hands) and survival floating (back float) for time, in deep water only if competent with a lifeguard.

- **Q:** Why is floating on your back useful in an emergency?
  **A:** It conserves energy while keeping your face out of the water, letting you breathe and wait for help.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Complete an outdoor pursuits quiz and log.

*How to teach:* Give the outdoor pursuits quiz and review the unit log.

- **Q:** Which way should a cyclist ride relative to traffic?
  **A:** In the same direction as traffic.

**Assessment:** Quiz and log.

**Answer key:** Outdoor pursuits quiz key: 1) Ten Essentials (any 8 correct). 2) Leave No Trace — pack out all trash. 3) Close contour lines = steep. 4) Back bearing of 45° = 225°. 5) ABC = Air, Brakes, Chain. 6) Ride with traffic. 7) Rip current: swim parallel to shore. 8) Reach-throw-don't-go. 9) Uphill hikers have right of way. Log: proficient = all unit activities logged with duration and intensity.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Human factors and ergonomics

**Prepare:** Gather a tape measure, several pairs of scissors, can openers and peelers of different quality, and a published anthropometric table (search 'hand anthropometry percentile table', e.g. from NASA or ergonomics texts). Recruit five people to measure.

**Watch for:**
- Designing for the 'average' person. Few people are average in every dimension; design for a range (often 5th to 95th percentile).
- Thinking percentiles mean percent of size. The 95th percentile means 95% of people are at or below that value.
- Assuming accessibility helps only people with disabilities. Universal design (curb cuts, large grips) benefits everyone.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ergonomics
*Student:* Compare well and poorly designed tools (scissors, can openers, peelers) and list ergonomic features.

*How to teach:* Compare good and poor tools: the student lists ergonomic features (cushioned grip, handle shape fitting the hand, neutral wrist angle, lower force needed).

- **Q:** What is ergonomics?
  **A:** Designing products, tools and workspaces to fit the human body and its abilities for comfort, safety and efficiency.
- **Q:** Name two ergonomic features of a good vegetable peeler.
  **A:** A thick, cushioned, non-slip handle and a shape that keeps the wrist straight (also low force needed).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Anthropometry
*Student:* Measure hand length, grip diameter, and reach for five people and compare to published percentile tables.

*How to teach:* Measure hand length, grip diameter (largest comfortable cylinder) and reach of five people; compare to a percentile table.

- **Q:** What does 'the 5th percentile' mean for hand length?
  **A:** 5% of the population has hands that short or shorter; 95% are longer.
- **Q:** Why don't five family members represent all users?
  **A:** The sample is tiny and not representative (similar ages and genetics); use published data from large studies.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Design for range
*Student:* Use 5th–95th percentile data to size a handle for a garden tool.

*How to teach:* Size a garden tool handle for the 5th–95th percentile range: length long enough for the largest hands, diameter small enough for the smallest grip.

- **Q:** Which percentile do you use to set a handle's minimum length? A door's minimum height?
  **A:** Large-user (e.g. 95th percentile) hand breadth for handle length; large-user (95th+ percentile) stature for door height — so big people fit.
- **Q:** Which percentile do you use to set maximum reach to a control?
  **A:** Small-user (5th percentile) reach, so short people can reach it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Accessibility
*Student:* Study universal design principles and evaluate a product for users with limited hand strength.

*How to teach:* Study the seven principles of universal design (equitable use, flexibility, simple and intuitive, perceptible information, tolerance for error, low physical effort, size and space for approach). Evaluate a product for users with limited hand strength.

- **Q:** Give one example of universal design that helps everyone.
  **A:** Curb cuts (help wheelchair users, strollers, rolling luggage); lever door handles instead of knobs.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Redesign a handle in CAD using anthropometric data and justify the dimensions.

*How to teach:* Redesign a handle in Onshape with dimensions justified by data; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Why would you make a handle larger in diameter and textured for someone with arthritis?
  **A:** A larger, non-slip grip requires less finger force and is easier to hold securely.

**Assessment:** Ergonomic redesign.

**Answer key:** Ergonomic redesign rubric: 4 = CAD handle with dimensions justified by data (e.g. length ≥ 95th-percentile male hand breadth so large hands fit; grip diameter in the roughly 30–40 mm range so 5th-percentile hands can close around it), notes on texture/shape for limited hand strength, and a comparison to the original; 3 = justified with one dimension unexplained; 2 = dimensions guessed; 1 = incomplete.
