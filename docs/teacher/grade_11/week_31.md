# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 31

## English 11 – American Literature: War and memory: The Things They Carried

**Prepare:** Tim O'Brien's The Things They Carried (library): the title story, 'On the Rainy River,' and 'How to Tell a True War Story'; also skim the short chapter 'Good Form.' Choose one interview from the Library of Congress Veterans History Project. Parent note: graphic war violence and strong profanity; preview.

**Watch for:**
- Assuming every event happened to the real Tim O'Brien. The book is fiction with a narrator who shares the author's name; O'Brien deliberately blurs fact and invention.
- Reading 'On the Rainy River' as a story about cowardice in combat. The narrator says going to war was his act of cowardice, because he was too embarrassed not to go.

### Day 1 (Monday): Title story
*Student:* Read 'The Things They Carried'; chart tangible vs. intangible burdens.

*How to teach:* Read the title story. The student charts tangible burdens (weights, weapons, letters) against intangible ones (fear, grief, love, shame) with quotations.

- **Q:** What does Lt. Jimmy Cross carry, and how does Ted Lavender's death change him?
  **A:** Letters and a pebble from Martha, a girl he loves; after Lavender is shot while Cross daydreams about her, he blames himself and burns her letters, resolving to be a stricter leader.
- **Q:** What does 'They carried all they could bear, and then some' suggest?
  **A:** The soldiers carried emotional weight—fear, grief, shame—that exceeded even their heavy physical loads.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rainy River
*Student:* Read 'On the Rainy River'; analyze the choice between courage and shame.

*How to teach:* Read 'On the Rainy River.' Discuss the scene in the boat with Elroy Berdahl near the Canadian shore. Today's task from the plan: Read 'On the Rainy River'; analyze the choice between courage and shame. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why does the narrator say 'I was a coward. I went to the war'?
  **A:** He could not bear the shame of being seen as a draft dodger, so he chose war over his convictions; giving in to embarrassment was his cowardice.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): True war story
*Student:* Read 'How to Tell a True War Story'; define story-truth vs. happening-truth.

*How to teach:* Read 'How to Tell a True War Story,' then 'Good Form.' Define the two kinds of truth with examples. Today's task from the plan: Read 'How to Tell a True War Story'; define story-truth vs. happening-truth. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What is the difference between story-truth and happening-truth?
  **A:** Happening-truth is what literally occurred; story-truth is an invented version that conveys the emotional reality more truly. O'Brien says 'story-truth is truer sometimes than happening-truth' (in 'Good Form').

### Day 4 (Thursday): Interviews
*Student:* Watch a veteran interview from the Library of Congress Veterans History Project; compare oral history to fiction.

*How to teach:* Watch a 15–20 minute veteran interview. The student compares how the veteran and O'Brien convey experience. Today's task from the plan: Watch a veteran interview from the Library of Congress Veterans History Project; compare oral history to fiction. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** How does an oral history differ from O'Brien's fiction?
  **A:** An oral history aims to record one person's actual memories as accurately as possible; O'Brien shapes and invents events to make readers feel the experience.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a 500-word response analyzing how O'Brien's structure blurs fact and fiction.

*How to teach:* The student writes a 500-word analysis of how O'Brien's structure blurs fact and fiction. Today's task from the plan: Write a 500-word response analyzing how O'Brien's structure blurs fact and fiction. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Name two techniques O'Brien uses to blur fact and fiction.
  **A:** A narrator named Tim O'Brien who comments on his own storytelling, and retelling the same event in different versions (e.g., Curt Lemon's death), so readers question what really happened.

**Assessment:** Analytical response.

**Answer key:** Analytical response rubric (4-point): 4 = clear thesis about how structure blurs fact and fiction; evidence from at least two stories (e.g., the narrator's direct addresses, repeated retellings, the story-truth idea); analysis of WHY O'Brien does this (to convey emotional truth); controlled conventions. 3 = sound thesis, less developed analysis. 2 = summary dominates. 1 = off-topic or incomplete.

## Algebra II: Data collection: surveys, experiments, and observational studies

**Prepare:** Two or three recent news articles that report on a study (health or education topics work well). A random number generator (random.org or a calculator).

**Watch for:**
- Concluding cause and effect from an observational study. Only a randomized experiment can support causation; observational studies show association.
- Thinking a larger sample fixes bias. A huge convenience sample is still biased; how you sample matters more than how many.

### Day 1 (Monday): Study types
*Student:* Classify 12 study descriptions as surveys, experiments, or observational studies.

*How to teach:* Define survey, experiment (treatment imposed), observational study (no treatment imposed). Student classifies 12 descriptions. Today's task from the plan: Classify 12 study descriptions as surveys, experiments, or observational studies. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** Researchers compare heart health of people who already drink coffee with those who don't. Which type?
  **A:** Observational study: no treatment was assigned.
- **Q:** Students are randomly assigned to study with or without music. Which type?
  **A:** Experiment: researchers impose the treatment with random assignment.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sampling methods
*Student:* Compare simple random, stratified, cluster, and convenience samples; identify bias.

*How to teach:* Model each sampling method with a school-of-1,000 example. Identify bias types (undercoverage, nonresponse, voluntary response, wording). Today's task from the plan: Compare simple random, stratified, cluster, and convenience samples; identify bias. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** How is stratified sampling different from cluster sampling?
  **A:** Stratified: divide into groups and randomly sample from EVERY group. Cluster: divide into groups, randomly pick some whole groups, and survey everyone in them.
- **Q:** An online poll on a sports site asks readers to vote. Main bias?
  **A:** Voluntary response bias: people with strong opinions choose to respond.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Experimental design
*Student:* Design a randomized experiment testing whether music affects reaction time.

*How to teach:* Student writes a design: question, treatments, random assignment method, control, replication, and what to measure. Today's task from the plan: Design a randomized experiment testing whether music affects reaction time. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** Why randomize in the music/reaction-time experiment?
  **A:** To balance unknown variables (sleep, skill) between groups so differences can be attributed to music.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Evaluating reports
*Student:* Critique two news articles reporting on data; write an evaluation.

*How to teach:* Student reads two articles and writes an evaluation: study type, sample, possible bias, and whether the headline's claim is justified. Today's task from the plan: Critique two news articles reporting on data; write an evaluation. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** A headline says 'Eating breakfast causes better grades' based on a survey. Is this justified?
  **A:** No. A survey is observational; confounding variables (family routines, income, sleep) could explain the association.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on study design and bias.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (about 30 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Quiz on study design and bias. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What is a confounding variable?
  **A:** A variable related to both the explanatory and response variables that makes it impossible to separate their effects.

**Assessment:** Study design quiz.

**Answer key:** Sample key: 1) classify 5 scenarios (survey/experiment/observational). 2) name sampling method in 4 scenarios (SRS, stratified, cluster, convenience). 3) identify bias in a mall survey: undercoverage/convenience. 4) design rubric: random assignment (1), control/comparison group (1), replication (1), clear response variable (1). 5) Explain why causation cannot be concluded from an observational study.

## Physics of the Universe: Evidence for the Big Bang

**Prepare:** A round balloon and a marker; a ruler; a short video or diagrams of galaxy spectra and the CMB map (NASA/ESA Planck).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Big Bang was an explosion into empty space from one point. Space itself expanded everywhere.
- Thinking galaxies are moving through space away from us at the center. Every observer sees others receding; there is no center.

### Day 1 (Monday): Expanding universe
*Student:* Draw dots on a balloon and inflate it to model expansion.

*How to teach:* Draw dots on a partly inflated balloon, measure distances between dots, inflate more, measure again. Today's task from the plan: Draw dots on a balloon and inflate it to model expansion. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Which dots moved apart fastest, and what does it model?
  **A:** The dots farthest apart moved apart most: in an expanding universe, more distant galaxies recede faster.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Redshift
*Student:* Analyze spectra showing redshift.

*How to teach:* Show a hydrogen spectrum from a lab lamp and the same lines in a distant galaxy shifted toward red. The student measures the shift and explains what it means about the galaxy's motion.

- **Q:** What is redshift and what does it show?
  **A:** Spectral lines shifted toward longer (redder) wavelengths, showing the galaxy is moving away from us.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hubble's law
*Student:* Graph galaxy velocity vs. distance and estimate the universe's age.

*How to teach:* Give a table of galaxy distances (Mpc) and recession velocities (km/s). The student graphs velocity vs distance, draws a best-fit line through the origin, and finds the slope (Hubble's constant), then estimates the universe's age as 1/H₀.

- **Q:** If H₀ ≈ 70 km/s/Mpc, how fast is a galaxy 100 Mpc away receding?
  **A:** v = H₀d = 7,000 km/s.
- **Q:** How does Hubble's constant estimate the age of the universe?
  **A:** Age ≈ 1/H₀ ≈ 14 billion years (current best value 13.8 billion years).

### Day 4 (Thursday): CMB
*Student:* Study the cosmic microwave background evidence.

*How to teach:* Show the Planck all-sky CMB map and explain it is the oldest light we can see. The student writes how the CMB was predicted, discovered accidentally in 1965 by Penzias and Wilson, and why its near-uniform 2.7 K temperature supports the Big Bang.

- **Q:** What is the cosmic microwave background?
  **A:** Leftover radiation from about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, now cooled to about 2.7 K and filling the whole sky.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a CER on the Big Bang.

*How to teach:* CER on the Big Bang. Today's task from the plan: Write a CER on the Big Bang. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Name three pieces of evidence for the Big Bang.
  **A:** Redshift of distant galaxies (Hubble's law), the CMB, and the observed abundance of light elements (~75% hydrogen, ~25% helium by mass).

**Assessment:** Big Bang CER.

**Answer key:** CER rubric (4 = exemplary, 3 = proficient, 2 = developing, 1 = beginning). Claim: answers the question about the evidence for the Big Bang in one sentence. Evidence: specific data from the student's own lab/simulation with numbers and units. Reasoning: connects the evidence to the scientific principle. A proficient response: redshift and Hubble's law (velocity proportional to distance), the uniform CMB at 2.7 K, and light-element abundances, explaining how each matches predictions of an expanding universe that began hot and dense.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Immigration and a changing population

**Prepare:** Print LBJ's remarks signing the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (Oct. 3, 1965, Statue of Liberty), Census data on immigrant origins before and after 1965, California demographic data 1970–2020 (non-Hispanic white about 77% in 1970 → about 35% in 2020; Hispanic about 40% in 2020; Asian about 15–16%; Black about 5–6%), IRCA (1986) and NAFTA (1994) overviews, and an oral history release form and recording device.

**Watch for:**
- Students think the 1965 Act was expected to change demographics dramatically. LBJ called it 'not a revolutionary bill'; ending national-origin quotas and emphasizing family reunification led to large immigration from Latin America and Asia.
- Students think IRCA only increased enforcement. It legalized about 2.7 million undocumented immigrants and created employer sanctions.
- Students think NAFTA's effects were all positive or all negative. Trade increased; some U.S. manufacturing jobs and many small Mexican farmers were hurt; migration pressures grew.

### Day 1 (Monday): 1965 Act
*Student:* Read LBJ's signing remarks and compare immigration sources before and after 1965 using Census data.

*How to teach:* Read LBJ's signing remarks and compare immigration sources before and after 1965 using Census data.

- **Q:** What did the 1965 Immigration Act end?
  **A:** The national-origins quota system from the 1920s.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): California demographics
*Student:* Graph California's changing demographics 1970–2020 and map Sunbelt migration.

*How to teach:* Graph California's demographics 1970–2020 and map Sunbelt migration.

- **Q:** What is the Sunbelt?
  **A:** Southern and Southwestern states that grew rapidly after WWII from migration, air conditioning, defense and new industries.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mexico relations
*Student:* Study IRCA (1986) and NAFTA (1994) and their effects on agriculture and border communities.

*How to teach:* Study IRCA and NAFTA and their effects on agriculture and border communities.

- **Q:** What two main things did IRCA (1986) do?
  **A:** Legalized about 2.7 million undocumented immigrants and penalized employers who knowingly hired unauthorized workers.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Oral history
*Student:* Interview a family member or neighbor about migration or moving, and record and transcribe 10 minutes.

*How to teach:* Interview a family member or neighbor about migration or moving; record and transcribe 10 minutes.

- **Q:** Turn 'Did you like moving?' into an open-ended question.
  **A:** 'What do you remember about your first months after moving, and what surprised you?'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a 400-word explanation of how immigration has reshaped California since 1965.

*How to teach:* The student writes a 400-word explanation of how immigration has reshaped California since 1965.

- **Q:** What was Prop 187 (1994)?
  **A:** A California ballot measure to deny public services to undocumented immigrants; it passed but was largely blocked by courts and never took effect.

**Assessment:** Oral history and explanation.

**Answer key:** Oral history: 10 minutes recorded and transcribed with consent; open-ended questions; context paragraph. Explanation rubric (400 words): 4 = explains how the 1965 Act ended national-origins quotas and favored family reunification and skills, with evidence of shifting sources (Europe → Latin America and Asia); uses California data (California became majority-minority around 1999–2000; Latino the largest group by 2014); IRCA, NAFTA and refugee flows (e.g., Southeast Asians after 1975); effects on culture, economy and politics (Prop 187, 1994); integrates the oral history. 3 = solid. 2 = general. 1 = inaccurate.

## Spanish IV: Unidad 7: ensayo literario

**Prepare:** Collect the unit's texts (Quiroga, Borges, Martí, Darío, Mistral, García Márquez, Lorca) and a comparative essay outline. Print a peer/parent revision checklist. Write the Unit 7 test (literary vocabulary, reported speech, past tenses, a short unseen passage or poem).

**Watch for:**
- Choosing texts with no real common ground: pick two that share a clear theme (death, power, confinement, identity, cruelty to outsiders).
- Dropping long quotations without explaining them: introduce, quote briefly, and explain how the quote supports the thesis.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tema
*Student:* Choose two Unit 7 texts that share a theme (e.g., death, power, identity).

*How to teach:* Your child chooses two texts sharing a theme and writes a working thesis.

- **Q:** Give a theme shared by 'El almohadón de plumas' and La casa de Bernarda Alba.
  **A:** Confinement and oppression in a cold, controlling household leading to death (Alicia's isolation; the daughters' imprisonment under Bernarda).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Evidencia
*Student:* Gather six quotations and explain each in Spanish.

*How to teach:* Your child gathers six quotations or specific references and explains each in Spanish.

- **Q:** How do you introduce a quotation in Spanish?
  **A:** Como dice el narrador, "..."; Según Bernarda, "..."; El poeta escribe: "..."

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Borrador
*Student:* Write a 400-word comparative literary essay.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a 400-word comparative literary essay.

- **Q:** Write a comparative thesis.
  **A:** Example: Tanto Quiroga como Lorca muestran cómo un hogar opresivo destruye a las mujeres, pero Quiroga usa el terror mientras Lorca usa el silencio y la tensión dramática.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revisión
*Student:* Revise with a peer/parent checklist for grammar and clarity.

*How to teach:* Revise with the peer/parent checklist (thesis, evidence, explanation, tense, agreement, accents).

- **Q:** Correct: 'En el cuento, Alicia se murió porque el animal la chupaba la sangre.' (literary present)

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Submit the final essay and take the Unit 7 test.

*How to teach:* Collect the final essay and give the Unit 7 test.

- **Q:** Convert to reported speech: 'No entiendo latín,' dijo el cura.
  **A:** El cura dijo que no entendía latín.

**Assessment:** Literary essay and Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Literary essay rubric (400 words): 4 = comparative thesis, organized by points of comparison, six brief quotations or specific references explained, literary vocabulary, accurate grammar in the literary present; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = summary-heavy or one text dominates; 1 = incomplete. Unit 7 test sample key: 1) el desenlace = ending/resolution; el ambiente = setting/atmosphere; el narrador = narrator; la estrofa = stanza 2) 'Vendré mañana,' dijo → Dijo que vendría al día siguiente 3) magical realism defined 4) Darío = modernismo; Mistral = Nobel 1945 5) passage items keyed to the text.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: The director's role and play selection

**Prepare:** Gather public-domain one-acts: Trifles (5 characters), Chekhov's The Proposal (3 characters), and an Oscar Wilde excerpt. Find an interview with a working director (e.g., from a theatre company website). Get a binder for the prompt book. If using a co-op, arrange audition time.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the director only tells actors where to stand. The director unifies interpretation, actors, and design.
- Choosing a play the available cast can't fill. Match cast size and audience.
- Forgetting royalties. Public-domain plays require no royalties; copyrighted plays need licenses even for small performances with an audience.

### Day 1 (Monday): The director
*Student:* Read about the history of the director and watch an interview with a working director.

*How to teach:* Read about the director's history (rise in the late 1800s with the Duke of Saxe-Meiningen and later Stanislavski) and watch an interview.

- **Q:** What does a director do?
  **A:** Interprets the script, sets the concept, casts, stages and coaches actors, and unifies all production elements.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Selection
*Student:* Read three public-domain one-acts and choose one for production, considering cast size and audience.

*How to teach:* Read three one-acts; choose one based on cast size, audience, and resources.

- **Q:** Why can a homeschool group perform Trifles without a license?
  **A:** It is in the public domain (published 1916), so no royalties are required.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Script analysis
*Student:* Analyze the chosen play's structure, characters, and themes in a prompt book.

*How to teach:* Prompt book: script analysis of structure, characters, themes, given circumstances.

- **Q:** What is a prompt book?
  **A:** The director's/stage manager's master script binder with analysis, blocking, cues, and notes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Concept
*Student:* Write a director's concept statement linking to the design concept from Unit 7.

*How to teach:* Director's concept statement linked to the Unit 7 design concept.

- **Q:** How should a director's concept relate to design?
  **A:** It provides the unifying interpretation that all design elements express.

### Day 5 (Friday): Casting
*Student:* Hold auditions with family/friends or a homeschool co-op and post a cast list.

*How to teach:* Hold auditions (cold reads from sides) and post a cast list.

- **Q:** What are 'sides'?
  **A:** Short excerpts from the script used for auditions.

**Assessment:** Prompt book check.

**Answer key:** Prompt book check. Proficient = script in binder with margins, analysis (structure, characters with objectives, themes), concept statement, cast list. 4 adds research on period/playwright and design references. 2 = analysis partial. 1 = missing.

## Lifetime Fitness: Sleep, recovery, and stress

**Prepare:** Prepare a sleep log (bedtime, wake time, quality 1–5). Find a short article on exercise and anxiety/mood (e.g., from the CDC or a major health organization). Gather a mat and foam roller if available.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking rest days are lazy. Adaptation happens during recovery.
- Believing more training is always better. Overtraining lowers performance and raises injury and illness risk.
- Making up lost sleep only on weekends. Consistency in bedtime and wake time matters most.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sleep log
*Student:* Track sleep for the week and research teen sleep recommendations; do a yoga session.

*How to teach:* Start the sleep log; review recommendations (8–10 hours for ages 13–18). Do a 20–30 minute yoga session.

- **Q:** How many hours of sleep do teens need?
  **A:** 8–10 hours per night (ages 13–18).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Overtraining
*Student:* Learn signs of overtraining and injury prevention; complete an active-recovery walk.

*How to teach:* Discuss overtraining signs (elevated resting HR, persistent fatigue/soreness, worsening performance, mood changes, frequent illness, poor sleep) and injury prevention. Active-recovery walk.

- **Q:** List three signs of overtraining.
  **A:** Rising resting heart rate, persistent fatigue or soreness, declining performance, irritability, trouble sleeping, frequent illness.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Stress & exercise
*Student:* Read research on exercise and anxiety; complete a 30-minute moderate cardio session and rate mood before/after.

*How to teach:* Read about exercise and anxiety; do 30 minutes of moderate cardio, rating mood before and after.

- **Q:** How does exercise help mood?
  **A:** It releases endorphins and other neurotransmitters, reduces stress hormones over time, improves sleep, and gives a sense of accomplishment.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Recovery strategies
*Student:* Practice foam rolling, stretching, and breathing; plan a weekly rest day.

*How to teach:* Practice foam rolling, stretching, and breathing. Plan a weekly rest/active-recovery day.

- **Q:** What is active recovery?
  **A:** Light, low-intensity activity (easy walk, swim, yoga) that promotes blood flow and recovery without adding stress.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a recovery plan based on your sleep log.

*How to teach:* Student writes a recovery plan based on the sleep log (bedtime target, wind-down routine, rest day, signs to watch).

- **Q:** What is one sleep-hygiene habit?
  **A:** Consistent sleep schedule, no screens 30–60 min before bed, cool dark quiet room, limiting caffeine after midday.

**Assessment:** Recovery plan.

**Answer key:** Recovery plan rubric. 4 – Uses the 7-day sleep log data (average hours), sets a bedtime meeting 8–10 h, specific sleep hygiene steps, weekly rest and active-recovery days, overtraining warning signs and responses, and stress strategies. 3 (proficient) – Data-based sleep target, hygiene steps, rest day, warning signs. 2 – General plan not tied to data. 1 – Incomplete.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Capstone: CAD and drawings

**Prepare:** Ensure Onshape access; review assembly and drawing tutorials. Help the student find supplier sites (hardware store, McMaster-Carr, Amazon) for BOM pricing.

**Watch for:**
- Modeling purchased parts in detail. Simplify standard parts (screws, hinges) or use Onshape's standard content.
- No clearance between mating parts. Add clearance for printed or hand-made parts (often 0.2–0.5 mm for 3D prints).
- BOM without quantities or sources. Every line needs item, quantity, material/part number, source and cost.

### Day 1 (Monday): Part modeling
*Student:* Model all custom parts in Onshape.

*How to teach:* Model all custom parts.

- **Q:** What clearance might you add between a 3D-printed pin and hole?
  **A:** Roughly 0.2–0.5 mm, depending on the printer — test with a small sample.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Assembly
*Student:* Assemble parts with mates and check for interference.

*How to teach:* Assemble with mates and check interference.

- **Q:** What does an interference check reveal?
  **A:** Places where parts overlap and couldn't physically fit together.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Drawings
*Student:* Create dimensioned working drawings for each part.

*How to teach:* Create dimensioned working drawings for each custom part.

- **Q:** What must every working drawing include?
  **A:** Necessary views, all dimensions, tolerances/notes and a completed title block.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Bill of materials
*Student:* Create a bill of materials with sourcing and cost.

*How to teach:* Create a BOM with sourcing and cost.

- **Q:** Three hinges at $2.49 each and 12 screws at $0.15 each. Total?
  **A:** $7.47 + $1.80 = $9.27.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Submit CAD files, drawings, and BOM for design review.

*How to teach:* Hold a design review; score with the checklist.

- **Q:** What is a design review?
  **A:** A formal check of a design by others to catch errors and risks before building.

**Assessment:** Design review.

**Answer key:** Design review checklist: 4 = all custom parts modeled with constrained sketches; assembly with appropriate mates and no interference; dimensioned working drawing for each custom part with title block; BOM with item numbers, quantities, materials, sources, unit and total costs within the budget constraint; 3 = minor omissions; 2 = missing drawings or BOM; 1 = incomplete. During the review, ask the student to explain one design decision and one risk.
