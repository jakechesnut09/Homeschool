# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 26

## English 11 – American Literature: Research: revision and presentation

**Prepare:** Word processor; slide software. Print the MLA sample paper from Purdue OWL for format comparison.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking revision means fixing commas. Revision means rethinking structure and argument; editing (commas, spelling) comes last.
- Overloading slides with paragraphs. Slides support the speaker; one idea and a visual per slide.

### Day 1 (Monday): Revision for argument
*Student:* Reverse-outline the draft; revise so each paragraph advances the thesis.

*How to teach:* Model a reverse outline: one sentence summarizing what each paragraph DOES. The student checks each against the thesis and moves, cuts, or rewrites. Today's task from the plan: Reverse-outline the draft; revise so each paragraph advances the thesis. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What is a reverse outline used for?
  **A:** To see the draft's actual structure, spot paragraphs that don't advance the thesis, and reorder for logic.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Line editing
*Student:* Edit for concision, active voice, and varied syntax; check hyphenation of compound modifiers.

*How to teach:* Line-edit two paragraphs together for wordiness and passive voice. Teach compound-modifier hyphens. Today's task from the plan: Edit for concision, active voice, and varied syntax; check hyphenation of compound modifiers. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Correct the hyphenation: 'a well known author' and 'the author is well-known.'
  **A:** 'A well-known author' (hyphenated before the noun); 'the author is well known' (no hyphen after the noun).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Format
*Student:* Format to MLA 9 with Works Cited; run a self-check for plagiarism and citation accuracy.

*How to teach:* Format to MLA 9 (header, title, double spacing, Works Cited). The student checks every quotation against its source. Today's task from the plan: Format to MLA 9 with Works Cited; run a self-check for plagiarism and citation accuracy. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Format an MLA in-text citation for page 180 of Gatsby.
  **A:** (Fitzgerald 180) — author's last name and page, no comma, inside the sentence's period.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Presentation
*Student:* Create a 6-minute presentation of findings with visuals and practice delivery.

*How to teach:* The student builds a 6-minute presentation (6–8 slides) and practices twice with a timer. Today's task from the plan: Create a 6-minute presentation of findings with visuals and practice delivery. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What makes slides effective?
  **A:** Few words, one idea per slide, readable fonts, and relevant images or data with sources.

### Day 5 (Friday): Final & present
*Student:* Submit final research paper and deliver the presentation with Q&A.

*How to teach:* Collect the paper; the student presents and takes questions. Today's task from the plan: Submit final research paper and deliver the presentation with Q&A. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** How should a presenter handle a question they can't answer?
  **A:** Acknowledge it honestly, offer what they do know, and explain how they would find the answer.

**Assessment:** Final research paper and presentation rubrics.

**Answer key:** Final paper rubric (4-point per row): Thesis (arguable, specific); Evidence (credible, cited, integrated); Analysis (explains significance, connects to literary text); Organization (logical, transitions); MLA format and conventions. Proficient = clear thesis supported by at least five credible sources with accurate citations. Presentation rubric: content accuracy, visuals, delivery (eye contact, pace), and Q&A.

## Algebra II: Graphing sine and cosine

**Prepare:** Desmos in radian mode. Spaghetti or string optional to 'unwrap' the unit circle.

**Watch for:**
- Computing period as b instead of 2π/b. Larger b means MORE cycles, so a SHORTER period.
- Confusing amplitude with the maximum value. Amplitude = (max − min)/2; midline = (max + min)/2.

### Day 1 (Monday): Unwrapping the circle
*Student:* Plot unit-circle y-values against angle to create the sine graph.

*How to teach:* Plot y-values of the unit circle at each special angle against the angle; connect into the sine curve. Repeat for cosine. Today's task from the plan: Plot unit-circle y-values against angle to create the sine graph. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Where are the zeros and maximum of y = sin x on [0, 2π]?
  **A:** Zeros at 0, π, 2π; maximum 1 at π/2; minimum −1 at 3π/2.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Amplitude & midline
*Student:* Graph y = a sin x + d and interpret amplitude and midline.

*How to teach:* Graph y = a sin x + d with sliders. Today's task from the plan: Graph y = a sin x + d and interpret amplitude and midline. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Give the amplitude, midline, max, and min of y = 3 sin x + 2.
  **A:** Amplitude 3, midline y = 2, maximum 5, minimum −1.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Period
*Student:* Graph y = sin(bx) and derive period = 2π/b.

*How to teach:* Graph y = sin(bx) for b = 1, 2, 1/2 and derive period = 2π/b. Today's task from the plan: Graph y = sin(bx) and derive period = 2π/b. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Find the periods of y = sin(4x) and y = cos(x/2).
  **A:** 2π/4 = π/2 and 2π/(1/2) = 4π.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Phase shift
*Student:* Graph y = a sin(b(x−h)) + d and write equations from graphs.

*How to teach:* Model reading max, min, period, and a starting point from a graph to write an equation. Today's task from the plan: Graph y = a sin(b(x−h)) + d and write equations from graphs. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** A cosine graph has maximum 7 at x = 0, minimum 1, and period 8. Write it.
  **A:** Amplitude 3, midline 4, b = 2π/8 = π/4: y = 3cos(πx/4) + 4.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: graph two sinusoids and write two equations from graphs.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (about 30 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Quiz: graph two sinusoids and write two equations from graphs. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What is the phase shift of y = 2 sin(3(x − π/6))?
  **A:** π/6 to the right.

**Assessment:** Sinusoid quiz.

**Answer key:** Sample key: Graph 1: y = 2 sin x − 1 (amp 2, midline −1, period 2π). Graph 2: y = cos(2x) (period π). Equation from graph with max 3, min −3, period π starting at max at x=0: y = 3cos(2x). Equation with midline 10, amplitude 5, period 24, rising through midline at x=0: y = 5 sin(πx/12) + 10. Rubric 4 pts each: amplitude, midline, period, phase/start correct.

## Physics of the Universe: Natural hazards and human activity

**Prepare:** USGS real-time earthquake map; a tray, sand, water, a heavy object; toothpicks and mini marshmallows; a baking pan as a shake table.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking earthquakes happen randomly. They cluster along plate boundaries.
- Thinking taller buildings always fail first. Design and soil matter more.

### Day 1 (Monday): Earthquakes
*Student:* Explore the USGS real-time earthquake map and identify patterns.

*How to teach:* Open the USGS 'Latest Earthquakes' map and set it to 30 days, magnitude 2.5+. The student describes the global pattern, then zooms into California and notes where quakes cluster.

- **Q:** Where do most earthquakes occur?
  **A:** Along plate boundaries, especially the Pacific 'Ring of Fire.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Liquefaction
*Student:* Model liquefaction with sand, water, and a weight.

*How to teach:* Fill a tray with sand, add water until just saturated, and set a heavy object (a brick or full can) on top. Tap or shake the tray and watch the object sink or tilt as the sand behaves like a liquid.

- **Q:** What is liquefaction?
  **A:** Shaking makes water-saturated sand lose strength and act like a liquid, so buildings sink or tilt.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Building design
*Student:* Build a toothpick/marshmallow structure and test it on a shake table (tray).

*How to teach:* The student builds a toothpick-and-marshmallow tower (at least 20 cm tall) and tests it on a baking pan resting on marbles or rubber balls shaken by hand for 10 seconds; redesign once with cross-bracing.

- **Q:** What design features improve earthquake resistance?
  **A:** Cross-bracing (triangles), wide base, low center of mass, flexible connections.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Hazards & society
*Student:* Research how earthquakes shaped California building codes.

*How to teach:* The student researches two major California earthquakes and the laws or code changes they triggered, then writes a short paragraph on how science shaped public policy.

- **Q:** Name a California earthquake that led to building code changes.
  **A:** 1933 Long Beach (Field Act for schools); 1971 San Fernando (Alquist-Priolo Act); 1994 Northridge (steel frame updates).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 6 test on Earth's interior and plate tectonics.

*How to teach:* Unit 6 test. Today's task from the plan: Unit 6 test on Earth's interior and plate tectonics. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Why did the Marina District suffer heavy damage in 1989?
  **A:** It was built on loose landfill that liquefied during the Loma Prieta earthquake.

**Assessment:** Shake table test and Unit 6 test.

**Answer key:** Shake table rubric: structure survives test, design explained with physics. Unit 6 test key: Earth's layers; S-wave shadow zone; convection; seafloor spreading; boundary types; San Andreas transform; liquefaction.

## Lifetime Fitness: Self-defense basics

**Prepare:** Gather a mat and a pillow or kick pad. Preview the techniques. Parental note: self-defense discussion may raise concerns about personal safety; keep it calm and empowering. Practice escapes slowly and gently with a trusted partner.

**Watch for:**
- Believing strength is required. Technique and leverage (attacking the weak point, e.g., the thumb side of a grip) matter more.
- Thinking self-defense is mainly fighting. Awareness, avoidance, and de-escalation come first; escaping and getting help is the goal.
- Practicing at full speed. Use slow, controlled practice to avoid injury.

### Day 1 (Monday): Awareness
*Student:* Learn situational awareness and verbal de-escalation strategies; role-play setting boundaries.

*How to teach:* Discuss situational awareness (head up, phone away, trust instincts) and de-escalation (calm voice, space, open hands). Role-play setting a firm boundary: 'Back off. I don't want to talk.'

- **Q:** What is the primary goal of self-defense?
  **A:** To stay safe — avoid danger, de-escalate, escape, and get help.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Stance & strikes
*Student:* Practice a balanced stance, palm-heel strike, and front kick on a pillow or pad.

*How to teach:* Teach a balanced fighting stance (feet staggered shoulder-width, hands up), palm-heel strike, and front kick into a pillow held by a partner.

- **Q:** Why use a palm-heel strike rather than a closed fist?
  **A:** It is less likely to injure your own hand and is easier to deliver with force.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Escapes
*Student:* Practice wrist-grab and bear-hug escapes slowly with a partner, focusing on leverage over strength.

*How to teach:* Wrist-grab escape: rotate the wrist toward the attacker's thumb (weakest point) and pull out sharply. Bear-hug escape: drop weight low, create space, strike/step to escape. Slow practice only.

- **Q:** Which direction do you pull to escape a one-hand wrist grab?
  **A:** Toward the gap between the grabber's thumb and fingers — the thumb side, the weakest point of the grip.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Conditioning combos
*Student:* Complete 6 rounds of 2-minute strike combinations on a pad as a cardio workout.

*How to teach:* Six rounds of 2-minute strike combos on the pad (palm strike, knee, front kick) with 1-minute rest.

- **Q:** What fitness components does this pad workout train?
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular endurance, power, and coordination.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Demonstrate techniques safely and complete a quiz on dance and group fitness concepts.

*How to teach:* Student demonstrates techniques safely, then takes the unit quiz on dance and group fitness.

- **Q:** How many beats are in a standard dance phrase count?
  **A:** 8 counts.

**Assessment:** Technique demonstration and quiz.

**Answer key:** Technique demonstration (proficient): balanced stance with hands up; palm-heel strike with hip rotation; front kick with chamber; correct wrist-grab escape toward thumb; safe, controlled partner practice; verbal boundary statement. Quiz key: 1) Music counted in 8s. 2) Salsa pauses on 4 and 8. 3) Cueing types: verbal, visual, tactile. 4) Modifications let all levels participate safely. 5) 40/20 circuit = 40 s work, 20 s rest. 6) Self-defense priority: awareness/avoid/escape.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Engineering ethics and safety

**Prepare:** Print or open the NSPE Code of Ethics (nspe.org). Find a reliable account of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse (1940; there is famous film footage) and the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster (1986), e.g. the Rogers Commission summary or a university engineering-ethics case study. Parental note: Challenger involves the deaths of seven astronauts, including teacher Christa McAuliffe.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Tacoma Narrows was simple resonance from steady wind. Engineers now explain it as aeroelastic flutter — wind and the twisting deck interacted so motion grew — made possible by a very slender, flexible deck with solid plate-girder sides.
- Blaming Challenger only on a 'broken part'. The O-ring problem was known; the deeper failure was a decision process that overruled engineers' safety concerns.
- Thinking a factor of safety means wasted material. It accounts for uncertainty in loads, materials and manufacturing.

### Day 1 (Monday): Code of ethics
*Student:* Read the NSPE Code of Ethics and summarize its fundamental canons.

*How to teach:* Read the NSPE Code; summarize the six fundamental canons in the student's own words.

- **Q:** What is the first fundamental canon of the NSPE Code of Ethics?
  **A:** Engineers shall hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public.
- **Q:** Name two other canons.
  **A:** Perform services only in areas of competence; issue public statements only in an objective and truthful manner; act as faithful agents or trustees for employers/clients; avoid deceptive acts; conduct themselves honorably, responsibly, ethically and lawfully.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Case study 1
*Student:* Analyze the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse and identify the design failure.

*How to teach:* Watch the 1940 footage and read about the collapse. Have the student explain the design failure and lessons learned (wind-tunnel testing of bridge decks, aerodynamic deck shapes, stiffening trusses).

- **Q:** Why did the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse?
  **A:** Its deck was extremely slender and flexible with solid plate-girder sides; moderate wind (about 40 mph) caused aeroelastic flutter — twisting oscillations that grew until the deck failed.
- **Q:** What did engineers change afterward?
  **A:** Bridges were designed with stiffer, more aerodynamic decks (open trusses, streamlined shapes) and tested in wind tunnels.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Case study 2
*Student:* Analyze the Challenger disaster and the engineers' ethical dilemma.

*How to teach:* Read about Challenger (January 28, 1986). Discuss the O-ring problem, the cold launch temperature, the engineers' warning and the decision to launch.

- **Q:** What was the technical cause of the Challenger explosion?
  **A:** An O-ring seal in a solid rocket booster joint failed in the cold, letting hot gas escape, which led to the breakup 73 seconds after launch.
- **Q:** What was the ethical dilemma?
  **A:** Engineers warned it was unsafe to launch in such cold temperatures, but managers overruled them under schedule pressure — raising the duty to put public safety first and to speak up.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Factor of safety
*Student:* Calculate factors of safety for a shelf bracket and discuss why they matter.

*How to teach:* Teach FOS = failure load ÷ working (design) load. Calculate for a shelf bracket.

- **Q:** A bracket fails at 1,200 N and must carry 400 N. What is the factor of safety?
  **A:** 1,200 ÷ 400 = 3.
- **Q:** If a required FOS is 4 and the working load is 250 N, what failure load must the bracket withstand?
  **A:** At least 1,000 N.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a 500-word ethics case analysis.

*How to teach:* The student writes a 500-word ethics case analysis; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Which NSPE canon is most relevant to Challenger, and why?
  **A:** Holding public safety paramount — the engineers' safety concern should have outweighed schedule pressure.

**Assessment:** Ethics case analysis.

**Answer key:** Ethics case analysis rubric (500 words, 4 levels): 4 = accurate facts of the case; identifies the technical cause (e.g. Challenger: cold temperatures made the rubber O-rings in the solid rocket booster joints less resilient so hot gas escaped); identifies the ethical dilemma (Morton Thiokol engineers, including Roger Boisjoly, recommended not launching below 53°F; managers reversed the recommendation under schedule pressure); applies at least two NSPE canons (hold paramount public safety; issue truthful statements; act as faithful agents while not compromising safety); states what should have been done; 3 = accurate, ethics less developed; 2 = factual errors or no canons; 1 = incomplete. Factor-of-safety key: FOS = failure load ÷ working load; bracket failing at 900 N carrying 300 N → FOS = 3.
