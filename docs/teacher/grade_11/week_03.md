# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 3

## English 11 – American Literature: The Crucible, Acts I–II

**Prepare:** Arthur Miller's The Crucible (library). Short readings: an encyclopedia article on the 1692 Salem witch trials and one on HUAC/McCarthyism. Parent note: the play involves an adulterous affair (discussed, not shown).

**Watch for:**
- Treating the play as an accurate history of Salem. Miller changed facts (e.g., Abigail was about 11 in reality, he made her 17 and invented the affair) to explore 1950s fears; check his note 'A Note on the Historical Accuracy of This Play.'
- Missing that characters act from motives beyond fear of witches. Have the student track land, reputation, and revenge (Putnam wants land; Parris fears for his position; Abigail wants Elizabeth gone).

### Day 1 (Monday): Context
*Student:* Read a short article on the Salem trials of 1692 and one on the 1950s House Un-American Activities Committee; list parallels.

*How to teach:* Read both short articles. The student lists parallels in a T-chart: accusations without evidence, pressure to name others, confession rewarded, reputations ruined.

- **Q:** Name two parallels Miller saw between Salem and the 1950s HUAC hearings.
  **A:** In both, people were pressured to confess and name others to save themselves, and accusations were treated as proof (guilt by association), destroying reputations.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Act I
*Student:* Read Act I aloud, taking several roles; track each character's motives (Abigail, Parris, Putnam, Proctor) in a chart.

*How to teach:* Read Act I aloud, each of you taking several roles. The student fills a motive chart for Abigail, Parris, Putnam, and Proctor. Today's task from the plan: Read Act I aloud, taking several roles; track each character's motives (Abigail, Parris, Putnam, Proctor) in a chart. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What does Abigail want in Act I?
  **A:** To avoid punishment for the forest dancing and to get John Proctor back; she resents Elizabeth Proctor, who dismissed her.
- **Q:** Why is Parris so worried about the rumors?
  **A:** He fears that witchcraft in his own house will cost him his reputation and his position as minister.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Act I analysis
*Student:* Write a paragraph on how Miller's stage directions shape Proctor before he speaks.

*How to teach:* Reread Miller's stage-direction introduction of John Proctor. Model one sentence of analysis, then the student writes a paragraph on how the description (powerful, even-tempered, a sinner against his own sense of decency) prepares us for his conflict.

- **Q:** What key trait does Miller give Proctor in the stage directions?
  **A:** He is respected and strong but sees himself as a sinner (a 'fraud') because of his affair, so he carries private guilt.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Act II
*Student:* Read Act II; identify three moments of dramatic irony and explain the poppet's role in the plot.

*How to teach:* Read Act II. Define dramatic irony (the audience knows something a character does not). The student finds three moments and explains the poppet. Today's task from the plan: Read Act II; identify three moments of dramatic irony and explain the poppet's role in the plot. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What role does the poppet play in the plot?
  **A:** Mary Warren made it in court and gave it to Elizabeth; Abigail stabs herself with a needle and claims Elizabeth's spirit did it, so the needle in the poppet becomes 'evidence' to arrest Elizabeth.
- **Q:** Give one example of dramatic irony in Act II.
  **A:** Cheever and Herrick treat the needle as proof of witchcraft, but the audience knows Mary Warren put it there and Abigail is faking.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Reading quiz on Acts I–II and a one-paragraph prediction defended with evidence.

*How to teach:* Give a 10-question reading quiz, then the student writes a prediction paragraph for Act III supported by two quotations. Today's task from the plan: Reading quiz on Acts I–II and a one-paragraph prediction defended with evidence. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** At the end of Act II, what does Proctor insist Mary Warren must do?
  **A:** Testify in court that the poppet was hers and that Abigail's accusations are false.

**Assessment:** Reading quiz and characterization paragraph.

**Answer key:** Reading quiz key (10 items): 1) Salem, Massachusetts, 1692. 2) Betty Parris is the girl who won't wake. 3) The girls were dancing in the forest with Tituba. 4) Abigail had an affair with John Proctor. 5) Elizabeth fired Abigail. 6) Thomas Putnam is motivated by land and grudges. 7) Reverend Hale is the 'expert' on witchcraft. 8) When Hale tests Proctor, he can't recall the commandment against adultery (Elizabeth supplies it). 9) Mary Warren gives Elizabeth the poppet. 10) Elizabeth is arrested. Characterization paragraph rubric (4-point): topic sentence about Proctor's trait; two stage-direction or dialogue quotations; explanation of how the description creates his conflict; conventions.

## Algebra II: Systems of equations and inequalities

**Prepare:** Graph paper and Desmos. Prepare a ticket problem and a bakery profit problem (below). Review elimination yourself with one 3×3 example.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting to multiply every term (including the constant) when scaling an equation for elimination. Have the student draw arrows to each term as they multiply.
- Assuming the maximum profit is at the 'middle' of the feasible region. The optimum of a linear objective always occurs at a vertex; have the student test every corner in a table.

### Day 1 (Monday): 2×2 systems review
*Student:* Solve systems by graphing, substitution, and elimination; classify one, none, or infinite solutions.

*How to teach:* Solve x + y = 10, x − y = 4 three ways (graph, substitution, elimination) and compare. Then show a parallel system and an identical-line system. Student solves and classifies six systems.

- **Q:** Solve x + y = 10 and x − y = 4.
  **A:** Add: 2x = 14, x = 7, y = 3. Solution (7, 3).
- **Q:** How many solutions does 2x + 4y = 8, x + 2y = 4 have?
  **A:** Infinitely many: the first equation is 2 times the second (same line).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): 3×3 systems
*Student:* Solve three-variable systems by elimination; model a ticket-sales problem with three prices.

*How to teach:* Model eliminating one variable from two different pairs to get a 2×2 system, then back-substitute. Student solves the ticket problem. Today's task from the plan: Solve three-variable systems by elimination; model a ticket-sales problem with three prices. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Solve x + y + z = 6, x − y + z = 2, 2x + y − z = 1.
  **A:** Subtract eq 2 from eq 1: 2y = 4, y = 2. Then x + z = 4 and 2x − z = −1; add: 3x = 3, x = 1, z = 3. (1, 2, 3).
- **Q:** 100 tickets sold for $720: adult $10, student $6, child $4, with as many adult as student tickets. How many of each?
  **A:** a = s, 2s + c = 100, 16s + 4c = 720 → s = 40, a = 40, c = 20. Check: 400 + 240 + 80 = 720.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Systems of inequalities
*Student:* Graph a feasible region for a budget-and-time constraint problem.

*How to teach:* Model graphing each inequality, shading, and testing (0, 0). Student writes constraints for a budget-and-time problem and shades the feasible region. Today's task from the plan: Graph a feasible region for a budget-and-time constraint problem. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** With x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0, x + y ≤ 8, 2x + y ≤ 12, is (3, 4) feasible? Is (5, 4)?
  **A:** (3, 4): 7 ≤ 8 and 10 ≤ 12, yes. (5, 4): 9 > 8, no.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Linear programming
*Student:* Find the maximum profit at the vertices of a feasible region for a bakery problem.

*How to teach:* Bakery: x cakes, y pies, constraints from yesterday, profit P = 5x + 3y. Model finding vertices by solving pairs of boundary lines, then evaluate P at each in a table.

- **Q:** Find the vertices and maximum profit.
  **A:** Vertices (0,0), (6,0), (4,4), (0,8). P = 0, 30, 32, 24. Maximum $32 at 4 cakes and 4 pies.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 1 test: transformations, piecewise functions, and systems.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 test (about 50 minutes). Allow graph paper; no Desmos except for the final check after submitting. Today's task from the plan: Unit 1 test: transformations, piecewise functions, and systems. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Solve 3x + 2y = 16 and x − y = 2.
  **A:** x = y + 2 → 3y + 6 + 2y = 16 → y = 2, x = 4. (4, 2).

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test key (sample items): 1) y=−(x+2)²+1: reflect, left 2, up 1. 2) Write rule: |x| right 3 down 4 → y=|x−3|−4. 3) f(x)={−x if x<0; √x if x≥0}: f(−4)=4, f(9)=3. 4) |x−6|=2 → 4, 8. 5) |2x−1|>7 → x>4 or x<−3. 6) 2x+3y=12, x−3y=−3 → x=3, y=2. 7) x+y+z=4, 2x−y+z=6, x+2y−z=0 → (2, 0, 2). 8) Linear programming with P=4x+5y on vertices (0,0),(6,0),(3,4),(0,5): max 32 at (3,4). Grading: 4 = all correct with work; 3 = minor arithmetic slips; 2 = method right but several errors; 1 = method errors.

## Physics of the Universe: Newton's third law and friction

**Prepare:** Balloon, straw, 5 m of string, tape; a luggage scale (or spring scale reading in newtons; if it reads in kg, multiply by 9.8 to get N); a shoe; surfaces (tile, carpet, wood).

**Watch for:**
- Believing action-reaction forces cancel. They act on DIFFERENT objects, so they can't cancel; only forces on the same object add up.
- Thinking the normal force always equals weight. It changes on inclines (mg cos θ) and in accelerating elevators.

### Day 1 (Monday): Third law
*Student:* Build a balloon rocket on a string; identify force pairs.

*How to teach:* Thread the string through the straw, tape on the inflated balloon, and release. The student identifies the force pair (balloon pushes air backward; air pushes balloon forward).

- **Q:** When you push against a wall, what is the reaction force?
  **A:** The wall pushes back on you with an equal and opposite force.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Friction lab
*Student:* Drag a shoe across different surfaces with a luggage scale; calculate coefficient of friction.

*How to teach:* Drag the shoe at constant speed with the luggage scale on each surface. μ = friction force ÷ normal force (the shoe's weight in N). Today's task from the plan: Drag a shoe across different surfaces with a luggage scale; calculate coefficient of friction. Before the student writes conclusions, check that the data table has units and repeated trials, and ask them to explain one result in their own words.

- **Q:** A 1.0 kg shoe (weight 9.8 N) needs 3.0 N to drag at constant speed. What is μ?
  **A:** μ = 3.0/9.8 ≈ 0.31.
- **Q:** Why drag at constant speed?
  **A:** Then net force is zero, so the scale reading equals the friction force.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Normal force
*Student:* Solve problems on inclines and elevators.

*How to teach:* Model an incline (components) and an elevator problem. Today's task from the plan: Solve problems on inclines and elevators. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** A 60 kg person is in an elevator accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s². What does a scale read?
  **A:** N = m(g + a) = 60(11.8) = 708 N (they feel heavier).
- **Q:** A 10 kg box sits on a 30° incline. What is the normal force?
  **A:** N = mg cos 30° = 98 × 0.866 ≈ 85 N.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Applications
*Student:* Analyze how tires and brakes use friction; discuss stopping distance in rain.

*How to teach:* Discuss tire tread, anti-lock brakes, and wet roads; compute how stopping distance changes when μ falls. Today's task from the plan: Analyze how tires and brakes use friction; discuss stopping distance in rain. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why are stopping distances longer in rain?
  **A:** Water lowers the coefficient of friction between tires and road, so the maximum braking force (μN) is smaller, giving less deceleration and a longer stopping distance.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on Newton's laws problems.

*How to teach:* Give the Newton's laws quiz (30 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Quiz on Newton's laws problems. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** A 1,200 kg car accelerates at 2.5 m/s². What net force acts on it?
  **A:** F = ma = 1,200 × 2.5 = 3,000 N.

**Assessment:** Newton's laws quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key (sample): 1) First law: objects keep their state of motion without net force. 2) 5 kg, 20 N net → 4 m/s². 3) Force pair example: foot pushes ground backward, ground pushes foot forward. 4) μ from 12 N friction on a 40 N box = 0.30. 5) 50 kg person in elevator accelerating down at 2 m/s² → N = 50(7.8) = 390 N. 6) Free-body diagram of hanging lamp: tension up = weight down. 7) Why action-reaction pairs don't cancel (different objects).

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Civil War, Reconstruction, and the Reconstruction Amendments

**Prepare:** Print the 13th, 14th and 15th Amendments; Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857) excerpts (Chief Justice Taney); Library of Congress photos of Freedmen's Bureau schools; a summary of the Compromise of 1877; examples of Black Codes (Mississippi 1865). Parental note: Taney's opinion contains racist language; discuss its historical context. Build the Unit 1 test with a short DBQ (6 documents) on the meaning of freedom.

**Watch for:**
- Students think Reconstruction failed completely. It brought real gains — Black voting and officeholding, public schools, the 14th and 15th Amendments — before being rolled back.
- Students think the 13th Amendment ended all forced labor. It allows involuntary servitude 'as a punishment for crime', a loophole exploited by convict leasing.
- Students think Black Codes came only after 1877. Southern states passed Black Codes in 1865–66; after 1877, Jim Crow laws and disfranchisement spread.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dred Scott to war
*Student:* Read excerpts of the Dred Scott decision and explain how it intensified sectional conflict.

*How to teach:* Read Dred Scott excerpts and explain how the decision intensified sectional conflict leading to war.

- **Q:** Why did Dred Scott enrage Northerners?
  **A:** It said Congress could not ban slavery in any territory, opening the West to slavery and invalidating the Missouri Compromise; it also denied Black citizenship.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Amendments
*Student:* Analyze the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments and their promises.

*How to teach:* Analyze the 13th, 14th and 15th Amendments clause by clause.

- **Q:** What does the Equal Protection Clause say?
  **A:** No state shall 'deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.'
- **Q:** Which amendment overturned the Dred Scott citizenship ruling?
  **A:** The 14th Amendment: all persons born or naturalized in the U.S. are citizens.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Reconstruction
*Student:* Examine photos and letters from Freedmen's Bureau schools; discuss gains of Reconstruction.

*How to teach:* Examine Freedmen's Bureau school photos and letters; list gains of Reconstruction.

- **Q:** Name two achievements of Reconstruction.
  **A:** Public schools and Black colleges founded; Black men voting and holding office (e.g., Hiram Revels in the Senate); the Reconstruction Amendments.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Retreat
*Student:* Study the Compromise of 1877 and rise of Black Codes and sharecropping.

*How to teach:* Study the Compromise of 1877, Black Codes and sharecropping; build a cause-effect chain.

- **Q:** What did the Compromise of 1877 do?
  **A:** Settled the disputed 1876 election by making Rutherford B. Hayes president in exchange for withdrawing federal troops from the South, ending Reconstruction.
- **Q:** Why did sharecropping trap families?
  **A:** Tenants owed a share of the crop plus debts for seed and supplies on credit at high interest, so they rarely got ahead.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 1 test with a short DBQ on the meaning of freedom after the Civil War.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 test with a short DBQ.

- **Q:** What is the main loophole in the 13th Amendment?
  **A:** 'Except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted.'

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test key (sample): 1) Locke's natural rights; 2) separation of powers (Montesquieu); 3) Federalist No. 10 argument; 4) First Amendment religion clauses; 5) Dred Scott (1857) — Black Americans were not citizens; Congress could not ban slavery in the territories; Missouri Compromise unconstitutional. 6) 13th (1865) — abolished slavery except as punishment for crime. 7) 14th (1868) — birthright citizenship, due process, equal protection. 8) 15th (1870) — vote can't be denied by race. 9) Freedmen's Bureau (1865) — schools, labor contracts, aid. 10) Compromise of 1877 — Hayes became president; federal troops withdrawn from the South, ending Reconstruction. 11) Sharecropping — tenant farming that trapped families in debt. DBQ rubric: thesis on what freedom meant (e.g., legal citizenship, education, land and fair labor, voting — partly achieved then undermined); uses 4+ documents with sourcing; outside evidence (Black Codes, KKK, Hiram Revels as first Black senator, 1870).

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Character through improvisation

**Prepare:** Gather costume pieces (hats, scarves, glasses, jackets). Choose a pet to observe or an animal video. Prepare 15 hot-seat questions (childhood, fears, daily routine, secrets).

**Watch for:**
- Building characters only from voice. Start with the body (center, tempo, gesture) and let the voice follow.
- Cartoonish animal work. Keep the animal's essence (rhythm, focus, weight) but make it a believable human.
- Answering hot-seat questions with 'I don't know.' Make a specific choice; commitment beats perfection.

### Day 1 (Monday): Leading centers
*Student:* Explore walking led by head, chest, belly, and knees; create a character from each.

*How to teach:* Walk with the head leading, then chest, belly, knees. After each, ask: Who is this person? How do they speak? Create a quick character for each.

- **Q:** What kind of character might a chest-led walk suggest?
  **A:** Often a proud, confident, or aggressive character — chest forward, assertive (answers may vary but should connect body to personality).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Animal work
*Student:* Observe an animal (pet or video) and transform its movement into a human character.

*How to teach:* Observe an animal for 5 minutes noting rhythm, weight, focus, and stillness. Move as the animal, then gradually stand and humanize it.

- **Q:** What qualities from an animal transfer to a human character?
  **A:** Tempo, rhythm, weight, the way it focuses or scans, how it reacts to sound and danger.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hot seating
*Student:* Answer interview questions in character for 5 minutes.

*How to teach:* Hot seat: student stays in character answering questions for 5 minutes. Push for specific details.

- **Q:** What is the purpose of hot seating?
  **A:** To deepen a character by forcing the actor to invent specific history, opinions, and voice in character.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Character monologue
*Student:* Improvise a 1-minute character monologue built from a costume piece.

*How to teach:* Student picks a costume piece and improvises a 1-minute monologue in character (who they are, what they want right now).

- **Q:** What are given circumstances?
  **A:** The facts of a character's situation — who, what, where, when, why — that shape choices.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Perform two contrasting improvised characters and write a character profile.

*How to teach:* Perform two contrasting characters; write a profile (name, age, walk, voice, want, fear, history).

- **Q:** What makes two characters 'contrasting'?
  **A:** Clear differences in body (center, tempo), voice, status, and objective.

**Assessment:** Character profile and performance.

**Answer key:** Character profile and performance rubric. 4 – Two clearly contrasting, sustained characters with distinct leading centers, tempo, voice, and objectives; profile is specific (history, want, fear, physical traits) and connects to performance choices. 3 (proficient) – Two distinct characters and a complete profile. 2 – Characters similar or inconsistent; profile thin. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness: Warm-up, cool-down, and safety

**Prepare:** Set out cones for a 20-m warm-up lane; have water bottles ready. Read the CDC heat illness signs (heat exhaustion vs. heat stroke) so you can teach them confidently. Plan to run the fartlek day in a shaded area or early morning.

**Watch for:**
- Doing long static stretches before vigorous activity as a warm-up. Dynamic movement raises muscle temperature better; save long static holds for the cool-down.
- Waiting until thirsty to drink. Drink before, during, and after activity, especially in heat.
- Thinking heat stroke victims always sweat a lot. Heat stroke may involve hot skin and confusion (sweating may stop); it is a 911 emergency.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dynamic warm-up
*Student:* Learn and perform a 10-minute dynamic warm-up (leg swings, walking lunges, high knees, A-skips).

*How to teach:* Lead the student through a 10-minute dynamic warm-up down and back the cone lane: easy jog, leg swings, walking lunges, high knees, butt kicks, A-skips. Cue tall posture and controlled movement; ask how breathing and heart rate changed.

- **Q:** What is the purpose of a dynamic warm-up?
  **A:** To raise heart rate and muscle temperature, increase range of motion, and prepare the nervous system for activity, lowering injury risk.
- **Q:** Give a cue for an A-skip.
  **A:** Drive one knee up to hip height, stay tall on the ball of the opposite foot, and swing the opposite arm.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cool-down
*Student:* Learn a static stretching cool-down for major muscle groups after a 20-minute jog.

*How to teach:* After a 20-minute easy jog, model a 5–8 minute cool-down walk then static stretches held 15–30 seconds each for calves, hamstrings, quadriceps, hip flexors, chest, and shoulders. Check that the student breathes and does not bounce.

- **Q:** How long should a static stretch be held?
  **A:** About 15–30 seconds (up to 60 for older adults), to mild tension, not pain.
- **Q:** Why walk before stretching in a cool-down?
  **A:** To let heart rate and breathing return gradually toward resting and prevent blood pooling or dizziness.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Heat safety
*Student:* Learn signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke and hydration guidelines; practice a fartlek run in shade.

*How to teach:* Teach heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, cool clammy skin, dizziness, nausea, headache — move to shade, cool, sip fluids) vs. heat stroke (body temperature 104°F+, confusion, possibly hot dry skin — call 911 and cool immediately). Then do a fartlek run in shade: easy jogging with unplanned surges to the next tree or post.

- **Q:** A runner is confused and has hot skin after a run on a hot day. What do you do?
  **A:** Treat as possible heat stroke: call 911, move them to shade, and cool them rapidly (cold water, ice packs at neck/armpits/groin).
- **Q:** What does 'fartlek' mean?
  **A:** Swedish for 'speed play' — continuous running with unstructured bursts of faster pace mixed with easy running.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Design
*Student:* Design your own warm-up and cool-down routines and lead a family member through them.

*How to teach:* The student writes a warm-up (5–10 min, dynamic, specific to their workout) and a cool-down (walk plus 5–6 static stretches) and leads a family member through both. Watch for clear cues and safe demonstration.

- **Q:** What should a warm-up include before a sprint workout specifically?
  **A:** General movement (easy jog), dynamic stretches for the legs and hips, then sport-specific drills and a few build-up strides (specificity).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Perform your routines with correct form and pass a safety quiz.

*How to teach:* Have the student perform both routines while you check form, then give a short safety quiz (sample below).

- **Q:** List three ways to prevent heat illness.
  **A:** Hydrate before/during/after, exercise in cooler times of day or shade, wear light breathable clothing, acclimatize gradually, and rest when symptoms start.

**Assessment:** Routine demonstration and safety quiz.

**Answer key:** Routine demonstration: proficient = warm-up is dynamic and progressive, cool-down includes a gradual walk and static stretches held 15–30 s for major groups, cues are clear and form safe. Safety quiz key: 1) Heat exhaustion signs: heavy sweating, cool clammy skin, dizziness, nausea, headache, weakness. 2) Heat stroke signs: body temp ≥104°F, confusion/altered behavior, hot skin (may be dry), possible collapse. 3) Heat stroke first step: call 911 and cool rapidly. 4) Dynamic stretching before, static after. 5) Drink water before, during, and after activity; don't wait for thirst.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Teamwork and documentation

**Prepare:** Get safety glasses for everyone, 20 sticks of uncooked spaghetti, 1 m of tape, 1 m of string and one marshmallow per team, and a timer (18 minutes). Print a shop-safety list. Prepare the safety quiz and Unit 1 quiz from the key below.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking safety glasses are only needed for power tools. Wear eye protection whenever cutting, snapping, hammering, drilling, hot gluing or working near someone who is.
- Planning so long that there's no time to build. The marshmallow challenge shows that early prototyping and testing beat one big final attempt.
- Writing test procedures that are vague ('test if it is strong'). Procedures must be repeatable: exact steps, tools, measurements and number of trials.

### Day 1 (Monday): Lab safety
*Student:* Learn shop safety rules, PPE, and tool handling; pass a safety quiz.

*How to teach:* Go over shop rules: PPE (safety glasses, closed-toe shoes, gloves when appropriate), tidy workspace, tool handling (cut away from body, carry tools pointed down), first-aid location. Give the safety quiz.

- **Q:** What does PPE stand for? Give two examples.
  **A:** Personal protective equipment — e.g. safety glasses, gloves, hearing protection, closed-toe shoes.
- **Q:** Which direction should you cut with a craft knife?
  **A:** Away from your body and hands, on a cutting mat, keeping fingers behind the blade.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Marshmallow challenge
*Student:* Complete the spaghetti-marshmallow tower challenge with family members and reflect on teamwork.

*How to teach:* Run the marshmallow challenge: 18 minutes to build the tallest free-standing tower with the marshmallow on top. Afterward discuss roles, communication and why testing early matters.

- **Q:** Why do kindergartners often beat business students at the marshmallow challenge?
  **A:** They build and test small prototypes quickly and adjust, rather than planning one perfect tower and discovering at the end that the marshmallow's weight makes it collapse.
- **Q:** What is the 'hidden assumption' that sinks many teams?
  **A:** That the marshmallow is light; it's actually heavy enough to make tall spaghetti structures buckle.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Project management
*Student:* Learn Gantt charts and build one for a hypothetical project.

*How to teach:* Show a Gantt chart: tasks listed down the side, time across the top, bars for task duration, and dependencies. The student makes one for a hypothetical project (e.g. building a birdhouse in 3 weeks).

- **Q:** What does a Gantt chart show?
  **A:** Each task as a bar across a timeline showing when it starts and ends, the order of tasks and dependencies.
- **Q:** What is a dependency?
  **A:** A task that can't start until another task is finished (e.g. painting must wait until assembly is done).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Documentation
*Student:* Practice writing a test procedure and data table.

*How to teach:* Model a repeatable test procedure: numbered steps, equipment, what is measured, number of trials, and an empty data table with units. The student writes one for testing a paper bridge.

- **Q:** What makes a test procedure 'repeatable'?
  **A:** Anyone could follow the exact steps with the same equipment and get comparable results — steps, measurements, units and number of trials are specified.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit check
*Student:* Unit 1 quiz on design process and careers.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 quiz in the assessment key.

- **Q:** Put in order: test, define, prototype, ideate.
  **A:** Define, ideate, prototype, test.

**Assessment:** Safety quiz and Unit 1 quiz.

**Answer key:** Safety quiz (pass = 100%, retake missed items): 1) Safety glasses must be worn whenever tools are used or anything can fly. 2) Tie back long hair, remove dangling jewelry, no loose sleeves. 3) Cut away from your body, using a cutting mat. 4) Report any injury immediately. 5) Unplug tools before changing blades/bits. 6) Know where the first-aid kit is. Unit 1 quiz: 1) Steps of the design process in order (define, research, ideate, select, prototype, test, iterate, communicate). 2) Criteria = measurable goals; constraints = limits. 3) A design brief states client, problem, criteria and constraints. 4) Decision matrix compares ideas against weighted criteria. 5) Notebook rules (bound, dated, pen, single-line cross-outs). 6) Name two engineering disciplines and what they design. 7) A Gantt chart shows tasks against a timeline. 8) PPE = personal protective equipment. Score 7–8 = mastery.
