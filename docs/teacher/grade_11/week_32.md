## English 11 – American Literature: Language and identity

**Prepare:** Amy Tan's 'Mother Tongue'; six vignettes from Sandra Cisneros' The House on Mango Street (e.g., 'The House on Mango Street,' 'My Name,' 'Hairs,' 'Four Skinny Trees,' 'Those Who Don't,' 'Bums in the Attic'); excerpts from Richard Rodriguez's 'Aria' and Gloria Anzaldúa's 'How to Tame a Wild Tongue.'

**Watch for:**
- Assuming all four writers agree. Rodriguez argues that learning public English, though painful, was necessary; Anzaldúa insists on keeping her home language as part of her identity.
- Treating vignettes as unfinished stories. They are compressed, image-rich snapshots that build meaning across the collection.

Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What 'Englishes' does Tan describe?
  **A:** The formal English of her writing and speeches, the simpler English she uses with her mother, and her mother's 'broken' or 'limited' English, which she says is vivid and full of meaning.
- **Q:** What evidence does Tan give that her mother is judged by her English?
  **A:** Stores, banks, and a hospital ignored or dismissed her mother until Tan called pretending to be her (as with the stockbroker); she got results only with 'perfect' English.

**Watch for:**
- Applying the 68-95-99.7 rule to skewed data. Check the histogram shape first.
- Comparing raw scores across different tests. Convert to z-scores to compare relative standing.

### Day 1 (Monday): Distributions

*How to teach:* Make a histogram of the data set; describe shape, center, spread, and outliers. - **Q:** What does 'approximately normal' look like?
  **A:** Single-peaked, symmetric, bell-shaped, with mean ≈ median and few extreme values.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Empirical rule
*Student:* Apply the 68-95-99.7 rule to estimate percentages.

*How to teach:* Draw a normal curve with mean and ±1, 2, 3 SD marked; shade regions. Today's task from the plan: Apply the 68-95-99.7 rule to estimate percentages. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** IQ scores: mean 100, SD 15. What percent lie between 85 and 115? Above 130?
  **A:** About 68% (±1 SD). Above 130 is beyond +2 SD: about 2.5%.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Z-scores
*Student:* Calculate and compare z-scores (e.g., comparing ACT and SAT performance).

*How to teach:* Model z = (x − μ)/σ. Compare two test results. Today's task from the plan: Calculate and compare z-scores (e.g., comparing ACT and SAT performance). Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** SAT 1300 (mean 1050, SD 200) vs ACT 28 (mean 21, SD 5). Which is better relative to peers?
  **A:** SAT z = 1.25; ACT z = 1.4. The ACT score is relatively better.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Areas
*Student:* Use technology to find areas under the normal curve; recognize when a normal model is not appropriate.

*How to teach:* Use technology to find areas; discuss when the normal model is inappropriate (skewed incomes, wait times). Today's task from the plan: Use technology to find areas under the normal curve; recognize when a normal model is not appropriate. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** With mean 100 and SD 10, find P(X > 120).
  **A:** z = 2; P ≈ 0.0228 (about 2.3%).
- **Q:** Find P(Z < 1).
  **A:** ≈ 0.8413.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (about 30 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Quiz on normal distributions. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

  **A:** The value is 1.5 standard deviations below the mean.

**Assessment:** Normal distribution quiz.

## Physics of the Universe: Composition of matter in the universe

**Prepare:** NASA element-abundance chart; string, washers, a tube (pen barrel) for the rotation model; NASA's dark matter and dark energy overviews.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking dark matter is just dark dust or black holes. It does not emit, absorb, or reflect light and doesn't interact with normal matter except through gravity.
- Confusing dark matter and dark energy. Dark matter pulls (gravity); dark energy is causing the expansion of the universe to accelerate.

### Day 1 (Monday): Element abundance
*Student:* Graph cosmic element abundance data and explain why ~75% H / ~25% He by mass supports Big Bang nucleosynthesis.

*How to teach:* Give the cosmic abundance data (by mass: H ≈ 74%, He ≈ 24%, everything else ≈ 2%). The student makes a bar or pie chart and explains why so much H and He points to Big Bang nucleosynthesis.

- **Q:** Why does the ~75% hydrogen / ~25% helium mix support the Big Bang?
  **A:** Big Bang nucleosynthesis predicts these ratios from the first few minutes, matching observations.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rotation curves
*Student:* Swing a washer on strings of different lengths to model orbital speed vs. radius, then compare to real galaxy rotation curves that stay flat.

*How to teach:* Swing a washer on a string threaded through a pen barrel with a weight hanging below; then look at a real galaxy rotation curve. The student compares the expected (Keplerian, falling) curve with the observed flat curve.

- **Q:** If most of a galaxy's mass were in its bright center, how should outer star speeds behave?
  **A:** They should decrease with distance (like planets around the Sun); instead they stay roughly flat, suggesting unseen mass.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dark matter evidence
*Student:* Read NASA's dark matter overview and list three independent lines of evidence (rotation curves, lensing, cluster motions).

*How to teach:* Read NASA's dark matter overview together; the student lists three independent lines of evidence and explains each in one sentence. Today's task from the plan: Read NASA's dark matter overview and list three independent lines of evidence (rotation curves, lensing, cluster motions). Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Name three pieces of evidence for dark matter.
  **A:** Flat galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing stronger than visible mass explains, and galaxy cluster motions too fast for visible mass.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Dark energy
*Student:* Read about the 1998 Type Ia supernova discovery and sketch a graph of expansion rate over time; hold a structured debate on what counts as 'evidence' for something unseen.

*How to teach:* Read about the 1998 supernova discovery; debate evidence for unseen things. Today's task from the plan: Read about the 1998 Type Ia supernova discovery and sketch a graph of expansion rate over time; hold a structured debate on what counts as 'evidence' for something unseen. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What did the 1998 Type Ia supernova observations show?
  **A:** Distant supernovae were dimmer than expected, meaning the expansion of the universe is accelerating—attributed to dark energy.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on composition of the universe with two short CER items.

*How to teach:* Quiz on the composition of the universe. Today's task from the plan: Quiz on composition of the universe with two short CER items. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** What are the approximate shares of ordinary matter, dark matter, and dark energy?
  **A:** About 5%, 27%, and 68%.

**Assessment:** Quiz with CER items.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) universe ≈ 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter, 68% dark energy. 2) H ≈ 75%, He ≈ 25% by mass. 3) dark matter evidence: rotation curves, lensing, cluster motions. 4) dark energy evidence: Type Ia supernovae. CER items scored with the 4-point CER rubric.

## Spanish IV: Historia hispana de California

**Prepare:** Explore Calisphere (calisphere.org) photographs of ranchos, missions, braceros, and the UFW; find Spanish-language articles and oral histories (e.g., the Bracero History Archive). Prepare a long paper strip or a digital timeline tool.

- Thinking California history begins with the Gold Rush: Indigenous peoples lived here for millennia, followed by the Spanish missions (1769), Mexican rule (1821–1848), and the Californio rancho era.
- Thinking Californios kept their land after 1848: the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo promised property rights, but the Land Act of 1851, legal costs, and squatters caused many to lose their ranchos.

*How to teach:* Read about the Californios and the rancho era; your child analyzes two Calisphere photos.

*How to teach:* Study the Bracero Program through Spanish-language oral histories.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Blocking and staging

- Actors turning their backs to the audience unintentionally. Use open positions and cheat out.

### Day 1 (Monday): Stage areas

*How to teach:* Review the nine stage areas (USR, USC, USL, CR, C, CL, DSR, DSC, DSL) and notation (X = cross, DSR, etc.). Tape the floor plan.

- **Q:** An actor faces the audience and moves to her right and toward the audience. Where does she go?
  **A:** Downstage right (DSR).
- **Q:** Write the notation for 'Mrs. Hale crosses to the stove downstage left.'
  **A:** Mrs. H X DSL to stove (X = cross).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pre-block
*Student:* Pre-block the first half of the play in the prompt book.

*How to teach:* Pre-block the first half in the prompt book, using composition (levels, triangles) and picturization (relationships shown in pictures).

*How to teach:* Block the first half with actors; adjust for sightlines.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rehearsal 2
*Student:* Block the second half and record blocking in the prompt book.

*How to teach:* Block the second half; record all blocking.

- **Q:** Which stage area is traditionally considered strongest?

### Day 5 (Friday): Run

*How to teach:* Stumble-through of the whole play; give notes.

## Lifetime Fitness: Fitness consumerism

**Prepare:** Collect three fitness product ads (online or magazines), and look up the FDA's dietary supplement page and your local parks & recreation website.

**Watch for:**
- Assuming supplements are FDA-approved. The FDA does not approve dietary supplements for safety or effectiveness before they are sold.
- Trusting testimonials and before/after photos as evidence. Look for peer-reviewed research and independent testing.
- Believing wearables are perfectly accurate. Heart rate and calorie estimates can be off substantially, especially calories.

### Day 1 (Monday): Evaluate claims
*Student:* Analyze three fitness product ads for red flags and evidence.

*How to teach:* Analyze three ads for red flags: 'quick results,' 'no effort,' 'spot reduction,' testimonials, 'secret' formulas, money-back pressure. Rate the evidence.

- **Q:** Can you 'spot reduce' belly fat with ab exercises?
  **A:** No. Fat loss occurs throughout the body; ab exercises strengthen muscles but don't target fat in one area.
- **Q:** Name three red flags in fitness ads.
  **A:** Promises of rapid results, 'no diet or exercise needed,' testimonials instead of research, 'miracle'/'secret' claims, spot reduction claims.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Supplements
*Student:* Research the FDA's role in supplements and evaluate one popular supplement.

*How to teach:* Research the FDA's role (supplements are regulated as foods under DSHEA 1994; manufacturers are responsible for safety; FDA acts after problems arise). Evaluate one supplement (e.g., creatine, pre-workout) for evidence and risks; look for third-party testing seals (USP, NSF Certified for Sport).

- **Q:** Does the FDA approve supplements before they are sold?
  **A:** No. Manufacturers are responsible for safety and labeling; the FDA can act against unsafe products after they're on the market.
- **Q:** What does a USP or NSF Certified for Sport seal indicate?
  **A:** Independent testing that the product contains what the label says and is free of specified contaminants/banned substances — not that it is effective.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Apps & wearables
*Student:* Compare two free fitness apps or wearables for accuracy and usefulness.

*How to teach:* Compare two free apps/wearables: test heart-rate accuracy against a manual pulse, step counts against counted steps, and usefulness.

- **Q:** Your watch reads 150 bpm; your manual 15-second count is 34. What's the difference?
  **A:** Manual = 136 bpm; the watch is 14 bpm higher.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Community resources
*Student:* Map free or low-cost fitness resources in your community (parks, trails, rec centers).

*How to teach:* Map free/low-cost community resources: parks, trails, rec centers, YMCA, school facilities, community classes.

- **Q:** Name two low-cost options for staying active after high school.
  **A:** Public parks and trails, community recreation center classes, campus gyms, free online workout videos, pickup games.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a consumer guide for teens.

*How to teach:* Student writes a consumer guide for teens: how to evaluate products, supplements, apps, and gyms.

- **Q:** What question should you ask about any fitness product claim?
  **A:** What is the evidence — is it supported by independent, peer-reviewed research?

**Assessment:** Consumer guide.

**Answer key:** Consumer guide rubric. 4 – Explains red flags with examples, accurately describes FDA supplement regulation and third-party testing, compares apps with data, lists local resources, gives clear checklist. 3 (proficient) – Accurate red flags, FDA role, app comparison, local resources. 2 – Some inaccuracies (e.g., claims supplements are FDA-approved). 1 – Incomplete.
