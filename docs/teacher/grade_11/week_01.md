# Teacher's Guide — 11th Grade, Week 1

## English 11 – American Literature: Origins: Native American and Puritan narratives

**Prepare:** Get a composition notebook for the reading journal. Find 'The World on the Turtle's Back' (Iroquois creation story, widely anthologized) and William Bradford's Of Plymouth Plantation, Book I ch. 9–10 and Book II's first-winter section (Gutenberg). Preview both.

**Watch for:**
- Treating a creation story as 'just a myth' with no argument. Show that it teaches values (balance of good and evil, cooperation of animals, respect for earth) just as Bradford's history does.
- Writing an 'objective summary' that includes opinions ('this beautiful story'). An objective summary states who/what/what happened and the central idea, with no judgments.

### Day 1 (Monday): Course launch
*Student:* Set up a reading journal and take a 20-minute diagnostic writing sample responding to 'What makes someone an American?'

*How to teach:* Explain the reading journal: date, title, a quotation, and a response each day. Then set a 20-minute timer for the diagnostic on 'What makes someone an American?' without help. Read it afterward only to note strengths and one goal; do not grade it.

- **Q:** What two things should every journal entry include?
  **A:** A quotation (with page or paragraph) and the student's own response or question about it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Creation story
*Student:* Read 'The World on the Turtle's Back' and annotate values it reveals; write a 150-word objective summary.

*How to teach:* Read the story aloud together. Have the student annotate values in the margin (e.g., the animals working together, the twins representing good and evil), then write a 150-word summary with no opinions. Check: could someone who never read it understand the plot from the summary?

- **Q:** How is the earth created in 'The World on the Turtle's Back'?
  **A:** The sky woman falls from the sky world through a hole by an uprooted tree; water animals dive for earth, and the mud one brings up is spread on the turtle's back, which grows into the land.
- **Q:** What do the twins represent?
  **A:** Opposing forces in the world: the good-minded twin creates useful and beautiful things, and the evil-minded twin creates harmful things, so the world holds a balance of both.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Of Plymouth Plantation
*Student:* Read Bradford's chapters on the Mayflower voyage and first winter; chart evidence of 'providence' language.

*How to teach:* Read Bradford's account of the voyage and first winter aloud. Have the student make a chart: event | how Bradford explains it. Point out that he credits God's 'providence' for both the deaths of enemies and the survival of the Pilgrims.

- **Q:** How does Bradford explain the death of the 'proud and very profane' young sailor who cursed the sick passengers?
  **A:** As God's judgment: it 'pleased God' to strike him with a grievous disease so he was the first to die, which Bradford calls 'the just hand of God upon him.'
- **Q:** What did the Pilgrims do when they first reached land, and what does it show?
  **A:** They 'fell upon their knees and blessed the God of heaven' who had brought them safely; it shows their belief that God directed every event (providence).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing worldviews
*Student:* Build a two-column comparison of the two texts' views of nature and community; vocabulary: build 10 cards from roots in the readings.

*How to teach:* Model one row of the two-column chart (nature: in the creation story the earth is a living gift built by animal cooperation; for Bradford the land is a 'hideous and desolate wilderness'). The student completes the chart, then makes 10 vocabulary cards using roots (provid- 'foresee', -cracy, etc.).

- **Q:** Contrast how the two texts view nature.
  **A:** The Iroquois story presents nature as sacred and cooperative, with animals as helpers; Bradford sees the New England wilderness as hostile and desolate, something to endure and tame with God's help.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Timed paragraph: how does each text explain hardship? Cite two quotations each; quiz on Puritan terms.

*How to teach:* Timed paragraph (25 min): how does each text explain hardship? Require two quotations from each. Then give a 10-term quiz (Puritan, providence, plain style, covenant, predestination, worldview, etc.).

- **Q:** Define 'plain style.'
  **A:** The Puritan writing style that uses simple, direct language and avoids ornament, because the goal was clarity and truth for God's glory, not display.

**Assessment:** Timed comparative paragraph scored on evidence and summary; vocabulary quiz.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric (4-point): 4 = clear comparative topic sentence; two accurate quotations from each text; explains that Bradford attributes hardship to God's providence/testing while the creation story frames struggle as part of the balance between creative and destructive forces; objective summary is accurate. 3 = all parts present, analysis thinner. 2 = quotations but little comparison. 1 = summary only. Vocabulary quiz key: Puritan = English Protestants seeking to 'purify' the Church; providence = God's guidance of all events; plain style = simple, direct writing; worldview = a set of beliefs used to interpret the world; predestination = belief that God has already chosen who is saved; covenant = a binding agreement (with God or among people, e.g., the Mayflower Compact).

## Algebra II: Parent functions and transformations

**Prepare:** Bookmark Desmos and set up a notebook with a 'function gallery' page. Print or draw six blank coordinate grids. Preview Khan Academy 'Transformations of functions'.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking f(x−3) moves the graph LEFT 3. Show with a table: the output f(0) now happens at x = 3, so the graph moves RIGHT. Inside changes act 'opposite' to what they look like.
- Confusing a vertical stretch a·f(x) with a horizontal change f(bx). Have the student plot both y = 2x² and y = (2x)² in Desmos and compare which points move.

### Day 1 (Monday): Parent function gallery
*Student:* Graph linear, quadratic, absolute value, square root, cube root, and cubic parents in Desmos; record domain, range, and intercepts.

*How to teach:* Model graphing y = x² and recording a table of domain, range, and intercepts. The student then graphs the other five parents (linear, absolute value, square root, cube root, cubic) in Desmos and fills the table. Check each row against the graphs before moving on.

- **Q:** State the domain and range of y = √x.
  **A:** Domain x ≥ 0; range y ≥ 0. You cannot take the real square root of a negative, and the outputs are never negative.
- **Q:** Which parent functions have domain AND range of all real numbers?
  **A:** Linear y = x, cube root y = ∛x, and cubic y = x³.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Translations
*Student:* Use sliders for f(x)+k and f(x−h); write rules for 10 transformed graphs from pictures.

*How to teach:* Show the slider for y = (x − h)² + k in Desmos and ask the student to predict before dragging each slider. Then have them write rules for the 10 pictured graphs, saying the shift in words first ('right 2, down 5').

- **Q:** Describe how g(x) = (x + 4)² − 3 is related to f(x) = x².
  **A:** Shift left 4 and down 3; the vertex moves from (0, 0) to (−4, −3).
- **Q:** Write the rule for y = |x| shifted right 2 and up 5.
  **A:** y = |x − 2| + 5.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Stretches & reflections
*Student:* Investigate a·f(x) and f(bx); match 12 equation cards to graphs.

*How to teach:* Graph y = x², 3x², ½x², and −x² together and have the student describe each change. Then explore f(bx) with y = √(4x) and y = √x. Student matches the 12 cards and explains two matches aloud.

- **Q:** Describe y = −½x² compared with y = x².
  **A:** Reflected over the x-axis and vertically compressed by a factor of ½ (it opens down and is wider).
- **Q:** Show that √(4x) is the same as a vertical stretch of √x.
  **A:** √(4x) = √4·√x = 2√x, so it is a vertical stretch by 2 (equivalently a horizontal compression by ¼).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Combined transformations
*Student:* Graph g(x) = −2|x−3|+1 and similar by hand by transforming key points; check with Desmos.

*How to teach:* Model g(x) = −2|x − 3| + 1 by transforming key points of |x|: (−1,1), (0,0), (1,1) become (2,−1), (3,1), (4,−1). Student graphs three similar functions by hand, then checks each in Desmos.

- **Q:** Give the vertex, direction, and range of g(x) = −2|x − 3| + 1.
  **A:** Vertex (3, 1); opens down; slopes −2 and 2 on each side; range y ≤ 1.
- **Q:** Where does (2, 4) on f(x) = x² go under y = (x + 1)² − 5?
  **A:** (1, −1): left 1 and down 5.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: describe transformations and write equations from graphs (10 items).

*How to teach:* Give the 10-item quiz closed-book (Desmos off), about 25 minutes. Grade together, then let the student verify any missed graph in Desmos and write the corrected rule.

- **Q:** Write the equation of an absolute value graph with vertex (−1, 4) that opens down with slopes ±1.
  **A:** y = −|x + 1| + 4.

**Assessment:** Transformations quiz.

**Answer key:** Suggested 10 items and answers: 1) y=(x−5)²: right 5. 2) y=x²+7: up 7. 3) y=−|x|: reflect over x-axis. 4) y=√(x+2)−1: left 2, down 1. 5) y=3x³: vertical stretch by 3. 6) y=|½x|: horizontal stretch by 2 (same as vertical compression by ½). 7) parabola vertex (2,−3) opening up, same shape as x²: y=(x−2)²−3. 8) square root starting at (−4,0): y=√(x+4). 9) V-shape vertex (0,6) opening down slopes ±2: y=−2|x|+6. 10) cubic x³ moved right 1, up 2: y=(x−1)³+2. Score 1 point each; 8+/10 is mastery.

## Physics of the Universe: Describing motion

**Prepare:** Toy car, meter stick, masking tape, phone with slow-motion video. Queue up a short crash-test video (IIHS has many). Make a large 'driving question board' on poster paper.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing velocity and acceleration: a fast car is not necessarily accelerating. Acceleration is the rate of CHANGE of velocity; a car at a steady 30 m/s has zero acceleration.
- Reading a position-time graph as a picture of the path. The slope is velocity; a flat line means the object is stopped, not moving along flat ground.

### Day 1 (Monday): Anchor phenomenon
*Student:* Watch crash-test footage and list questions about forces in collisions; start a driving question board.

*How to teach:* Show the crash-test video twice. The student writes at least 10 questions about forces and safety and sorts them on the driving question board (we'll return to it in the egg-crash challenge).

- **Q:** Why do crash-test dummies move forward when the car stops suddenly?
  **A:** They tend to keep moving at the car's original speed (inertia) until a force—seatbelt, airbag, or dashboard—stops them.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Position-time graphs
*Student:* Roll a toy car along a taped track, time each meter, and graph position vs. time.

*How to teach:* Tape marks every meter for 5 m. Release the car and record the time at each mark (several trials). The student graphs position vs. time and finds the slope.

- **Q:** A car's position-time graph is a straight line from (0 s, 0 m) to (4 s, 2 m). What is its velocity?
  **A:** Slope = 2 m ÷ 4 s = 0.5 m/s, constant velocity.
- **Q:** What does a curved, steepening position-time graph mean?
  **A:** The object is speeding up (accelerating): the slope (velocity) increases.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Velocity & acceleration
*Student:* Roll a ball down a ramp; use slow-motion video to measure velocity changes and calculate acceleration.

*How to teach:* Film a ball rolling down a ramp in slow motion beside a meter stick. Step through frames to get position at equal time intervals, then compute velocities and acceleration a = Δv/Δt.

- **Q:** A ball's velocity goes from 0.2 m/s to 1.0 m/s in 0.8 s. What is its acceleration?
  **A:** a = (1.0 − 0.2)/0.8 = 1.0 m/s².

### Day 4 (Thursday): Kinematic equations
*Student:* Solve constant-acceleration problems (braking distances) using the kinematic equations.

*How to teach:* Model one braking problem using v² = v₀² + 2aΔx, writing knowns and unknowns first. The student solves five more. Today's task from the plan: Solve constant-acceleration problems (braking distances) using the kinematic equations. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** A car traveling 25 m/s brakes at −5.0 m/s². How far does it travel before stopping?
  **A:** 0 = 25² + 2(−5)Δx → Δx = 625/10 = 62.5 m.
- **Q:** How long does that stop take?
  **A:** t = (0 − 25)/(−5) = 5.0 s.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on motion graphs and kinematic calculations.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (30 minutes). Afterwards check graphs by acting them out (walk toward/away from a wall). Today's task from the plan: Quiz on motion graphs and kinematic calculations. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** A car accelerates from rest at 3 m/s² for 4 s. Find its final speed and distance.
  **A:** v = 0 + 3(4) = 12 m/s; Δx = ½(3)(4²) = 24 m.

**Assessment:** Motion graphs quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key (sample): 1) slope of position-time = velocity. 2) horizontal line = at rest. 3) velocity-time slope = acceleration; area = displacement. 4) 0→20 m/s in 4 s: a = 5 m/s². 5) from rest at 2 m/s² for 5 s: v = 10 m/s, Δx = 25 m. 6) braking from 30 m/s at −6 m/s²: Δx = 75 m. 7) sketch: constant velocity = straight sloped line on x-t, flat line on v-t. 8) a ball thrown up has a = −9.8 m/s² even at the top. Score 1 pt each with units.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: Enlightenment and the founding documents

**Prepare:** Print the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution (archives.gov), an excerpt of Locke's Second Treatise (chapters on the state of nature and the ends of government), Montesquieu's Spirit of the Laws (Book XI, ch. 6 on separation of powers), Federalist No. 10 and Brutus No. 1 excerpts. Make a SOAPS organizer (Subject, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Speaker).

**Watch for:**
- Students think the Declaration created the U.S. government. It announced independence and justified it; the Constitution (1787, effective 1789) set up the federal government (after the Articles of Confederation).
- Students think 'separation of powers' and 'checks and balances' are the same. Separation divides power among three branches; checks and balances give each branch ways to limit the others.
- Students think the Anti-Federalists opposed any constitution. They feared a strong central government and a large republic, and demanded a bill of rights.

### Day 1 (Monday): Enlightenment roots
*Student:* Read excerpts from Locke and Montesquieu and chart ideas that appear in the Declaration.

*How to teach:* Read the Locke and Montesquieu excerpts together, pausing to paraphrase. The student builds a chart: idea → thinker → where it appears in the Declaration.

- **Q:** What did Locke say government is for, and what may people do if it fails?
  **A:** Government exists to protect natural rights (life, liberty and property) with the consent of the governed; if it violates those rights, the people may alter or abolish it.
- **Q:** Which Declaration phrase echoes Locke?
  **A:** 'Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness' and 'deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed'.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Declaration
*Student:* Analyze the Declaration's preamble and list of grievances using a SOAPS organizer.

*How to teach:* Model SOAPS on the preamble, then the student analyzes the list of grievances, grouping them (taxation without consent, standing armies, trial by jury, dissolving legislatures).

- **Q:** Who is the audience of the Declaration?
  **A:** The world ('a decent respect to the opinions of mankind'), the colonists, and King George III/Britain; it sought foreign support (e.g., France).
- **Q:** Name two grievances against the King.
  **A:** Imposing taxes without consent; keeping standing armies in peacetime; depriving colonists of trial by jury; dissolving representative houses.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Constitution
*Student:* Map the structure of the Constitution and identify checks and balances in Articles I–III.

*How to teach:* The student maps Articles I–III (Congress, President, courts) and lists at least six checks.

- **Q:** How can Congress check the President?
  **A:** Override a veto by two-thirds of both houses, reject appointments and treaties in the Senate, control funding, and impeach and remove.
- **Q:** How do the courts check the other branches?
  **A:** Through judicial review — declaring laws or actions unconstitutional (established in Marbury v. Madison, 1803).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Federalist debate
*Student:* Compare Federalist No. 10 and Anti-Federalist Brutus No. 1 on the size of the republic.

*How to teach:* Compare Federalist No. 10 (Madison) and Brutus No. 1 on the size of the republic in a T-chart.

- **Q:** How did Madison argue a large republic would control factions?
  **A:** A large republic includes many factions and interests, making it hard for any single majority faction to dominate and oppress others.
- **Q:** What was Brutus's concern?
  **A:** A large, diverse republic could not truly represent the people; a distant, powerful national government would swallow the states and threaten liberty.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining how the Constitution put Enlightenment ideas into practice.

*How to teach:* The student writes a paragraph explaining how the Constitution put Enlightenment ideas into practice. Score with the rubric and revise.

- **Q:** What does federalism mean?
  **A:** Power is divided between the national government and the state governments.

**Assessment:** Constitution analysis paragraph.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric (4 levels): 4 = clear claim; at least three specific connections with evidence — Locke's natural rights and consent of the governed (Declaration: 'Governments are instituted among Men, deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed'; Constitution's Preamble 'We the People'), Montesquieu's separation of powers (Articles I, II, III), checks and balances (presidential veto and two-thirds override, Senate confirmation of appointments and two-thirds treaty ratification, impeachment by the House and trial in the Senate), federalism dividing power between national and state governments, and the Bill of Rights protecting natural rights; explains how each puts an idea into practice. 3 = two connections with evidence. 2 = general. 1 = inaccurate.

## Spanish IV: ¿Quién soy? Repaso del presente y los tiempos pasados

**Prepare:** Print preterite and imperfect charts (regular endings; irregulars ser/ir, tener, estar, hacer, decir, poder, poner, querer, venir, traer; i→y and stem-changing -ir verbs: pidió, durmió). Find a short authentic blog post by a young Latino writer about identity (e.g., from a teen or university blog, Remezcla, or BBC Mundo testimonials). Set up a phone or computer to record.

**Watch for:**
- Choosing the tense from the English verb: 'was' can be fue/estuvo (completed) or era/estaba (background). Decide by aspect: completed event = preterite; ongoing background, description, habit = imperfect.
- Forgetting stem changes in third-person preterite of -ir verbs: pedir → pidió/pidieron, dormir → durmió/durmieron, sentir → sintió.
- Saying 'Me llamo es…' or 'Mi apodo se llama…': Me llamo… / Mi apodo es…

### Day 1 (Monday): Presentación personal
*Student:* Write and record a 2-minute self-introduction including heritage, interests, and goals.

*How to teach:* Your child writes and records a 2-minute self-introduction (heritage, personality, interests, goals) after you model a short one. Listen for verb accuracy and note errors by category.

- **Q:** Translate: 'My ancestors came from Michoacán and I am proud of my heritage.'
  **A:** Mis antepasados vinieron de Michoacán y estoy orgulloso/a de mi herencia.
- **Q:** How would you state a goal for this year in Spanish?
  **A:** Example: Este año quiero hablar con más fluidez y leer una novela en español.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Repaso del pretérito
*Student:* Review preterite regular and irregular forms with a 30-verb drill and a story chain.

*How to teach:* Drill 30 preterite verbs orally, then play a story chain: each person adds a sentence in the preterite to a shared story.

- **Q:** Give the preterite of: hacer (ella), poner (yo), traer (ellos), pedir (él), dormir (ellos).
  **A:** hizo, puse, trajeron, pidió, durmieron
- **Q:** Translate: 'We went to the beach and had a good time.'
  **A:** Fuimos a la playa y lo pasamos bien (nos divertimos).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pretérito vs. imperfecto
*Student:* Contrast the tenses in English and Spanish; rewrite a childhood memory using both correctly.

*How to teach:* Contrast English and Spanish past tenses with a timeline. Your child rewrites a childhood memory, labeling each verb B (background) or E (event).

- **Q:** Explain the tenses in: 'Llovía mucho cuando salimos de casa.'
  **A:** Llovía = imperfect (ongoing background); salimos = preterite (completed event that happened within it).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Lectura
*Student:* Read a short authentic blog post by a young Latino about identity and answer comprehension questions.

*How to teach:* Read the blog post together; your child answers comprehension questions and identifies the author's main idea and tone.

- **Q:** What strategy helps when you meet an unknown word in an authentic text?
  **A:** Use context, cognates, and word parts first; look it up only if it blocks the main idea.

### Day 5 (Friday): Evaluación
*Student:* Write a 200-word narrative 'Un recuerdo que me define' and take a preterite/imperfect quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child writes the 200-word narrative and takes the preterite/imperfect quiz.

- **Q:** Fill in: Cuando yo ___ (ser) pequeño, mi familia ___ (mudarse) a California.
  **A:** era; se mudó

**Assessment:** Narrative and grammar quiz.

**Answer key:** Narrative rubric ('Un recuerdo que me define', 200 words): 4 = clear memory with setting in the imperfect and events in the preterite, explains why it defines the writer, 10+ accurate past verbs, transitions; 3 = minor errors; 2 = tense confusion or under 150 words; 1 = incomplete. Quiz sample key: 1) Cuando tenía seis años, vivíamos en Fresno 2) Un día mi abuelo me regaló una guitarra 3) Ella dormía cuando sonó el teléfono 4) Ellos pidieron pizza 5) Yo fui al parque y vi a mi amiga 6) De niño, siempre jugaba al fútbol 7) Supe la verdad ayer (found out) 8) Hacía sol y los pájaros cantaban.

## Theatre & Performing Arts: Building the ensemble

**Prepare:** Clear an open space and get a notebook for the performance journal. Learn the rules of Zip-Zap-Zop and mirror so you can play along; recruit one or two family members for focus games and as the Day 5 audience. Pick a familiar fairy tale for the tableaux.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking theatre is only about the lead actor. Ensemble means every member serves the whole story; praise listening and support, not just big moments.
- Treating focus games as just 'fun.' Tie each game to a skill (concentration, eye contact, reacting) in the debrief.
- Tableaux that are flat lines facing the audience. Use levels (high, medium, low), clear focus, and open body positions.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is theatre?
*Student:* Discuss what makes theatre different from film; set up a performance journal with goals for the year.

*How to teach:* Discuss how theatre differs from film: live, shared space with the audience, no retakes, the audience's imagination fills in. The student sets up the journal with 3 goals for the year.

- **Q:** Name two ways theatre differs from film.
  **A:** Theatre is live and unrepeatable, with performers and audience sharing the same space and time; film is recorded, edited, and the camera controls what the viewer sees.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Focus games
*Student:* Play mirror, Zip-Zap-Zop (with family), and 'counting to 20' focus games; journal what improved concentration.

*How to teach:* Play mirror (partners mirror slowly so an observer can't tell the leader), Zip-Zap-Zop (pass energy with eye contact and a clap), and 'count to 20' (group counts one at a time; restart if two speak). Student journals what helped concentration.

- **Q:** What does the mirror game train?
  **A:** Focus, close observation, and responding to a partner — the leader must move slowly so they stay connected.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Trust & physical awareness
*Student:* Practice trust walks, neutral stance, and space-walking with tempo changes 1–10.

*How to teach:* Lead a trust walk (partner guides a 'blind' walker by the shoulder, safely). Teach neutral stance (feet under hips, arms relaxed, weight even, eyes forward). Space walk at tempos 1 (slowest) to 10 (fastest) filling empty spaces.

- **Q:** Describe neutral stance.
  **A:** Feet hip-width and parallel, weight balanced, knees soft, arms relaxed at sides, spine long, eyes forward, face relaxed — no character choices.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Group storytelling
*Student:* Build a one-word-at-a-time story and a tableau series of a fairy tale in three frozen images.

*How to teach:* Build a story one word at a time. Then create three frozen images (beginning, middle, end) of a fairy tale; check for levels and clear focus.

- **Q:** What is a tableau, and what makes it readable?
  **A:** A frozen stage picture; clear focus (everyone directs attention to the important point), varied levels, open body positions, and expressive faces make it readable.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect & check
*Student:* Perform a 3-tableau story for family and write a journal reflection on ensemble skills.

*How to teach:* Perform the 3-tableau story for family (with a title for each frame), then write a journal reflection on ensemble skills.

- **Q:** What does 'ensemble' mean in theatre?
  **A:** A group of performers working together as a unit, supporting each other rather than competing for focus.

**Assessment:** Tableau performance and journal.

**Answer key:** Tableau performance and journal rubric. 4 – Three clear, frozen images with varied levels, strong focus, and expression that tell the beginning, middle, and end; reflection names specific ensemble skills and personal goals. 3 (proficient) – Three readable tableaux with levels and focus; reflection describes ensemble skills. 2 – Images unclear or unstill; reflection vague. 1 – Incomplete.

## Lifetime Fitness: Baseline fitness testing

**Prepare:** Gather a measuring tape, stopwatch, sit-and-reach box (or a yardstick taped to a box), cones, and a notebook to serve as the fitness journal. Measure a flat 1-mile route (4 laps of a standard track) and look up current Fitnessgram Healthy Fitness Zone (HFZ) charts for your student's age and sex on the CDE site. Have the student wear athletic shoes and bring water.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a low score is a 'fail.' Explain that a baseline is just a starting point; the goal is personal improvement and reaching the Healthy Fitness Zone.
- Rushing push-ups or curl-ups to get a higher count. Fitnessgram uses a cadence (1 rep every 3 seconds); reps that break form or cadence do not count.
- Bouncing in the sit-and-reach. Only a slow, held reach (1 second) counts; bouncing risks strain and inflates scores.

### Day 1 (Monday): Course launch & PACER prep
*Student:* Learn the five fitness components; measure resting heart rate and complete a 1-mile walk/run for time.

*How to teach:* Introduce the five health-related fitness components (cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, body composition). Have the student take resting heart rate for 60 seconds at the wrist or neck, warm up 5 minutes, then walk/run the measured mile while you time it. Record time and how they felt (RPE 1–10) in the journal.

- **Q:** Name the five health-related components of fitness.
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory (aerobic) endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, and body composition.
- **Q:** Where can you find your pulse, and why count for a full 60 seconds at rest?
  **A:** At the radial artery (thumb side of the wrist) or carotid artery (side of the neck, press gently). A full minute at rest gives a more accurate count than a short sample.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Muscular endurance
*Student:* Complete curl-up and 90° push-up tests using Fitnessgram cadence; record results.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate a curl-up (knees bent ~140°, feet flat, hands slide across a 3-inch strip) and a 90° push-up (elbows bend to 90°, body straight). Play or tap a cadence of one rep every 3 seconds; the test stops at the second form correction or when cadence is broken. Record both counts.

- **Q:** What is the Fitnessgram cadence for curl-ups and push-ups?
  **A:** One repetition every 3 seconds (20 per minute).
- **Q:** What does the 90° push-up test measure?
  **A:** Upper-body muscular strength and endurance.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Flexibility
*Student:* Complete back-saver sit-and-reach and trunk lift; practice correct technique first.

*How to teach:* Model the back-saver sit-and-reach: one leg straight against the box, other knee bent with foot flat, reach forward slowly four times and hold the fourth for 1 second; test each side. Then the trunk lift: lie face down and lift the upper body slowly, hold, and measure chin height (max 12 in counted). Practice once before recording.

- **Q:** Why is the sit-and-reach done one leg at a time ('back-saver')?
  **A:** Bending one knee reduces strain on the lower back while still measuring hamstring flexibility of the straight leg.
- **Q:** In the trunk lift, why is the score capped at 12 inches?
  **A:** Lifting higher than 12 inches can hyperextend and stress the lower back; it adds no health benefit.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Body composition & activity log
*Student:* Measure waist-to-height ratio and start a 7-day activity log with a phone or pedometer.

*How to teach:* Measure waist circumference at the navel and height in the same units; compute waist ÷ height. Explain that a ratio under about 0.5 is generally considered healthy and that this is a screening tool, not a judgment. Set up a 7-day activity log (minutes, type, intensity) using a phone or pedometer.

- **Q:** A student's waist is 30 in and height is 66 in. What is the waist-to-height ratio, and is it under 0.5?
  **A:** 30 ÷ 66 ≈ 0.45, which is under 0.5 (the general healthy guideline).
- **Q:** How many minutes of activity per day do guidelines recommend for teens?
  **A:** At least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily, including vigorous, muscle-strengthening, and bone-strengthening activity at least 3 days per week.

### Day 5 (Friday): Interpret results
*Student:* Compare results to Healthy Fitness Zones and write three findings in a fitness journal.

*How to teach:* Help the student look up the HFZ chart for their age/sex and mark each result as 'in HFZ' or 'needs improvement.' They write three findings in the journal (one strength, one need, one surprise). Check that each finding cites an actual number.

- **Q:** What does 'Healthy Fitness Zone' mean?
  **A:** A Fitnessgram criterion score that indicates a level of fitness associated with good health for that age and sex — it is a health standard, not a ranking against others.
- **Q:** Which fitness component does the 1-mile run mainly measure?
  **A:** Aerobic capacity (cardiorespiratory endurance).

**Assessment:** Baseline fitness profile.

**Answer key:** Baseline fitness profile rubric (4 levels). 4 – All tests completed with correct technique; every result recorded with units, compared with the correct HFZ, and three findings each cite data. 3 (proficient) – All tests recorded and compared with HFZ; findings reference results. 2 – Some tests missing or HFZ comparison incomplete. 1 – Data mostly missing or unlabeled.

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): What engineers do

**Prepare:** Buy a bound grid-paper notebook (pages numbered, not spiral) and a pen. Bookmark the Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook (bls.gov/ooh) for career data. Find a short video interview with a practicing engineer (e.g. 'Engineer Your Life', TryEngineering or a company career video), or arrange a call with an engineer you know.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking engineers mostly fix engines or only do math. Engineers design solutions to problems — products, structures, systems, processes — and spend much of their time communicating and testing.
- Treating the engineering notebook like a scratch pad. It is a legal, permanent record: bound, numbered pages, dated entries in pen, mistakes crossed out with one line (not erased), and no torn-out pages.
- Confusing science and engineering. Scientists ask 'why does this happen?'; engineers use that knowledge to ask 'how can we make something that solves this problem?'

### Day 1 (Monday): Engineering careers
*Student:* Research five engineering disciplines (mechanical, civil, electrical, biomedical, industrial) and record salary, education, and a California employer for each.

*How to teach:* Have the student research five disciplines in the Occupational Outlook Handbook, filling a table: what they design, typical education (bachelor's in that engineering field; licensure as a PE later for some), median pay, and a California employer (e.g. mechanical — Tesla; civil — Caltrans; electrical — PG&E; biomedical — Medtronic or Edwards Lifesciences; industrial — a manufacturing plant or Amazon fulfillment). Discuss which surprised them.

- **Q:** What does a civil engineer design? A biomedical engineer?
  **A:** Civil: infrastructure such as roads, bridges, water systems and buildings. Biomedical: medical devices, equipment and technologies, such as prosthetics, implants and imaging machines.
- **Q:** What education do most engineering jobs require?
  **A:** A bachelor's degree in an engineering field (usually ABET-accredited); some roles require a Professional Engineer (PE) license or a graduate degree.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Engineering notebook
*Student:* Set up a bound engineering notebook with table of contents, dated entries, and signing conventions.

*How to teach:* Model the notebook rules: write the table of contents on pages 1–2, number every page, date and title each entry, use pen, cross out mistakes with one line, and sign at the bottom. Have the student set it up and make the first entry (today's career table).

- **Q:** Why must an engineering notebook be bound, in pen, with no torn-out pages?
  **A:** It is a permanent, trustworthy record of the design process and can serve as evidence of when ideas were created (for patents or legal disputes).
- **Q:** How do you correct a mistake in the notebook?
  **A:** Draw a single line through it so it's still readable, and initial/date it — never erase or white it out.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Design that changed the world
*Student:* Read about an innovation (e.g., the Golden Gate Bridge, the iPhone) and summarize the problem it solved.

*How to teach:* Have the student read about one innovation — e.g. the Golden Gate Bridge (opened 1937; chief engineer Joseph Strauss with key design work by Charles Ellis and Leon Moisseiff) or the iPhone (2007) — and summarize the problem, constraints and solution in the notebook.

- **Q:** What problem did the Golden Gate Bridge solve, and what made it hard?
  **A:** It connected San Francisco to Marin County across the Golden Gate strait, replacing slow ferries; challenges included strong currents, deep water, fog, high winds and earthquakes.
- **Q:** What problem did the original iPhone address?
  **A:** Combining a phone, music player and internet device into one easy-to-use touchscreen device, replacing small physical keyboards.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Interview
*Student:* Watch or conduct an interview with a practicing engineer and record five takeaways.

*How to teach:* Watch the interview (or hold one). The student records five takeaways, focusing on what the engineer does day to day and how they got there.

- **Q:** What is one skill besides math and science that engineers say they use daily?
  **A:** Communication — writing, presenting and teamwork (also problem solving and project management).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a 300-word career reflection and submit the notebook for a format check.

*How to teach:* The student writes the 300-word reflection; check the notebook format against the list in the assessment key and score the reflection with the rubric.

- **Q:** Which engineering discipline interests you most, and why?
  **A:** Answers vary; a strong answer cites specific tasks and data from the week's research.

**Assessment:** Notebook check and career reflection.

**Answer key:** Notebook format check (pass when all present): numbered pages; table of contents on the first pages; each entry dated and titled; written in pen; errors struck through with a single line and initialed; blank space crossed out; entries signed (and witnessed on key pages). Career reflection rubric (300 words, 4 levels): 4 = compares at least two disciplines using the researched data (typical tasks, education — usually a bachelor's degree in engineering — and salary), connects to the student's interests and takeaways from the engineer interview, and names a next step; 3 = data and personal connection but little comparison; 2 = general opinions with little evidence; 1 = incomplete.
