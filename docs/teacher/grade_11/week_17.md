## English 11 – American Literature: Realist and Naturalist short fiction

**Prepare:** Chopin 'The Story of an Hour,' Bierce 'Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge,' Gilman 'The Yellow Wallpaper,' London 'To Build a Fire' (Gutenberg/CommonLit). Parent note: hanging, mental illness, and death.

**Watch for:**

## Algebra II: Radical functions, equations, and inverses

**Prepare:** String and a washer (pendulum), a meter stick, stopwatch. Desmos.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting to check radical-equation answers. Squaring can introduce extraneous solutions; substitute back every time.
- Writing f⁻¹(x) as 1/f(x). The inverse undoes f; it is not the reciprocal.

### Day 1 (Monday): Radical graphs
*Student:* Graph transformations of √x and ∛x and state domain and range.

*How to teach:* Graph √x and ∛x, then transformations; student states domain/range for six. Today's task from the plan: Graph transformations of √x and ∛x and state domain and range. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** State domain and range of y = √(x − 2) + 3.
  **A:** Domain x ≥ 2; range y ≥ 3.
- **Q:** What is the domain of y = ∛(x + 1)?
  **A:** All real numbers (cube roots of negatives exist).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Radical equations
*Student:* Solve √(x+3) = x−3 type equations and identify extraneous roots.

*How to teach:* Model isolate the radical, square both sides, solve, CHECK. Student solves six. Today's task from the plan: Solve √(x+3) = x−3 type equations and identify extraneous roots. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Solve √(x + 3) = x − 3.
  **A:** x + 3 = x² − 6x + 9 → x² − 7x + 6 = 0 → x = 1 or 6. Check: x = 1 gives 2 = −2 ✗; x = 6 gives 3 = 3 ✓. Solution x = 6.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Composition
*Student:* Compose functions and evaluate f(g(x)) in context (currency conversions).

*How to teach:* Model f(g(x)) as 'do g first.' Use a currency chain: dollars → euros → yen with made-up rates. Today's task from the plan: Compose functions and evaluate f(g(x)) in context (currency conversions). Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** If f(x) = 2x + 1 and g(x) = x², find f(g(3)) and g(f(3)).
  **A:** f(g(3)) = f(9) = 19. g(f(3)) = g(7) = 49. Order matters.
- **Q:** If E(d) = 0.9d converts dollars to euros and Y(e) = 160e converts euros to yen, what is Y(E(d))?
  **A:** Y(E(d)) = 160(0.9d) = 144d yen per d dollars.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Inverses
*Student:* Find inverses algebraically, verify by composition, and graph reflections over y = x; model pendulum period T = 2π√(L/g) and invert to find length.

*How to teach:* Model swap x and y, solve for y, verify f(f⁻¹(x)) = x. Then time 10 swings for three string lengths and compare to T = 2π√(L/g). Today's task from the plan: Find inverses algebraically, verify by composition, and graph reflections over y = x; model pendulum period T = 2π√(L/g) and invert to find length. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Find the inverse of f(x) = 3x − 4 and of g(x) = x³ + 1.
  **A:** f⁻¹(x) = (x + 4)/3; g⁻¹(x) = ∛(x − 1).
- **Q:** How long should a pendulum be for a 2-second period (g = 9.8 m/s²)?
  **A:** L = g(T/2π)² = 9.8·(1/π)² ≈ 0.99 m.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on radical equations and inverses.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (about 30 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Quiz on radical equations and inverses. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Solve √(2x + 1) = 5.
  **A:** 2x + 1 = 25, x = 12. Check: √25 = 5 ✓.

**Assessment:** Radical functions quiz.

**Answer key:** Sample key: 1) √(x−4) = 3 → x=13. 2) √(x+7) = x+1 → x=2 (x=−3 extraneous). 3) ∛(x−2) = −3 → x=−25. 4) f(x)=2x+5, g(x)=x−1: f(g(x)) = 2x+3. 5) inverse of y=(x−1)/2 is y=2x+1. 6) inverse of y=√x (x≥0) is y=x², x≥0. 7) Verify inverses by showing f(g(x)) = x. Scoring 1 pt each plus 1 pt for checks shown.

## Physics of the Universe: Materials science

**Prepare:** Samples to test: copper wire, aluminum foil, plastic spoon, rubber band, glass, wood, pencil graphite (sharpened at both ends), salt water. The circuit tester from Week 14.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all nonmetals are insulators. Graphite (carbon) conducts because of delocalized electrons in its layers.
- Thinking semiconductors are just poor conductors. Their conductivity can be precisely controlled by doping, light, and voltage, which is why they make switches (transistors).

### Day 1 (Monday): Conductors & insulators
*Student:* Test household materials in a circuit for conductivity.

*How to teach:* The student predicts, then tests each sample in the circuit and records whether the bulb lights. Today's task from the plan: Test household materials in a circuit for conductivity. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Which samples conduct and why?
  **A:** Copper, aluminum, graphite, and salt water: metals and graphite have free electrons; salt water has mobile ions.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Molecular structure
*Student:* Research how molecular structure gives polymers flexibility and metals conductivity.

*How to teach:* Research how bonding gives properties: metals (sea of electrons), polymers (long chains), ceramics/glass (rigid networks). Today's task from the plan: Research how molecular structure gives polymers flexibility and metals conductivity. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why are metals good conductors and malleable?
  **A:** Their valence electrons are delocalized: they move freely to carry current, and the layers of ions can slide without breaking bonds.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Semiconductors
*Student:* Read about silicon chips and how doping works.

*How to teach:* Read about silicon chips and doping. Today's task from the plan: Read about silicon chips and how doping works. Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** What is doping?
  **A:** Adding small amounts of impurity: phosphorus adds extra electrons (n-type), boron creates 'holes' (p-type), changing how silicon conducts.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Communication
*Student:* Make an infographic on a designed material (Kevlar, Gore-Tex, graphene).

*How to teach:* The student builds an infographic on Kevlar, Gore-Tex, or graphene explaining structure → property → use. Today's task from the plan: Make an infographic on a designed material (Kevlar, Gore-Tex, graphene). Use the questions below as a quick oral check; if an answer is shaky, reteach with one more example before moving on.

- **Q:** Why is Kevlar so strong?
  **A:** Its long, rigid polymer chains line up and are linked by hydrogen bonds between chains, so force spreads across many bonds.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Present the infographic.

*How to teach:* The student presents the infographic (3–5 minutes). Today's task from the plan: Present the infographic. Grade it the same day with the key below and have the student correct every missed item with a one-line note on what went wrong.

- **Q:** Why is graphene a promising material?
  **A:** It is a single layer of carbon atoms in a hexagonal lattice; it is extremely strong, thin, and conducts electricity and heat very well.

**Assessment:** Infographic rubric.

**Answer key:** Infographic rubric (4-point per row): accurate molecular/atomic structure; clear link from structure to property; real-world application; at least two credible cited sources; readable design. Proficient = correct structure-property explanation with one minor gap.

## U.S. History & Geography: Continuity and Change in the Twentieth Century: The home front and Japanese American incarceration

**Prepare:** Print rationing coupons and war bond posters, a map of wartime migration to the West Coast, Executive Order 8802 (1941), the 1942 Bracero agreement summary, Executive Order 9066 (DocsTeach), Dorothea Lange and Ansel Adams Manzanar photographs (Calisphere/LOC), Korematsu v. United States (1944) majority and Murphy dissent excerpts, and the Civil Liberties Act of 1988.

**Watch for:**
- Students think most incarcerated Japanese Americans were noncitizens. About two-thirds of the roughly 120,000 people removed were U.S. citizens.
- Students think Korematsu was overruled at the time. The Court upheld it 6–3 in 1944; his conviction was vacated in 1983, and the Court said in Trump v. Hawaii (2018) that Korematsu was 'gravely wrong'.
- Students think the Bracero Program ended with the war. It ran from 1942 to 1964.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mobilization
*Student:* Study rationing coupons, war bonds, and the growth of California shipyards and aircraft plants; map wartime migration to the West Coast.

*How to teach:* Study rationing, war bonds and the growth of California shipyards and aircraft plants; map wartime migration.

- **Q:** How did WWII change California's economy?
  **A:** Massive federal spending built shipyards (e.g., Kaiser in Richmond) and aircraft plants (Douglas, Lockheed), drawing hundreds of thousands of workers, including many Black and women workers.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): EO 8802 and Bracero Program
*Student:* Analyze A. Philip Randolph's threatened March on Washington, EO 8802, and the 1942 Bracero agreement.

*How to teach:* Analyze Randolph's threatened march, EO 8802 and the Bracero agreement.

- **Q:** What did Executive Order 8802 do?
  **A:** Banned discrimination in defense industries and government based on race, creed, color or national origin, and created the Fair Employment Practices Committee.
- **Q:** What was the Bracero Program?
  **A:** A 1942–1964 agreement bringing Mexican workers on short-term contracts to fill U.S. farm and railroad labor shortages.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Incarceration
*Student:* Read EO 9066 and compare Dorothea Lange's (censored) and Ansel Adams' Manzanar photographs from Calisphere.

*How to teach:* Read EO 9066 and compare Lange's (censored) and Adams' Manzanar photographs.

- **Q:** Why were Lange's photographs impounded?
  **A:** They showed the injustice and hardship of incarceration too clearly for the Army's liking.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Korematsu

*How to teach:* Read Korematsu excerpts and the 1988 Civil Liberties Act; discuss in a recorded conversation.

- **Q:** What did Justice Murphy argue in his dissent?
  **A:** That the exclusion order fell 'into the ugly abyss of racism' and was based on racial prejudice, not real military necessity.
- **Q:** What did the Civil Liberties Act of 1988 provide?
  **A:** A formal apology and $20,000 to each surviving incarcerated Japanese American.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check

*How to teach:* The student writes a 400-word response.

**Assessment:** Civil liberties response.

## Lifetime Fitness: Badminton and racquet strategy

**Prepare:** Get badminton rackets and shuttles, or book time at a community center gym. Mark six 'corners' with cones on a rough court (front, mid, back on each side). Read badminton rally scoring: to 21, win by 2, cap 30.

**Watch for:**
- Hitting overhead shots with a stiff arm. Power comes from the kinetic chain: legs, hips, trunk rotation, then shoulder, elbow, wrist/forearm.
- Staying where you hit. Return to base (center) after every shot.
- Thinking only the server scores (that was the old system). Badminton uses rally scoring — a point on every rally.

### Day 1 (Monday): Grip & clears
*Student:* Learn forehand grip and overhead clear; practice 30 clears.

*How to teach:* Teach the forehand grip and overhead clear: turn sideways, racket up, reach high, rotate and hit high and deep to the back. 30 clears.

- **Q:** What is the purpose of a clear?
  **A:** To hit high and deep to the opponent's back court, buying time and pushing them back.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Drops & smashes
*Student:* Practice drop shots and smashes, focusing on deception and kinetic chain.

*How to teach:* Practice the drop (looks like a clear, softly lands near the net) and the smash (steep, powerful downward shot). Emphasize the same preparation for deception and the kinetic chain.

- **Q:** Why use the same preparation for clear, drop, and smash?
  **A:** Deception — the opponent can't tell which shot is coming until late.
- **Q:** Describe the kinetic chain in a smash.
  **A:** Force is transferred sequentially from the legs through hip and trunk rotation to the shoulder, elbow, forearm, and wrist.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Footwork
*Student:* Learn base position and six-corner footwork drills.

*How to teach:* Teach base position (center, slight crouch, racket up) and six-corner footwork: move to each cone with chasse steps or a lunge and return to base. Repeat in sets of 30 s.

- **Q:** Why return to base after each shot?
  **A:** It minimizes the distance to any next shot so you can cover the whole court.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Strategy
*Student:* Play games focusing on moving opponents front-to-back.

*How to teach:* Play games with the strategy goal of moving the opponent front to back (clear then drop). Teach rally scoring to 21.

- **Q:** Describe badminton singles scoring.
  **A:** Rally scoring: a point on every rally; games to 21, win by 2, capped at 30 (30-29 wins).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Skill test and written comparison of pickleball and badminton strategy.

*How to teach:* Skill test: 10 clears to the back third and 10 drops to the front third; student writes a pickleball vs. badminton strategy comparison.

- **Q:** How is strategy similar in pickleball and badminton?
  **A:** Both use placement and changes of depth/pace to move opponents and create openings; both reward patience and returning to a ready position.

**Assessment:** Skill test and comparison.

**Answer key:** Skill test: proficient = 6/10 clears land in back third and 6/10 drops land in front third with correct grip and preparation. Comparison rubric: proficient answer notes similarities (placement, moving opponents, ready position) and differences (pickleball's kitchen and double-bounce rule and soft game vs. badminton's overhead power shots, shuttle never bounces, rally scoring to 21 vs. pickleball's side-out scoring to 11).

## Introduction to Engineering Design (CTE): Reverse engineering: analysis and redesign

**Prepare:** Have calipers, Onshape and the disassembled product. Preview how to identify plastics (recycling codes molded into parts) and common processes (injection molding marks: ejector pin circles, parting lines, gates).

**Watch for:**
- Guessing materials without evidence. Use clues: magnets stick to steel (not aluminum or most stainless), molded recycling codes on plastics, weight/density.
- Assuming all plastic parts are 3D printed. Mass-produced plastic parts are usually injection molded — look for parting lines, ejector-pin marks and draft angles.
- Proposing improvements with no justification. Tie every redesign to a problem observed (cost, durability, ease of assembly, user complaint).

### Day 1 (Monday): Measure parts
*Student:* Measure two key parts with calipers.

*How to teach:* Measure two key parts with calipers; record dimensions on sketches.

- **Q:** Why choose 'key' parts for modeling?
  **A:** They carry the main function or have critical fits, so their dimensions matter most.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): CAD
*Student:* Model the two parts in Onshape.

*How to teach:* Model the two parts in Onshape from the measurements.

- **Q:** What tool checks that your CAD part matches the real one?
  **A:** The Measure tool, compared against caliper readings.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Material & process
*Student:* Identify the material and likely manufacturing process for each part.

*How to teach:* Identify material and process for each part, citing evidence.

- **Q:** A plastic part has small circles on the inside and a thin line around its edge. How was it likely made?
  **A:** Injection molding — the circles are ejector-pin marks and the line is the parting line where the mold halves met.
- **Q:** How can you tell steel from aluminum quickly?
  **A:** A magnet sticks to ordinary steel but not to aluminum; aluminum is also much lighter.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Redesign
*Student:* Propose an improvement with sketches and justification.

*How to teach:* Propose an improvement with sketches and justification (e.g. snap fits instead of screws to speed assembly).

- **Q:** What is Design for Assembly (DFA)?
  **A:** Designing products to be easy and cheap to assemble — fewer parts and fasteners, parts that only fit one way, self-aligning features.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Reassemble the product and submit a reverse engineering report.

*How to teach:* Reassemble the product, test it works, and submit the report.

- **Q:** Why is it important that the product still works after reassembly?
  **A:** It shows the disassembly was documented and careful, and confirms understanding of how parts work together.

**Assessment:** Reverse engineering report.

**Answer key:** Reverse engineering report rubric (4 levels): 4 = includes purpose, black-box diagram, parts list, two accurately modeled parts with drawings, evidence-based material and manufacturing-process identification for each part (e.g. 'ejector pin marks and draft → injection-molded ABS'), a sketched and justified improvement (e.g. fewer fasteners for Design for Assembly), and confirmation that the product was reassembled and works; 3 = all parts, less evidence; 2 = missing models or analysis; 1 = incomplete.
