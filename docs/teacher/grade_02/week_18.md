# Teacher's Guide — 2nd Grade, Week 18

## ELA 2: Alliteration, repetition, and two-syllable words

**Prepare:** Get Where the Sidewalk Ends (Shel Silverstein) or a library poetry anthology, Mother Goose rhymes, and the 'Peter Piper' tongue twister. Copy Christina Rossetti's 'Who Has Seen the Wind?' Prepare a mid-year phonics inventory (vowel teams, r-controlled, two-syllable words) and a fluency passage.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking alliteration means words that rhyme. Alliteration repeats the BEGINNING sound (Peter Piper picked).
- Splitting two-syllable words between vowel-team letters (ra-in-coat). Keep vowel teams together: rain-coat.

### Day 1 (Monday): Alliteration
*Student:* Read tongue twisters ('Peter Piper') and Mother Goose rhymes; underline repeated beginning sounds. Write your own alliterative sentence about your name.

*How to teach:* Read 'Peter Piper' and Mother Goose rhymes. Underline repeated beginning sounds. The child writes an alliterative sentence about their name.

- **Q:** What sound repeats in 'Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers'?
  **A:** The /p/ sound.
- **Q:** Write an alliterative sentence for the name Sam.
  **A:** Example: Silly Sam sings sweet songs.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Repeated lines
*Student:* Read Christina Rossetti's 'Who Has Seen the Wind?' and talk about how the repeated question gives the poem its rhythm and meaning.

*How to teach:* Read Rossetti's 'Who Has Seen the Wind?' Discuss how the repeated question gives rhythm and meaning.

- **Q:** How do we know the wind is there if no one sees it?
  **A:** The leaves hang trembling and the trees bow down their heads — we see what the wind does.
- **Q:** Why does the poet repeat 'Who has seen the wind?'
  **A:** It makes a rhythm and reminds us that no one can see the wind itself.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Two-syllable words
*Student:* Phonics: split two-syllable words with long vowels (re-peat, pa-per, rain-coat, ex-plain) and read 20 words. Clap syllables in poem lines.

*How to teach:* Split two-syllable words (re-peat, pa-per, rain-coat, ex-plain) and read 20. Clap syllables in poem lines.

- **Q:** Split 'paper' and 'raincoat.'
  **A:** pa-per; rain-coat.
- **Q:** How many syllables in 'explain'?
  **A:** 2 (ex-plain).

### Day 4 (Thursday): List poem
*Student:* Write a list poem about 'Things I Hear at Night' using describing words and a repeated line. Illustrate it.

*How to teach:* Write a list poem 'Things I Hear at Night' with describing words and a repeated line. Illustrate it.

- **Q:** Give a line for a night-sounds list poem.
  **A:** Example: the tick-tock of the hallway clock.
- **Q:** What is a repeated line?
  **A:** A line used more than once, like 'At night I hear…'

### Day 5 (Friday): Week 18 mid-year check
*Student:* Mid-year assessment: phonics inventory (vowel teams, r-controlled, two-syllable words), fluency read, and a poem comprehension question about rhythm and repetition.

*How to teach:* Give the mid-year phonics inventory, a fluency read, and the poem comprehension question.

- **Q:** What does repetition do in a poem?
  **A:** It gives rhythm and makes an idea stand out.
- **Q:** Read and split 'repeat.'
  **A:** re-peat.

**Assessment:** Mid-year phonics inventory and fluency record; list poem.

**Answer key:** Mid-year phonics inventory: record correct/incorrect for vowel teams (rain, play, tree, beach, boat, snow), r-controlled (car, corn, her, bird, turn), and two-syllable words (repeat, paper, raincoat, explain); note patterns to review. Fluency: record WCPM and compare to fall. Poem question: 'Why does Rossetti repeat "Who has seen the wind?"' — model answer: the repeated question gives the poem a rhythm and reminds us we can't see the wind, only what it does (leaves trembling, trees bowing). List poem rubric: 3 = 6+ items about night sounds with describing words and a repeated line.

## Math 2: Measurement data and line plots

**Prepare:** Inch ruler, cm ruler, 15 pencils or crayons of different lengths. Prepare data for Friday's test (e.g., lengths in inches: 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 7, 7, 8; colors: red 6, blue 9, green 4, yellow 3).

**Watch for:**
- Leaving numbers off the scale when no object has that length. Every number in the range stays on the scale, even with no X.
- Confusing 'most common length' with 'longest.' Most common = tallest stack of Xs; longest = the X farthest right.

### Day 1 (Monday): Measure a collection
*Student:* Measure 15 pencils or crayons to the nearest inch and list the lengths.

*How to teach:* The child measures 15 pencils or crayons to the nearest inch (starting at 0) and lists the lengths. Double-check two or three together.

- **Q:** A crayon ends between 3 and 4 inches, closer to 4. What do you record?
  **A:** 4 inches.
- **Q:** Why start at 0 on the ruler?
  **A:** So you measure the full length.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Line plot
*Student:* Draw a number line scale and place an X above each length to make a line plot; title and label it.

*How to teach:* Draw a number line from the shortest to the longest length (e.g., 2–7 inches). Place one X above the number for each object. Add a title and the label 'Length in inches.'

- **Q:** Lengths 3, 4, 4, 5. How many Xs go above 4?
  **A:** 2.
- **Q:** If no object is 6 inches, do you still write 6 on the line?
  **A:** Yes — write 6 with no X above it.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hand spans
*Student:* Measure hand spans of family members in centimeters and make a second line plot.

*How to teach:* Measure family hand spans (thumb tip to pinky tip, hand stretched) in centimeters and make a second line plot with a cm scale.

- **Q:** Spans 15, 17, 17, 18, 20 cm. Which length is most common?
  **A:** 17 cm.
- **Q:** What is the difference between the longest and shortest?
  **A:** 5 cm; 20 − 15 = 5.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Interpret
*Student:* Answer questions about the line plots: most common length, longest, shortest, and difference between them.

*How to teach:* Ask: most common length, longest, shortest, and the difference. The child answers in full sentences.

- **Q:** Lengths 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 7. Which is most common?
  **A:** 5 inches (3 Xs).
- **Q:** What is the difference between the longest and shortest?
  **A:** 4 inches; 7 − 3 = 4.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 test
*Student:* Make a line plot from given measurements and a bar graph from given data; answer 6 questions.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test: a line plot from given measurements and a bar graph from given data, then 6 questions.

- **Q:** How many Xs for 10 measurements?
  **A:** 10 Xs.
- **Q:** Red 6, blue 9. How many more blue?
  **A:** 3.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test (suggested data 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 7, 7, 8 inches): line plot with scale 3–8 and Xs: 3(1), 4(2), 5(3), 6(1), 7(2), 8(1). 1) Most common length: 5 inches. 2) Longest: 8 in. 3) Shortest: 3 in. 4) Longest − shortest: 5 in. 5) How many objects are 6 inches or longer? 4. Bar graph (red 6, blue 9, green 4, yellow 3): 6) How many more blue than green? 5. Construction rubric: title, labeled scale with equal spacing, all data shown.

## Science 2: Pollination

**Prepare:** Gather a flower to dissect (lily or tulip works well), cheese puffs or colored chalk dust, paper flowers, cotton swabs, and a tally sheet. Note bee-sting allergies before observing pollinators outside.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking bees visit flowers to help plants. They visit for nectar and pollen as food; pollination happens by accident.
- Thinking only bees pollinate. Butterflies, hummingbirds, bats, moths, and wind also pollinate.

### Day 1 (Monday): Flower parts
*Student:* Dissect a flower (or use a diagram) to find petals and pollen; draw and label.

*How to teach:* Dissect a flower and find petals and pollen; draw and label.

- **Q:** Where is pollen found?
  **A:** On the tips (anthers) inside the flower.
- **Q:** What do petals do?
  **A:** Their colors and scents attract pollinators.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pollinator observation
*Student:* Watch flowers outside for 15 minutes and tally bees, butterflies, and birds.

*How to teach:* Watch flowers outside for 15 minutes and tally visitors.

- **Q:** Which pollinator did you see most?
  **A:** Answer from tally.
- **Q:** Why do bees visit flowers?
  **A:** To get nectar and pollen for food.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cheese puff model
*Student:* Use fingers dusted with cheese puff powder to 'visit' paper flowers; observe how 'pollen' moves.

*How to teach:* Dust fingers with cheese-puff powder and 'visit' paper flowers.

- **Q:** What happened to the powder?
  **A:** It moved from flower to flower.
- **Q:** What does the powder represent?
  **A:** Pollen.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Pollinator research
*Student:* Read about bees, hummingbirds, and bats as pollinators; record one fact each.

*How to teach:* Read about bees, hummingbirds, and bats as pollinators; record one fact each.

- **Q:** How do hummingbirds pollinate?
  **A:** Pollen sticks to their heads and beaks while they drink nectar.
- **Q:** When do bats pollinate?
  **A:** At night.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Explain why plants need pollinators and pollinators need plants.

*How to teach:* Explain why plants need pollinators and pollinators need plants.

- **Q:** Why do plants need pollinators?
  **A:** To move pollen so they can make seeds.
- **Q:** Why do pollinators need plants?
  **A:** For food — nectar and pollen.

**Assessment:** Pollination model and explanation.

**Answer key:** Model explanation: dusty fingers (like a bee's body) picked up 'pollen' from one flower and left some on the next, showing how pollinators move pollen. Explanation (3 = proficient): 'Plants need pollinators to move pollen so they can make seeds; pollinators need plants for food (nectar and pollen).'

## People Who Make a Difference: Diplomacy and solving conflicts

**Prepare:** Photo of United Nations headquarters (New York) and of the 1945 Charter signing in San Francisco; problem scenario cards.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the UN is a country: it is an organization where many countries meet to work for peace.
- Thinking conflicts must end with a winner: diplomacy looks for solutions both sides accept.

### Day 1 (Monday): Diplomacy
*Student:* Learn that diplomats talk to solve problems between nations; role-play two 'countries' solving a water-sharing problem.

*How to teach:* Teach that diplomats talk to solve problems; role-play a water-sharing problem.

- **Q:** What is diplomacy?
  **A:** Talking and working out agreements to solve problems between nations.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): United Nations
*Student:* Learn about the United Nations, founded in San Francisco in 1945; look at a photo of the signing.

*How to teach:* Learn the UN was founded in San Francisco in 1945; look at the signing photo.

- **Q:** Where was the UN Charter signed?
  **A:** San Francisco, California, in 1945.
- **Q:** Why was the UN created?
  **A:** To help countries keep peace and work together after World War II.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Conflict resolution
*Student:* Practice peaceful problem-solving steps with scenario cards.

*How to teach:* Practice peaceful problem-solving steps with scenarios.

- **Q:** What are the peaceful problem-solving steps?
  **A:** Calm down, say the problem, listen, think of solutions, agree on one.

### Day 4 (Thursday): When talking fails
*Student:* Discuss briefly that sometimes nations use military force and why peace is preferred.

*How to teach:* Discuss that sometimes nations use military force and why peace is preferred.

- **Q:** Why is peace better than fighting?
  **A:** People stay safe, and nations can trade and work together.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 check
*Student:* Quiz on trade, cultural contacts, treaties, and diplomacy.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 4 quiz.

- **Q:** What is a conflict?
  **A:** A disagreement or fight.

**Assessment:** Role-play and unit quiz.

**Answer key:** Role-play: two 'countries' share water fairly by talking (state the problem, listen, offer ideas, agree). Unit 4 quiz: trade (imports/exports), cultural contact (cherry tree gift), treaty (agreement between nations), diplomacy (talking to solve problems), UN founded 1945 in San Francisco. Proficient = peaceful solution reached + 80% quiz.

## Art & Music 2: Clay forms: pinch pots

**Prepare:** Air-dry clay, forks, toothpicks, placemat, water cup; household spheres, cubes, cylinders; paint for later.

**Watch for:**
- Pinching walls too thin or uneven: rotate the pot and pinch evenly.
- Attaching a handle without scoring: scratch both surfaces and add a little water (score and slip).

### Day 1 (Monday): 2-D vs. 3-D
*Student:* Compare shapes (flat) and forms (sphere, cube, cylinder) using household objects; draw a circle and roll a clay sphere.

*How to teach:* Compare flat shapes with forms (ball = sphere, box = cube, can = cylinder); draw a circle and roll a sphere.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a circle and a sphere?
  **A:** A circle is flat (2-D); a sphere is a 3-D form like a ball.
- **Q:** What form is a soup can?
  **A:** A cylinder.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pinch pot
*Student:* Make a pinch pot by pressing a thumb into a clay ball and pinching walls evenly; follow cleanup steps.

*How to teach:* Press a thumb into a clay ball and pinch walls evenly while turning; clean up.

- **Q:** How do you make the walls even?
  **A:** Pinch gently while turning the pot.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Add texture
*Student:* Use forks and toothpicks to press textures into the pot and add a small clay animal handle.

*How to teach:* Press textures with forks and toothpicks; add a small animal handle with score and slip.

- **Q:** How do you attach clay pieces?
  **A:** Score (scratch) both pieces, add slip or water, press together.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Dry and paint
*Student:* Let the pot dry, then paint it with a pattern using colors from Unit 3.

*How to teach:* When dry, paint a pattern using colors from Unit 3.

- **Q:** Why wait until it's dry?
  **A:** Wet clay smears and paint won't stick well.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Show the pot and describe its form and texture and one thing that was hard to make.

*How to teach:* Show the pot; describe form, texture and a challenge.

- **Q:** Is your pot a shape or a form?
  **A:** A form (3-D).

**Assessment:** Finished pinch pot and reflection.

**Answer key:** Pinch pot: even walls, stable base, pressed texture, small animal handle attached securely, painted pattern. Reflection describes form (3-D, like a bowl or cylinder) and texture and one challenge. Proficient = all.

## PE 2: Catching high and low

**Prepare:** Scarves, soft ball, beanbag; (optional) water balloon for egg toss outdoors.

**Watch for:**
- Using thumbs together for a low ball: low balls use pinkies together.
- Stiff arms: 'give' by pulling the ball in toward the body.

### Day 1 (Monday): Scarf catching
*Student:* Toss and catch scarves high and low to practice tracking with your eyes.

*How to teach:* Toss and catch scarves high and low, tracking with eyes.

- **Q:** Where do your eyes go?
  **A:** On the object the whole time.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Thumbs and pinkies
*Student:* Catch tossed balls above the waist with thumbs together and below with pinkies together.

*How to teach:* Toss above and below the waist; thumbs together high, pinkies together low.

- **Q:** A ball comes at your knees. Thumbs or pinkies together?
  **A:** Pinkies together.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Soft hands
*Student:* Practice 'giving' with your arms to pull the ball in; play egg-toss with a water balloon or bean bag.

*How to teach:* Practice 'giving'; play egg-toss with a beanbag (or water balloon outside).

- **Q:** Why give with your arms?
  **A:** To soften the catch so the ball doesn't bounce out.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Self-toss
*Student:* Toss and catch to yourself, adding a clap, then a spin, before the catch.

*How to teach:* Self-toss and catch, adding a clap then a spin.

- **Q:** How high should you toss?
  **A:** Just high enough for the clap or spin.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Catch 8 of 10 tosses at mixed heights and explain thumbs vs. pinkies.

*How to teach:* 10 mixed-height tosses; explain cues.

- **Q:** When do you put thumbs together?
  **A:** When catching above the waist.

**Assessment:** Catch count out of 10 and cue explanation.

**Answer key:** Catch count out of 10 mixed-height tosses (goal 8). Cue explanation: above the waist = thumbs together; below the waist = pinkies together. Proficient = 8+/10 and correct explanation.

## Health 2: Screens and balance

**Prepare:** Activity log, timer, list paper.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking screens are always bad: balance matters.
- Counting screen time with active games as active: sitting games are sedentary.

### Day 1 (Monday): Screen count
*Student:* Track screen time for 2 days and compare with active minutes.

*How to teach:* Track screen time 2 days; compare with active minutes.

- **Q:** Which was more: screen or active minutes?
  **A:** Child compares from data.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Swap list
*Student:* Make a list of 10 active swaps for screen time.

*How to teach:* List 10 active swaps for screen time.

- **Q:** Give an active swap.
  **A:** Ride a bike instead of watching a show.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Screen-free day
*Student:* Plan and enjoy a screen-free afternoon outdoors.

*How to teach:* Plan and enjoy a screen-free afternoon outdoors.

- **Q:** What does sedentary mean?
  **A:** Sitting still, not moving much.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Goal check
*Student:* Check progress on last week's activity goal and update the log.

*How to teach:* Check last week's goal; update the log.

- **Q:** Did you meet your goal?
  **A:** Uses numbers from log.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain how balancing screens and movement helps your health.

*How to teach:* Explain how balance helps health.

- **Q:** Why balance screens with movement?
  **A:** Moving keeps your body strong and helps you sleep and feel good.

**Assessment:** Activity goal progress and explanation.

**Answer key:** Goal progress compared with week 17 goal (met/not met with numbers). Explanation: balancing screens with movement keeps heart, muscles and bones strong, helps sleep and mood; too much sitting (sedentary time) can make you tired and less healthy. Proficient = accurate progress + explanation.
