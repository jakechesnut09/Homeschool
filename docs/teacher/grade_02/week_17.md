# Teacher's Guide — 2nd Grade, Week 17

## ELA 2: Poems with rhythm and rhyme

**Prepare:** Get A Child's Garden of Verses (Robert Louis Stevenson, public domain) and mark 'My Shadow,' 'The Swing,' 'Rain,' and 'Where Go the Boats?' Have a drum or pot to tap the beat. Make a list of 10 compound words. Pick a new short rhyming poem for Friday.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking any two words that end with the same letter rhyme (cow/snow). Say them aloud — rhymes share the same ending sound.
- Splitting compound words at the wrong spot (no-tebook). Look for two real words: note + book.

### Day 1 (Monday): My Shadow
*Student:* Read Stevenson's 'My Shadow' aloud and tap the beat; highlight rhyming pairs at line ends. Discuss what the shadow does.

*How to teach:* Read 'My Shadow' aloud and tap the beat. Highlight rhyming pairs at the line ends. Discuss what the shadow does.

- **Q:** Name a rhyming pair from the first stanza of 'My Shadow.'
  **A:** me/see or head/bed.
- **Q:** What does the shadow do that is funny?
  **A:** It grows taller or smaller — sometimes it shoots up tall like a rubber ball and sometimes there's none of it at all.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): The Swing
*Student:* Read 'The Swing' and act out the rhythm while swinging arms; discuss how the beat feels like swinging. Phonics: compound words (sunshine, rainbow, playground) — split and define.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Swing' while swinging arms to the beat. Discuss how the beat feels like swinging. Split and define compound words.

- **Q:** How does the rhythm of 'The Swing' feel like swinging?
  **A:** The beat goes up and down, back and forth, like a swing.
- **Q:** Split 'playground.'
  **A:** play + ground — ground where you play.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compound word meaning
*Student:* Use the two smaller words to figure out meanings of 10 compound words (birdhouse, bookshelf, notebook). Write a rhyming couplet using one.

*How to teach:* Use smaller words to define 10 compound words. Write a rhyming couplet with one.

- **Q:** What does 'bookshelf' mean?
  **A:** A shelf that holds books.
- **Q:** Write a rhyming couplet using 'rainbow.'
  **A:** Example: I saw a rainbow in the sky, / its colors bright and way up high.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Memorize
*Student:* Memorize the first stanza of 'My Shadow' and practice reciting with expression and clear volume. Read 'Rain' and 'Where Go the Boats?' for fun.

*How to teach:* Memorize the first stanza of 'My Shadow' and practice reciting with expression and volume. Read 'Rain' and 'Where Go the Boats?' for fun.

- **Q:** What does reciting with expression mean?
  **A:** Saying it with feeling, changing your voice to match the words.
- **Q:** In 'Where Go the Boats?', what does the child float on the river?
  **A:** Boats (toy boats) that float away down the river.

### Day 5 (Friday): Week 17 review
*Student:* Recite the stanza, find rhymes and beats in a new poem, and take a spelling check of 10 compound words.

*How to teach:* The child recites the stanza, finds rhymes and beats in a new poem, and takes the spelling check.

- **Q:** How do you know two words rhyme?
  **A:** They have the same ending sound (bed/head).
- **Q:** Spell 'notebook' and 'sunshine.'
  **A:** n-o-t-e-b-o-o-k; s-u-n-s-h-i-n-e.

**Assessment:** Poem recitation (memory, expression, volume), rhyme/beat identification on a new poem, spelling check.

**Answer key:** Recitation rubric (3 = proficient): first stanza of 'My Shadow' recited with no more than 1–2 prompts, with expression, and loud enough to hear. New poem: child names rhyming pairs at line ends and taps a steady beat. Spelling (suggested 10): sunshine, rainbow, playground, birdhouse, bookshelf, notebook, cupcake, bedroom, backpack, football.

## Math 2: Picture graphs and bar graphs

**Prepare:** Graph paper, a tally sheet with 4 fruit choices, and stickers. Line up 10–20 people to survey (family, neighbors, phone calls). Draw a new bar graph for Friday (e.g., pets: dogs 8, cats 5, fish 3, birds 6).

**Watch for:**
- Starting the scale at 1 or using uneven spacing. Count the grid lines together from 0.
- Answering 'how many more' by adding. Find both bars, then subtract the smaller from the larger.

### Day 1 (Monday): Survey
*Student:* Survey 10–20 family members, friends, or neighbors about their favorite fruit (4 choices) and record with tally marks.

*How to teach:* Make a tally sheet with 4 fruits. The child asks each person and makes tally marks, crossing every fifth mark to make a bundle. Count the totals together.

- **Q:** How do you show 7 with tally marks?
  **A:** One bundle of 5 (four lines crossed by a fifth) and 2 more lines.
- **Q:** Why do we bundle tallies in fives?
  **A:** Groups of 5 are quick to count.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Picture graph
*Student:* Make a picture graph of the survey with a title, labels, and key (each picture = 1 vote).

*How to teach:* Make a picture graph: title, a row for each fruit, one picture per vote, and a key 'each picture = 1 vote.' Check that pictures are the same size and lined up.

- **Q:** What does the key tell you?
  **A:** How many votes each picture stands for (1 vote).
- **Q:** If apples got 6 votes, how many apple pictures do you draw?
  **A:** 6.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Bar graph
*Student:* Turn the same data into a bar graph with a single-unit scale and labeled axes.

*How to teach:* Turn the same data into a bar graph: label the axes, number the scale 0, 1, 2… evenly, and color each bar to the right height.

- **Q:** Where does the scale start?
  **A:** At 0.
- **Q:** If bananas got 4 votes, how high is the bar?
  **A:** Up to the 4 line.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Graph questions
*Student:* Answer 'how many more/fewer' and 'how many in all' questions from 2 graphs and write 3 of your own.

*How to teach:* Ask questions from 2 graphs: how many in all, how many more, how many fewer. The child writes 3 questions of their own and answers them.

- **Q:** Apples 7, bananas 4. How many more chose apples?
  **A:** 3 more; 7 − 4 = 3.
- **Q:** Apples 7, bananas 4, grapes 5, oranges 2. How many voted in all?
  **A:** 18 votes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Read a new bar graph and answer 6 put-together, take-apart, and compare questions.

*How to teach:* Show the new bar graph and ask the 6 questions. The child answers each with an equation.

- **Q:** Dogs 8, cats 5. How many more chose dogs?
  **A:** 3 more.
- **Q:** How many votes in all (8, 5, 3, 6)?
  **A:** 22.

**Assessment:** Bar graph construction and graph-question quiz.

**Answer key:** Graph construction rubric: 3 = title, labeled categories and axis, scale from 0 with equal spaces, bars match data; 2 = one element missing; 1 = several missing. Quiz on the sample graph (dogs 8, cats 5, fish 3, birds 6): 1) How many chose dogs? 8 2) How many more dogs than cats? 3 3) How many fewer fish than birds? 3 4) Dogs and cats together? 13 5) How many votes in all? 22 6) If 2 more chose fish, how many fish? 5.

## Science 2: Modeling seed dispersal

**Prepare:** Gather an old sock, pom-poms, Velcro, fabric scraps, pipe cleaners, a tray of seeds or rice, and tape.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking any material will grab seeds. Test which surfaces (fuzzy, hooked) work best.
- Counting differently each test. Use the same tray and same drag distance each time.

### Day 1 (Monday): Design challenge
*Student:* Design a model 'animal' that picks up and carries seeds like fur does; sketch two ideas.

*How to teach:* Design a model 'animal' that picks up and carries seeds like fur; sketch two ideas.

- **Q:** What feature of fur helps carry seeds?
  **A:** It is fuzzy and seeds' hooks catch in it.
- **Q:** What materials could act like fur?
  **A:** Velcro, fuzzy fabric, pipe cleaners.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Build
*Student:* Build the model 'animal' from a sock, fabric scraps, Velcro, or pipe-cleaner 'fur,' following your sketch.

*How to teach:* Build the model following the sketch.

- **Q:** Why follow your sketch?
  **A:** So you build what you planned.
- **Q:** Which part will touch the seeds?
  **A:** The fuzzy or Velcro side.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Test
*Student:* Drag the model through a tray of seeds or rice and count seeds picked up and dropped.

*How to teach:* Drag the model through seeds and count those picked up and dropped.

- **Q:** How many seeds stuck?
  **A:** Answer from count.
- **Q:** Why count dropped seeds too?
  **A:** Dropping in new places is how seeds spread.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Improve
*Student:* Change the model's shape or material and retest; record changes.

*How to teach:* Change the model's shape or material and retest.

- **Q:** What did you change?
  **A:** Example: added more Velcro.
- **Q:** Did more seeds stick?
  **A:** Answer from data.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Explain how the model is like a real animal and how its shape helps it work.

*How to teach:* Explain how the model is like a real animal and how its shape helps.

- **Q:** How is your model like a dog walking through a field?
  **A:** Both carry seeds stuck in fur to new places.
- **Q:** Why does the shape matter?
  **A:** More surface area catches more seeds.

**Assessment:** Model test data and explanation.

**Answer key:** Model test data: seeds picked up and dropped counted for each version. Explanation (3 = proficient): 'My model is like a real animal's fur because the fuzzy/hooked surface catches seeds; as it moves, some seeds fall off in new places.'

## People Who Make a Difference: Sharing cultures and making agreements

**Prepare:** Library of Congress photo of the 1912 cherry tree planting in Washington, D.C.; Peace Arch photos; paper for a 'treaty.'

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a treaty is a gift: a treaty is a written agreement between nations.
- Thinking cultures only share by trade: also through gifts, music, food, games and visits.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cherry trees
*Student:* Look at a Library of Congress photo of the 1912 planting of cherry trees given by Japan to Washington, D.C., and discuss why nations give gifts.

*How to teach:* Study the 1912 cherry tree photo; discuss gifts between nations.

- **Q:** Which country gave the cherry trees?
  **A:** Japan (the city of Tokyo).
- **Q:** Why do nations give gifts?
  **A:** To show friendship and build good relations.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Peace Arch
*Student:* Learn about the Peace Arch on the U.S.–Canada border and what it symbolizes.

*How to teach:* Learn about the Peace Arch on the U.S.-Canada border (Washington State and British Columbia).

- **Q:** What does the Peace Arch stand for?
  **A:** Peace and friendship between the United States and Canada.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Treaties
*Student:* Learn that a treaty is an agreement between nations; make a class 'treaty' with a sibling about sharing toys.

*How to teach:* Learn that a treaty is an agreement; write a toy-sharing 'treaty' with a sibling.

- **Q:** What is a treaty?
  **A:** A written agreement between nations.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cultural exchange
*Student:* Learn a song, food, or game from another country and share it.

*How to teach:* Learn and share a song, food or game from another country.

- **Q:** What is culture?
  **A:** The way of life of a group: food, language, music, holidays, beliefs.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Explain one way nations share culture and one way they make agreements.

*How to teach:* Explain one way nations share culture and one way they make agreements.

- **Q:** Give an example of sharing culture.
  **A:** Japan giving cherry trees; sharing music or food.

**Assessment:** Primary source analysis and treaty.

**Answer key:** Primary source analysis: identifies who (Japan gave, U.S. received; First Lady Helen Taft helped plant the first trees in 1912), what (cherry trees), why (a gift of friendship). Peace Arch: symbol of peace and friendship between the U.S. and Canada. Treaty: written agreement with terms both siblings sign. Proficient = all.

## Art & Music 2: Texture: rubbings and implied texture

**Prepare:** Crayons with paper removed, thin paper, leaves, coins, mesh, bark, scissors, glue.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all texture can be touched: implied texture only looks like texture (drawn).
- Pressing with the crayon tip in rubbings: use the flat side.

### Day 1 (Monday): Texture walk
*Student:* Touch and describe 10 textures around home and yard (bark, carpet, brick, sponge) and record words for each.

*How to teach:* Touch 10 textures; record words (rough, smooth, bumpy, soft, prickly).

- **Q:** Describe brick's texture.
  **A:** Rough, bumpy.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rubbings
*Student:* Make crayon rubbings of 12 textured surfaces and label each.

*How to teach:* Make 12 crayon rubbings; label each.

- **Q:** How do you make a rubbing?
  **A:** Paper over a textured surface, rub with the side of a crayon.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Texture collage
*Student:* Cut up rubbings and arrange them into an animal collage (bark for fur, mesh for scales).

*How to teach:* Cut and arrange rubbings into an animal collage.

- **Q:** Which rubbing works for fur?
  **A:** Bark, carpet, or something with many lines.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Implied texture
*Student:* Draw 4 objects (a pinecone, a sweater, a rock, a feather) using lines and dots to show texture.

*How to teach:* Draw a pinecone, sweater, rock, feather using lines and dots for texture.

- **Q:** How do you show a fuzzy sweater with lines?
  **A:** Short, soft, repeated lines or loops.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Explain the difference between real and implied texture using your collage and drawings.

*How to teach:* Explain real vs. implied texture.

- **Q:** What is implied texture?
  **A:** Texture that is drawn to look like how something feels, but is flat to touch.

**Assessment:** Texture collage and implied-texture drawings.

**Answer key:** Collage: animal from cut rubbings with textures chosen to suggest fur, scales, etc. Implied texture drawings of pinecone, sweater, rock, feather with lines/dots. Explanation: real texture can be felt; implied texture is drawn to look like how something would feel. Proficient = all + correct explanation.

## PE 2: Overhand throw

**Prepare:** Tennis balls or beanbags, tape square on a wall, plastic cups, tape at 10 feet.

**Watch for:**
- Facing the target: turn your side to the target.
- Stepping with the throwing-side foot: step with the opposite foot.

### Day 1 (Monday): T-pose throw
*Student:* Learn cues: side to target, arm back in a 'T', step with opposite foot, throw and follow through.

*How to teach:* Teach cues; model slowly: side to target, arm back in a T, step opposite, throw and follow through.

- **Q:** What is the 'T'?
  **A:** Arms spread out to the sides like the letter T before the throw.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Wall throws
*Student:* Throw tennis balls overhand at a taped square on a wall from 10 feet, 10 times.

*How to teach:* Throw 10 times at the taped wall square from 10 feet; count hits.

- **Q:** How many hits out of 10?
  **A:** Record child's count.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Distance throws
*Student:* Throw for distance in the yard and mark the best of 5 throws.

*How to teach:* Throw for distance; mark best of 5.

- **Q:** Does stepping help distance?
  **A:** Yes, stepping adds power.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Target game
*Student:* Knock down a pyramid of plastic cups with overhand throws; count throws needed.

*How to teach:* Knock down a cup pyramid; count throws needed.

- **Q:** How many throws did it take?
  **A:** Record count.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Throw overhand 5 times while a parent checks off the 4 cues.

*How to teach:* Parent checks 4 cues over 5 throws.

- **Q:** Say the four overhand cues.
  **A:** Side to target, arm back (T), step opposite, throw and follow through.

**Assessment:** Overhand throw cue checklist.

**Answer key:** Overhand cue checklist (5 throws): side to target; arm back in a T; step with opposite foot; throw and follow through across the body. Proficient = all 4 cues on at least 4 throws.

## Health 2: Active every day

**Prepare:** Activity log, timer; ideas list.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking only sports count: walking, dancing, playing and biking all count.
- Thinking activity must happen all at once: minutes can add up throughout the day.

### Day 1 (Monday): 60 minutes
*Student:* Learn kids need 60 minutes of activity daily; brainstorm active things to do.

*How to teach:* Teach kids need 60 minutes of activity daily; brainstorm activities.

- **Q:** How many minutes of activity do kids need each day?
  **A:** 60 minutes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mood and movement
*Student:* Rate your mood before and after a 10-minute walk.

*How to teach:* Rate mood before and after a 10-minute walk.

- **Q:** How does activity affect mood?
  **A:** It usually makes you feel happier and calmer.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Activity log
*Student:* Start a week-long activity log of minutes moved.

*How to teach:* Start a week-long activity log.

- **Q:** If you played 20 minutes before lunch and 40 after, total?
  **A:** 60 minutes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Family challenge
*Student:* Plan a family active challenge (dance party, bike ride, hike).

*How to teach:* Plan a family active challenge.

- **Q:** Name an activity the whole family can do.
  **A:** Bike ride, hike, dance party.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Total your active minutes and set a goal for next week.

*How to teach:* Total minutes; set next week's goal.

- **Q:** How does activity give you energy?
  **A:** It strengthens your heart and muscles so your body works better.

**Assessment:** Activity log and goal.

**Answer key:** Activity log: daily minutes for a week, total correct (e.g., 45 + 60 + 30 + 70 + 50 + 65 + 40 = 360 minutes). Goal is specific (e.g., 60 minutes on at least 5 days). Proficient = log complete + accurate total + goal.
