# Teacher's Guide — 2nd Grade, Week 10

## ELA 2: Life cycles: connecting steps in a process

**Prepare:** Get Monarch Butterfly (Gail Gibbons) and preview it, especially the migration pages. Gather paper plates, brads, crayons, a note-taking sheet, and an er/ir/ur word list. Print a ReadWorks butterfly passage with 4 questions for Friday.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking caterpillars and butterflies are different animals. Show the life-cycle wheel: the caterpillar (larva) becomes the butterfly.
- Copying whole sentences as notes. Model writing 2–4 key words: 'eats milkweed.'

### Day 1 (Monday): Monarch life cycle
*Student:* Read Monarch Butterfly by Gail Gibbons and list the 4 stages (egg, larva, pupa, adult). Phonics: er/ir/ur words (her, bird, turn) — same sound, different spellings.

*How to teach:* Read Monarch Butterfly and list the 4 stages. Teach that er, ir, ur make the same sound.

- **Q:** What are the 4 stages of a monarch's life?
  **A:** Egg, larva (caterpillar), pupa (chrysalis), adult.
- **Q:** What plant do monarch caterpillars eat?
  **A:** Milkweed.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Connecting steps
*Student:* Make a paper-plate life-cycle wheel and explain how each stage leads to the next using because and then. Learn the subject words larva and chrysalis from context.

*How to teach:* Make a paper-plate life-cycle wheel. The child explains each step using 'because' and 'then.' Use context to define larva and chrysalis.

- **Q:** What is a chrysalis?
  **A:** The hard case a monarch caterpillar forms around itself while it changes into a butterfly (the pupa stage).
- **Q:** Explain how one stage leads to the next.
  **A:** The egg hatches into a caterpillar, then the caterpillar eats and grows, then it makes a chrysalis, then a butterfly comes out and lays eggs.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Taking notes
*Student:* Learn to take notes in words, not sentences; record 6 facts about monarchs on a note sheet. Phonics: er/ir/ur spelling sort.

*How to teach:* Model taking notes in a few words. The child records 6 monarch facts. Sort er/ir/ur words.

- **Q:** Turn this into a note: 'The caterpillar eats milkweed leaves all day long.'
  **A:** eats milkweed leaves.
- **Q:** Sort: bird, her, turn.
  **A:** ir: bird; er: her; ur: turn.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Migration question
*Student:* Read the migration section and answer in writing 'Why do monarchs fly to California and Mexico?' using notes. Share answers and ask a partner a clarifying question.

*How to teach:* Read the migration section. The child writes why monarchs fly to California and Mexico using notes, then asks a partner a clarifying question.

- **Q:** Why do monarchs fly to California and Mexico?
  **A:** To escape the cold winter — they cannot survive freezing weather, so they fly to warmer places.
- **Q:** What is a clarifying question?
  **A:** A question that asks someone to explain more, e.g., 'What do you mean by…?'

### Day 5 (Friday): Week 10 review
*Student:* Sequence the life cycle in a labeled drawing, spell 10 r-controlled words, and answer 4 questions on a ReadWorks butterfly passage.

*How to teach:* The child draws and labels the life cycle, takes the spelling check, and answers 4 questions on a butterfly passage.

- **Q:** What comes after the larva stage?
  **A:** The pupa (chrysalis) stage.
- **Q:** Spell 'bird' and 'turn.'
  **A:** b-i-r-d; t-u-r-n.

**Assessment:** Labeled life-cycle drawing, written migration answer from notes, and spelling check.

**Answer key:** Life-cycle drawing: 4 stages labeled in order — egg → larva (caterpillar) → pupa (chrysalis) → adult butterfly — with arrows back to egg. Migration answer: proficient says monarchs fly south to warmer places (Mexico and the California coast) in fall because they cannot survive the cold winter up north, using at least one fact from notes. Spelling (suggested 10): her, fern, bird, girl, first, turn, burn, hurt, nurse, shirt.

## Math 2: Measuring in centimeters and meters

**Prepare:** Get a centimeter ruler, a meter stick (or cut string exactly 100 cm long), and sticky notes. Benchmarks: the width of a fingertip is about 1 cm; a giant step is about 1 meter.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking an object got longer because the number in centimeters is bigger. Measure the same pencil in inches and cm: the pencil didn't change — smaller units need more of them.
- Reading the inch side of a ruler by mistake. Label the cm side with a sticky note.

### Day 1 (Monday): Centimeters
*Student:* Measure 10 small objects to the nearest centimeter and record; use a fingertip as a 1-cm benchmark.

*How to teach:* Show that a fingertip is about 1 cm wide. The child measures 10 small objects (eraser, crayon, coin) to the nearest centimeter, starting at 0, and records them.

- **Q:** A paper clip measures between 3 and 4 cm but closer to 3. What do you record?
  **A:** 3 cm (nearest centimeter).
- **Q:** What part of your body is about 1 cm?
  **A:** The width of a fingertip.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Meters
*Student:* Measure the length of rooms and a hallway with a meter stick; estimate first using a giant step as about 1 meter.

*How to teach:* Walk with giant steps (about 1 meter each) to estimate a room's length, then measure with the meter stick, marking each meter with a sticky note. Compare estimate and measurement.

- **Q:** How many centimeters are in 1 meter?
  **A:** 100 centimeters.
- **Q:** Would you measure a hallway in cm or m?
  **A:** Meters — it is long.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Two units
*Student:* Measure the same 5 objects in inches and centimeters and explain why there are more centimeters (smaller unit).

*How to teach:* Measure 5 objects in inches and in centimeters. Ask 'Why is the cm number bigger?' Guide to: a centimeter is a smaller unit, so it takes more of them.

- **Q:** A book is 10 inches long. Will its length in centimeters be more or less than 10?
  **A:** More than 10 (about 25 cm), because centimeters are smaller units.
- **Q:** Which is longer: 1 inch or 1 centimeter?
  **A:** 1 inch (about 2½ cm).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Benchmark estimates
*Student:* Estimate 8 lengths in cm or m using benchmarks, then measure; color estimates that were within 5 cm.

*How to teach:* Estimate 8 lengths using benchmarks (fingertip = 1 cm, giant step = 1 m), then measure. Color estimates that were within 5 cm.

- **Q:** You estimated 30 cm and measured 34 cm. Was it within 5 cm?
  **A:** Yes, it was 4 cm off.
- **Q:** Estimate a door's height: about 2 cm, 2 m, or 20 m?
  **A:** About 2 m.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: measure in cm, compare inch vs. cm results for 3 objects, and explain the unit-size relationship in a sentence.

*How to teach:* Give the metric quiz and have the child write one sentence explaining unit size. Check for the word 'smaller' and the idea of 'more units.'

- **Q:** Explain why you get a bigger number in centimeters than in inches.
  **A:** Centimeters are smaller units, so more of them fit along the same length.
- **Q:** A line starts at 0 and ends at the 7 cm mark. How long is it?
  **A:** 7 cm.

**Assessment:** Metric measuring quiz and written unit-size explanation.

**Answer key:** Metric quiz (suggested): measure 4 drawn lines of 5 cm, 8 cm, 12 cm, 3 cm (accept exact or ±1 cm). Compare inch vs. cm for 3 objects — the cm number must be larger each time (about 2½ times the inches). Unit-size sentence rubric: 3 = 'A centimeter is smaller than an inch, so it takes more centimeters to measure the same object'; 2 = says the cm number is bigger without the reason; 1 = incorrect relationship.

## Science 2: Irreversible changes

**Prepare:** Gather eggs, bread and a toaster, pancake batter ingredients, and 12 picture cards (melting snow, baking a cake, freezing juice, burning wood, cooking an egg, melting butter, toasting bread, frying, freezing water, melting chocolate, burning a candle wick, popping popcorn). Safety: an adult handles the stove and toaster.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking cooling undoes every heated change. A cooled cooked egg is still cooked.
- Sorting by temperature instead of whether it can be undone. Ask 'Can it go back to how it was?'

### Day 1 (Monday): Cooking an egg
*Student:* Observe a raw egg, then cook it with an adult; discuss whether it can become raw again.

*How to teach:* Observe a raw egg, then cook it with an adult. Discuss whether it can become raw again.

- **Q:** Can a cooked egg become raw again?
  **A:** No — cooking is an irreversible change.
- **Q:** How did the egg change?
  **A:** The clear liquid turned white and solid; the yolk got firm.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Toast
*Student:* Toast a slice of bread and compare to untoasted bread; try to 'untoast' it (cooling) and record.

*How to teach:* Toast bread and compare to untoasted bread. Try to 'untoast' it by cooling, and record.

- **Q:** Did cooling make the toast soft bread again?
  **A:** No — toasting can't be reversed.
- **Q:** Describe the toast vs. bread.
  **A:** Toast is brown, hard, and crunchy; bread is soft and lighter.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pancakes
*Student:* Make pancakes and describe how liquid batter becomes solid when heated.

*How to teach:* Make pancakes and describe how liquid batter becomes solid when heated.

- **Q:** What happened to the batter in the pan?
  **A:** Heat turned the runny liquid into a solid pancake.
- **Q:** Can the pancake turn back into batter?
  **A:** No.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sort changes
*Student:* Sort 12 picture cards (melting snow, baking a cake, freezing juice, burning wood) into reversible/irreversible.

*How to teach:* Sort 12 picture cards into reversible and irreversible.

- **Q:** Is burning wood reversible?
  **A:** No — it turns into ash and smoke.
- **Q:** Is freezing juice reversible?
  **A:** Yes — it melts back into juice.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Write an argument: 'Some changes caused by heating can be reversed and some cannot,' with two pieces of evidence each.

*How to teach:* Write an argument: some heated changes can be reversed and some cannot, with two pieces of evidence each.

- **Q:** Give evidence of a reversible heated change.
  **A:** Melted chocolate became solid again when cooled.
- **Q:** Give evidence of an irreversible heated change.
  **A:** A cooked egg stayed cooked when it cooled.

**Assessment:** Change-sort and written argument with evidence.

**Answer key:** Change sort: reversible — melting snow, freezing juice, melting butter, freezing water, melting chocolate; irreversible — baking a cake, burning wood, cooking an egg, toasting bread, frying, burning a candle wick, popping popcorn. Argument (3 = proficient): claim stated; 2 reversible examples (ice melting/refreezing, chocolate) and 2 irreversible (egg, toast/pancake) with evidence from the week's tests.

## People Who Make a Difference: Where my family came from

**Prepare:** World or U.S. map, yarn and pins or stickers; family interview notes. Be sensitive if family history is unknown (adoption, etc.): the child can map the family's moves within their own lifetime instead.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking only people from other countries are immigrants: an immigrant moves into a new country; people also migrate within a country.
- Thinking people move for no reason: they move for jobs, family, safety, land or opportunity.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ask about moves
*Student:* Interview a family member about where ancestors lived and when and why the family moved to your community.

*How to teach:* Interview a family member about where ancestors lived and why they moved.

- **Q:** What does migrate mean?
  **A:** To move from one place to another to live.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Map the journey
*Student:* Mark ancestors' homes on a world or U.S. map and connect them to your town with yarn or lines.

*How to teach:* Mark ancestors' homes on a map; connect to your town with yarn.

- **Q:** Where did your family's journey start?
  **A:** Answer from interview.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): How they traveled
*Student:* Learn how people traveled long ago (ship, wagon, train) and draw your family's journey.

*How to teach:* Learn how people traveled long ago (ship, wagon, train); draw your family's journey.

- **Q:** How did many immigrants cross the ocean long ago?
  **A:** By ship.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Why people move
*Student:* Discuss reasons people move (jobs, family, safety, land) and write one sentence about why your family moved.

*How to teach:* Discuss reasons people move; write one sentence about your family.

- **Q:** Name two reasons people move.
  **A:** Jobs, to be near family, safety, land, schools, opportunity.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Add the map to the family history book and present it.

*How to teach:* Add the map to the family book; present it.

- **Q:** What is an immigrant?
  **A:** A person who moves to a new country to live.

**Assessment:** Family migration map and explanation.

**Answer key:** Migration map: ancestors' homes marked and connected to the current town; explanation gives where they came from, how they traveled, and one reason they moved (e.g., 'Great-grandpa came from Mexico by train in 1950 to find work'). Proficient = accurate map + clear reason.

## Art & Music 2: Warm and cool colors and mood

**Prepare:** Image of Van Gogh's 'The Starry Night' (1889), tempera paints, old magazines, scissors.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking warm colors are just 'hot' colors: they also feel energetic, happy or angry; cool colors feel calm, sad or peaceful.
- Thinking Van Gogh painted what he saw exactly: he used swirling, exaggerated brushstrokes to show feeling.

### Day 1 (Monday): Warm and cool sort
*Student:* Cut 12 pictures from magazines and sort them into warm-color and cool-color piles; describe how each pile makes you feel.

*How to teach:* Cut and sort 12 magazine pictures into warm and cool piles; describe feelings.

- **Q:** Name the warm colors.
  **A:** Red, orange, yellow.
- **Q:** Name the cool colors.
  **A:** Blue, green, purple.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): The Starry Night
*Student:* Look closely at Van Gogh's 'The Starry Night' for 3 minutes, then describe its mood, colors, and swirling lines using 'I see… I think… I wonder…'.

*How to teach:* Look at 'The Starry Night' 3 minutes; use 'I see… I think… I wonder…'.

- **Q:** What do you see in 'The Starry Night'?
  **A:** Swirling sky, bright stars, a crescent moon, a small village with a church steeple, a tall dark cypress tree.
- **Q:** What mood does it have?
  **A:** Answers vary with reasons: dreamy, exciting, restless, peaceful.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cool night painting
*Student:* Paint a night sky over your own house using only cool colors and swirling brushstrokes like Van Gogh.

*How to teach:* Paint a night sky over your house using only cool colors and swirling strokes.

- **Q:** What is a palette?
  **A:** The group of colors an artist chooses (or the tray for mixing).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Warm sunset
*Student:* Paint a sunset over the same house using only warm colors.

*How to teach:* Paint a sunset over the same house using only warm colors.

- **Q:** What colors are in your sunset?
  **A:** Warm colors: red, orange, yellow.

### Day 5 (Friday): Compare
*Student:* Hang both paintings side by side and tell how changing the palette changed the mood, using the words warm, cool, and mood.

*How to teach:* Hang both; explain how the palette changed the mood.

- **Q:** How did the mood change?
  **A:** Cool = calm, quiet night; warm = bright, energetic sunset.

**Assessment:** Warm and cool paintings with oral mood comparison.

**Answer key:** Paintings: night painting uses only cool colors with swirling strokes; sunset uses only warm colors. Oral comparison: 'The cool palette makes the house feel calm/quiet/sleepy; the warm palette makes it feel cozy/bright/exciting.' Proficient = both palettes correct + uses warm, cool, mood.

## PE 2: Balance beam and weight transfer

**Prepare:** A 2x4 board or chalk line, beanbag, mat, rope.

**Watch for:**
- Looking at feet on the beam: eyes forward at the end.
- Thinking bones move on their own: muscles pull on bones.

### Day 1 (Monday): Beam walk
*Student:* Walk a 2x4 board or chalk line forward and sideways with arms out; then try with a beanbag on your head.

*How to teach:* Walk forward and sideways with arms out; then with a beanbag on head.

- **Q:** Where do your eyes look?
  **A:** At the end of the beam.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Backward and turns
*Student:* Walk the beam backward and practice a half-turn in the middle.

*How to teach:* Walk backward; practice a half-turn on the balls of the feet in the middle.

- **Q:** How do you turn on a beam?
  **A:** Rise onto the balls of your feet and pivot slowly.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Feet to hands
*Student:* Practice bunny hops (feet to hands to feet) over a rope and a cartwheel-shape step-over on the grass.

*How to teach:* Bunny hops over a rope (feet-hands-feet) and a cartwheel-shape step-over.

- **Q:** What is weight transfer?
  **A:** Moving your weight from one body part to another, like feet to hands.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Muscles at work
*Student:* Feel your leg and stomach muscles tighten while balancing; learn that muscles pull on bones to move us.

*How to teach:* Feel leg and stomach muscles tighten while balancing.

- **Q:** How do muscles move bones?
  **A:** They tighten (contract) and pull on the bones.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Cross the beam three ways without falling off and show one weight-transfer move.

*How to teach:* Cross the beam three ways; show one weight transfer.

- **Q:** What three ways can you cross?
  **A:** Forward, sideways, backward.

**Assessment:** Beam and weight-transfer checklist.

**Answer key:** Beam: crosses forward, sideways and backward without stepping off. Weight transfer: bunny hop (feet to hands to feet) or cartwheel-shape step-over. Proficient = 3 ways + one transfer with control.

## Health 2: My healthy habits goal

**Prepare:** Goal chart, stickers; travel soap, tissues, toothbrush for a hygiene kit.

**Watch for:**
- Making goals too big or vague ('be healthy'): make them specific ('brush twice a day').
- Giving up after a missed day: just start again the next day.

### Day 1 (Monday): Habit check
*Student:* Review habits from this unit and rate yourself on each with a smiley scale.

*How to teach:* Review unit habits; rate each with a smiley scale.

- **Q:** Which habit are you best at?
  **A:** Child names one.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pick a goal
*Student:* Choose one habit to improve (brush twice daily, wash hands before meals) and write it as a goal.

*How to teach:* Choose one habit to improve; write it as a goal.

- **Q:** What is a goal?
  **A:** Something you plan and work to do.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Make a tracker
*Student:* Make a 2-week sticker tracker for your goal.

*How to teach:* Make a 2-week sticker tracker.

- **Q:** Why track a habit?
  **A:** To see progress and remember to do it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Hygiene kit
*Student:* Pack a personal hygiene kit (toothbrush, tissues, travel soap) and explain each item.

*How to teach:* Pack a hygiene kit; explain each item.

- **Q:** Why carry travel soap?
  **A:** To wash hands and remove germs when away from home.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: germs, teeth, sleep and sun protection (10 oral questions); begin tracking your goal.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-question oral quiz; begin tracking.

- **Q:** What is a habit?
  **A:** Something you do so often it becomes automatic.

**Assessment:** Unit quiz and goal tracker.

**Answer key:** Oral quiz (10): 1) Germs are tiny living things that can make you sick. 2) Scrub hands at least 20 seconds. 3) Sneeze into your elbow. 4) Brush 2 minutes, twice a day. 5) Plaque is a sticky germ film that causes cavities. 6) Floss cleans between teeth. 7) Kids need 9-12 hours of sleep. 8) Turn off screens about an hour before bed. 9) Slip, slop, slap, wrap protects skin and eyes. 10) Use SPF 30+ sunscreen and reapply. Proficient = 8/10 + a specific goal with tracker started.
