# Teacher's Guide — 2nd Grade, Week 1

## ELA 2: Reading routines with Frog and Toad

**Prepare:** Get Frog and Toad Are Friends (Arnold Lobel) and read 'Spring' and 'The Story' yourself first. Make a reading log (date, title, pages), 12 short-a/short-i word cards (cap, cab, map, ham, bag, pin, fit, sit, big, lip, tap, rib), a whiteboard, and a 10-word spelling list.

**Watch for:**
- Writing sentences that are fragments ('Frog and Toad.'). Ask 'Who or what? What did they do?' — both parts are needed: 'Frog and Toad went for a walk.'
- Answering comprehension questions from memory or imagination instead of the text. Ask 'Where in the story did it say that?' and flip back to find the page.

### Day 1 (Monday): Meet Frog and Toad
*Student:* Read aloud 'Spring' from Frog and Toad Are Friends, then answer who/what/where questions orally and draw the setting. Start a reading log and practice the book-talk rule 'listen, then add on.'

*How to teach:* Hold up the book and read 'Spring' aloud, pausing to let the child look at the pictures. Ask who, what, and where questions, then the child draws the setting. Start the reading log together and practice 'listen, then add on' ('I agree, and also…').

- **Q:** In 'Spring,' what did Frog want Toad to do?
  **A:** Wake up and come outside, because spring had come.
- **Q:** How did Frog get Toad to get up?
  **A:** Toad said to wake him at half past May, so Frog tore the calendar pages (November through April) off until it showed May.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Short a and short i
*Student:* Phonics: blend and read short-a/short-i words (cap, cab, pin, fit), then sort 12 word cards into two columns. Reread 'Spring' to a parent for fluency and write 2 sentences telling what Frog wanted.

*How to teach:* Blend short-a and short-i words sound by sound (c-a-p, cap) and sort 12 cards into short a / short i columns. The child rereads 'Spring' aloud to you; then writes 2 sentences about what Frog wanted.

- **Q:** Sort: cap, pin, cab, fit. Which have short a?
  **A:** cap and cab (short a); pin and fit have short i.
- **Q:** Write a sentence telling what Frog wanted in 'Spring.'
  **A:** Model: Frog wanted Toad to wake up and play with him in the spring.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): What makes a sentence
*Student:* Grammar: learn that a sentence tells a whole idea and starts with a capital and ends with a period, question mark, or exclamation point; fix 6 scrambled sentences. Read 'The Story' and answer 3 why/how questions.

*How to teach:* Show a sentence: capital at the start, end mark at the end, and a whole idea. Fix 6 scrambled sentences together ('went Toad home' → 'Toad went home.'). Read 'The Story' and ask why/how questions.

- **Q:** In 'The Story,' why does Toad try to think of a story?
  **A:** Frog is not feeling well, and he asks Toad to tell him a story.
- **Q:** How does Toad try to think of a story?
  **A:** He walks up and down the porch, stands on his head, pours water over his head, and bangs his head against the wall — but he can't think of one. Then Frog tells a story about what Toad just did.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Character talk
*Student:* Partner talk: describe Toad using two words and evidence from the pictures in 'The Story'. Write 3 complete sentences about a friend you like to be with.

*How to teach:* Partner talk: the child describes Toad with two words (e.g., kind, silly) and points to a picture or sentence that proves each. Then the child writes 3 complete sentences about a friend.

- **Q:** Give one word that describes Toad in 'The Story' and your evidence.
  **A:** Example: caring — he tries very hard to tell Frog a story because Frog is sick; or silly — he pours water on his head to get an idea.
- **Q:** Is 'my friend Sam' a complete sentence? Fix it.
  **A:** No. Example: My friend Sam likes to play tag.

### Day 5 (Friday): Week 1 review
*Student:* Spelling check of 10 short-a/short-i words, a one-minute timed read of a Frog and Toad page, and a 3-question oral comprehension check. Record books read in the reading log.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-word spelling check. Time the child reading one page for one minute and note words read correctly. Ask 3 oral comprehension questions and record the books read this week in the log.

- **Q:** Where does 'Spring' take place?
  **A:** At Toad's house (Frog comes over to wake him).
- **Q:** Spell 'pin' and 'map.'
  **A:** p-i-n; m-a-p.

**Assessment:** Spelling check (10 words), oral who/what/where answers, and correctly punctuated sentences in journal.

**Answer key:** Spelling (suggested 10): cap, map, bag, ham, tap, pin, fit, sit, big, lip — score out of 10 (8+ proficient). Oral who/what/where (on 'Spring'): Who? Frog and Toad. What happened? Frog tried to wake Toad up because spring had come; Toad wanted to sleep until May, so Frog tore pages off the calendar until it said May. Where? At Toad's house. Journal sentences: proficient = 3 sentences, each a whole idea with a capital letter and end punctuation.

## Math 2: Facts within 10 and counting on

**Prepare:** Gather a ten-frame (draw a 2×5 grid), 20 counters (beans or cereal), dot cards 1–10 (index cards with dots), two dice, and graph paper for the year-long fluency graph. Print or write a 40-fact quiz of sums and differences within 10.

**Watch for:**
- Counting on from the first number instead of the larger one (2 + 8 counted as 3, 4, …, 10). Show that 8 + 2 = 2 + 8 with counters, then always start from the bigger number.
- Starting the count on the number you already have (8 + 3 said as 8, 9, 10). Have the child put the 8 'in their head,' tap it, then say the NEXT numbers: 9, 10, 11.

### Day 1 (Monday): Number talk & ten-frames
*Student:* Use quick-image dot cards and ten-frames to name numbers 1–10 and the 'partners of 10' (7 and 3, 6 and 4); write all ten facts that make 10.

*How to teach:* Flash a dot card or filled ten-frame for 2 seconds and ask 'How many? How did you see it?' Then fill a ten-frame with 7 counters and ask how many empty spaces (3) — that makes 7 + 3 = 10. The child writes all the facts that make 10, from 0 + 10 to 10 + 0, and looks for the pattern.

- **Q:** What number goes with 6 to make 10?
  **A:** 4, because 6 + 4 = 10 (4 empty boxes on a ten-frame with 6 filled).
- **Q:** Name all the pairs of numbers that make 10.
  **A:** 0+10, 1+9, 2+8, 3+7, 4+6, 5+5, 6+4, 7+3, 8+2, 9+1, 10+0.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Count on
*Student:* Add 1, 2, or 3 by counting on from the larger addend (8 + 3: 9, 10, 11); play 'Roll and Count On' with two dice and record 15 equations.

*How to teach:* Model 8 + 3: 'Put 8 in your head (tap your head), then count on 3 fingers: 9, 10, 11.' Show that 3 + 8 is easier if you start with 8. The child rolls two dice, counts on from the larger number, and writes 15 equations.

- **Q:** Solve 3 + 9 by counting on.
  **A:** 12 — start at 9 (the larger number) and count on 3: 10, 11, 12.
- **Q:** Solve 7 + 2.
  **A:** 9 — start at 7, count 8, 9.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Doubles
*Student:* Learn doubles 1+1 to 10+10 with pictures (spider legs 4+4, egg carton 6+6) and quiz with flash cards.

*How to teach:* Show pictures for doubles (spider legs 4 + 4 = 8, egg carton 6 + 6 = 12, crayons 8 + 8 = 16). Say each double together, then quiz with flash cards; set aside any card that takes more than 3 seconds to practice again.

- **Q:** What is 7 + 7?
  **A:** 14.
- **Q:** What is 9 + 9? What is 5 + 5?
  **A:** 18; 10.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Subtraction within 10
*Student:* Act out take-away stories with counters and write subtraction equations; relate 9 − 4 to 4 + 5 = 9.

*How to teach:* Tell a take-away story ('You had 9 grapes and ate 4') while the child acts it out with counters and writes 9 − 4 = 5. Then ask 'What goes with 4 to make 9?' to connect it to 4 + 5 = 9. Do 5–6 stories.

- **Q:** What is 9 − 4, and what addition fact helps?
  **A:** 5, because 4 + 5 = 9.
- **Q:** There were 8 birds. 3 flew away. How many are left?
  **A:** 5 birds; 8 − 3 = 5.

### Day 5 (Friday): Fact check
*Student:* Take a 2-minute baseline fact quiz (40 facts within 10) and graph your score to track all year. Solve 4 short add-to/take-from story problems.

*How to teach:* Give the 40-fact quiz with a 2-minute timer; stop at the timer and count correct facts. Help the child color the first bar of the fluency graph. Then read 4 short stories aloud; the child draws and writes an equation for each.

- **Q:** Lily has 6 stickers. She gets 3 more. How many now?
  **A:** 9 stickers; 6 + 3 = 9.
- **Q:** What is 10 − 7?
  **A:** 3, because 7 + 3 = 10.

**Assessment:** Baseline fluency quiz and story problem check.

**Answer key:** Fluency quiz: score = number correct out of 40; check each fact against an addition chart and graph the score (this is the baseline). Story problems (suggested): 1) Maya had 6 shells and found 3 more: 6 + 3 = 9 shells. 2) There were 10 ducks; 4 swam away: 10 − 4 = 6 ducks. 3) Ben has 5 cars and gets 2 more: 5 + 2 = 7 cars. 4) 8 cookies, 3 eaten: 8 − 3 = 5 cookies. Proficient = 34+ facts and 4/4 problems with a matching equation.

## Science 2: Scientists observe and ask questions

**Prepare:** Buy a composition notebook and gather a magnifying glass, ruler, kitchen scale, and a bag for collecting. Plan a short outdoor walk to collect 5 natural objects (leaves, rocks, shells, sticks, seed pods).

**Watch for:**
- Mixing observations with opinions ('the rock is pretty'). An observation uses the senses and can be checked by anyone ('the rock is gray and rough'); an opinion is what someone thinks or likes.
- Asking questions that can't be tested ('Why is the leaf beautiful?'). Testable questions can be answered by measuring or trying something ('Which leaf is longest?').

### Day 1 (Monday): Science notebook
*Student:* Set up a science notebook with a cover, table of contents, and a page on 'What scientists do.' Draw and label a magnifying glass.

*How to teach:* Help the child decorate the notebook cover, number the pages, and make a table of contents. Talk about what scientists do (observe, ask questions, test, record). The child draws and labels a magnifying glass (lens, handle).

- **Q:** What do scientists do?
  **A:** They observe carefully, ask questions, test ideas, and record what they find.
- **Q:** What does a magnifying glass do?
  **A:** Its lens makes small things look bigger so you can see details.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Nature observation
*Student:* Collect 5 natural objects outside and observe each with a magnifier; record color, shape, texture, and size in a chart.

*How to teach:* Collect 5 natural objects outside. Model using the magnifier and describing words. The child records color, shape, texture, and size in a chart.

- **Q:** Describe a rock using your senses.
  **A:** Example: It is gray with white specks, rough on one side, smooth on the other, and about as big as my thumb.
- **Q:** Which sense do you use to describe texture?
  **A:** Touch.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Asking questions
*Student:* Sort questions about the objects into 'can test' and 'can't test' and write 3 testable questions (Which rock is heaviest?).

*How to teach:* Brainstorm questions about the objects. Sort them into 'can test' and 'can't test.' The child writes 3 testable questions.

- **Q:** Is 'Which rock is heaviest?' testable? Why?
  **A:** Yes — you can weigh the rocks and compare.
- **Q:** Is 'Which shell is the nicest?' testable?
  **A:** No — it is an opinion.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Measure and compare
*Student:* Use a ruler and kitchen scale to measure the objects and order them by size and weight.

*How to teach:* Measure each object's length with a ruler and weigh it on the kitchen scale. Order them by size and weight.

- **Q:** Is the longest object always the heaviest?
  **A:** No — a long leaf can weigh less than a short rock; you have to measure both.
- **Q:** What tool measures weight?
  **A:** A scale.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Share notebook observations aloud and explain the difference between an observation and an opinion.

*How to teach:* The child shares observations aloud and explains observation vs. opinion with examples.

- **Q:** Is 'The leaf has jagged edges' an observation or opinion?
  **A:** Observation.
- **Q:** Is 'Rocks are boring' an observation or opinion?
  **A:** Opinion.

**Assessment:** Notebook observation chart and 3 testable questions.

**Answer key:** Observation chart (3 = proficient): 5 objects each with color, shape, texture, and size recorded using describing words or measurements, plus a labeled drawing. Testable questions: 3 questions that can be answered by measuring, comparing, or doing a test (e.g., 'Which rock is heaviest?', 'Does the shell sink or float?', 'Which leaf is longest?'). Not acceptable: opinion questions like 'Which rock is prettiest?'

## People Who Make a Difference: What is history? Long ago and yesterday

**Prepare:** Calendar; old and new household objects or photos (rotary phone/smartphone, washboard/washing machine); 10 then-and-now picture cards; a Library of Congress photo of children around 1910 (loc.gov); 6 source cards (diary, textbook, photo, birth certificate, encyclopedia article, letter).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 'long ago' means last week: in history, long ago usually means before you (or your parents) were born.
- Thinking a textbook is a primary source: a primary source was made at the time by someone who was there; a textbook is written later (secondary).

### Day 1 (Monday): Yesterday, today, tomorrow
*Student:* Use a calendar to label yesterday, today, and tomorrow and write one thing you did yesterday; discuss what 'long ago' means (before you were born).

*How to teach:* Label yesterday, today and tomorrow on a calendar; write one thing done yesterday. Explain 'long ago' means before you were born.

- **Q:** If today is Wednesday, what was yesterday?
  **A:** Tuesday.
- **Q:** What does 'long ago' mean?
  **A:** A time far in the past, before you were born.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Then and now objects
*Student:* Compare pairs of objects (rotary phone vs. smartphone, washboard vs. washing machine photo) and sort 10 picture cards into 'long ago' and 'today.'

*How to teach:* Compare object pairs; sort 10 cards into long ago and today.

- **Q:** Which is from long ago: a washboard or a washing machine?
  **A:** A washboard.
- **Q:** How did people call each other long ago?
  **A:** With phones attached to the wall, like rotary phones, or by writing letters.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Primary sources
*Student:* Look at a Library of Congress photo of children around 1910 and ask: Who? What? When? What clues tell us it is long ago? Record 3 clues.

*How to teach:* Study an LOC photo of children around 1910. Ask Who? What? When? and record 3 clues of long ago.

- **Q:** What clues show the photo is from long ago?
  **A:** Black-and-white photo, old-fashioned clothes (long dresses, caps, overalls), no modern cars or electronics.
- **Q:** What is history?
  **A:** The story of what happened in the past.

### Day 4 (Thursday): History detectives
*Student:* Learn the difference between a primary source (made at the time) and a secondary source (made later); sort 6 items (a diary, a textbook, a photo, a birth certificate).

*How to teach:* Teach primary (made at the time) vs. secondary (made later) sources; sort 6 items.

- **Q:** Is a diary primary or secondary?
  **A:** Primary.
- **Q:** Is a textbook primary or secondary?
  **A:** Secondary.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review
*Student:* Draw a 'long ago' and a 'today' picture of a child's day and label 3 differences.

*How to teach:* Draw a long-ago and a today picture of a child's day; label 3 differences.

- **Q:** Name one way children's lives were different long ago.
  **A:** Example: they did more chores by hand, walked to school, had no TV or computers.

**Assessment:** Then/now sort and labeled comparison drawing.

**Answer key:** Then/now sort: long ago - rotary phone, washboard, horse and buggy, oil lamp, typewriter; today - smartphone, washing machine, car, electric light, computer. Drawing labels 3 real differences (e.g., walked to school vs. ride in car; chores by hand vs. machines; played with simple toys vs. video games). Proficient = sort all correct + 3 labeled differences.

## Art & Music 2: Kinds of lines

**Prepare:** New sketchbook, pencils, markers, crayons, a few pieces of yarn; calm and energetic music clips. Set up a cleanup routine (caps on, tools back in bin, wipe table).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a line must be straight or drawn with a ruler: lines can be curved, zigzag, wavy, spiral, dotted, thick or thin.
- Thinking a contour line is just the outside 'scribble': a contour line carefully follows the edge of a shape.

### Day 1 (Monday): Line hunt
*Student:* Walk inside and outside to find 10 kinds of lines (fence, branches, stairs, waves on a rug) and sketch each in a new sketchbook.

*How to teach:* Walk inside and outside hunting lines (fence = vertical/straight, branches = curved/diagonal, stairs = zigzag). Sketch each of 10 lines in the new sketchbook.

- **Q:** What kind of line does a staircase make from the side?
  **A:** Zigzag (step-like) lines.
- **Q:** What kind of lines are fence posts?
  **A:** Straight vertical lines.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Line sampler
*Student:* Fold paper into 12 boxes and fill each with a different line type using markers; label each line.

*How to teach:* Fold paper into 12 boxes; fill each with a different line using markers and label it.

- **Q:** What is a spiral line?
  **A:** A line that curls around a center point, getting bigger or smaller.
- **Q:** What is a contour line?
  **A:** A line that follows the outside edge of an object.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Lines show feelings
*Student:* Listen to calm and energetic music and draw lines that match each; discuss which lines feel calm or excited. Practice art-tool cleanup routine.

*How to teach:* Play calm music and draw lines to match, then energetic music. Discuss: calm = long, wavy, horizontal; excited = zigzag, short, jagged. Practice cleanup.

- **Q:** What kind of line feels calm?
  **A:** Smooth, wavy or horizontal lines.
- **Q:** What kind of line feels excited?
  **A:** Zigzag, jagged or short quick lines.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Yarn lines
*Student:* Drop pieces of yarn on paper, trace the shapes they make, and turn them into an imaginative drawing.

*How to teach:* Drop yarn on paper, trace its shape, and turn it into an imaginative drawing.

- **Q:** What did your yarn line become?
  **A:** Child describes their drawing.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Share the line sampler and say which line is your favorite using art words (zigzag, curved, thick, thin).

*How to teach:* Share the sampler; name a favorite line using words like zigzag, curved, thick, thin.

- **Q:** Which is your favorite line and why?
  **A:** Names a line type with a reason, e.g., 'thick zigzag, it looks like lightning.'

**Assessment:** Line sampler with labeled line types and oral reflection.

**Answer key:** Line sampler: 12 boxes each with a different, correctly labeled line (e.g., straight, curved, zigzag, wavy, spiral, dotted, dashed, thick, thin, broken, vertical, horizontal, diagonal, contour). Reflection names a favorite line with art words. Proficient = 10-12 correct labels + art words; developing = 6-9; beginning = under 6.

## PE 2: Safe spaces and signals

**Prepare:** Cones or plastic cups to mark a boundary, a whistle or drum, water bottle, sunscreen/hat; clear a safe area outside.

**Watch for:**
- Freezing slowly or sliding into a stop: on the signal, stop within 2 steps, feet apart and knees bent.
- Skipping the warm-up when excited: the warm-up raises heart rate and warms muscles to prevent injury.

### Day 1 (Monday): Warm-up routine
*Student:* Learn a 5-move warm-up (jog in place, arm circles, jumping jacks, toe touches, high knees) to use all year; set up a cone boundary in the yard.

*How to teach:* Teach the 5-move warm-up: jog in place, arm circles, jumping jacks, toe touches, high knees (about 20 seconds each). Set cones as the boundary.

- **Q:** Name the 5 warm-up moves.
  **A:** Jog in place, arm circles, jumping jacks, toe touches, high knees.
- **Q:** Why do we warm up?
  **A:** To warm muscles and get the heart pumping so we don't get hurt.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Signal game
*Student:* Walk, jog and skip in general space and freeze on the whistle; add 'down' (sit) and 'switch' (change direction) signals.

*How to teach:* Walk, jog and skip in the boundary. Whistle = freeze; 'down' = sit; 'switch' = change direction.

- **Q:** What do you do on 'switch'?
  **A:** Change direction.
- **Q:** What do you do on the whistle?
  **A:** Freeze right away.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Safety & sun
*Student:* Make a PE safety checklist: water bottle, sunscreen/hat, clear space, shoes tied; then play Simon Says with movement commands.

*How to teach:* Make a safety checklist: water bottle, sunscreen/hat, clear space, shoes tied. Then play Simon Says.

- **Q:** Why wear sunscreen and a hat?
  **A:** To protect skin from sunburn.
- **Q:** Why tie shoes?
  **A:** So you don't trip.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Shadow tag
*Student:* Play shadow tag inside the cone boundary, practicing safe tagging (two-finger touch on shoulder).

*How to teach:* Play shadow tag inside the boundary; demonstrate a safe two-finger tag on the shoulder.

- **Q:** How do you tag safely?
  **A:** Two fingers on the shoulder, gentle, no pushing.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Respond to 5 random signals without bumping cones and explain two safety rules and why warm-ups come first.

*How to teach:* Give 5 random signals; ask for 2 safety rules and why warm-ups come first.

- **Q:** Tell me two safety rules and why.
  **A:** Example: stay inside cones so we don't run into things; freeze on the whistle so we can stop safely.

**Assessment:** Observation: safe spacing and correct response to signals; oral safety rules.

**Answer key:** Observation: responds correctly to 5 random signals (freeze, down, switch) without touching cones or people. Oral rules (any two, with why): stay in the boundary (so no one gets hurt), freeze on the whistle (so we can stop safely), tag gently (so no one is pushed), drink water/wear sunscreen (stay healthy). Warm-up comes first to warm muscles and raise heart rate. Proficient = 5/5 signals + 2 rules with reasons.

## Health 2: Feelings and how we show them

**Prepare:** 'In My Heart: A Book of Feelings' by Jo Witek (library), paper for a feelings thermometer, index cards for the anger-sort (yelling, hitting, talking, drawing, walking away, taking deep breaths, throwing things).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking some feelings are bad: all feelings are okay; what we DO with them can be healthy or unhealthy.
- Thinking everyone feels the same way about an event: people can feel differently about the same thing.

### Day 1 (Monday): Feelings book
*Student:* Read 'In My Heart' by Jo Witek and list every feeling word in the book.

*How to teach:* Read 'In My Heart.' Stop on each page and have your child name the feeling and point to clues in the pictures. List every feeling word together.

- **Q:** Name three feelings from the book.
  **A:** Any three named in the book, e.g., happy, sad, brave, angry, scared, calm, hopeful, shy.
- **Q:** What is an emotion?
  **A:** A feeling, like happy, sad, angry or proud.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Feelings thermometer
*Student:* Make a feelings thermometer from calm to very upset and color where you are today.

*How to teach:* Draw a thermometer from 'calm' (bottom) to 'very upset' (top); your child colors today's level and tells why.

- **Q:** What does the top of the thermometer mean?
  **A:** Very upset (big feelings).
- **Q:** Where are you today and why?
  **A:** Child names a level with a reason.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Same situation, different feelings
*Student:* Discuss how two people might feel differently about the same event (a thunderstorm, a new baby).

*How to teach:* Discuss how two people could feel differently about a thunderstorm or a new baby.

- **Q:** How might two people feel about a thunderstorm?
  **A:** One might feel scared, another excited or cozy.
- **Q:** Is it okay to feel differently from someone else?
  **A:** Yes; people can feel different ways about the same event.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Healthy vs. unhealthy
*Student:* Sort cards of ways to show anger (yelling, hitting, talking, drawing, walking away) into healthy and unhealthy.

*How to teach:* Sort cards of ways to show anger into healthy and unhealthy.

- **Q:** Is hitting a healthy way to show anger?
  **A:** No, it hurts others.
- **Q:** Name a healthy way to show anger.
  **A:** Talk about it, draw it, walk away, take deep breaths.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write 3 sentences: 'I feel ___ when ___. I can ___.'

*How to teach:* Write 3 sentences: 'I feel ___ when ___. I can ___.'

- **Q:** What does 'frustrated' mean?
  **A:** Upset because something is hard or not working.
- **Q:** What does 'proud' mean?
  **A:** Feeling good about something you did.

**Assessment:** Feeling sentences and sorting activity.

**Answer key:** Sort key: healthy = talking about it, drawing, walking away to calm down, deep breaths; unhealthy = yelling, hitting, throwing things. Sentences: 3 complete 'I feel ___ when ___. I can ___.' with a real feeling word, a matching situation, and a healthy action (e.g., 'I feel frustrated when my tower falls. I can take three deep breaths and try again.'). Proficient = sort all correct + 3 sentences with healthy actions.
