# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 28

## ELA 4: Oz chapters 19-24 and the 1939 film

**Prepare:** Novel chapters 19-24 (19 Attacked by the Fighting Trees, 20 The Dainty China Country, 21 The Lion Becomes the King of Beasts, 22 The Country of the Quadlings, 23 Glinda the Good Witch Grants Dorothy's Wish, 24 Home Again). The 1939 MGM film (rated G; the Witch scenes can frighten sensitive children). Prepare a novel test (key below).

**Watch for:**
- Assuming the film is 'the real story.' The book came first (1900); films adapt and change.
- Opinion paragraphs without reasons. Each opinion needs reasons with examples from both versions.

### Day 1 (Monday): Finish
*Student:* Read chapters 19-24 (the journey to Glinda) and write a 5-sentence summary of how Dorothy gets home.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 19-24. The child writes a 5-sentence summary of how Dorothy gets home.

- **Q:** How does Dorothy get home?
  **A:** Glinda, the Good Witch of the South, tells her the silver shoes can carry her anywhere: she knocks her heels together three times and commands them to take her home.
- **Q:** What happens to the Scarecrow, Tin Woodman and Lion?
  **A:** The Scarecrow rules the Emerald City, the Tin Woodman rules the Winkies, and the Lion rules the beasts of the forest (after killing a giant spider).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Film
*Student:* Watch selected scenes of the 1939 film and note differences (silver shoes vs ruby slippers).

*How to teach:* Watch selected scenes of the 1939 film. The child notes differences.

- **Q:** Name two differences between the book and the film.
  **A:** Silver shoes vs. ruby slippers; in the film Oz is a dream and Kansas is in black-and-white/sepia; the film leaves out the China Country and Quadlings; in the film Glinda is the Good Witch of the North.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compare chart
*Student:* Complete a book-vs-film comparison chart covering characters, events, setting colors and the ending.

*How to teach:* Complete the book-vs-film chart: characters, events, setting colors, ending.

- **Q:** How do the endings differ?
  **A:** In the book Dorothy really travels home (the silver shoes fall off in the desert); in the film she wakes up in bed as if it were a dream.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Opinion
*Student:* Write a paragraph: which version tells the story better and why?

*How to teach:* The child writes an opinion paragraph: which version tells the story better?

- **Q:** What makes a strong answer?
  **A:** A clear opinion with 2-3 reasons supported by specific examples from both book and film.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take the novel comprehension test.

*How to teach:* Give the novel comprehension test.

- **Q:** What color are Dorothy's shoes in the book?
  **A:** Silver.

**Assessment:** Novel test and comparison paragraph.

**Answer key:** Novel test key: 1) Dorothy is carried to Oz by a cyclone 2) Her house kills the Wicked Witch of the East 3) Each friend's wish (brains, heart, courage; Dorothy home) 4) Oz demands they kill the Witch of the West 5) Dorothy melts her with a bucket of water 6) The Wizard is a humbug from Omaha 7) Gifts: bran brains, silk heart, courage drink 8) Glinda reveals the silver shoes' power 9) Theme supported with evidence. Comparison paragraph rubric: 4 = clear opinion, 2+ accurate book/film differences used as reasons, conclusion; 3 = one reason; 2 = opinion with vague reasons; 1 = no opinion. Proficient: 7/9.

## Math 4: Decimals and money problems

**Prepare:** Play money (bills, dimes, pennies), grocery ads or printed prices. Unit 7 test for Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Writing $1.05 as $1.5. Cents are hundredths: 5 cents = 0.05.
- Lining up the last digits instead of the decimal points when adding money. Line up decimal points (or think in cents).

### Day 1 (Monday): Money as decimals
*Student:* Write 10 amounts of money as decimals and fractions of a dollar.

*How to teach:* Show $2.45 with bills and coins and write it as 2 45/100 dollars. The child writes 10 amounts as decimals and fractions.

- **Q:** Write 3 dollars and 45 cents as a decimal and a mixed number.
  **A:** $3.45 = 3 45/100 dollars.
- **Q:** Write 6 dimes and 2 pennies as a decimal of a dollar.
  **A:** $0.62 = 62/100 of a dollar.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Store simulation
*Student:* Use grocery ads to compare prices and find which of two items costs less.

*How to teach:* Give two prices from the ads (e.g., cereal $3.89 vs $3.98) and have the child decide which costs less by comparing places.

- **Q:** Which costs less: $3.89 or $3.98?
  **A:** $3.89 — 8 dimes is less than 9 dimes.
- **Q:** Which is greater: $5.09 or $5.10?
  **A:** $5.10.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Money word problems
*Student:* Solve 5 problems adding and subtracting money amounts using whole-cent reasoning.

*How to teach:* Model thinking in cents: $4.75 + $2.80 = 475 + 280 = 755 cents = $7.55. The child solves 5 problems.

- **Q:** You buy items for $4.75 and $2.80 and pay with $10. How much change?
  **A:** $4.75 + $2.80 = $7.55; $10.00 - $7.55 = $2.45.
- **Q:** Milk costs $3.29. How much for 2 cartons?
  **A:** $6.58.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit 7 review
*Student:* Mixed review of tenths, hundredths and comparison.

*How to teach:* Mixed review of tenths, hundredths and comparison; the child self-checks.

- **Q:** Write 0.4 as hundredths.
  **A:** 0.40 = 40/100.
- **Q:** 3/10 + 6/100 = ?
  **A:** 36/100 = 0.36.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 7 test on tenths, hundredths, decimal comparison and money.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test independently. Correct together.

- **Q:** Order $1.50, $1.05, $1.55.
  **A:** $1.05, $1.50, $1.55.

**Assessment:** Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 7 test key: 1) 45/100 = 0.45 2) 0.7 = 7/10 = 70/100 3) 2/10 + 5/100 = 25/100 = 0.25 4) 0.6 > 0.06 5) 0.39 < 0.4 6) Order 0.8, 0.08, 0.88: 0.08, 0.8, 0.88 7) $3.45 = 3 45/100 dollars 8) $4.75 + $2.80 = $7.55 9) $10.00 - $7.55 = $2.45 10) Place 0.25 on a number line: one quarter of the way from 0 to 1 (between 0.2 and 0.3). Proficient: 8/10.

## Science 4: Natural resources and energy

**Prepare:** 12 resource picture cards (sun, wind, water, trees, soil, coal, oil, natural gas, uranium, copper, geothermal heat, plants for biofuel), a family energy bill, a pie chart of California's electricity sources (California Energy Commission 'Total System Electric Generation').

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all renewable resources are unlimited. Renewable means replaced naturally in a human lifetime; trees and water can still be overused.
- Thinking fossil fuels come from dinosaurs. Most come from ancient plants (coal) and tiny ocean organisms (oil and gas).

### Day 1 (Monday): Resource sort
*Student:* Sort 12 resources into renewable and nonrenewable.

*How to teach:* Sort the 12 cards.

- **Q:** Is natural gas renewable?
  **A:** No — it takes millions of years to form.
- **Q:** Is wind renewable?
  **A:** Yes — it is constantly replenished.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Fossil fuels
*Student:* Read about how coal, oil and natural gas formed from ancient living things and draw a 3-step diagram.

*How to teach:* Read how fossil fuels formed and draw 3 steps.

- **Q:** What are the 3 steps of fossil fuel formation?
  **A:** 1) Ancient plants and tiny sea organisms died; 2) they were buried under layers of sediment; 3) over millions of years heat and pressure turned them into coal, oil and natural gas.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Home energy
*Student:* Read a family energy bill and identify energy sources.

*How to teach:* Read the energy bill; find kilowatt-hours used and sources if listed.

- **Q:** What unit measures electricity use on a bill?
  **A:** Kilowatt-hours (kWh).

### Day 4 (Thursday): California energy
*Student:* Read a pie chart of California's electricity sources (solar, natural gas, hydro, wind, nuclear) and list the top three.

*How to teach:* Read California's electricity pie chart and list the top three sources.

- **Q:** What are the largest sources?
  **A:** Answer from your chart; in recent California Energy Commission data, natural gas has been the largest single source, followed by solar, with wind, large hydro and nuclear next.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz classifying resources as renewable or nonrenewable and naming their uses.

*How to teach:* Give the resource quiz.

- **Q:** Name a use of oil.
  **A:** Gasoline for cars (also plastics, heating).

**Assessment:** Resource quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: Renewable — sunlight (solar power), wind (turbines), moving water (hydroelectric), geothermal heat, trees (wood/paper, if replanted), biofuel plants. Nonrenewable — coal (electricity), oil (gasoline, plastics), natural gas (electricity, heating, cooking), uranium (nuclear power), copper (wires). Proficient: 10/12 classified and used correctly.

## California, A Changing State: The transcontinental railroad

**Prepare:** Calisphere set 'The Transcontinental Railroad,' map. Facts: the Central Pacific (from Sacramento, begun 1863) was led by the 'Big Four' — Leland Stanford, Collis Huntington, Mark Hopkins and Charles Crocker; Chinese workers (about 12,000-20,000, most of the workforce) blasted tunnels and built through the Sierra Nevada for lower pay than white workers and without board; the lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah, on May 10, 1869 (Golden Spike) — Chinese workers were not shown in the famous celebration photograph.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the railroad was built by one company from both coasts. Central Pacific built east from Sacramento; Union Pacific built west from Omaha.
- Overlooking Chinese workers' role. They did the most dangerous work in the Sierra.

### Day 1 (Monday): The Big Four
*Student:* Read about Stanford, Huntington, Hopkins and Crocker and the Central Pacific Railroad.

*How to teach:* Read about the Big Four.

- **Q:** Who were the Big Four?
  **A:** Leland Stanford, Collis Huntington, Mark Hopkins and Charles Crocker — Sacramento merchants who ran the Central Pacific.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Chinese workers
*Student:* Read about Chinese workers who blasted tunnels through the Sierra Nevada and their unequal pay.

*How to teach:* Read about Chinese workers.

- **Q:** Why was the Sierra Nevada the hardest part?
  **A:** Workers had to blast tunnels through solid granite, build on steep cliffs and work through deep snow and avalanches.
- **Q:** How were Chinese workers treated unfairly?
  **A:** They were paid less than white workers, had to buy their own food and lodging, and did the most dangerous jobs.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Primary sources
*Student:* Analyze photographs from the Calisphere set 'The Transcontinental Railroad' and write captions.

*How to teach:* Analyze railroad photographs and write captions.

- **Q:** What should a good caption include?
  **A:** Who/what is shown, where and when, and why it matters.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Golden Spike
*Student:* Read about the May 10, 1869 ceremony at Promontory Summit and who was left out of the famous photo.

*How to teach:* Read about the Golden Spike ceremony.

- **Q:** When and where were the rails joined?
  **A:** May 10, 1869, at Promontory Summit, Utah.
- **Q:** Who was left out of the famous photo?
  **A:** Chinese workers.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on how the railroad changed California's economy.

*How to teach:* Write how the railroad changed California's economy.

- **Q:** Name two economic effects.
  **A:** Goods and people traveled coast to coast in about a week instead of months; California farm products could be shipped east; more settlers and businesses came.

**Assessment:** Railroad paragraph.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric: 4 = topic sentence plus 3 accurate effects (faster travel ~1 week, shipping crops east, population growth, cheaper goods, rise of railroad monopoly), mention of Chinese labor, conclusion; 3 = two effects; 2 = one; 1 = inaccurate.

## Art & Music 4: Sculpture revision and installation

**Prepare:** Gather the dried clay sculpture, acrylic or tempera paint, brushes, index cards for labels, and a stable display spot.

**Watch for:**
- Displaying art in direct sunlight. Light fades paper and paint.
- Placing sculpture near edges. Choose a stable surface away from traffic.

### Day 1 (Monday): Feedback
*Student:* Get feedback on sculpture and prints using 'I notice / I wonder / I suggest'.

*How to teach:* Get feedback using 'I notice / I wonder / I suggest.' Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** Give an 'I suggest' comment for a sculpture.
  **A:** 'I suggest painting the otter's fur darker to show texture.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Revise
*Student:* Paint the dried clay sculpture with acrylic or tempera and add details suggested in feedback.

*How to teach:* Paint the sculpture and add suggested details. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** Why wait until clay is fully dry to paint?
  **A:** Wet clay can crack and paint won't stick well.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Display plan
*Student:* Plan where to display the sculpture safely (stable surface, away from edges and sunlight) and sketch the setup.

*How to teach:* Plan the display: stable surface, away from edges and sun; sketch it. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** Why keep art out of direct sunlight?
  **A:** Sunlight fades colors and can damage materials.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Labels
*Student:* Write gallery labels for the sculpture and best print with title, medium, date and a one-sentence description.

*How to teach:* Write gallery labels (title, medium, date, description). Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** What is the medium of your sculpture?
  **A:** Air-dry clay with acrylic (or tempera) paint.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & share
*Student:* Install the sculpture and prints and give a 2-minute talk to family about your process.

*How to teach:* Install the work; give a 2-minute talk. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** What should your talk include?
  **A:** Your process, techniques and what you learned.

**Assessment:** Installed sculpture and labels.

**Answer key:** Installation: sculpture painted and stable, prints displayed, labels complete with title, medium, date and description. Talk proficient if it explains process and one revision.

## PE 4: Striking with bats

**Prepare:** Plastic bat, tee or tall cone, whiffle balls, a glove optional, bases (paper plates), safety zone marked around the batter.

**Watch for:**
- Swinging down at the ball (chopping) or up (uppercut). Swing level through the ball.
- Fielding grounders standing straight up. Get low, glove/hands on the ground, and bring the ball to the belly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Stance & grip
*Student:* Practice batting stance and grip with knuckles aligned.

*How to teach:* Grip: hands together, knuckles aligned (door-knocking knuckles in a line), right-handers left hand bottom. Stance: side-on, feet shoulder-width, knees bent, bat up behind the back shoulder. 20 dry swings.

- **Q:** What does 'knuckles aligned' mean in the grip?
  **A:** The middle (door-knocking) knuckles of both hands line up along the bat.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tee hitting
*Student:* Hit 30 balls off a tee aiming for level swings.

*How to teach:* Hit 30 balls off a tee at waist height with a level swing, stepping toward the target.

- **Q:** Why place the tee in front of the front foot area?
  **A:** You make contact out in front of your body, where the swing is strongest.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Soft toss
*Student:* Hit 20 soft-tossed balls from a parent, stepping toward the pitcher and swinging level.

*How to teach:* Soft toss: parent tosses underhand from the side or front from a safe distance; the child steps toward the pitcher and swings level. 20 swings.

- **Q:** Which way do you step when hitting?
  **A:** Toward the pitcher (target) with the front foot.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fielding
*Student:* Practice fielding grounders and throwing to a base.

*How to teach:* Fielding: ready position, move in front of the ball, glove/hands down, bring it in, then step and throw to a base.

- **Q:** How do you field a ground ball?
  **A:** Get in front of it, low with hands down (glove open), watch it in, then bring it to your belly before throwing.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Play a game of whiffle ball with family and track hits.

*How to teach:* Play family whiffle ball and track hits.

- **Q:** Why place the bat down after hitting?
  **A:** A thrown bat can hurt someone.

**Assessment:** Batting self-assessment.

**Answer key:** Batting self-assessment checklist: grip with knuckles aligned, side stance, step toward pitcher, level swing, eyes on ball, bat placed down. Child checks each after the game and records hits/at-bats (e.g., 5 hits in 8 at-bats). Proficient = 5 of 6 cues checked by both child and parent.

## Health 4: Alcohol and the growing brain

**Prepare:** Parent preview. Brain diagram (KidsHealth 'Your Brain & Nervous System' or NIDA for Teens). A chart of youth alcohol data (e.g., the CDC Youth Risk Behavior Survey or the California Healthy Kids Survey) showing most young people don't drink. Talk ahead about your family's plan if an adult who has been drinking offers a ride (call a parent, code word).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the brain is fully grown by age 10. The brain keeps developing until about age 25, especially the part used for decisions (prefrontal cortex).
- Thinking you must accept a ride from an adult you know. If the driver has been drinking, you can refuse and call a parent or trusted adult.

### Day 1 (Monday): Brain parts
*Student:* Label brain parts and their jobs (thinking, memory, balance).

*How to teach:* Label brain parts: cerebrum (thinking, learning, memory, decisions), cerebellum (balance and coordination), brain stem (breathing, heartbeat).

- **Q:** Which part of the brain controls balance and coordination?
  **A:** The cerebellum.
- **Q:** Which part controls breathing and heartbeat?
  **A:** The brain stem.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Growing brain
*Student:* Read why young brains are more affected by alcohol.

*How to teach:* Read why young brains are more affected: the brain is still growing until about 25; alcohol can harm memory and learning and increases the chance of addiction later.

- **Q:** Why is alcohol more harmful for young people?
  **A:** Their brains are still developing, so alcohol can harm memory, learning and decision-making, and makes addiction more likely.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Norms
*Student:* Read data showing most young people don't drink alcohol.

*How to teach:* Read data showing most young people don't drink alcohol; graph or discuss it.

- **Q:** What does the data tell us about young people and alcohol?
  **A:** Most young people choose not to drink.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Safety
*Student:* Discuss drunk driving and what to do if an adult who has been drinking offers you a ride.

*How to teach:* Discuss drunk driving dangers. Make a plan: if an adult who has been drinking offers a ride, say no, call a parent or trusted adult, use a code word, never get in the car.

- **Q:** What should you do if the adult giving you a ride has been drinking?
  **A:** Don't get in the car; say no politely, call a parent or another trusted adult for a ride.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write 3 reasons kids choose not to drink alcohol, using facts from this week's readings.

*How to teach:* Write 3 reasons kids choose not to drink, using facts from the week.

- **Q:** Give one fact-based reason to not drink.
  **A:** E.g., 'Alcohol can harm the developing brain's memory and learning,' or 'It's illegal under 21,' or 'It slows reaction time and causes accidents.'

**Assessment:** Reasons paragraph.

**Answer key:** Reasons paragraph rubric: 4 = 3 clear reasons each supported by a fact from this week (brain develops until about 25 and alcohol harms memory/learning; illegal under 21; impairs judgment and coordination leading to injuries; most young people don't drink; risk of addiction), with a topic and concluding sentence; 3 = 3 reasons, 1–2 with facts; 2 = 1–2 reasons; 1 = incomplete.
