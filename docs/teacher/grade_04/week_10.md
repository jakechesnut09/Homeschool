# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 10

## ELA 4: Text structures

**Prepare:** Short nonfiction articles on the Golden Gate Bridge, the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, drought solutions, and a comparison of two animals; graphic organizers (timeline, cause-effect, problem-solution, Venn). Key facts: Golden Gate Bridge construction began 1933, opened May 1937; the 1906 earthquake struck April 18, 1906 and the fires that followed destroyed much of the city.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking text structure is the topic. Structure is HOW information is organized (sequence, cause/effect, compare/contrast, problem/solution, description).
- Confusing cause and effect. The cause happens first and makes the effect happen; test with 'because'.

### Day 1 (Monday): Chronology
*Student:* Read an article on the building of the Golden Gate Bridge and make a timeline.

*How to teach:* Read the Golden Gate Bridge article and build a timeline. Point out signal words (first, in 1933, then, finally).

- **Q:** When did construction start and when did the Golden Gate Bridge open?
  **A:** Construction began in 1933; it opened in 1937.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cause/effect
*Student:* Read about the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and chart causes and effects.

*How to teach:* Read about the 1906 earthquake and chart causes and effects.

- **Q:** What caused most of the destruction in 1906?
  **A:** The fires that broke out after the earthquake (broken gas lines, damaged water mains made them hard to fight) burned for days and destroyed much of the city.
- **Q:** Name an effect of the earthquake on people.
  **A:** Hundreds of thousands were left homeless and lived in camps/tents; much of the city had to be rebuilt.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compare/problem-solution
*Student:* Read about drought solutions and record the problem and solutions; compare two animals in another passage.

*How to teach:* Read about drought and record the problem and solutions; then read the animal comparison and fill in a Venn diagram.

- **Q:** Name two solutions to drought in California.
  **A:** Examples: water conservation (shorter showers, drought-tolerant plants), recycling wastewater, storing water in reservoirs/groundwater.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Structure sort
*Student:* Identify the structure of 6 short paragraphs by their signal words.

*How to teach:* The child reads 6 short paragraphs, circles signal words, and names each structure.

- **Q:** Which structure uses 'as a result' and 'therefore'?
  **A:** Cause and effect.
- **Q:** Which structure uses 'however' and 'both'?
  **A:** Compare and contrast.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: name the structure of 4 passages and draw the matching organizer.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz: name the structure of 4 passages and draw the matching organizer.

- **Q:** Which organizer matches problem/solution?
  **A:** A box for the problem with arrows to boxes for solutions.

**Assessment:** Text structure quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: Passage 1 (sequence of the bridge's construction) = chronology/sequence, timeline. Passage 2 (why the earthquake caused fires) = cause/effect, cause→effect chart. Passage 3 (drought and conservation) = problem/solution, problem box with solution boxes. Passage 4 (otters vs. seals) = compare/contrast, Venn diagram. 2 points each (name + organizer); proficient 6/8.

## Math 4: Multiplying by 10s, 100s and 1,000s; 1-digit by multi-digit

**Prepare:** Grid paper, base-ten blocks. Prepare 8 problems for Day 3 (4-digit × 1-digit) and 8 for Day 4 (3-digit × 1-digit).

**Watch for:**
- Partial products added incorrectly because places weren't lined up. Write each partial product on its own line, right-aligned.
- In the algorithm, adding the regrouped digit before multiplying (e.g., in 6 × 378, adding the carried 4 to 7 before multiplying). Rule: multiply first, then add what was regrouped.

### Day 1 (Monday): Patterns with zeros
*Student:* Use place value to solve 4 x 3, 4 x 30, 4 x 300, 4 x 3,000 and explain the pattern.

*How to teach:* Write 4 × 3, 4 × 30, 4 × 300, 4 × 3,000 and have the child solve each and say '4 times 3 tens is 12 tens = 120.' The child explains the pattern in words.

- **Q:** Solve 4 × 3, 4 × 30, 4 × 300, 4 × 3,000.
  **A:** 12; 120; 1,200; 12,000.
- **Q:** What is 6 × 500?
  **A:** 3,000 (6 × 5 hundreds = 30 hundreds).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Area model
*Student:* Draw area models for 6 x 47 and 3 x 215 and add partial products.

*How to teach:* Draw a rectangle for 6 × 47 split into 6 × 40 and 6 × 7. The child computes each section and adds, then does 3 × 215 split into three sections.

- **Q:** 6 × 47 using an area model.
  **A:** 240 + 42 = 282.
- **Q:** 3 × 215 using an area model.
  **A:** 600 + 30 + 15 = 645.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Four digits by one digit
*Student:* Solve 8 problems like 4 x 2,316 using area models and partial products.

*How to teach:* The child solves 8 problems like 4 × 2,316 by splitting into thousands, hundreds, tens and ones. Check that each partial product is labeled.

- **Q:** 4 × 2,316 = ?
  **A:** 8,000 + 1,200 + 40 + 24 = 9,264.
- **Q:** 5 × 1,623 = ?
  **A:** 5,000 + 3,000 + 100 + 15 = 8,115.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Standard algorithm intro
*Student:* Connect partial products to the standard algorithm for 3-digit by 1-digit problems (8 problems).

*How to teach:* Solve 3 × 254 with partial products, then with the standard algorithm, and point out where 12 (from 3 × 4) shows up as a regrouped 1. The child does 8 problems both ways at first, then algorithm only.

- **Q:** 3 × 254 = ?
  **A:** 762.
- **Q:** 6 × 378 = ?
  **A:** 2,268.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on 1-digit by multi-digit multiplication.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz independently; area model items must show sections. Correct together.

- **Q:** 7 × 43 = ?
  **A:** 301.

**Assessment:** Quiz with area model and algorithm items.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) 5 × 600 = 3,000 2) 7 × 43 = 301 3) 8 × 325 = 2,600 4) 6 × 1,407 = 8,442 5) 9 × 2,135 = 19,215 6) Area model for 4 × 286: 800 + 320 + 24 = 1,144 7) 3 × 4,000 = 12,000 8) 2 × 3,759 = 7,518. Proficient: 7/8 with work shown.

## Science 4: Waves cause motion

**Prepare:** Tub of water, sand, pebbles, a small toy boat or plastic block, ruler. Video on California surf/tsunamis (e.g., NOAA or USGS educational clips).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking waves carry water across the ocean. Waves carry energy; near shore that energy moves sand and objects.
- Thinking only big waves move things. All waves transfer energy; bigger amplitude transfers more.

### Day 1 (Monday): Wave pushes
*Student:* Show that larger amplitude waves move a toy boat more and measure distance.

*How to teach:* Make small and large waves toward a floating toy; measure how far it moves.

- **Q:** Which waves moved the boat farther?
  **A:** Larger-amplitude waves — they carry more energy.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Beach model
*Student:* Build a sand beach in a tub and make waves to observe sand movement.

*How to teach:* Build a sloped sand beach in the tub and make waves for 2 minutes.

- **Q:** What happened to the sand?
  **A:** Waves moved sand down the slope and along the beach — the shape of the beach changed.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ocean waves
*Student:* Watch a video on California surf and tsunamis and record how waves carry energy.

*How to teach:* Watch the video and list how waves carry energy.

- **Q:** Why can a tsunami cause damage far inland?
  **A:** It carries a huge amount of energy and a large volume of water, so it rushes far inland when it reaches shore.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sound as waves
*Student:* Connect sound waves to wave patterns with a slinky.

*How to teach:* Stretch the slinky and push one end to send a pulse (compression wave). Connect to sound.

- **Q:** How is a slinky pulse like sound?
  **A:** Sound is also a wave — vibrations pass along through air (or other matter) without the air itself traveling to your ear.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a CER on how waves cause objects to move.

*How to teach:* The child writes a CER on how waves cause objects to move.

- **Q:** Write the claim.
  **A:** Waves transfer energy that can move objects; bigger waves move them more.

**Assessment:** CER on waves causing motion.

**Answer key:** CER rubric: 4 = claim that waves carry energy which moves objects; evidence from the boat measurements and sand beach; reasoning connecting amplitude to energy; 3 = claim and evidence, weak reasoning; 2 = observation only; 1 = incorrect.

## California, A Changing State: Land routes and the Russians

**Prepare:** Map, Calisphere set 'Early California Exploration and Settlement.' Facts: Juan Bautista de Anza led expeditions in 1774 and 1775-76 from Tubac (Sonora, now Arizona) — the second brought about 240 settlers who founded the San Francisco presidio and mission in 1776. Russians built Fort Ross in 1812 on the Sonoma coast to hunt sea otters and grow food for Russian Alaska; it was sold to John Sutter in 1841. Unit 3 test for Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking only Spain was interested in California. Russia and England also had interests, which pushed Spain to settle.
- Thinking Fort Ross was only Russians. Alaska Native hunters (Alutiiq and others) and local Kashaya Pomo also lived and worked there.

### Day 1 (Monday): Anza Trail
*Student:* Trace Juan Bautista de Anza's overland route from Sonora to San Francisco.

*How to teach:* Trace Anza's route.

- **Q:** Why was Anza's overland route important?
  **A:** It created a land route to bring settlers and livestock to Alta California instead of risky sea voyages; his settlers founded San Francisco in 1776.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Russians
*Student:* Read about Fort Ross and the Russian-American Company's sea otter trade.

*How to teach:* Read about Fort Ross.

- **Q:** Why did the Russians build Fort Ross?
  **A:** To hunt sea otters for valuable furs and to grow food for Russian settlements in Alaska.
- **Q:** What happened to Fort Ross?
  **A:** Sea otters were overhunted, farming failed, and Russia sold it to John Sutter in 1841.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Primary sources
*Student:* Analyze images from the Calisphere set 'Early California Exploration and Settlement'.

*How to teach:* Analyze Calisphere images.

- **Q:** What can a drawing of a mission or ship tell us?
  **A:** Clothing, buildings, tools and who was present at the time; also the artist's point of view.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compare empires
*Student:* Compare Spanish, English and Russian goals in a chart.

*How to teach:* Compare Spanish, English and Russian goals.

- **Q:** What were Spain's goals?
  **A:** Claim land, spread Catholicism, protect trade routes, keep other empires out.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 3 test on explorers, their routes and motives, and Fort Ross.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 test.

- **Q:** Which explorer named Monterey?
  **A:** Sebastián Vizcaíno (1602).

**Assessment:** Unit 3 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 3 test key: 1) Cabrillo 1542, Spain, San Diego Bay 2) Drake 1579, England, Nova Albion 3) Vizcaíno 1602, named Monterey 4) Manila galleons needed a port for supplies 5) Anza's land route, San Francisco settled 1776 6) Fort Ross 1812, Russians, sea otters 7) Fort Ross sold to Sutter 1841 8) Compare motives (wealth, claim land, religion, furs). Proficient: 80%.

## Art & Music 4: Landscape space: foreground, middle ground, background

**Prepare:** Find a painting by California Impressionist Guy Rose (e.g., 'Laguna Eucalyptus' or 'Incoming Tide') and photos of Yosemite, the coast and a desert. Gather paper and colored pencils.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the background should be as detailed as the foreground. Distant things show less detail.
- Placing far objects lower on the page. Objects farther away are placed higher, nearer the horizon.

### Day 1 (Monday): Depth tricks
*Student:* Look at a California landscape painting by a California Impressionist (e.g., Guy Rose) and find foreground, middle and background.

*How to teach:* Look at a Guy Rose painting; find foreground, middle ground and background. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** Which ground is closest to the viewer?
  **A:** The foreground (bottom of the picture).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Atmospheric perspective
*Student:* Draw three mountain ranges getting lighter as they recede.

*How to teach:* Draw three mountain ranges getting lighter as they recede. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** Why do distant mountains look lighter and bluer?
  **A:** Air and haze between you and the mountains soften color and detail (atmospheric perspective).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sketch
*Student:* Sketch a California landscape (Yosemite, coast or desert) with overlapping layers.

*How to teach:* Sketch a California landscape with overlapping layers. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** How does overlapping show depth?
  **A:** An object in front blocks part of the one behind it, so we know it's closer.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Color
*Student:* Add warm colors in front and cool colors behind.

*How to teach:* Add warm colors in front, cool behind. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** Why use cool colors in the background?
  **A:** Cool colors seem to recede, making things look farther away.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & share
*Student:* Explain to family how overlap, size, placement and color show depth in your landscape.

*How to teach:* Explain how overlap, size, placement and color show depth. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** How does size show depth?
  **A:** Farther objects are drawn smaller.

**Assessment:** Landscape sketch.

**Answer key:** Landscape sketch: 4 = clear foreground/middle/background, overlap, size change, placement and warm/cool color; 3 = three of these; 2 = two; 1 = flat.

## PE 4: Creating space in small-sided soccer

**Prepare:** Soccer ball, cones for a small field and two small goals (2 cones each, about 4 feet apart), pinnies or colored shirts, paper for the strategy diagram. Gather 2–3 players.

**Watch for:**
- Bunching around the ball. Spread out; width and depth make passing lanes.
- Defenders chasing the ball everywhere. Stay goal-side (between attacker and goal) and wait for a chance to win the ball.

### Day 1 (Monday): Open space
*Student:* Play keep-away 2-on-1, moving to open space to receive.

*How to teach:* 2-on-1 keep-away in a square: the receiver moves to open space (a passing lane not blocked by the defender) and calls for the ball.

- **Q:** What is a passing lane?
  **A:** A clear path between the passer and a teammate that a defender isn't blocking.
- **Q:** What should you do after you pass?
  **A:** Move to a new open space to get the ball back.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Defense
*Student:* Practice defensive stance and staying goal-side.

*How to teach:* Defensive stance: low, knees bent, side-on, on balls of feet. Shadow an attacker, staying goal-side and between them and the goal.

- **Q:** What does 'goal-side' mean?
  **A:** Positioned between the attacker and the goal you are defending.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Small-sided game
*Student:* Play a 2-on-2 soccer game with small cone goals, calling for the ball when open.

*How to teach:* Play 2-on-2 with small goals. Freeze play to point out open space; reward calling for the ball when open.

- **Q:** Why call for the ball?
  **A:** To let your teammate know you're open and where you are.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Strategy talk
*Student:* Draw a diagram showing how to create space on offense.

*How to teach:* Draw a diagram (dots for players, arrows for movement and passes) showing how to create space: spread wide, move away from the defender, then cut into space.

- **Q:** On offense, why spread out wide?
  **A:** It stretches the defense, creating gaps and passing lanes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Self-assess moving to space using a 3-point checklist.

*How to teach:* Self-assess on a 3-point checklist: I moved to open space; I called for the ball; I passed and moved.

- **Q:** Which checklist item will you improve?
  **A:** Answers vary; should name one item and how.

**Assessment:** Strategy diagram and self-assessment.

**Answer key:** Strategy diagram and self-assessment: diagram shows at least 2 attackers spreading out and moving into space with arrows for movement and passes and a labeled defender. Self-assessment rated honestly against observed play. 4 = clear diagram plus all 3 checklist behaviors seen in play; 3 = 2 behaviors; 2 = 1 behavior; 1 = none.

## Health 4: Comparing snacks and making choices

**Prepare:** An apple, a cup of applesauce (preferably unsweetened plus a sweetened label if available) and an apple-flavored fruit snack package; 4 snack options for the decision; labels for chips, pretzels, popcorn and nuts; ingredients for a snack (veggies and hummus or yogurt, fruit and granola).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 'made with real fruit' means the snack is as healthy as fruit. Fruit snacks are mostly sugar and lack the fiber and vitamins of whole fruit.
- Thinking only salty-tasting foods are high in sodium. Pretzels, bread, cheese and many processed foods are high in sodium.

### Day 1 (Monday): Whole vs processed
*Student:* Compare an apple, applesauce and an apple-flavored fruit snack label and rank them by nutrients.

*How to teach:* Compare the apple, applesauce and fruit snack. Rank by nutrients (fiber, vitamins, added sugar). Explain 'whole' vs. 'processed.'

- **Q:** Which is most nutrient-dense: an apple, applesauce or apple fruit snacks? Why?
  **A:** The whole apple — it has the most fiber and vitamins and no added sugar; fruit snacks are mostly added sugar.
- **Q:** What does 'processed' mean?
  **A:** Changed from its natural form by cooking, adding ingredients, or packaging; heavily processed foods often have added sugar, salt or fat.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Snack decision
*Student:* Use the decision model to choose an after-school snack from 4 options and explain the choice.

*How to teach:* Use the decision model to choose from 4 after-school snacks (e.g., cookies, cheese and crackers, chips, apple with peanut butter). The child explains the choice.

- **Q:** Which snack gives lasting energy: apple with peanut butter or cookies? Why?
  **A:** Apple with peanut butter — fiber from the apple and protein/healthy fat from peanut butter keep you full longer; cookies are mostly sugar.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sodium check
*Student:* Compare sodium in chips, pretzels, popcorn and nuts and graph the results.

*How to teach:* Record sodium per serving for chips, pretzels, popcorn and nuts and make a bar graph. Note daily limit for kids is roughly 1,800–2,300 mg.

- **Q:** Pretzels have 450 mg sodium per serving and unsalted nuts have 0 mg. How much more sodium do the pretzels have?
  **A:** 450 mg more.
- **Q:** Why limit sodium?
  **A:** Too much sodium over time can raise blood pressure and harm the heart.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Make a snack
*Student:* Prepare a nutrient-dense snack (veggies and hummus, yogurt parfait) and list its food groups.

*How to teach:* Prepare a nutrient-dense snack and list its food groups.

- **Q:** What does 'nutrient-dense' mean?
  **A:** A food with lots of nutrients (vitamins, minerals, fiber, protein) for its calories.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a 'Snack Smart' guide with 5 tips and 5 snack ideas.

*How to teach:* The child writes a 'Snack Smart' guide with 5 tips and 5 snack ideas.

- **Q:** Give a Snack Smart tip.
  **A:** E.g., combine two food groups; choose whole foods; check the label for added sugar and sodium; drink water; portion out snacks instead of eating from the bag.

**Assessment:** Snack Smart guide.

**Answer key:** Snack Smart guide rubric: 4 = 5 accurate tips (e.g., pair 2 food groups, choose whole over processed, use the 5/20 rule for sodium and added sugar, portion snacks, drink water) and 5 nutrient-dense snack ideas with food groups named; neat and clear; 3 = 4 tips/ideas; 2 = 2–3; 1 = incomplete.
