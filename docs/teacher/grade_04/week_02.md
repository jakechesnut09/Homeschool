# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 2

## ELA 4: Character and personal narrative beginnings

**Prepare:** Just So Stories ('The Elephant's Child'), writer's notebook. Write 20 prefix words (unhappy, unlock, unfair, unkind, untie, retell, rewrite, reread, rebuild, return, replay, disagree, disappear, dislike, dishonest, disobey, disconnect, unwrap, reheat, discover) and 12 sentences (half fragments/run-ons) for Day 4.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a run-on is just a long sentence. A run-on joins two complete thoughts without correct punctuation; fix with a period, or a comma plus and/but/so.
- Assuming every word starting with re- or dis- has the prefix (read, dish). Test it: remove the prefix and see if a real base word remains.

### Day 1 (Monday): Character traits
*Student:* Read 'The Elephant's Child' and chart the Elephant's Child's traits with evidence from his words and actions.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Elephant's Child' aloud. The child charts traits (curious, persistent, brave) with evidence from his words and actions.

- **Q:** What is the Elephant's Child's main trait? Give evidence.
  **A:** He is extremely curious ('full of 'satiable curtiosity') — he keeps asking questions, like what the Crocodile has for dinner, even when he is spanked for asking.
- **Q:** How does the Elephant's Child get his trunk?
  **A:** The Crocodile grabs his nose at the Limpopo River and pulls; the Elephant's Child pulls back (helped by the Bi-Coloured-Python-Rock-Snake) and his nose is stretched into a trunk.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Prefixes un-, re-, dis-
*Student:* Sort 20 words by prefix, define each using the prefix meaning, and use 5 in sentences.

*How to teach:* Teach un- = not/opposite, re- = again/back, dis- = not/opposite. The child sorts the 20 words, defines each using the prefix, and writes 5 sentences.

- **Q:** What does 'disobey' mean?
  **A:** To not obey.
- **Q:** What does 'rebuild' mean?
  **A:** To build again.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Narrative planning
*Student:* Choose a small moment from the heart map and plan it on a story mountain (beginning, rising events, high point, ending).

*How to teach:* Model a story mountain: beginning (setting, characters), rising events, high point (most exciting/emotional moment), ending. The child plans one small moment from the heart map.

- **Q:** Where on the story mountain does the most exciting moment go?
  **A:** At the top — the high point (climax).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Complete sentences
*Student:* Fix 12 fragments and run-ons, then check the narrative plan's first sentences for completeness.

*How to teach:* Model fixing a fragment (add a subject or verb) and a run-on (split or join with comma + conjunction). The child fixes 12 items and checks their own first sentences.

- **Q:** Fix: 'Ran to the park after lunch.'
  **A:** Add a subject: 'We ran to the park after lunch.'
- **Q:** Fix: 'I was tired I went to bed.'
  **A:** 'I was tired, so I went to bed.' or 'I was tired. I went to bed.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draft the narrative's opening scene with a hook and share it aloud; take a quiz on prefixes and sentence types.

*How to teach:* The child drafts an opening scene with a hook (action, dialogue, sound or question) and reads it aloud. Give the prefix/sentence quiz.

- **Q:** Name two ways to hook a reader.
  **A:** Start with action, dialogue, a sound effect, or a question.

**Assessment:** Character chart and prefix/sentence quiz.

**Answer key:** Character chart: proficient lists at least 3 traits each with a quote or action (curious — asks everyone questions; persistent — keeps asking despite spankings; brave — goes alone to the Limpopo). Quiz key (sample): un- 'not': unkind = not kind; re- 'again': retell = tell again; dis- 'not/opposite': disagree = not agree; dislike = not like; reread = read again. Sentence types: 'Under the table.' = fragment; 'The dog barked it ran away.' = run-on (fix: The dog barked, and it ran away.); 'My sister laughed.' = complete. Proficient: 80%.

## Math 4: Ten times as much

**Prepare:** Base-ten blocks, the place value chart extended to millions, a calculator. Look up (or use) these 2020 census populations: Los Angeles 3,898,747; San Diego 1,386,932; San Jose 1,013,240; San Francisco 873,965; Fresno 542,107.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a digit one place to the left is worth '1 more' instead of '10 times as much.' Use blocks: 10 rods make a flat, so each place is ten of the place to its right.
- Saying 'add a zero' when multiplying by 10 — that rule fails later with decimals. Say 'each digit moves one place to the left.'

### Day 1 (Monday): 10 ones = 1 ten
*Student:* Trade ten unit cubes for a rod and ten rods for a flat; write 'the 4 in 40 is 10 times the 4 in 4' sentences.

*How to teach:* Have the child count 10 unit cubes and trade them for a rod, then 10 rods for a flat. Model the sentence frame 'The 4 in 40 is 10 times the 4 in 4,' then have the child write 4 more sentences for hundreds and thousands.

- **Q:** How many tens are in one hundred? How many hundreds in one thousand?
  **A:** 10 tens = 100; 10 hundreds = 1,000.
- **Q:** Finish: The 6 in 600 is ___ times the 6 in 60.
  **A:** 10 times.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Calculator x10 chain
*Student:* Enter 3, then multiply by 10 repeatedly to 3,000,000; record each result and describe how the digits shift.

*How to teach:* Start at 3 on the calculator and press × 10 = repeatedly, recording each result in the chart. Ask the child to describe what happens to the 3 each time (it moves one place left; zeros hold the empty places).

- **Q:** List the results from 3 to 3,000,000.
  **A:** 30; 300; 3,000; 30,000; 300,000; 3,000,000.
- **Q:** How many times did you multiply by 10 to get from 3 to 3,000,000?
  **A:** 6 times (six zeros/six places moved).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Millions
*Student:* Read populations of California's five largest cities and write each in word and expanded form.

*How to teach:* Show how the millions period works (millions, thousands, ones). The child writes each of the five city populations in word and expanded form and reads them aloud.

- **Q:** Write 3,898,747 (Los Angeles) in word form.
  **A:** Three million, eight hundred ninety-eight thousand, seven hundred forty-seven.
- **Q:** Write 873,965 (San Francisco) in expanded form.
  **A:** 800,000 + 70,000 + 3,000 + 900 + 60 + 5.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compare values
*Student:* Solve 10 problems like 'How many times greater is the 5 in 5,200 than the 5 in 520?' and explain one in writing.

*How to teach:* Model one problem in the chart: place 5,200 and 520 under each other and see the 5 is one place farther left, so it is 10 times as much. The child solves 10 problems and explains one in writing.

- **Q:** How many times greater is the 5 in 5,200 than the 5 in 520?
  **A:** 10 times — 5,000 is 10 × 500.
- **Q:** How many times greater is the 3 in 3,000 than the 3 in 30?
  **A:** 100 times — two places left (10 × 10).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Exit ticket: explain with a drawing why the 2 in 2,000 is ten times the 2 in 200.

*How to teach:* The child draws 2 thousand-cubes and 2 flats, then explains in writing why the first is ten times the second. Check that the explanation mentions that 1,000 = 10 hundreds.

- **Q:** Why is the 2 in 2,000 ten times the 2 in 200?
  **A:** 2,000 is 20 hundreds and 200 is 2 hundreds; 20 hundreds is 10 times 2 hundreds (each thousand cube is 10 flats).

**Assessment:** Written explanation with drawing plus 10-problem practice set.

**Answer key:** Practice set answers (10 × relationship): 1) 7 in 700 vs 70: 10×. 2) 4 in 4,000 vs 400: 10×. 3) 9 in 90,000 vs 9,000: 10×. 4) 6 in 6,300 vs 630: 10×. 5) 8 in 800 vs 8: 100×. 6) 2 in 25,000 vs 2,500: 10×. 7) 1 in 100,000 vs 10,000: 10×. 8) 3 in 3,000 vs 30: 100×. 9) 5 in 50 vs 5: 10×. 10) 4 in 40,000 vs 400: 100×. Written explanation rubric: 4 = drawing shows 2 thousands as 20 hundreds and the sentence says 2,000 is 10 times 200 because each place is ten times the one to its right; 3 = correct drawing and statement but reasoning brief; 2 = correct answer, drawing or reasoning missing; 1 = incorrect relationship.

## Science 4: Speed and energy

**Prepare:** Cardboard ramp (1 m if possible), stack of books, marble or toy car, meter stick, stopwatch (phone), paper cup with a doorway cut in the rim. Copy a data table: height, trial 1-3 time, average, cup distance.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a heavier object always goes faster down a ramp. The main factor here is height; only change one variable at a time.
- Recording only one trial. Do 3 trials and average to reduce errors.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ramp setup
*Student:* Build a ramp with a book stack and practice timing a marble over 1 meter.

*How to teach:* Build the ramp with 2 books, mark a 1-meter run at the bottom, and practice timing the marble over that distance until times are consistent.

- **Q:** Why practice timing before collecting data?
  **A:** To make measurements more accurate and consistent (reduce reaction-time errors).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Height and speed
*Student:* Release a marble from 3 ramp heights, timing 3 trials each and recording data.

*How to teach:* Release the marble from 3 heights (2, 4, 6 books), 3 trials each; record times and averages.

- **Q:** Which height gave the fastest (shortest) time?
  **A:** The highest ramp — the marble moves faster when released from higher.
- **Q:** What variable did you change? What stayed the same?
  **A:** Changed: ramp height. Same: marble, release method, distance timed, surface.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cup push test
*Student:* Measure how far the marble pushes a paper cup at each height as evidence of energy.

*How to teach:* Put the cup at the bottom with its opening facing the ramp; measure how far the marble pushes it at each height.

- **Q:** What does the cup distance tell us?
  **A:** How much energy the marble had: the faster marble pushed the cup farther, showing it had more energy.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Graph and explain
*Student:* Graph height vs. cup distance and write a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph.

*How to teach:* Graph ramp height vs. cup distance; the child writes a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph.

- **Q:** Write a claim for this investigation.
  **A:** The faster an object moves, the more energy it has.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: predict and explain which marble has more energy in 4 scenarios.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz with 4 scenarios.

- **Q:** Which has more energy: a bike rolling slowly or the same bike speeding downhill?
  **A:** The speeding bike — faster motion means more energy.

**Assessment:** CER explanation relating speed and energy.

**Answer key:** CER rubric: 4 = Claim: faster objects have more energy; Evidence: specific data (e.g., from 6 books the marble pushed the cup 30 cm vs. 10 cm from 2 books); Reasoning: higher release → greater speed → more energy transferred to the cup; 3 = claim and evidence, weak reasoning; 2 = claim only; 1 = incorrect. Quiz key: faster object has more energy in each scenario (same object compared) — e.g., speeding car > parked car; ball thrown hard > ball tossed gently; marble from higher ramp > lower ramp; fast runner > walker.

## California, A Changing State: California's regions

**Prepare:** California physical map, blank outline map, colored pencils, photos of Imperial Valley farms, a Sierra ski town, a Central Valley orchard and a coastal harbor.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Central Valley is mountainous or a desert. It is a large, flat, fertile valley between the Coast Ranges and the Sierra Nevada.
- Thinking the desert region is useless. Irrigation turned desert valleys like the Imperial Valley into farmland.

### Day 1 (Monday): Capital and regions
*Student:* Color a blank California map showing the coastal, mountain, Central Valley and desert regions and star Sacramento.

*How to teach:* Color the four regions and star Sacramento.

- **Q:** Name California's four main regions.
  **A:** Coastal, mountain, Central Valley and desert.
- **Q:** What is California's capital?
  **A:** Sacramento.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Region study
*Student:* Read about each region's climate, landforms and vegetation and make a 4-column comparison chart.

*How to teach:* Read about each region and make a 4-column chart (climate, landforms, vegetation).

- **Q:** Describe the desert region's climate.
  **A:** Hot, very dry, little rain; includes the Mojave and Colorado Deserts and Death Valley.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Living in a region
*Student:* Compare photos of Imperial Valley farms, a Sierra ski town, a Central Valley orchard and a coastal fishing harbor; explain how land shapes jobs.

*How to teach:* Compare the four photos and explain how land shapes jobs.

- **Q:** Why is the Central Valley good for farming?
  **A:** Flat land, rich soil, long sunny growing season and irrigation water from rivers.
- **Q:** What jobs fit the coastal region?
  **A:** Fishing, shipping, tourism, trade at ports.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Our region
*Student:* Describe the region you live in using five geographic details and one photo you take.

*How to teach:* The child describes their own region with five details and a photo.

- **Q:** What details describe a region?
  **A:** Landforms, climate, plants, animals, water and how people use the land.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on regions and write a paragraph on how one region's environment affects people.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz and the paragraph.

- **Q:** Which mountain range runs along California's eastern side?
  **A:** The Sierra Nevada.

**Assessment:** Region chart and paragraph.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Regions: coast, mountains, Central Valley, desert 2) Capital: Sacramento 3) Sierra Nevada = eastern mountain range 4) Central Valley lies between the Coast Ranges and the Sierra Nevada 5) Mojave Desert in the southeast 6) Mild climate along the coast. Paragraph rubric: 4 = names a region, describes 2+ environmental features, explains how they affect jobs, housing or travel with examples; 3 = one feature and effect; 2 = description only; 1 = off-topic.

## Art & Music 4: Shape and form: shading with value

**Prepare:** Gather pencils (2B, 4B), paper, a flashlight, a ball and a cylinder object (can or roll). Darken a room for Tuesday. Vocabulary: value = lightness or darkness.

**Watch for:**
- Shading with an outline around the shape. Use value changes to show the edge.
- Putting the cast shadow on the same side as the light. The cast shadow falls on the side away from the light.

### Day 1 (Monday): Value scale
*Student:* Create a 7-step value scale from white to black with a 4B pencil.

*How to teach:* Your child draws 7 boxes and shades from white to darkest black with a 4B, pressing harder step by step.

- **Q:** What is value?
  **A:** How light or dark a color or tone is.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Light and shadow
*Student:* Shine a flashlight on a ball and identify highlight, midtone, core shadow and cast shadow.

*How to teach:* Shine a flashlight on a ball in a dark room; your child points to highlight, midtone, core shadow and cast shadow.

- **Q:** Where is the highlight on the ball?
  **A:** On the spot facing the light most directly.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Shade a sphere
*Student:* Draw and shade a sphere, cube and cylinder using the value scale.

*How to teach:* Draw and shade a sphere, cube and cylinder with one light source. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** On a cube lit from the upper left, which face is lightest?
  **A:** The top (facing the light); the face away from the light is darkest.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Hatching
*Student:* Practice hatching, cross-hatching and stippling in a 3-box chart.

*How to teach:* Make a 3-box chart for hatching, cross-hatching and stippling (dots). Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** How do you make stippling darker?
  **A:** Put the dots closer together.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & share
*Student:* Shade a piece of fruit from observation and compare it with the week 1 contour drawing.

*How to teach:* Shade a fruit from observation; compare with week 1's contour drawing. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** How does shading change a drawing?
  **A:** It makes flat shapes look three-dimensional (form).

**Assessment:** Shaded fruit drawing.

**Answer key:** Shaded fruit: 4 = light source consistent, highlight, midtone, core shadow and cast shadow all present, smooth value range; 3 = one element missing; 2 = flat with little value; 1 = outline only.

## PE 4: Fitness pre-assessment

**Prepare:** Set up a 20-meter PACER lane with cones (free PACER audio cadence tracks are online) or a measured 1-mile route. Mat, a 12-inch ruler or yardstick for trunk lift, a box for back-saver sit-and-reach (or yardstick taped to a step), a cadence app or recording (one curl-up/push-up every 3 seconds). Print a fitness record sheet and look up the FITNESSGRAM Healthy Fitness Zone charts for the child's age and sex.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking these tests are a contest. They measure your own health-related fitness so you can set goals and track growth.
- Trading good form for more reps. Only reps with correct form at the cadence count.

### Day 1 (Monday): Five components
*Student:* Match 10 activities to the five health-related fitness components.

*How to teach:* Teach the 5 components: cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, body composition. Match 10 activities (e.g., swimming laps, push-ups, plank, yoga, biking, carrying groceries, toe-touch stretch, jump rope, long hike, eating balanced meals plus activity).

- **Q:** Which component does a long bike ride build?
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance.
- **Q:** What's the difference between muscular strength and muscular endurance?
  **A:** Strength is the most force a muscle can make at once; endurance is how many times or how long a muscle can keep working.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): PACER or mile
*Student:* Complete the 20-meter PACER (or a timed 1-mile walk/run) and record laps or time.

*How to teach:* Warm up. Run the PACER: run 20 m before each beep, which gets faster each minute; the test ends the second time the child doesn't reach the line before the beep. Record laps (or time the 1-mile walk/run). Cool down.

- **Q:** When does the PACER end?
  **A:** The second time you don't reach the line before the beep.
- **Q:** Which fitness component does the PACER measure?
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory (aerobic) endurance.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Curl-ups & push-ups
*Student:* Complete cadence curl-ups and 90-degree push-ups tests and record results.

*How to teach:* Curl-ups: knees bent, feet flat, arms straight, fingertips slide 3 inches (a measuring strip) at one every 3 seconds until two form errors. Push-ups: straight body, lower to a 90-degree elbow, one every 3 seconds until two form errors. Record.

- **Q:** What is the cadence for curl-ups and push-ups?
  **A:** One every 3 seconds (20 per minute).
- **Q:** What does 90 degrees mean in the push-up?
  **A:** Lowering until the elbows are bent at a right angle (upper arms parallel to the floor).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Trunk lift & sit-and-reach
*Student:* Complete trunk lift and back-saver sit-and-reach tests and record results.

*How to teach:* Trunk lift: lie face down, toes pointed, hands under thighs; slowly lift the upper body as high as comfortable (up to 12 inches) and hold while you measure from floor to chin; best of 2. Back-saver sit-and-reach: one leg straight, the other bent; reach forward 4 times, hold the 4th; measure each side.

- **Q:** Why lift slowly in the trunk lift and stop at 12 inches?
  **A:** To protect the lower back; lifting higher or jerking can cause injury and isn't needed.
- **Q:** What does the sit-and-reach test measure?
  **A:** Flexibility of the hamstrings (back of the legs) and lower back.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Compare results to Healthy Fitness Zone charts with a parent.

*How to teach:* Compare each result to the Healthy Fitness Zone chart for the child's age and sex. Circle areas in the zone and areas to improve.

- **Q:** What does it mean if a score is in the Healthy Fitness Zone?
  **A:** Your fitness level in that area is enough for good health for your age.

**Assessment:** Baseline fitness data recorded.

**Answer key:** Baseline fitness data check: the record sheet should include PACER laps (or mile time), curl-ups, push-ups, trunk lift (inches, max 12), sit-and-reach left and right (inches), and each compared to the Healthy Fitness Zone chart (in zone / needs improvement). Proficient = all tests completed with correct form and cadence, all scores recorded, comparison done with a parent.

## Health 4: Decision making and goal setting

**Prepare:** Make a decision-making worksheet with the 5 steps: Stop, Think of options, Consider consequences (healthy? safe? legal? respectful? family rules?), Choose, Check (how did it go?). Prepare 3 scenario cards and a 2-week tracking chart template.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a goal like 'eat better' is enough. SMART goals are Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound so you can tell if you met them.
- Skipping the 'Check' step. Reflecting after a decision helps you make better choices next time.

### Day 1 (Monday): Decision steps
*Student:* Learn a decision model (Stop, Think of options, Consider consequences, Choose, Check) and apply it to a snack choice.

*How to teach:* Teach the 5 steps with hand motions. Apply them together to choosing a snack (chips, apple with peanut butter, cookies, yogurt): list options, consider consequences, choose and explain.

- **Q:** What are the 5 decision-making steps?
  **A:** Stop, Think of options, Consider consequences, Choose, Check.
- **Q:** What questions help you consider consequences?
  **A:** Is it healthy? Safe? Legal? Respectful? Does it follow family rules? How will I feel later?

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Scenario practice
*Student:* Work through 3 scenarios (skipping breakfast, choosing an after-school activity, drink at a party) with the model.

*How to teach:* Work through 3 scenarios with the model: skipping breakfast because you're late, choosing between two after-school activities, and someone at a party offers you a drink you don't recognize.

- **Q:** At a party someone offers you an unknown drink. What is a healthy decision?
  **A:** Say no thanks, choose a drink you know is safe (like water or one from a trusted adult), and tell a trusted adult if it seems unsafe.
- **Q:** What's a better option than skipping breakfast when you're late?
  **A:** Grab a quick healthy breakfast like a banana and a cheese stick, or yogurt, or whole-grain toast to eat on the way.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): SMART goals
*Student:* Rewrite 4 vague goals ('eat better') as SMART goals.

*How to teach:* Teach SMART. The child rewrites 4 vague goals: 'eat better,' 'be more active,' 'drink more water,' 'sleep more.'

- **Q:** Rewrite 'drink more water' as a SMART goal.
  **A:** E.g., 'I will drink 5 cups of water every day for the next 2 weeks and track it on my chart.'
- **Q:** What does the T in SMART stand for?
  **A:** Time-bound — the goal has a deadline or time frame.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Your goal
*Student:* Write a personal 2-week SMART goal for eating or activity and make a tracking chart.

*How to teach:* The child writes a personal 2-week SMART goal for eating or activity and makes a 14-day tracking chart.

- **Q:** How will you know if you met your goal?
  **A:** The chart shows checkmarks or numbers each day that can be compared to the goal.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain your decision-making steps to a parent using a real choice from today.

*How to teach:* The child explains the decision steps to a parent using a real choice they made today.

- **Q:** Walk me through a real choice you made using the steps.
  **A:** The child names the situation, at least 2 options, consequences, the choice and how it turned out.

**Assessment:** SMART goal and tracking chart.

**Answer key:** SMART goal and tracking chart rubric: 4 = goal is Specific (what), Measurable (number), Achievable, Relevant to eating/activity, Time-bound (2 weeks), e.g., 'I will eat 1 vegetable at lunch and dinner every day for 2 weeks'; chart has 14 days and a way to record; 3 = missing one SMART part; 2 = vague goal or no chart; 1 = no goal.
