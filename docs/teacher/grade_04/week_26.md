# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 26

## ELA 4: Oz chapters 7-12

**Prepare:** Novel chapters 7-12 (7 The Journey to the Great Oz, 8 The Deadly Poppy Field, 9 The Queen of the Field Mice, 10 The Guardian of the Gate, 11 The Wonderful City of Oz, 12 The Search for the Wicked Witch). Write 12 idiom cards (e.g., 'cold feet,' 'piece of cake,' 'break the ice,' 'hold your horses,' 'under the weather,' 'let the cat out of the bag').

**Watch for:**
- Taking idioms literally. An idiom's meaning is different from its words; use context.
- Missing the turning point. A turning point changes the direction of the story — here, the Witch's death.

### Day 1 (Monday): The journey
*Student:* Read chapters 7-9 and track obstacles and how friends solve them.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 7-9. The child charts obstacles and how the friends solve them.

- **Q:** How do the friends escape the Kalidahs?
  **A:** The Tin Woodman chops the tree bridge over the ditch as the Kalidahs cross, and they fall.
- **Q:** How is the Lion rescued from the poppy field?
  **A:** The Queen of the Field Mice (whom the Tin Woodman saved from a wildcat) has thousands of mice pull the Lion out on a truck the Tin Woodman builds.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Idioms
*Student:* Learn 12 idioms and write a short dialogue for the characters using four.

*How to teach:* Teach the 12 idioms. The child writes a short dialogue for the characters using four.

- **Q:** What does 'got cold feet' mean?
  **A:** Became too nervous to do something.
- **Q:** What does 'piece of cake' mean?
  **A:** Something very easy.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): The Emerald City
*Student:* Read chapters 10-11 and discuss the Wizard's demands.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 10-11. Discuss what the Wizard demands.

- **Q:** Why does everyone in the Emerald City wear green spectacles?
  **A:** The Guardian of the Gates says it is to protect their eyes from the city's brightness (we later learn it makes everything look green).
- **Q:** What does Oz demand before he grants their wishes?
  **A:** That they kill the Wicked Witch of the West.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Wicked Witch
*Student:* Read chapter 12 and describe the turning point.

*How to teach:* Read chapter 12 and describe the turning point.

- **Q:** How is the Wicked Witch of the West destroyed?
  **A:** Dorothy, angry that the Witch stole one of her silver shoes, throws a bucket of water on her and she melts away.
- **Q:** Why is this a turning point?
  **A:** The friends are freed, the Winkies are free, and the group can return to claim their rewards.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz explaining 6 idioms and answering text-dependent questions on chapters 7-12.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz: explain 6 idioms and answer text-dependent questions.

- **Q:** What does 'break the ice' mean?
  **A:** To do or say something to ease tension when meeting people.

**Assessment:** Idioms and reading quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: Idioms — cold feet = nervous; piece of cake = easy; break the ice = ease first awkwardness; hold your horses = wait/be patient; under the weather = sick; let the cat out of the bag = reveal a secret. Text questions — 1) Kalidahs escaped by chopping the tree bridge 2) Field mice rescue the Lion from the poppies 3) Green spectacles in the Emerald City 4) Oz demands the Witch of the West be killed 5) Water melts the Witch 6) Dorothy throws it because the Witch stole her silver shoe. Proficient: 10/12.

## Math 4: Tenths and hundredths

**Prepare:** 10 dimes and 30 pennies (real or play), printed hundred grids (10+), a 0-1 number line strip marked in tenths.

**Watch for:**
- Writing 3/100 as 0.3. Tenths are the first place after the decimal point and hundredths the second: 3/100 = 0.03.
- Thinking 0.30 is more than 0.3 because it has more digits. 3 dimes = 30 pennies: 0.3 = 0.30.

### Day 1 (Monday): Money model
*Student:* Use dimes and pennies to show 3/10 and 30/100 of a dollar and write as decimals.

*How to teach:* Show 3 dimes as 3/10 of a dollar and trade them for 30 pennies: 30/100. Write both as $0.30 and as decimals 0.3 and 0.30.

- **Q:** Write 3 dimes as a fraction of a dollar and as a decimal.
  **A:** 3/10 = 0.3 (also 30/100 = 0.30).
- **Q:** 7 pennies is what fraction of a dollar? As a decimal?
  **A:** 7/100 = 0.07.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Hundred grids
*Student:* Shade 10 hundred grids for given decimals and write each as a fraction and decimal.

*How to teach:* Shade 45 squares on a hundred grid and write 45/100 = 0.45. The child shades 10 grids for given decimals and writes both forms.

- **Q:** Shade 0.6 on a hundred grid. How many squares?
  **A:** 60 squares (6 columns) — 6/10 = 60/100.
- **Q:** Write 0.08 as a fraction.
  **A:** 8/100.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tenths to hundredths
*Student:* Rename tenths as hundredths and add (3/10 + 4/100 = 34/100) in 10 problems.

*How to teach:* Model 3/10 = 30/100, so 3/10 + 4/100 = 34/100. The child solves 10 problems.

- **Q:** 3/10 + 4/100 = ?
  **A:** 34/100 (= 0.34).
- **Q:** 6/10 + 25/100 = ?
  **A:** 85/100 (= 0.85).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Decimals on a number line
*Student:* Place 0.3, 0.45, 0.7 and 0.09 on a number line from 0 to 1.

*How to teach:* The child places 0.3, 0.45, 0.7 and 0.09 on a 0-1 line marked in tenths, estimating hundredths between tenths.

- **Q:** Where does 0.45 go?
  **A:** Halfway between 0.4 and 0.5.
- **Q:** Put 0.3, 0.45, 0.7, 0.09 in order.
  **A:** 0.09, 0.3, 0.45, 0.7.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz writing 10 fractions with denominators 10 and 100 as decimals and vice versa.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz independently. Correct together.

- **Q:** Write 52/100 as a decimal and 0.9 as a fraction.
  **A:** 0.52; 9/10.

**Assessment:** Decimal notation quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) 7/10 = 0.7 2) 35/100 = 0.35 3) 4/100 = 0.04 4) 90/100 = 0.90 (= 0.9) 5) 0.6 = 6/10 6) 0.25 = 25/100 7) 0.03 = 3/100 8) 0.8 = 8/10 (= 80/100) 9) 2/10 + 7/100 = 27/100 = 0.27 10) 1 5/10 = 1.5. Proficient: 8/10.

## Science 4: Reducing hazard impacts

**Prepare:** Toothpicks, mini marshmallows, a 9×13 pan of firm gelatin (make the day before), rubber bands or marbles and cardboard for base isolation, timer. Family earthquake kit list (Ready.gov / earthquakecountry.org). Great ShakeOut is held each October.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking taller is always weaker. Design (bracing, base isolation) matters more than height alone.
- Thinking you should run outside during an earthquake. Drop, Cover and Hold On where you are.

### Day 1 (Monday): Shake table
*Student:* Build toothpick-and-marshmallow buildings and test on a gelatin shake table.

*How to teach:* Build a 2-story toothpick building; shake the gelatin pan the same way each time.

- **Q:** Why use the same shake each time?
  **A:** So the test is fair; only the building design changes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Improve
*Student:* Add cross-bracing to the toothpick buildings and retest on the gelatin shake table, counting seconds until collapse.

*How to teach:* Add diagonal cross-braces; retest and count seconds until collapse.

- **Q:** Why do triangles (cross-bracing) help?
  **A:** Triangles are rigid shapes that keep the frame from swaying and collapsing.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compare solutions
*Student:* Build a base-isolation version on rubber bands or marbles and compare its shaking with the braced design.

*How to teach:* Build a base-isolation version (building on a cardboard base sitting on marbles); compare shaking.

- **Q:** How does base isolation protect a building?
  **A:** The base moves with the ground while the building on top shakes less, because the rollers/pads separate it from ground motion.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Earthquake kit
*Student:* Plan a family earthquake kit and practice 'Drop, Cover and Hold On' as in California's Great ShakeOut drill.

*How to teach:* Plan a family earthquake kit and practice Drop, Cover and Hold On.

- **Q:** What are the three steps?
  **A:** Drop to hands and knees, Cover your head and neck (under a sturdy table), Hold On until shaking stops.
- **Q:** Name four items for an earthquake kit.
  **A:** Water (1 gallon per person per day), food, flashlight, batteries, first aid kit, whistle, medicines, copies of documents.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a comparison of bracing and base isolation using test data, cost and materials.

*How to teach:* The child writes a comparison of bracing and base isolation using data, cost and materials.

- **Q:** Which solution is cheaper for a small house?
  **A:** Cross-bracing is usually cheaper and simpler; base isolation costs more and is used for large important buildings (hospitals, city halls).

**Assessment:** Shake table results and comparison.

**Answer key:** Comparison rubric: 4 = data from both tests (seconds standing, amount of sway), comparison of cost/materials, recommendation with reasons; 3 = data for both, weak recommendation; 2 = one design tested; 1 = incomplete. Kit plan: includes water, food, flashlight, first aid, and meeting place.

## California, A Changing State: New government compared

**Prepare:** Calisphere set 'Civic Buildings in California History,' an image of the Great Seal of California. Capitals: San Jose (1849-51), Vallejo (1852-53), Benicia (1853-54), Sacramento (1854-present). Unit 8 test for Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the new government gave everyone a voice. Only white men could vote; Native people could not testify against whites in court.
- Thinking the bear on the seal is the main symbol. The central figure is Minerva, Roman goddess of wisdom.

### Day 1 (Monday): Comparing governments
*Student:* Compare who made laws and who had rights under Spanish, Mexican and early American rule in a 3-column chart.

*How to teach:* Compare lawmaking and rights under Spanish, Mexican and American rule.

- **Q:** Who made laws under Spain?
  **A:** The king and his appointed officials (viceroy, governors); under Mexico, a national congress and appointed governors with local councils; under the U.S. state system, elected legislators.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Who was left out
*Student:* Discuss how statehood laws limited rights of Native Californians, Chinese immigrants and women.

*How to teach:* Discuss who was left out of rights.

- **Q:** Name one way early state laws limited Native Californians.
  **A:** They could not vote, could not testify against white people in court, and a 1850 law allowed them to be forced into labor.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Moving capital
*Student:* Trace the capital's moves (San Jose, Vallejo, Benicia, Sacramento) and examine images from the Calisphere set 'Civic Buildings in California History'.

*How to teach:* Trace the capital's moves.

- **Q:** List California's capitals in order.
  **A:** San Jose, Vallejo, Benicia, Sacramento.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Seal and symbols
*Student:* Analyze the Great Seal of California and explain each symbol.

*How to teach:* Analyze the Great Seal.

- **Q:** What do the symbols on the Great Seal mean?
  **A:** Minerva (wisdom; California became a state without being a territory, like Minerva born grown), grizzly bear (strength), wheat and grapes (agriculture), miner (Gold Rush), ships (trade), Sierra Nevada, 'Eureka' ('I have found it'), 31 stars (31st state).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 8 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 8 test on the 1849 convention, the Compromise of 1850 and how the new government differed.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test.

- **Q:** What does 'Eureka' mean?
  **A:** 'I have found it.'

**Assessment:** Unit 8 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 8 test key: 1) Need for government during the Gold Rush 2) Colton Hall, Monterey, 1849 3) Constitution: no slavery, English/Spanish 4) Compromise of 1850; statehood Sept 9, 1850 5) 31st state 6) Comparison of Spanish, Mexican and American government 7) Groups denied rights: Native Californians, Chinese, African Americans, women 8) Capital moves ending in Sacramento 9) Seal symbols. Proficient: 80%.

## Art & Music 4: Printmaking

**Prepare:** Gather foam meat trays (washed) or styrofoam sheets, a dull pencil, tempera paint, a brayer or small roller, paper, and newspaper. Find Japanese woodblock prints (Hokusai, Hiroshige) online.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting the print reverses. Letters must be written backward on the plate.
- Pressing too lightly. Lines must be deep enough that paint won't fill them.

### Day 1 (Monday): Printmaking history
*Student:* Look at Japanese woodblock prints and discuss repetition.

*How to teach:* Look at Japanese woodblock prints; discuss how printmaking allows many copies and repeated designs.

- **Q:** Why was printmaking useful in Japan?
  **A:** Artists could make many copies of the same image to sell, so more people could own art.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Design
*Student:* Brainstorm 3 designs and choose one; remember it will print in reverse.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm 3 designs; choose one, remembering reversal. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** If you carve the word 'CAT' normally, how will it print?
  **A:** Backward (mirror image).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Carve plate
*Student:* Incise the design into a foam plate with a dull pencil.

*How to teach:* Press the design into foam with a dull pencil. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** Why use a dull pencil?
  **A:** To press grooves without tearing through the foam.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Print
*Student:* Roll paint and pull an edition of 4 prints in different colors.

*How to teach:* Roll paint thinly; pull 4 prints in different colors. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** Why roll paint in a thin, even layer?
  **A:** Thick paint fills the grooves and blurs the image.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & share
*Student:* Sign and number the edition and choose the best print.

*How to teach:* Sign and number the edition (1/4, 2/4...); choose the best. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What does '3/4' on a print mean?
  **A:** The third print in an edition of four.

**Assessment:** Print edition.

**Answer key:** Print edition: 4 prints, clear lines, even ink, correctly numbered and signed. Proficient = 3 clean prints with correct numbering.

## PE 4: Create a dance

**Prepare:** Music of the child's choice (preview content), planning sheet with four 8-count sections, a family audience.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking dance needs special training. Planned movement with clear changes in level, pathway, tempo and force makes a strong dance.
- Repeating the same level and direction throughout. Include intentional contrasts.

### Day 1 (Monday): Plan
*Student:* Choose music and plan 4 eight-count sections using changes in level, pathway, tempo and force.

*How to teach:* Choose music and plan four 8-count sections, each with at least one change in level, pathway, tempo or force. Write it down.

- **Q:** How many counts are in four 8-count sections?
  **A:** 32 counts.
- **Q:** Give an example of a force change.
  **A:** Moving from strong, sharp punches to light, floating arms.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Teach
*Student:* Teach your dance to a family member one eight-count at a time.

*How to teach:* Teach the dance to a family member one 8-count at a time, demonstrating and counting.

- **Q:** Why teach one 8-count at a time?
  **A:** Small chunks are easier to learn and remember.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rehearse
*Student:* Rehearse the full dance with music and smooth out the transitions between sections.

*How to teach:* Rehearse with music and smooth the transitions.

- **Q:** How do you make a transition smooth?
  **A:** End one section in a position that leads naturally into the first move of the next.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Perform
*Student:* Perform the dance for an audience and ask them to identify one level and one pathway change.

*How to teach:* Perform; ask the audience to identify one level change and one pathway change.

- **Q:** What did your audience notice?
  **A:** Answers vary; should be specific changes, e.g., 'You went low in section 3 and traveled in a zigzag.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit assessment
*Student:* Self-assess the dance with a rubric and complete a dance steps check.

*How to teach:* Self-assess with the rubric; dance steps check: perform grapevine, do-si-do and a La Raspa bleking step on cue.

- **Q:** Perform a grapevine right. Describe it.
  **A:** Step right, left foot crosses behind, step right, touch left beside.

**Assessment:** Original dance (rubric).

**Answer key:** Original dance rubric: 4 = four 8-count sections (32 counts) with clear changes in level, pathway, tempo and force, on the beat, smooth transitions, taught one section to someone; 3 = most elements; 2 = few changes or off beat; 1 = incomplete. Dance steps check: grapevine, do-si-do and bleking each correct (3/3 proficient).

## Health 4: Secondhand smoke and social norms

**Prepare:** Find a simple chart of California youth tobacco data (California Department of Public Health 'California Student Tobacco Survey' or the CDC National Youth Tobacco Survey) showing most students do not use tobacco. Poster paper. Prepare the Unit 7 test (see key).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking secondhand smoke only matters if you're in the same room for a long time. There is no safe level of secondhand smoke; children's lungs are especially sensitive.
- Thinking 'everyone' smokes or vapes. Data show most young people do not use tobacco or vape.

### Day 1 (Monday): Secondhand smoke
*Student:* Learn how secondhand smoke and vape aerosol affect others, especially children.

*How to teach:* Explain secondhand smoke (smoke from the tip of a cigarette and smoke breathed out) and secondhand vape aerosol. In children it can trigger asthma attacks, cause ear infections, bronchitis and more colds.

- **Q:** What is secondhand smoke?
  **A:** Smoke breathed in by people nearby from someone else's cigarette (from the burning end and what the smoker exhales).
- **Q:** How can secondhand smoke affect children?
  **A:** It can cause or worsen asthma, ear infections, coughing, bronchitis and pneumonia.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Smoke-free laws
*Student:* Read about California's smoke-free laws for restaurants, parks and cars with kids.

*How to teach:* Read about California laws: it's illegal to sell tobacco or vapes to anyone under 21; smoking is banned in workplaces, restaurants and bars and in cars when anyone under 18 is present; many cities ban smoking in parks and beaches, and state parks and beaches are smoke-free.

- **Q:** In California, can someone smoke in a car with a child?
  **A:** No — it is illegal to smoke in a vehicle when a minor (under 18) is present.
- **Q:** What is the legal age to buy tobacco or vapes in California?
  **A:** 21.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Social norms data
*Student:* Read survey data showing most California students have never used tobacco and graph it.

*How to teach:* Read the survey data and make a bar graph showing that most students have never used tobacco or vapes. Discuss 'social norms' (what most people actually do).

- **Q:** What is a social norm?
  **A:** What most people in a group actually do or think is normal behavior.
- **Q:** Why is it helpful to know most kids don't vape?
  **A:** It shows that not using is normal, making it easier to say no.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Avoiding smoke
*Student:* Role-play 3 polite ways to avoid secondhand smoke or vape aerosol (move away, ask to open a window, tell a trusted adult).

*How to teach:* Role-play 3 polite ways to avoid secondhand smoke: move away, ask to open a window or for the person to smoke outside, tell a trusted adult.

- **Q:** Give a polite way to ask someone not to smoke near you.
  **A:** E.g., 'Excuse me, the smoke bothers my breathing. Could you please smoke farther away?' — or simply move away.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit assessment
*Student:* Take the Unit 7 test on effects of tobacco, vaping and secondhand smoke and on social norms.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test (see key).

- **Q:** Name the 3 harmful substances in cigarette smoke we studied.
  **A:** Nicotine, tar and carbon monoxide.

**Assessment:** Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 7 test key: 1) Addictive drug in tobacco and most vapes — nicotine. 2) Sticky substance that coats lungs — tar. 3) Gas that replaces oxygen in blood — carbon monoxide. 4) Two effects of tobacco on the body — e.g., lung cancer, heart disease, yellow teeth, bad breath, less stamina. 5) Is vape aerosol water vapor? — no; it can contain nicotine, metals and harmful chemicals. 6) Why is nicotine especially harmful to kids — the brain is still developing (attention, learning, mood) and gets addicted more easily. 7) What is secondhand smoke — smoke breathed in by people near a smoker. 8) Legal age to buy tobacco/vapes in California — 21. 9) True or false: most students vape — false. 10) A way to avoid secondhand smoke — move away, ask politely, tell a trusted adult. Proficient = 8 of 10.
