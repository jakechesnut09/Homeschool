# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 3

## ELA 4: Drafting narratives with dialogue and detail

**Prepare:** Just So Stories ('How the Leopard Got His Spots' — preview first: it contains a racial term and dated depictions; you may read a selected passage or discuss why such language is hurtful), highlighters, the narrative draft, the independent reading book and a timer.

**Watch for:**
- Placing end punctuation outside quotation marks ("Let's go". ). The comma, period, question mark or exclamation point goes inside the closing quotation marks.
- Starting dialogue for each new speaker on the same line. Start a new paragraph when the speaker changes.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mentor text study
*Student:* Reread 'How the Leopard Got His Spots' and highlight dialogue and description; note how Kipling shows feelings.

*How to teach:* Reread a selected section of 'How the Leopard Got His Spots' and highlight dialogue in one color and description in another. Discuss how Kipling shows feelings through what characters say.

- **Q:** Why did the Giraffe and Zebra become hard to see?
  **A:** They moved into the forest, where the shade and sunlight made them blotchy and stripy, so they blended into the shadows.
- **Q:** How does the Leopard get his spots?
  **A:** The Ethiopian presses his fingertips all over the Leopard's coat, leaving spots so he can hide in the forest.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Draft the middle
*Student:* Draft the narrative's middle events using at least two time-order transitions.

*How to teach:* Model time-order transitions (first, next, after that, suddenly, later, finally). The child drafts the middle events and underlines at least two transitions.

- **Q:** Give three time-order transition words.
  **A:** First, next, later (also: then, after that, suddenly, finally).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Quotation marks
*Student:* Practice punctuating dialogue in 10 sentences and add 3 lines of dialogue to the draft.

*How to teach:* Model the rules: quotation marks around spoken words, comma before the closing mark when the tag follows, capital letter to start the quote. The child punctuates 10 sentences and adds 3 lines of dialogue to the draft.

- **Q:** Punctuate: we're late said Mom
  **A:** "We're late," said Mom.
- **Q:** Punctuate: Sam asked where is my hat
  **A:** Sam asked, "Where is my hat?"

### Day 4 (Thursday): Show, don't tell
*Student:* Rewrite 4 'telling' sentences as 'showing' sentences with sensory details and revise one draft paragraph.

*How to teach:* Show 'I was scared' vs 'My hands shook and my heart thumped.' The child rewrites 4 telling sentences and revises one paragraph with sensory details.

- **Q:** Rewrite 'It was cold' as a showing sentence.
  **A:** Example: 'Wind bit my cheeks, and I could see my breath puffing like smoke.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draft the ending and do a timed fluency read of a passage from the independent book.

*How to teach:* The child drafts the ending (how the moment resolved and how they felt or what they learned). Then do a timed 1-minute read from the independent book and record WCPM.

- **Q:** What should a narrative ending do?
  **A:** Wrap up the moment and show the writer's feelings or what they learned, rather than just 'The end.'

**Assessment:** Complete narrative draft with dialogue.

**Answer key:** Complete draft rubric (4-point): 4 = clear beginning-middle-end focused on one moment, at least two time-order transitions, 3+ lines of correctly punctuated dialogue, sensory details, an ending that reflects; 3 = all parts present with minor punctuation errors; 2 = sequence unclear or dialogue missing; 1 = incomplete draft.

## Math 4: Comparing and ordering large numbers

**Prepare:** Digit cards (two sets 0-9), long paper strips for number lines. Write these mountain heights on cards: Whitney 14,505 ft; White Mountain Peak 14,252; Shasta 14,179; Lassen Peak 10,457; San Gorgonio 11,503; Mount Diablo 3,849; Mount Tamalpais 2,571; Half Dome 8,839.

**Watch for:**
- Comparing by the first digit you see without lining up places (thinking 9,876 > 12,001). First count digits: more digits means larger.
- Placing a number like 47,800 at '47.8' on the number line or near the middle: find the two thousands it falls between first, then decide which it is closer to.

### Day 1 (Monday): Compare by place
*Student:* Line up numbers by place value and compare 12 pairs, writing the symbol and the deciding place.

*How to teach:* Write two numbers stacked with places lined up and model comparing from the left until the digits differ. The child compares 12 pairs, writing the symbol and naming the deciding place.

- **Q:** Compare 45,302 and 45,230. Which place decides?
  **A:** 45,302 > 45,230; the hundreds place decides (3 hundreds > 2 hundreds).
- **Q:** Compare 9,876 and 12,001.
  **A:** 9,876 < 12,001 — a 4-digit number is less than a 5-digit number.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ordering
*Student:* Order the heights of eight California mountains (e.g., Whitney 14,505 ft) from least to greatest.

*How to teach:* Lay out the eight mountain cards; have the child order them least to greatest by lining up places on paper, then read the order aloud.

- **Q:** Order Whitney 14,505, Shasta 14,179 and White Mountain 14,252 from least to greatest.
  **A:** 14,179 (Shasta) < 14,252 (White Mountain) < 14,505 (Whitney).
- **Q:** Which is the shortest of the eight mountains?
  **A:** Mount Tamalpais, 2,571 ft.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Digit card war
*Student:* Play 'Place Value War' with digit cards: each player builds the largest 6-digit number and records the comparison.

*How to teach:* Each player draws 6 digit cards and builds the largest number possible, then both write the comparison with >, < or =. Ask the child to explain the strategy (largest digits in the biggest places).

- **Q:** Build the largest and smallest 6-digit numbers with 8, 2, 5, 9, 0, 4.
  **A:** Largest 985,420; smallest 204,589 (0 cannot be first).
- **Q:** Who wins: 853,210 or 853,201?
  **A:** 853,210 — they differ first in the tens place (1 ten > 0 tens).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Number line placement
*Student:* Place 5-digit numbers on an open number line between 40,000 and 50,000 and justify positions.

*How to teach:* Draw a line from 40,000 to 50,000 and mark 45,000 in the middle. The child places five numbers, marking the nearest thousands first, and explains each position aloud.

- **Q:** Where does 47,800 go?
  **A:** Between 47,000 and 48,000, closer to 48,000 — to the right of the 45,000 halfway mark.
- **Q:** Is 43,900 to the left or right of 45,000?
  **A:** Left, because 43,900 < 45,000.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz with 10 comparisons using >, =, < and 2 sets of numbers to order.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz without help. Correct together and have the child redo any wrong comparison by lining up places.

- **Q:** Order 30,450; 30,045; 34,005 from least to greatest.
  **A:** 30,045 < 30,450 < 34,005.

**Assessment:** Comparison and ordering quiz.

**Answer key:** Sample quiz key: 1) 6,354 < 6,543 2) 70,210 > 70,201 3) 89,999 < 90,000 4) 405,612 = 405,612 5) 123,456 < 123,465 6) 52,030 > 52,003 7) 9,999 < 10,000 8) 600,060 > 600,006 9) 34,500 < 35,400 10) 781,000 > 718,000. Ordering: A) 4,509; 4,590; 4,905; 5,049. B) 120,300; 123,000; 130,200; 132,000. 10 points each set of comparisons, 2 points per ordering; 80% proficient.

## Science 4: Collisions

**Prepare:** Marbles of two sizes, a ruler with a groove (or two rulers taped side by side), dominoes, two wooden blocks. Find a short car crash test video (e.g., IIHS crash test).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking energy disappears in a collision. It transfers to the other object and changes to sound and heat.
- Testing two variables at once. Change only one (e.g., speed) and keep the rest the same.

### Day 1 (Monday): Marble collisions
*Student:* Roll one marble into a stationary marble in a ruler groove and observe what happens to motion.

*How to teach:* Roll one marble into a still marble in the groove. Observe: the moving marble slows or stops and the other moves.

- **Q:** What happens to the energy when the moving marble hits the still one?
  **A:** Energy transfers to the still marble, so it starts moving while the first slows or stops.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Question generation
*Student:* Write 5 testable questions about collisions (mass, speed, number of marbles).

*How to teach:* The child writes 5 testable questions (what will change, what will be measured).

- **Q:** Make this testable: 'Do marbles like to crash?'
  **A:** Example: 'Does a heavier rolling marble make the target marble roll farther?'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Test a question
*Student:* Investigate one question with 3 trials and record results.

*How to teach:* The child picks one question, runs 3 trials and records results.

- **Q:** Why run 3 trials?
  **A:** To check that results are consistent and not a fluke.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sound and heat
*Student:* Clap hands and bang blocks to observe that collisions also make sound and heat.

*How to teach:* Clap hard and bang blocks together; feel palms warm and hear sound.

- **Q:** What other forms of energy appear when objects collide?
  **A:** Sound and heat (the hands get warm).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain in writing where the energy went in a car crash test video.

*How to teach:* Watch the crash test video; the child explains in writing where the car's energy went.

- **Q:** Where does a crashing car's motion energy go?
  **A:** Into crushing (bending) metal, sound, heat, and moving other objects (the dummies, the barrier).

**Assessment:** Collision investigation write-up.

**Answer key:** Write-up rubric: 4 = testable question, prediction, controlled variables named, data from 3 trials, conclusion that explains energy transfer (e.g., more speed → target moved farther); 3 = one part weak; 2 = data incomplete; 1 = no investigation. Crash explanation: energy transferred to deforming the car, sound and heat.

## California, A Changing State: Rivers, valleys and mountain passes

**Prepare:** California physical map, tracing paper; Library of Congress online 1850s California map (search loc.gov 'California 1850 map').

**Watch for:**
- Thinking cities grew randomly. Most grew near water, harbors, river junctions, passes or fertile valleys.
- Thinking the San Joaquin River flows south. It flows north to meet the Sacramento River in the Delta.

### Day 1 (Monday): Water features
*Student:* Trace the Sacramento and San Joaquin Rivers, the Delta and San Francisco Bay on a map.

*How to teach:* Trace the Sacramento and San Joaquin Rivers, the Delta and San Francisco Bay.

- **Q:** Where do the Sacramento and San Joaquin Rivers meet?
  **A:** In the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta, which flows into San Francisco Bay.
- **Q:** Which direction does the Sacramento River flow?
  **A:** South (the San Joaquin flows north); both meet in the Delta.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Passes
*Student:* Locate Donner Pass, Tehachapi Pass and Cajon Pass and explain why travelers needed them.

*How to teach:* Locate Donner, Tehachapi and Cajon Passes and explain why travelers needed them.

- **Q:** What is a mountain pass?
  **A:** A low gap through mountains that travelers can cross more easily.
- **Q:** Which pass crosses the Sierra Nevada near Lake Tahoe?
  **A:** Donner Pass.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Town growth
*Student:* Mark 8 major cities and explain why each grew where it did (harbor, river, pass, valley).

*How to teach:* Mark 8 cities and explain why each grew there.

- **Q:** Why did San Francisco grow?
  **A:** Its large natural harbor on San Francisco Bay made it a port for shipping and Gold Rush arrivals.
- **Q:** Why did San Diego grow?
  **A:** It has a protected natural harbor.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Primary source map
*Student:* Examine an 1850s map of California from the Library of Congress and compare place names to today.

*How to teach:* Compare place names on an 1850s map to today.

- **Q:** Why might place names be in Spanish?
  **A:** Spanish explorers, missionaries and Mexican Californios named many places before statehood (e.g., Los Angeles, San Francisco, Sacramento).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining why Sacramento grew where two rivers meet.

*How to teach:* The child writes why Sacramento grew where two rivers meet.

- **Q:** Which two rivers meet at Sacramento?
  **A:** The Sacramento and American Rivers.

**Assessment:** Map analysis paragraph.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric: 4 = explains that Sacramento grew where the American and Sacramento Rivers meet; rivers allowed boats to carry goods and people from San Francisco Bay; nearness to the gold fields (gold found on the American River) and Sutter's Fort; fertile valley; 3 = two reasons; 2 = one reason; 1 = inaccurate.

## Art & Music 4: Observational drawing: nature study

**Prepare:** Plan a nature walk. Cut a cardstock viewfinder (a 1 × 1.5 inch window). Find John James Audubon's bird watercolors (Birds of America) online.

**Watch for:**
- Drawing what you think an object looks like instead of what you see. Keep looking back at the real object.
- Drawing too small. Fill the page so details can show.

### Day 1 (Monday): Nature walk
*Student:* Collect 5 natural objects (leaves, pinecone, feather) on a walk.

*How to teach:* Take a 20-minute walk and collect 5 natural objects with interesting shapes and textures (leaf, pinecone, feather, shell, seed pod). Only collect things already on the ground and leave protected areas alone.

- **Q:** Why do artists collect objects to study?
  **A:** To observe real shapes, textures and details closely.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Viewfinder
*Student:* Make a cardstock viewfinder and sketch 4 small compositions of the objects.

*How to teach:* Your child cuts a viewfinder, arranges the objects, and looks through it to find 4 interesting views, sketching each as a small thumbnail box.

- **Q:** What does a viewfinder help you do?
  **A:** Choose and frame a composition by blocking out everything else.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Detailed study
*Student:* Draw one object large, filling the page, with contour and value.

*How to teach:* Your child picks the best thumbnail and draws one object large enough to touch at least three edges of the page, first in contour and then adding value and texture.

- **Q:** Why fill the page?
  **A:** It shows more detail and makes a stronger composition.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Artist look
*Student:* Look at John James Audubon bird drawings and compare your response before and after your own nature study.

*How to teach:* Look at two Audubon birds; your child writes a first reaction, then after the drawing week looks again and notes what they now appreciate (feather detail, pose, life-size scale).

- **Q:** What did Audubon do to make his birds so accurate?
  **A:** He observed real birds closely and drew them life-size with detailed feathers and natural poses.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & share
*Student:* Write 3 sentences on what you noticed by drawing that you didn't notice by looking.

*How to teach:* Your child writes 3 sentences about details they noticed only by drawing, then shares the drawing with family.

- **Q:** What's one detail you noticed only by drawing?
  **A:** Answer varies; e.g., veins on the leaf, layered scales on a pinecone.

**Assessment:** Nature study drawing and reflection.

**Answer key:** Nature study: 4 = object fills page, accurate contour, value, and fine details; 3 = most; 2 = small or few details; 1 = incomplete. Reflection: three specific observations.

## PE 4: Goal setting and circuit training

**Prepare:** Baseline results from week 2, 6 station cards, cones, jump rope, mat, a sturdy step, a timer that beeps every 40/20 seconds (interval app). Circuit log sheet.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the circuit is a race. Quality reps with good form matter more than speed.
- Setting goals in areas you already do well in only. Choose at least one area below the Healthy Fitness Zone or where you want to grow.

### Day 1 (Monday): SMART goals
*Student:* Write two SMART fitness goals based on pre-assessment results.

*How to teach:* The child writes two SMART goals from the baseline data (e.g., 'I will increase my PACER from 18 to 25 laps by the spring test by jogging 20 minutes 3 times a week').

- **Q:** Why must a goal be measurable?
  **A:** So you can tell exactly whether you reached it by comparing numbers.
- **Q:** Which area should you pick for a goal?
  **A:** An area that was below the Healthy Fitness Zone or that you most want to improve.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Circuit design
*Student:* Set up a 6-station circuit (jumping jacks, plank, wall sit, jump rope, step-ups, crab walk) for 40 seconds each.

*How to teach:* Set up 6 stations: jumping jacks, plank, wall sit, jump rope, step-ups, crab walk. Label each card with the fitness component it builds. Do a walk-through.

- **Q:** Which component does a wall sit build?
  **A:** Muscular endurance (legs).
- **Q:** Which component does jump rope build most?
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Circuit day
*Student:* Complete the circuit twice with 20-second rests and log repetitions.

*How to teach:* Complete the circuit twice: 40 seconds work, 20 seconds rest/rotate. Log reps or seconds held at each station.

- **Q:** How long is one full round of 6 stations with 40 s work and 20 s rest?
  **A:** 6 × 60 seconds = 6 minutes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Family challenge
*Student:* Teach a family member the circuit and complete it together.

*How to teach:* The child teaches a family member each station's form and safety cues, then they do the circuit together.

- **Q:** What form cue would you give for a plank?
  **A:** Straight line from head to heels, tight stomach, elbows under shoulders, don't let hips sag or pike up.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Repeat the circuit and compare repetitions to day 3.

*How to teach:* Repeat the circuit and compare reps to Day 3.

- **Q:** Day 3: 24 step-ups. Today: 29. How many more?
  **A:** 5 more.

**Assessment:** Goal sheet and circuit log.

**Answer key:** Goal sheet and circuit log rubric: 4 = two SMART goals tied to baseline data (specific test, number, plan, date) and a circuit log with reps for every station on Day 3 and Day 5 with improvements noted; 3 = goals missing one SMART part or log missing a station; 2 = vague goals or incomplete log; 1 = missing.

## Health 4: Trusted adults and reliable information

**Prepare:** Paper for a hand tracing, phone numbers for trusted adults. Find a health product ad (e.g., a supplement or 'miracle' snack ad) and a KidsHealth (kidshealth.org/kids) article on a related topic. Write the Poison Control number 1-800-222-1222 on a card.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking anything online that looks professional is reliable. Check who wrote it, why (to inform or to sell), when it was updated, and whether it uses evidence.
- Thinking you should handle health worries alone. Trusted adults and health helpers are there to help.

### Day 1 (Monday): Trusted adults
*Student:* Make a 'helping hand' tracing listing 5 trusted adults and how to reach them.

*How to teach:* Trace the child's hand. On each finger write a trusted adult (parent, grandparent, teacher, doctor, coach, neighbor) and how to reach them.

- **Q:** What makes an adult 'trusted'?
  **A:** They are caring and responsible, keep you safe, listen, and help you get what you need.
- **Q:** Who could you ask if a parent isn't available?
  **A:** Another trusted adult from your hand, e.g., a grandparent, teacher, school nurse or doctor.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Reliable vs unreliable
*Student:* Compare a health ad with a KidsHealth article and list clues that show which is reliable.

*How to teach:* Compare the ad and the KidsHealth article. List clues: author/organization, purpose (inform vs. sell), date, evidence, exaggerated claims ('cures everything').

- **Q:** Name 3 clues that a health source is reliable.
  **A:** Written by experts or a trusted health organization (.gov, .edu, hospitals), up to date, shows evidence, aims to inform rather than sell, no 'miracle' claims.
- **Q:** Why is an ad less reliable than a health article?
  **A:** The ad's goal is to sell, so it may exaggerate or leave out facts.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Asking for help
*Student:* Role-play asking a trusted adult for help with a health question.

*How to teach:* Role-play asking a trusted adult a health question: get their attention, say what's going on, ask clearly, and keep asking someone else if the first adult can't help.

- **Q:** What should you do if the first adult you ask doesn't help?
  **A:** Keep telling other trusted adults until someone helps.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Health helpers
*Student:* Identify community health helpers (school nurse, pharmacist, doctor, poison control 1-800-222-1222).

*How to teach:* Identify community health helpers and their roles: school nurse, pharmacist (explains medicines), doctor, dentist, Poison Control (1-800-222-1222), 911 for emergencies.

- **Q:** What is the Poison Control phone number?
  **A:** 1-800-222-1222.
- **Q:** When do you call 911 instead?
  **A:** In a life-threatening emergency, like someone not breathing, unconscious, a fire, or serious injury.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a quiz on reliable sources and trusted adults; check your goal tracker.

*How to teach:* Quiz (see key) and a quick check of the SMART goal tracker.

- **Q:** A website selling vitamins says they 'cure all illnesses.' Reliable?
  **A:** No — it is trying to sell and makes an exaggerated claim without evidence.

**Assessment:** Trusted adult tracing and quiz.

**Answer key:** Trusted adult tracing: 5 trusted adults with contact info. Quiz key: 1) Name a reliable source of health information — e.g., a doctor, school nurse, KidsHealth, CDC, MyPlate.gov. 2) What is the main purpose of an ad? — to sell a product. 3) Poison Control number — 1-800-222-1222. 4) Who explains how to take medicine safely at the pharmacy? — the pharmacist. 5) What do you do if a trusted adult can't help? — tell another trusted adult. Proficient = tracing complete and 4 of 5 correct.
