# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 25

## ELA 4: Oz chapters 1-6

**Prepare:** The Wonderful Wizard of Oz (L. Frank Baum, 1900, Gutenberg). Preview chapters 1-6: 1 The Cyclone, 2 The Council with the Munchkins, 3 How Dorothy Saved the Scarecrow, 4 The Road Through the Forest, 5 The Rescue of the Tin Woodman, 6 The Cowardly Lion. Note: in the book Dorothy's shoes are SILVER, not ruby.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up the book with the 1939 film. Answer only from the book this week; the film comes in week 28.
- Predictions without reasons. A good prediction says 'I think... because in the text...'

### Day 1 (Monday): Kansas vs Oz
*Student:* Read chapters 1-2 and contrast the settings with details.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 1-2. The child makes a two-column chart contrasting Kansas and Oz with details.

- **Q:** How does Baum describe Kansas?
  **A:** Gray everywhere — the prairie, the house, even Aunt Em and Uncle Henry have turned gray; it is flat and dry.
- **Q:** How is the Munchkin country different?
  **A:** Beautiful and colorful: green grass, stately trees with fruit, bright flowers, birds and a sparkling brook.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Scarecrow
*Student:* Read chapters 3-4 and describe the Scarecrow's traits.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 3-4. The child lists the Scarecrow's traits with evidence.

- **Q:** What does the Scarecrow want, and why?
  **A:** Brains, because he thinks he is foolish since his head is stuffed with straw.
- **Q:** Give evidence that the Scarecrow is already clever or kind.
  **A:** Example: he is cheerful and helpful, and later in the story he thinks of plans that save the group (accept accurate details from chapters 3-4).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tin Woodman
*Student:* Read chapters 5-6 and describe the Tin Woodman and Cowardly Lion.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 5-6. The child describes the Tin Woodman and the Cowardly Lion.

- **Q:** Why does the Tin Woodman want a heart?
  **A:** He was once a man (a Munchkin woodman) who loved a Munchkin girl; the Wicked Witch of the East enchanted his axe and he was replaced piece by piece with tin, losing his heart and the ability to love.
- **Q:** How does the Cowardly Lion show he is not really a coward?
  **A:** He roars to frighten enemies, and later in the journey he protects the group despite being afraid.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Prediction
*Student:* Write predictions about what the Wizard will grant each character.

*How to teach:* The child writes predictions: what will the Wizard grant each character, with a reason from the text.

- **Q:** What do Dorothy and her friends hope to get from the Wizard?
  **A:** Dorothy: a way home to Kansas; Scarecrow: brains; Tin Woodman: a heart; Lion: courage.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Answer comprehension questions on chapters 1-6.

*How to teach:* The child answers comprehension questions on chapters 1-6.

- **Q:** How does Dorothy's house kill the Wicked Witch of the East?
  **A:** The cyclone carries the house to Oz and it lands on the Witch.

**Assessment:** Comprehension questions.

**Answer key:** Key: 1) Kansas is gray, flat and dry. 2) The cyclone carries Dorothy and Toto to Oz. 3) The house lands on and kills the Wicked Witch of the East; Dorothy takes her silver shoes. 4) The Good Witch of the North kisses Dorothy's forehead for protection and sends her to the Emerald City. 5) Scarecrow wants brains; Tin Woodman a heart; Lion courage. 6) Tin Woodman was rusted and Dorothy oiled him. Proficient: 5/6 with evidence.

## Math 4: Line plots with fractional data

**Prepare:** A ruler marked in eighths of an inch, 15 pencils or leaves, grid paper for the line plot. Unit 6 test for Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Starting measurement at the end of the ruler instead of the 0 mark. Line the object up with 0.
- Uneven or unlabeled scales on the line plot. Mark every 1/8 between the smallest and largest values at equal spaces, even if no data lands there.

### Day 1 (Monday): Measure to 1/8 inch
*Student:* Measure 15 pencils or leaves to the nearest 1/8 inch and record the data.

*How to teach:* Model measuring one pencil: line up at 0, find the last whole inch, then count eighths past it. The child measures and records all 15 to the nearest 1/8 inch.

- **Q:** A pencil ends 3 marks past 5 on a ruler with eighths. How long is it?
  **A:** 5 3/8 inches.
- **Q:** How many eighths are in 1/2 inch?
  **A:** 4 eighths (4/8).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Build the line plot
*Student:* Make a line plot of the measurements with a labeled fraction scale.

*How to teach:* Draw a number line from the shortest to the longest measurement in eighths, label every tick, and place an X for each item. Check that equal spaces represent equal amounts.

- **Q:** If the data run from 4 2/8 to 6 1/8 inches, what are the first and last labels on the plot?
  **A:** 4 2/8 (or 4 1/4) and 6 1/8, with every eighth between them labeled.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Answer questions
*Student:* Use the plot to find the difference between longest and shortest and the total of the 1/2-inch items.

*How to teach:* Ask questions from the plot; the child shows each computation with fractions.

- **Q:** The longest pencil is 6 7/8 in and the shortest 5 3/8 in. What is the difference?
  **A:** 1 4/8 inches (= 1 1/2 in).
- **Q:** Three leaves each measure 4 1/2 inches. What is their total length?
  **A:** 13 1/2 inches (3 × 4 1/2 = 3 × 9/2 = 27/2).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit 6 review
*Student:* Mixed review of fraction addition, subtraction, multiplication and line plots.

*How to teach:* Mixed review of fraction addition, subtraction, multiplication and line plots; self-check with a key.

- **Q:** 2 3/8 + 1 7/8 = ?
  **A:** 3 10/8 = 4 2/8 (= 4 1/4).
- **Q:** 6 × 2/3 = ?
  **A:** 12/3 = 4.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 6 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 6 test on fraction addition, subtraction, multiplication and line plots.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 6 test independently and collect the line plot as the product.

- **Q:** 5 - 1 3/4 = ?
  **A:** 3 1/4.

**Assessment:** Unit 6 test plus line plot product.

**Answer key:** Unit 6 test key: 1) 3/8 + 4/8 = 7/8 2) 4 2/5 + 1 4/5 = 6 1/5 3) 1 - 5/12 = 7/12 4) 5 1/3 - 2 2/3 = 2 2/3 5) 4 × 2/5 = 8/5 = 1 3/5 6) 3 × 3/4 cup = 2 1/4 cups 7) 3 6/8 - 2 3/8 = 1 3/8 (line plot range) 8) 5 - 1 3/4 = 3 1/4. Line plot rubric: 4 = title, fraction scale in equal eighths labeled, all 15 data points correct, two questions answered correctly; 3 = one labeling or plotting error; 2 = scale uneven or several data errors; 1 = incomplete.

## Science 4: Earthquakes and volcanoes patterns

**Prepare:** World map, red and orange dot stickers, USGS 'Latest Earthquakes' map (earthquake.usgs.gov), a list of active volcanoes (USGS/Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking earthquakes happen randomly everywhere. Most occur in belts along plate boundaries.
- Thinking California has only earthquakes, not volcanoes. Mount Shasta and Lassen Peak are active Cascade volcanoes (Lassen last erupted 1914-1917).

### Day 1 (Monday): USGS map
*Student:* Plot 20 recent magnitude 4+ earthquakes from the USGS map on a world map with red dots.

*How to teach:* Filter the USGS map to M4.5+ for the past week; plot 20 quakes.

- **Q:** Where did most quakes cluster?
  **A:** Around the edges of the Pacific Ocean (and other plate boundaries), not randomly.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Volcanoes
*Student:* Plot 15 active volcanoes (including Mount Shasta and Lassen Peak) with orange triangles on the same map.

*How to teach:* Plot 15 active volcanoes including Mount Shasta and Lassen Peak.

- **Q:** When did Lassen Peak last erupt?
  **A:** 1914-1917.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pattern
*Student:* Describe the Ring of Fire pattern formed by the dots and triangles around the Pacific Ocean.

*How to teach:* Describe the Ring of Fire pattern.

- **Q:** What is the Ring of Fire?
  **A:** A horseshoe-shaped belt of earthquakes and volcanoes around the edges of the Pacific Ocean.

### Day 4 (Thursday): San Andreas
*Student:* Locate the San Andreas Fault and explain why California has earthquakes.

*How to teach:* Locate the San Andreas Fault (about 750-800 miles long) and explain why California has earthquakes.

- **Q:** Why does California have many earthquakes?
  **A:** The Pacific Plate and North American Plate slide past each other along the San Andreas Fault; when stuck rock suddenly slips, an earthquake occurs.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining the patterns of earthquakes, volcanoes and mountains on your map.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph explaining the patterns on the map.

- **Q:** What is the main pattern?
  **A:** Earthquakes, volcanoes and many mountain ranges occur together in belts, especially around the Pacific (Ring of Fire), along plate boundaries.

**Assessment:** Map analysis writing.

**Answer key:** Map analysis rubric: 4 = map with 20 quakes and 15 volcanoes plotted accurately; paragraph identifies the Ring of Fire, notes that quakes, volcanoes and mountains occur together along boundaries, and explains California's San Andreas Fault; 3 = pattern described without explanation; 2 = map only; 1 = incomplete.

## California, A Changing State: The road to statehood

**Prepare:** Simplified excerpts of the 1849 California Constitution (Online Archive of California / California State Archives), timeline strip. Facts: delegates met at Colton Hall, Monterey, in September-October 1849; 48 delegates including 8 Californios such as Mariano Vallejo; the constitution was published in English and Spanish, banned slavery, set the capital at San Jose, and protected married women's separate property; voting was limited to white men. California became the 31st state on September 9, 1850, as part of the Compromise of 1850.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking California was a territory first. It skipped the territory stage and became a state directly.
- Thinking 'free state' meant everyone had equal rights. Native Californians, Chinese immigrants, African Americans and women were denied many rights.

### Day 1 (Monday): Need for government
*Student:* Discuss why Gold Rush California needed a government (crime, land claims, no courts).

*How to teach:* Discuss why Gold Rush California needed a government.

- **Q:** Why did California need a government quickly?
  **A:** Crime, disputes over mining claims and land, and no courts or laws to settle them as the population exploded.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Colton Hall
*Student:* Read about the 1849 constitutional convention at Colton Hall in Monterey, including Californio delegates like Vallejo.

*How to teach:* Read about the convention at Colton Hall.

- **Q:** Where and when was California's first constitution written?
  **A:** Colton Hall in Monterey, in the fall of 1849.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): The constitution
*Student:* Read simplified excerpts of the 1849 California Constitution (bilingual publication, ban on slavery) and list 4 key decisions.

*How to teach:* Read constitution excerpts; list 4 key decisions.

- **Q:** Name four decisions in the 1849 constitution.
  **A:** Banned slavery; printed laws in English and Spanish; set boundaries; married women could own separate property; (also capital in San Jose; voting limited to white men).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compromise of 1850
*Student:* Read about how California's admission as a free state was part of the Compromise of 1850 on September 9, 1850.

*How to teach:* Read about the Compromise of 1850.

- **Q:** Why was California's admission controversial?
  **A:** Adding a free state would upset the balance between free and slave states in the Senate; the Compromise of 1850 settled this with deals for both sides.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Build an illustrated statehood timeline from the Bear Flag Revolt (1846) to admission on September 9, 1850.

*How to teach:* Build the statehood timeline.

- **Q:** When did California become a state and which number?
  **A:** September 9, 1850; the 31st state.

**Assessment:** Statehood timeline.

**Answer key:** Timeline key: June 1846 Bear Flag Revolt; July 1846 U.S. flag raised at Monterey; Dec 1846 Battle of San Pasqual; Jan 1848 gold discovered; Feb 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo; 1849 Gold Rush and constitutional convention at Colton Hall; Sept 9, 1850 statehood (31st state, free state, Compromise of 1850). Proficient: 6 events in correct order with illustrations.

## Art & Music 4: Clay sculpture: form and texture

**Prepare:** Gather air-dry clay, a plastic knife, toothpicks, a water cup, a placemat, and found objects for texture (buttons, leaves, a fork, lace). Make slip by mixing a little clay with water to a yogurt-like paste. Wash hands after clay work.

**Watch for:**
- Joining pieces by pressing only. Without score and slip, parts fall off as the clay dries.
- Adding too much water. Soggy clay slumps and cracks; use just enough to keep it workable.

### Day 1 (Monday): Clay basics
*Student:* Learn safe clay handling and make a pinch pot.

*How to teach:* Show safe handling (work on a mat, keep clay away from face, wash hands). Your child makes a pinch pot: thumb in the center, pinch while turning.

- **Q:** How do you make a pinch pot?
  **A:** Roll a ball, press your thumb into the center, then pinch the walls evenly while turning the ball.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Coils
*Student:* Roll coils and add them to build a taller vessel or animal.

*How to teach:* Roll even coils and stack them onto the pinch pot, scoring and slipping each layer, to build a taller vessel or animal body.

- **Q:** What does 'score and slip' mean?
  **A:** Scratch both surfaces and add slip (clay paste) before pressing them together.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Texture
*Student:* Press found objects into clay to create textures.

*How to teach:* Press found objects into a clay slab to make a texture sampler. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What is texture?
  **A:** How a surface feels or looks like it would feel.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sculpt
*Student:* Sculpt a California animal (sea otter, quail, bear) using score-and-slip joins.

*How to teach:* Sculpt a California animal (sea otter, quail, bear), joining legs, ears or tails with score and slip.

- **Q:** Why should parts be joined with score and slip?
  **A:** So they stick firmly and don't crack off when dry.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & share
*Student:* Let dry and describe your process and safety steps.

*How to teach:* Let dry slowly (cover loosely); your child describes process and safety steps. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** Name two clay safety steps.
  **A:** Work on a mat, keep clay away from your mouth/face, wash hands, clean tools.

**Assessment:** Clay sculpture.

**Answer key:** Clay sculpture: 4 = recognizable California animal, secure score-and-slip joins, intentional texture, even walls; 3 = minor weak joins; 2 = parts fall off or little texture; 1 = incomplete. Process description includes at least two methods and two safety steps.

## PE 4: American square and line dance

**Prepare:** Square dance music with a caller (or you call), Virginia Reel music (fiddle tune), an open floor, and as many family members as possible (4 couples is a full square; adapt with fewer). Cha-Cha Slide recording (preview).

**Watch for:**
- Turning around to face backward in a do-si-do. Partners pass right shoulders, go back to back and return walking backward, always facing the same direction.
- Confusing allemande left with swing. Allemande left: face your corner, take left hands (or forearms), walk around each other and return to your partner.

### Day 1 (Monday): Square dance calls
*Student:* Learn do-si-do, swing your partner, promenade and allemande left.

*How to teach:* Teach calls: do-si-do (pass right shoulders, back to back, return), swing your partner (hook elbows or hold hands and turn), promenade (walk with partner counterclockwise around the square, hands crossed), allemande left (left hand to corner, walk around, return home).

- **Q:** Which direction do you promenade?
  **A:** Counterclockwise around the square (with your partner).
- **Q:** What is your 'corner'?
  **A:** The person beside you who is not your partner.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Virginia reel
*Student:* Dance the Virginia reel with family members, including the reel and the arch.

*How to teach:* Dance the Virginia Reel: lines facing, forward and back, right-hand turn, left-hand turn, two-hand turn, do-si-do, head couple sashays down and back, reel (alternate turning partner and the others), then head couple forms an arch and others pass under.

- **Q:** What does the head couple do during the arch?
  **A:** They join hands and raise them to make an arch, and the other couples pass under it.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Grapevine
*Student:* Learn the grapevine step and a 4-wall line dance (e.g., Cha Cha Slide).

*How to teach:* Teach the grapevine (step side, cross behind, step side, touch) and a 4-wall line dance like the Cha-Cha Slide.

- **Q:** What is a 4-wall line dance?
  **A:** A dance that repeats a pattern, turning a quarter each time so you face all four walls.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Heart rate
*Student:* Measure heart rate after dancing and compare to jogging.

*How to teach:* Dance 3 songs, then take heart rate (15 s × 4). Compare to a jogging heart rate.

- **Q:** Dance HR 132 bpm vs. jogging HR 148 bpm. Which is more intense?
  **A:** Jogging (16 bpm higher), though dancing is still moderate-to-vigorous activity.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform a sequence of called square dance moves without stopping.

*How to teach:* Perform a sequence of called square dance moves without stopping.

- **Q:** In what order would you dance: allemande left, promenade, do-si-do, swing?
  **A:** Any order called; the child must respond correctly to each call without stopping.

**Assessment:** Called sequence check.

**Answer key:** Called sequence check: call 5 moves in random order (e.g., do-si-do, swing, allemande left, promenade, do-si-do). 1 point each for correct, on-time response. 4 = 5; 3 = 4; 2 = 2–3; 1 = 0–1.

## Health 4: Vaping and e-cigarettes

**Prepare:** Parent preview: vaping unit. Print the FDA's 'The Real Cost' or CDC fact sheets on e-cigarettes for kids/teens. Prepare 10 myth/fact cards (see Day 2). Have 2–3 sample e-cigarette ad images from health-education sites (e.g., Stanford Tobacco Prevention Toolkit) rather than live marketing.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking vape aerosol is 'just water vapor.' It is an aerosol that can contain nicotine, ultrafine particles, flavoring chemicals, heavy metals like nickel, tin and lead, and cancer-causing chemicals.
- Thinking vapes without nicotine are always nicotine-free. Labels are sometimes wrong, and some products labeled nicotine-free have been found to contain nicotine.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is vaping?
*Student:* Read an FDA fact sheet on e-cigarettes and list what is in the aerosol.

*How to teach:* Read the fact sheet. List what can be in e-cigarette aerosol: nicotine, flavorings (some linked to lung disease), ultrafine particles, heavy metals (nickel, tin, lead), volatile organic compounds, cancer-causing chemicals.

- **Q:** What is an e-cigarette?
  **A:** A battery-powered device that heats a liquid (usually containing nicotine and flavorings) into an aerosol that is inhaled.
- **Q:** Name 3 things that can be in vape aerosol.
  **A:** Any 3: nicotine, ultrafine particles, flavoring chemicals, heavy metals (nickel, tin, lead), cancer-causing chemicals.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Myth or fact
*Student:* Sort 10 statements about vaping into myth or fact.

*How to teach:* Sort 10 statements. Myths: 'It's just water vapor'; 'Vaping isn't addictive'; 'Most kids vape'; 'Flavors are harmless'; 'Vaping can't hurt your lungs.' Facts: 'Most e-cigarettes contain nicotine'; 'Nicotine is addictive'; 'Nicotine can harm the developing brain'; 'Aerosol can contain heavy metals'; 'It is illegal to sell vapes to anyone under 21.'

- **Q:** Myth or fact: 'Vaping is just harmless water vapor.'
  **A:** Myth — it is an aerosol with nicotine and other harmful chemicals.
- **Q:** Myth or fact: 'Most kids vape.'
  **A:** Myth — most young people do not vape.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Flavors and marketing
*Student:* Analyze how flavors and colorful packaging target young people.

*How to teach:* Look at sample ad images. Note bright colors, candy and fruit flavors, cartoon-like designs and devices shaped like USB drives or highlighters — features that appeal to young people.

- **Q:** Why do companies use candy and fruit flavors?
  **A:** Flavors appeal to young people and make vaping seem harmless, which can get them started and addicted.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Brain effects
*Student:* Learn how nicotine affects the developing brain.

*How to teach:* Explain the brain keeps developing until about age 25. Nicotine can harm the parts that control attention, learning, mood and impulse control, and makes young people more easily addicted.

- **Q:** How can nicotine affect a young person's brain?
  **A:** It can harm attention, learning, memory, mood and impulse control, and causes addiction more easily.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a public service message correcting one vaping myth.

*How to teach:* Write a public service message correcting one myth, with a fact and a source.

- **Q:** What makes a strong PSA?
  **A:** A clear, short message, an accurate fact from a reliable source (FDA, CDC), and a call to action.

**Assessment:** PSA message.

**Answer key:** PSA message rubric: 4 = names a specific vaping myth, corrects it with an accurate fact (e.g., 'Vape aerosol is not water vapor — it can contain nicotine, heavy metals and cancer-causing chemicals'), cites a reliable source (FDA, CDC, NIDA), and ends with a positive call to action; 3 = accurate correction but no source; 2 = vague message; 1 = inaccurate or incomplete.
