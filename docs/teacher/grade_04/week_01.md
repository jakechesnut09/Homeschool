# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 1

## ELA 4: Story elements in Just So Stories

**Prepare:** Download Just So Stories (Kipling) from Project Gutenberg and preview 'How the Whale Got His Throat' and 'How the Camel Got His Hump' (read aloud — Kipling's playful language and 'O Best Beloved' asides are meant to be heard). Choose a 200-250 word grade 4 passage for the fluency read, a timer, a reader's notebook and a library book. Note: some Just So stories use dated or racially insensitive language (notably 'How the Leopard Got His Spots'); preview and plan to discuss or skip those lines.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing the problem with the solution in a story map. The problem is what goes wrong or what a character wants; the solution is how it is fixed.
- Answering 'from the text' questions from memory or opinion. Teach the habit: point to the sentence, then answer.

### Day 1 (Monday): Reading inventory
*Student:* Complete a reading interest survey, choose an independent library book, and do a 1-minute fluency read of a grade 4 passage to set a baseline.

*How to teach:* Have the child complete an interest survey and choose a library book they can read with few errors. For the fluency read, the child reads a grade 4 passage aloud for 1 minute while you mark errors; record words correct per minute (WCPM) as the baseline (spring benchmark for grade 4 is roughly 120-140 WCPM).

- **Q:** How do you compute words correct per minute?
  **A:** Count all words read in 1 minute and subtract the errors.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): How the Whale Got His Throat
*Student:* Read Kipling's 'How the Whale Got His Throat' aloud and fill in a story map with character, setting, problem and solution.

*How to teach:* Read 'How the Whale Got His Throat' aloud, pausing to let the child predict. Together fill in the story map: characters, setting, problem, events, solution.

- **Q:** What is the Whale's problem at the start, and who causes it?
  **A:** The Whale has eaten almost all the fish in the sea; the small 'Stute Fish tricks him into swallowing a man (the Mariner).
- **Q:** How does the Mariner solve his problem?
  **A:** He jumps and dances inside the Whale until it gets hiccups and lets him out, and he leaves a grating (from his raft, tied with his suspenders) in the Whale's throat so it can only eat small fish.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Details as evidence
*Student:* Reread 'How the Camel Got His Hump' and answer 4 questions citing a detail from the text for each.

*How to teach:* Reread 'How the Camel Got His Hump.' Model one answer: state the answer, then quote or point to a detail. The child answers 4 questions the same way.

- **Q:** What does the Camel say whenever the other animals ask him to work? What does it show?
  **A:** He only says 'Humph!' — it shows he is lazy and rude.
- **Q:** How does the Camel get his hump?
  **A:** The Djinn of All Deserts magics his 'humph' into a hump on his back so he can work three days without eating, as punishment for his idleness.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Writer's notebook launch
*Student:* Make a 'heart map' of memories in the writer's notebook and quick-write for 10 minutes about one memory.

*How to teach:* Model drawing a heart map with important people, places and memories. The child draws one, chooses a memory, and quick-writes for 10 minutes without stopping.

- **Q:** What makes a good memory for a personal narrative?
  **A:** A small, specific moment the writer remembers clearly and has feelings about (e.g., the first time I jumped off the diving board), not a whole vacation.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Discuss the two stories with a parent using sentence stems ('I agree because...'), then answer a short story-element check.

*How to teach:* Discuss both stories using stems like 'I agree because...' and 'In the text it says...'. Then give a short check: name the character, setting, problem and solution of one story.

- **Q:** Name the setting of 'How the Camel Got His Hump.'
  **A:** The middle of a Howling Desert at the beginning of the world.

**Assessment:** Story map, cited answers and fluency baseline.

**Answer key:** Story map: proficient = names the Whale, the 'Stute Fish and the Mariner; setting the sea; problem (Whale ate all the fishes / Mariner swallowed); solution (Mariner makes Whale let him out and blocks his throat with a grating). Cited answers: 3 points each — correct answer + supporting detail (e.g., 'Humph!'; the Djinn gives him a hump to work three days without food). Fluency baseline: record WCPM and accuracy (aim for 95%+ accuracy); no grade, used for comparison in week 35.

## Math 4: Place value to the hundred thousands

**Prepare:** Print or write a 20-question diagnostic (10 multiplication/addition facts, 5 place-value items, 5 third-grade fraction items). Draw a place value chart from hundred thousands to ones with a comma between periods. Gather base-ten blocks (or paper flats/rods/units) and 10 index cards numbered 0-9.

**Watch for:**
- Reading 406,215 as 'four hundred six, two hundred fifteen' — teach that the comma is where you say the period name: 'four hundred six THOUSAND, two hundred fifteen.'
- Forgetting zeros in standard form (writing 70,000 + 3,000 + 8 as 7,308). Have the child write the number in a place value chart so empty places get a 0.

### Day 1 (Monday): Diagnostic & number talk
*Student:* Take a 20-question beginning-of-year diagnostic (facts, place value, 3rd-grade fractions) and do a number talk on 99 + 46.

*How to teach:* Give the diagnostic without help and mark only what is wrong; note which areas (facts, place value, fractions) need review. Then do the number talk: write 99 + 46, let the child solve mentally and explain, and model 'make 100': 99 is 1 less than 100, so 100 + 46 - 1.

- **Q:** Solve 99 + 46 in your head and explain.
  **A:** 145 — 100 + 46 = 146, then subtract the extra 1.
- **Q:** What is 7 × 8?
  **A:** 56.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Building big numbers
*Student:* Build 4- and 5-digit numbers with base-ten blocks and record them on a place value chart with period commas.

*How to teach:* Model 2,345 with blocks (2 thousand-cubes or drawn squares, 3 flats, 4 rods, 5 units), then write it in the chart. Have the child build 3-4 more numbers, including a 5-digit one, and record each with a comma separating the thousands period.

- **Q:** In 34,215, how many ten-thousands are there and what is their value?
  **A:** 3 ten-thousands, worth 30,000.
- **Q:** Where does the comma go in 52307?
  **A:** 52,307 — after the thousands period, three digits from the right.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Number names
*Student:* Write ten numbers up to 999,999 in standard form, word form and expanded form; read them aloud to a parent.

*How to teach:* Model one number in all three forms on the board, reading it aloud by periods. The child writes ten numbers in standard, word and expanded form and reads each aloud to you; check that 'and' is not used for whole numbers.

- **Q:** Write 406,215 in word form and expanded form.
  **A:** Four hundred six thousand, two hundred fifteen; 400,000 + 6,000 + 200 + 10 + 5.
- **Q:** Write 70,000 + 3,000 + 40 + 8 in standard form.
  **A:** 73,048 (zero in the hundreds place).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Value of a digit
*Student:* Play 'Digit Detective': draw 6 digit cards, make a number, and state the value of the 7 or 3 in each number made.

*How to teach:* Draw 6 digit cards and arrange them into a 6-digit number; ask 'What is the value of the 7?' The child explains using the chart (digit × place). Repeat with new arrangements, alternating who makes the number.

- **Q:** In 573,129, what is the value of the 7? Of the 3?
  **A:** 7 is in the ten-thousands place: 70,000. 3 is in the thousands place: 3,000.
- **Q:** Make the largest number with 9, 7, 3, 1, 0, 5. What is the value of the 7?
  **A:** 975,310; the 7 is worth 70,000.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: write 8 numbers in three forms and identify the value of underlined digits.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz independently (about 20 minutes). Score it together right away and have the child correct each miss, saying out loud where the error was.

- **Q:** What is the value of the 8 in 284,615?
  **A:** 80,000.

**Assessment:** Quiz on three forms of numbers and digit value (80% target).

**Answer key:** Sample 8-item quiz with answers (each item: standard, word, expanded; 1 point per form). 1) 52,084 = fifty-two thousand, eighty-four = 50,000 + 2,000 + 80 + 4. 2) 310,560 = three hundred ten thousand, five hundred sixty = 300,000 + 10,000 + 500 + 60. 3) 9,407 = nine thousand, four hundred seven = 9,000 + 400 + 7. 4) 765,312 = seven hundred sixty-five thousand, three hundred twelve = 700,000 + 60,000 + 5,000 + 300 + 10 + 2. Digit value: 5) the 6 in 465,201 = 60,000; 6) the 9 in 19,358 = 9,000; 7) the 4 in 804,117 = 4,000; 8) the 2 in 723,980 = 20,000. 80% target = at least 26 of 32 points; reteach any form missed twice.

## Science 4: What is energy?

**Prepare:** Composition notebook for science, a ball, wind-up or pull-back toy car, rubber bands. Preview a Mystery Science (free lessons available) energy intro or any short video on 'what makes things go.'

**Watch for:**
- Thinking energy is a substance or only 'electricity.' Energy is the ability to cause change or motion; it shows up as motion, sound, light, heat and electricity.
- Thinking a still object has no energy. A stretched rubber band or a lifted ball has stored energy that can become motion.

### Day 1 (Monday): Energy hunt
*Student:* Walk through the house listing 15 examples of energy (moving, sound, light, heat, electrical) in a notebook table.

*How to teach:* Walk through the house together; the child lists 15 energy examples in a table with columns for type (motion, sound, light, heat, electrical).

- **Q:** Is a lamp an example of light, heat or electrical energy?
  **A:** All three: electricity goes in, and it gives off light and some heat.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Notebook setup
*Student:* Set up a science notebook with table of contents and an investigation template (question, prediction, data, conclusion).

*How to teach:* Help the child set up a table of contents and an investigation template: question, prediction, materials, data, conclusion.

- **Q:** Why do scientists write a prediction before testing?
  **A:** So they can compare what they expected to what happened and learn from the difference.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Stored and moving energy
*Student:* Stretch a rubber band, wind a toy, lift a ball; describe where energy is stored and when it becomes motion.

*How to teach:* Stretch a rubber band, wind the toy and lift the ball. Ask where the energy is stored and when it becomes motion.

- **Q:** Where is energy stored in a stretched rubber band, and what happens when you let go?
  **A:** It is stored in the stretched band; when released, it changes to motion energy (and some sound).
- **Q:** Where did the lifted ball's stored energy come from?
  **A:** From your arm — you used energy to lift it; when dropped, it changes to motion.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mystery question
*Student:* Watch a Mystery Science energy intro and discuss 'What makes things go?'

*How to teach:* Watch the intro video and discuss 'What makes things go?' The child writes 3 ideas in the notebook.

- **Q:** What makes a moving car go?
  **A:** Energy stored in fuel (gas) or a battery is converted into motion.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draw and label a diagram of 4 energy examples and explain each in a sentence.

*How to teach:* The child draws and labels 4 energy examples and writes a sentence for each.

- **Q:** Give an example of sound energy at home.
  **A:** A doorbell, a vacuum, music from a speaker (anything vibrating to make sound).

**Assessment:** Labeled energy diagram.

**Answer key:** Diagram rubric: 4 = four different, correctly labeled examples (e.g., moving fan = motion, ringing phone = sound, lamp = light, stove = heat) each with an accurate sentence about the energy; 3 = four examples, one mislabeled; 2 = two correct; 1 = incomplete.

## California, A Changing State: Latitude, longitude and the globe

**Prepare:** Globe, world and California maps (with latitude/longitude lines), an orange and a marker. Coordinates for reference: Sacramento about 38.6°N, 121.5°W; San Francisco 37.8°N, 122.4°W; Los Angeles 34.1°N, 118.2°W; San Diego 32.7°N, 117.2°W; Fresno 36.7°N, 119.8°W; Eureka 40.8°N, 124.2°W.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up latitude and longitude. Latitude lines run east-west like ladder rungs ('lat-itude is flat') and measure north-south; longitude lines run pole to pole.
- Writing coordinates without N/S or E/W. 37°N, 122°W is in California; 37°S, 122°E is in the ocean near Australia.

### Day 1 (Monday): Globe tour
*Student:* Mark the equator and prime meridian on an orange 'globe' and name the four hemispheres California lies in.

*How to teach:* Draw the equator and prime meridian on the orange and name the four hemispheres. Find California.

- **Q:** Which two hemispheres is California in?
  **A:** The Northern and Western Hemispheres.
- **Q:** What is the equator's latitude?
  **A:** 0°.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Latitude lines
*Student:* Use a world map to find the latitude of Sacramento, San Diego and the North Pole and record them in a table.

*How to teach:* Use the map to find latitudes of Sacramento, San Diego and the North Pole and record them.

- **Q:** What are the approximate latitudes of Sacramento and San Diego?
  **A:** Sacramento about 38.5°N; San Diego about 32.7°N.
- **Q:** What is the latitude of the North Pole?
  **A:** 90°N.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Longitude lines
*Student:* Find longitude for five California cities and explain why longitude lines meet at the poles.

*How to teach:* Find longitudes for five California cities. Discuss why lines meet at the poles.

- **Q:** Why do longitude lines meet at the poles?
  **A:** They run from pole to pole around the round Earth, so they come together at the North and South Poles.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Coordinate game
*Student:* Play 'Where in California?': a parent gives coordinates and you locate the place (e.g., 37.7 N, 122.4 W = San Francisco).

*How to teach:* Give coordinates; the child locates the place.

- **Q:** What city is near 37.7°N, 122.4°W?
  **A:** San Francisco.
- **Q:** What city is near 34°N, 118°W?
  **A:** Los Angeles.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz: plot 6 locations from coordinates and label the tropics, poles and hemispheres on a blank globe map.

*How to teach:* Give the plotting quiz.

- **Q:** What are the Tropic of Cancer and Tropic of Capricorn latitudes?
  **A:** About 23.5°N and 23.5°S.

**Assessment:** Coordinate plotting quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: plot 6 locations within about 1° (e.g., San Diego 32.7°N, 117.2°W; Sacramento 38.6°N, 121.5°W; Los Angeles 34.1°N, 118.2°W; Eureka 40.8°N, 124.2°W; Fresno 36.7°N, 119.8°W; San Francisco 37.8°N, 122.4°W). Label: equator 0°, Tropic of Cancer 23.5°N, Tropic of Capricorn 23.5°S, North Pole 90°N, South Pole 90°S, prime meridian 0°, Northern/Southern/Eastern/Western Hemispheres. Proficient: 80%.

## Art & Music 4: Line and contour drawing

**Prepare:** Gather a sketchbook, 2B and 4B pencils (softer, darker than a school HB), a kneaded eraser, and three kitchen objects (mug, spoon, piece of fruit). Find a Van Gogh reed-pen drawing online (e.g., 'Wheat Field with Cypresses' drawing or 'The Harvest' at the Van Gogh Museum / Google Arts & Culture).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a good drawing needs one even outline. Varying line weight (thick and thin) makes drawings livelier and shows depth.
- Peeking during blind contour. The point is to train your eye to look closely; wobbly results are expected.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sketchbook start
*Student:* Decorate a sketchbook cover and fill one page with 15 kinds of lines (zigzag, broken, thick-thin, spiral).

*How to teach:* Let your child decorate the cover. Demonstrate a few lines, then they fill a page with 15 labeled line types (zigzag, broken, thick-thin, spiral, wavy, dotted, hatched).

- **Q:** Name three kinds of lines and a feeling each could show.
  **A:** Examples: zigzag — excited or angry; wavy — calm or flowing; broken/dotted — uncertain or delicate; thick — strong.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Blind contour
*Student:* Draw your own hand three times without looking at the paper, then once looking; compare the results.

*How to teach:* Your child draws their hand three times without looking at the paper, moving slowly along edges, then once while looking. Compare.

- **Q:** What did blind contour help you notice?
  **A:** Details of the edges — wrinkles, knuckles, fingernail shapes — because you had to look carefully the whole time.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Contour still life
*Student:* Draw a contour line drawing of three kitchen objects (mug, spoon, fruit) arranged on a table.

*How to teach:* Arrange a mug, spoon and fruit; your child draws only the edges and important inner lines (contour), mostly looking at the objects.

- **Q:** What is a contour line?
  **A:** A line that follows the edges and important shapes of an object.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Line in art
*Student:* Look at a Van Gogh reed-pen drawing online and list the lines he used for grass, sky and trees.

*How to teach:* Look at the Van Gogh drawing; your child lists marks used for grass (short strokes), sky (swirls/dots) and trees (curving lines).

- **Q:** How did Van Gogh make different textures with only lines?
  **A:** He changed the kind of mark — dots, dashes, short strokes, swirls — for each area.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & share
*Student:* Choose the best contour drawing, add a title and explain your line choices to a family member.

*How to teach:* Your child titles the best contour drawing and explains line choices to a family member. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** Why might you make some lines darker than others?
  **A:** To show edges closer to you, shadows or important parts (line weight adds emphasis and depth).

**Assessment:** Contour drawing and line explanation.

**Answer key:** Contour drawing: 4 = three objects drawn with careful continuous contour lines and intentional line variety; 3 = contour accurate, little variety; 2 = sketchy or incomplete; 1 = not attempted. Explanation proficient if the child names at least two line types/weights and why they were used.

## PE 4: Warm-ups, safety and heart rate

**Prepare:** Clear a safe area with cones for boundaries; sneakers and a water bottle. Stopwatch or phone timer. Make a heart-rate log (activity, beats in 15 seconds, × 4 = bpm) and a water tally.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking static stretching is the best warm-up. Warm up with moving (dynamic) exercises; save held stretches for the cool-down.
- Counting the pulse for a full minute while it drops. Count for 15 seconds right after exercise and multiply by 4.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dynamic warm-up
*Student:* Learn a 5-minute warm-up (high knees, butt kicks, arm circles, lunges, inchworms) and lead it once.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate the 5 moves (high knees, butt kicks, arm circles, walking lunges, inchworms), about 1 minute each. Then the child leads it once, calling out each move and checking form (knee over ankle in lunges, flat back in inchworms).

- **Q:** Name the 5 moves in our warm-up.
  **A:** High knees, butt kicks, arm circles, lunges, inchworms.
- **Q:** Why do we warm up?
  **A:** To raise heart rate and muscle temperature and loosen joints so the body performs better and is less likely to get hurt.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Find your pulse
*Student:* Count your pulse for 15 seconds at rest, multiply by 4, and log it; repeat after 2 minutes of jumping jacks.

*How to teach:* Find the pulse at the neck or wrist with two fingers. Count 15 seconds at rest, × 4, log it. Do 2 minutes of jumping jacks and count again right away.

- **Q:** You counted 21 beats in 15 seconds. What is your heart rate?
  **A:** 21 × 4 = 84 beats per minute.
- **Q:** Why don't you use your thumb to take a pulse?
  **A:** The thumb has its own pulse, which can confuse the count.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Heart rate zones
*Student:* Do 3 activities of different intensity (walk, jog, sprint) and record heart rate after each.

*How to teach:* Walk 2 minutes, then take a pulse; jog 2 minutes, pulse; sprint 20–30 seconds, pulse. Record and compare.

- **Q:** Which activity gave the highest heart rate? Why?
  **A:** Sprinting, because it is the most intense, so muscles need the most oxygen and the heart pumps fastest.
- **Q:** Walk: 100 bpm; jog: 140 bpm; sprint: 176 bpm. How much higher is sprint than walk?
  **A:** 76 bpm higher.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cool-down & hydration
*Student:* Learn 4 static stretches for the cool-down and track water intake for the day.

*How to teach:* After a short walk, teach 4 static stretches held 20–30 seconds (quad, hamstring, calf, cross-body shoulder). Track water cups through the day.

- **Q:** How long should you hold a static stretch?
  **A:** About 20–30 seconds without bouncing.
- **Q:** When should you drink water during activity?
  **A:** Before, during and after.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain to a parent why heart rate rises with intensity and lead the full warm-up and cool-down.

*How to teach:* The child explains why heart rate rises with intensity and leads the full warm-up and cool-down for the family.

- **Q:** Why does heart rate rise as exercise gets harder?
  **A:** Harder work means muscles need more oxygen and fuel, so the heart beats faster to pump more blood to them.

**Assessment:** Heart rate log and led warm-up.

**Answer key:** Heart rate log and led warm-up: log should show resting heart rate (15-second count × 4), heart rate after jumping jacks, and after walk, jog and sprint, with values increasing as intensity increases. Rubric: 4 = log complete and correctly calculated, explains the link between intensity and heart rate, leads all 5 warm-up moves and 4 cool-down stretches with good form; 3 = one calculation error or one move missed; 2 = log incomplete or needs prompts; 1 = cannot lead.

## Health 4: What is health?

**Prepare:** Set up a health journal (composition notebook) and markers. Print or write a 15-item habit checklist (e.g., slept 9+ hours, drank water, ate fruit, ate vegetables, 60 minutes of activity, brushed teeth twice, washed hands before eating, ate breakfast, limited screens, talked with a friend, helped someone, felt calm/managed stress, wore a helmet/seatbelt, read for fun, went to bed on time). Prepare the 5 quick-check questions in the key.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking health only means not being sick or being physically fit. Health has physical, mental/emotional and social sides, and all three affect each other.
- Thinking one healthy choice makes you healthy. Health comes from habits repeated over time.

### Day 1 (Monday): Health triangle
*Student:* Draw a health triangle (physical, mental/emotional, social) and list 3 habits for each side.

*How to teach:* Draw a triangle and label the sides physical, mental/emotional and social. Explain each (body; thoughts and feelings; relationships). The child lists 3 habits per side.

- **Q:** Name the 3 sides of the health triangle.
  **A:** Physical, mental/emotional, and social health.
- **Q:** Give a habit for social health.
  **A:** E.g., spending time with friends, being kind, solving conflicts calmly, talking with family.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Habit inventory
*Student:* Complete a 15-item checklist of daily habits (sleep, water, activity, fruits and vegetables) and circle strengths.

*How to teach:* The child completes the 15-item habit checklist honestly for a typical day and circles strengths. Point out one area to work on without judgment.

- **Q:** Which side of the triangle does 'getting 9–12 hours of sleep' help?
  **A:** Physical health (and also mental/emotional health, because rest helps mood and focus).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Health journal
*Student:* Set up a health journal and write about one healthy habit your family already has.

*How to teach:* Set up the journal with a title page and date. The child writes 4–5 sentences about a healthy habit the family already has and why it helps.

- **Q:** Why is a health journal useful?
  **A:** It helps you track habits and goals and notice patterns over time.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Healthy day schedule
*Student:* Plan an ideal healthy day schedule with meals, activity, screen time and 9-12 hours of sleep.

*How to teach:* Plan an ideal healthy day on a schedule: wake and bed times (9–12 hours of sleep), 3 meals and snacks, 60 minutes of activity, limited recreational screen time, homework and family time.

- **Q:** If you go to bed at 8:30 p.m. and wake at 6:30 a.m., how much sleep do you get? Is it enough?
  **A:** 10 hours — yes, within the 9–12 hours recommended for children ages 6–12.
- **Q:** How many minutes of physical activity should kids get each day?
  **A:** At least 60 minutes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Share the health triangle with family and answer 5 quick-check questions on health dimensions.

*How to teach:* The child explains the health triangle to family, then answers the 5 quick-check questions in the key orally or on paper.

- **Q:** Feeling stressed before a test affects which side of the triangle?
  **A:** Mental/emotional health (and it can also affect physical health, like a stomachache).

**Assessment:** Health triangle and quick check.

**Answer key:** Health triangle rubric: 4 = triangle labeled physical, mental/emotional, social with 3 accurate habits each. Quick check key: 1) Name the 3 dimensions of health — physical, mental/emotional, social. 2) Sleeping and eating vegetables belong to — physical health. 3) Talking about your feelings belongs to — mental/emotional health. 4) Playing a game fairly with friends belongs to — social health. 5) How much sleep do 9-year-olds need? — 9–12 hours. Proficient = triangle complete and 4 of 5 correct.
