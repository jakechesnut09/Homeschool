# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 17

## ELA 4: Fiction meets fact: the Lone Woman of San Nicolas

**Prepare:** Novel; a nonfiction article on the Lone Woman of San Nicolas Island (National Park Service Channel Islands site or a library article). Key facts: she was left behind when the island's people were moved to the mainland in 1835; she lived alone about 18 years; she was found by George Nidever's party in 1853; she was taken to Santa Barbara, given the name Juana Maria at baptism, and died about seven weeks later; no one could understand her language.

**Watch for:**
- Assuming everything in historical fiction really happened. Separate documented facts from invented details (Karana's name, Ramo, Rontu's story).
- Quoting without explaining. Every quotation needs a sentence telling how it proves the point.

### Day 1 (Monday): Nonfiction
*Student:* Read a nonfiction account of the Lone Woman of San Nicolas Island (Juana Maria) and take notes.

*How to teach:* Read the nonfiction account; the child takes notes on key facts (dates, rescue, what happened afterward).

- **Q:** When and how was the Lone Woman found?
  **A:** In 1853, by a party led by George Nidever, after she had lived alone on San Nicolas Island for about 18 years.
- **Q:** What happened after she came to the mainland?
  **A:** She was taken to Santa Barbara, baptized Juana Maria, and died about seven weeks later; no one understood her language.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Compare
*Student:* Make a two-column chart of what is historical fact and what Scott O'Dell invented, citing the novel and the article for each row.

*How to teach:* The child makes a fact/fiction chart citing the novel and the article for each row.

- **Q:** Name one fact and one invention in the novel.
  **A:** Fact: a woman lived alone on San Nicolas for about 18 years and was rescued. Invention: her name Karana, her brother Ramo, the dog Rontu and the friendship with Tutok are O'Dell's creations.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Essay plan
*Student:* Plan an essay: 'How does Karana survive, and what does it show about her?'

*How to teach:* Plan the essay: claim answering 'How does Karana survive, and what does it show about her?' plus 3 reasons with evidence.

- **Q:** Write a possible claim.
  **A:** Example: 'Karana survives by building shelter, making weapons and befriending animals, which shows she is resourceful, brave and compassionate.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Draft & revise
*Student:* Draft and revise the essay with at least 3 quotations.

*How to teach:* The child drafts and revises the essay with at least 3 quotations, each introduced and explained.

- **Q:** How should you introduce a quote?
  **A:** With a lead-in like 'Karana explains,' then the quote in quotation marks, then a sentence explaining how it supports the claim.

### Day 5 (Friday): Present & assess
*Student:* Share the essay in a book talk; scored with the literary essay rubric.

*How to teach:* The child shares the essay as a book talk; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** What makes a book talk engaging?
  **A:** A strong opening, a few key points about the book, a short quote, and clear speaking.

**Assessment:** Literary response essay.

**Answer key:** Literary essay rubric: 4 = clear claim answering both parts of the prompt; 3 body reasons each with an accurately cited quotation and explanation; conclusion; organized with transitions; few errors. 3 = claim and evidence present, explanations brief. 2 = mostly summary, 1-2 quotes. 1 = no clear claim. Fact/fiction chart: proficient lists at least 3 accurate facts and 3 invented elements.

## Math 4: Equivalent fractions with models

**Prepare:** Paper strips (5 per child) for folding, fraction strips, crayons. Draw three aligned number lines 0-1 split into fourths, eighths and twelfths for Day 3.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 4/8 is bigger than 1/2 because the numbers are bigger. Fold the strips: same amount of paper is shaded.
- Adding the same number to top and bottom (2/3 = 3/4). Equivalent fractions come from multiplying both by the same number (cutting each piece into equal smaller pieces).

### Day 1 (Monday): Folding fractions
*Student:* Fold paper strips into halves, fourths and eighths and record equivalent fractions like 1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8.

*How to teach:* Fold one strip in half, shade 1/2; fold again into fourths and eighths and see the shading still covers half. The child records 1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8 and finds 1/4 = 2/8.

- **Q:** What fractions equal 1/2 on your strips?
  **A:** 2/4 and 4/8.
- **Q:** What is 3/4 in eighths?
  **A:** 6/8.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Area models
*Student:* Draw area models to show 2/3 = 8/12 by partitioning each part into 4 equal pieces.

*How to teach:* Draw a rectangle in thirds, shade 2, then draw lines to split each third into 4 pieces. Count: 8 of 12. The child repeats with 3/4 = 9/12.

- **Q:** Show 2/3 = 8/12 with an area model. Why does it work?
  **A:** Each third is split into 4 pieces, so there are 4 times as many pieces in the whole (12) and in the shaded part (8).
- **Q:** Use an area model: 1/2 = ?/6.
  **A:** 3/6.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Number lines
*Student:* Mark 3/4, 6/8 and 9/12 on aligned number lines and explain why they share a point.

*How to teach:* The child marks 3/4, 6/8 and 9/12 on the three aligned lines and explains why they line up.

- **Q:** Why do 3/4, 6/8 and 9/12 land on the same point?
  **A:** They are the same distance from 0 — each fourth is split into 2 or 3 equal parts, so the length doesn't change.
- **Q:** Name another fraction at the same point as 1/3 on a twelfths line.
  **A:** 4/12.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Multiply by n/n
*Student:* Generate 3 equivalent fractions for 8 given fractions using n x a / n x b and explain the rule with a model.

*How to teach:* Teach the rule: multiply numerator and denominator by the same number (n/n = 1). The child generates 3 equivalents for 8 fractions and explains one with a drawing.

- **Q:** Write 3 fractions equivalent to 2/5.
  **A:** 4/10, 6/15, 8/20.
- **Q:** Why does multiplying by 3/3 not change the value?
  **A:** 3/3 equals 1, and multiplying by 1 doesn't change a number; it just cuts each piece into 3 smaller pieces.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on generating and explaining equivalent fractions.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz independently; one item requires a model. Correct together.

- **Q:** Is 3/4 = 6/8? Show how.
  **A:** Yes: 3 × 2 = 6 and 4 × 2 = 8; a drawing of fourths split into halves shows 6 of 8.

**Assessment:** Equivalent fractions quiz with a model explanation.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) 1/3 = 2/6 = 3/9 = 4/12 2) 3/5 = 6/10 3) 2/4 = 4/8 4) 5/6 = 10/12 5) 3/4 = 6/8 (yes) 6) 1/2 = 50/100 7) 2/3 = 4/6 = 6/9. Model explanation rubric: 4 = accurate area model or number line, explains that each part is split into the same number of smaller parts so the amount stays the same; 3 = correct model, explanation brief; 2 = correct answer, model inaccurate; 1 = incorrect.

## Science 4: Animal external structures

**Prepare:** Tweezers, spoons, clothespins, cups; seeds, rice, gummy worms; colored paper 'insects' (some matching lawn colors, some bright); egg carton and raw eggs (do the drop outside); pictures of a desert tortoise and a sea otter.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking animals choose or grow structures because they need them. Structures are inherited traits that helped ancestors survive.
- Thinking camouflage means being invisible. It means blending in so predators/prey notice it less.

### Day 1 (Monday): Bird beak lab
*Student:* Use tweezers, spoons and clothespins as beaks to pick up seeds, rice and gummy worms.

*How to teach:* Each 'beak' tool gets 30 seconds to pick up each food; record counts.

- **Q:** Which beak worked best for gummy worms? Seeds?
  **A:** Usually tweezers (like a long thin beak) for worms; clothespin or tweezers for seeds; use your data.
- **Q:** How does a beak's shape relate to food?
  **A:** Different beak shapes are suited to different foods (e.g., a hummingbird's long beak reaches nectar).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Camouflage
*Student:* Hide colored paper 'insects' outside and count which are found first.

*How to teach:* Scatter paper insects in the yard for 1 minute; count which you find first.

- **Q:** Which colors were found first? Why?
  **A:** Bright colors that don't match the background; green/brown ones blended in (camouflage).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Shell protection
*Student:* Test how a shell structure (egg carton) protects an egg in a drop.

*How to teach:* Drop an egg in an egg-carton 'shell' and one without from the same height.

- **Q:** What does this model about shells?
  **A:** A hard or cushioned outer structure protects soft body parts, like a turtle or tortoise shell.

### Day 4 (Thursday): California animals
*Student:* Research external structures of the desert tortoise and sea otter.

*How to teach:* Research the desert tortoise and sea otter.

- **Q:** How do a sea otter's external structures help it survive?
  **A:** Very dense fur traps air to keep it warm in cold ocean water (it has no blubber); webbed hind feet for swimming.
- **Q:** How does a desert tortoise's structure help?
  **A:** A hard shell protects it, and strong front legs dig burrows to escape heat.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph about how one external structure (e.g., a sea otter's dense fur) helps it survive.

*How to teach:* The child writes a CER about one external structure.

- **Q:** Give an example claim.
  **A:** A sea otter's dense fur helps it survive in cold water by keeping it warm.

**Assessment:** Beak lab data and CER.

**Answer key:** Beak lab: data table with counts for each tool and food. CER rubric: 4 = claim about one external structure, evidence (lab data or research facts), reasoning on how it aids survival; 3 = weak reasoning; 2 = claim only; 1 = incorrect.

## California, A Changing State: Secularization and land grants

**Prepare:** Calisphere set 'Californio Society,' an image of a diseño (hand-drawn rancho map — many at the Library of Congress/Bancroft Library).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Native people received mission lands. The law intended that, but most land went to Californio families through land grants.
- Thinking diseños were exact surveys. They were rough sketches using landmarks like rivers, trees and hills.

### Day 1 (Monday): Secularization
*Student:* Read about the 1834 secularization of the missions.

*How to teach:* Read about the 1834 secularization.

- **Q:** What does secularization of the missions mean?
  **A:** Taking control of mission lands away from the Catholic Church and placing them under the Mexican government.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Land grants
*Student:* Examine a diseño (rancho map) and explain how land was granted.

*How to teach:* Examine a diseño and explain how land was granted.

- **Q:** What is a diseño?
  **A:** A hand-drawn map showing a rancho's boundaries using landmarks, submitted to request a land grant.
- **Q:** Who could apply for land grants?
  **A:** Mexican citizens, including Californios and some naturalized foreigners.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Native outcomes
*Student:* Discuss what happened to Native peoples after secularization.

*How to teach:* Discuss outcomes for Native peoples.

- **Q:** What happened to most Native people after secularization?
  **A:** Most did not receive land; many worked as laborers on ranchos, and some returned to their homelands or moved inland.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Calisphere
*Student:* Analyze images from the Calisphere set 'Californio Society'.

*How to teach:* Analyze Californio Society images.

- **Q:** Who were the Californios?
  **A:** Spanish-speaking people of Mexican and Spanish heritage born in California.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on mission secularization, diseños and rancho land grants.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz.

- **Q:** When were the missions secularized?
  **A:** Beginning in 1834.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Secularization = mission lands taken from the Church by Mexico, 1834 2) Lands were supposed to go to Native people 3) Most went to Californios as ranchos 4) Diseño = hand-drawn rancho map with landmarks 5) Land grants were given by the Mexican governor 6) Native people often became rancho workers. Proficient: 5/6.

## Art & Music 4: California Native basketry and pattern

**Prepare:** Open Calisphere's 'Native Americans: Arts and Traditions' set (Pomo and Chumash baskets). Gather a paper plate, scissors, yarn in 2–3 colors, and paper strips. Cut an odd number of slits around the plate rim for the loom.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking California basketry is only in the past. Pomo, Chumash and other California Native weavers continue the tradition today.
- Copying exact designs. Create your own motifs inspired by, not copied from, cultural designs.

### Day 1 (Monday): Look closely
*Student:* Study Pomo and Chumash basket images on Calisphere and describe shapes, patterns and materials.

*How to teach:* Study Pomo and Chumash baskets; describe shapes, patterns and materials. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** What materials did California Native weavers use?
  **A:** Local plants such as willow, sedge root, bulrush, juncus and sumac, and sometimes feathers and shell beads.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Infer
*Student:* Write what the baskets suggest about the makers' environment, skills and daily life.

*How to teach:* Write what baskets suggest about makers' environment, skills and life. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** What can you infer from a very tightly woven basket?
  **A:** The weaver was highly skilled; it might hold water or seeds; the plants came from local wetlands or hillsides.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Weaving loom
*Student:* Make a paper-plate loom and begin weaving a yarn pattern.

*How to teach:* Make the paper-plate loom; begin weaving. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What is the over-under pattern?
  **A:** Yarn alternates over one spoke and under the next.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Pattern design
*Student:* Design a repeating geometric motif inspired by (not copied from) the baskets and finish the weaving.

*How to teach:* Design a repeating geometric motif and finish the weaving. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** What is a motif?
  **A:** A repeated design element or shape in a pattern.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & share
*Student:* Explain your pattern choices and what you learned about basketry as a living tradition.

*How to teach:* Explain pattern choices and basketry as a living tradition. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** Why is basketry called a living tradition?
  **A:** Native weavers today still gather materials and make baskets, passing skills to new generations.

**Assessment:** Weaving and written inference.

**Answer key:** Weaving: complete with an intentional repeating pattern and tight over-under weaving. Written inference rubric: 4 = three inferences supported by details from the baskets; 3 = two; 2 = one; 1 = none.

## PE 4: Tumbling skills

**Prepare:** A tumbling mat or soft lawn; a cushion under one end to make a gentle incline. Clear space. Parent spots forward rolls. Skip forward rolls if the child has neck/back problems.

**Watch for:**
- Rolling on the top of the head. Tuck the chin to the chest so the back of the head and shoulders touch first.
- Doing a cartwheel with bent arms and hands together. Hands go down one at a time, shoulder-width apart, arms straight; hand-hand-foot-foot along a line.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rolls
*Student:* Practice log rolls and egg rolls, then a forward roll down a gentle incline with chin tucked.

*How to teach:* Practice log and egg rolls, then the forward roll down an incline: squat, hands shoulder-width, chin to chest, push with legs, roll on rounded back, stand. Spot at the back of the neck and hips.

- **Q:** Where should your chin be during a forward roll?
  **A:** Tucked to your chest.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cartwheel progression
*Student:* Practice hand-hand-foot-foot along a line, then a lunge-to-cartwheel.

*How to teach:* Cartwheel progression: lunge facing the line, place hand-hand on the line, kick legs over landing foot-foot. Start with low 'bunny' kicks, then higher.

- **Q:** What is the order of hands and feet in a cartwheel?
  **A:** Hand, hand, foot, foot.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Flexibility
*Student:* Do bridge, straddle stretch and butterfly stretch to improve tumbling flexibility.

*How to teach:* Hold bridge (from lying on back), straddle stretch and butterfly stretch for 20–30 seconds each.

- **Q:** Why does flexibility help tumbling?
  **A:** It allows a greater range of motion, so skills are smoother and safer.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Combine
*Student:* Link a forward roll, stand, and balance into a smooth sequence.

*How to teach:* Link forward roll → stand → balance (e.g., arabesque) in a smooth sequence.

- **Q:** What makes a sequence smooth?
  **A:** No extra steps or pauses between skills; each finishes in a controlled position.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform the forward roll and cartwheel for a parent who checks safety cues.

*How to teach:* Perform the forward roll and cartwheel; parent checks safety cues.

- **Q:** Name one safety cue for each skill.
  **A:** Forward roll: chin to chest. Cartwheel: straight arms, hands shoulder-width apart.

**Assessment:** Tumbling cue checklist.

**Answer key:** Tumbling cue checklist: Forward roll — chin tucked (1), hands shoulder-width (1), rounded back (1), stands up without hands (1). Cartwheel — lunge start (1), hand-hand-foot-foot along line (1), straight arms (1), controlled finish (1). Proficient = 6+ of 8 with all safety cues (chin tucked, straight arms).

## Health 4: Benefits of physical activity

**Prepare:** Stopwatch, health journal, a safe area for jumping and walking. CDC guideline: kids 6–17 need 60 minutes or more of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily, including vigorous aerobic, muscle-strengthening and bone-strengthening activity on at least 3 days a week each.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking activity only helps the body. It also improves mood, sleep, focus and friendships.
- Thinking only sports count. Walking, dancing, biking, playing tag and chores like raking all count.

### Day 1 (Monday): Benefits
*Student:* List physical, mental and social benefits of physical activity.

*How to teach:* Make a 3-column list: physical benefits (strong heart, muscles, bones, healthy weight, better sleep), mental benefits (better mood, less stress, better focus), social benefits (friends, teamwork).

- **Q:** Name one physical, one mental and one social benefit of activity.
  **A:** Physical: stronger heart/muscles/bones. Mental: better mood, less stress. Social: making friends, teamwork.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Activity types
*Student:* Sort activities into aerobic, muscle-strengthening and bone-strengthening.

*How to teach:* Sort activities: aerobic (running, biking, swimming), muscle-strengthening (climbing, push-ups, tug-of-war), bone-strengthening (jumping rope, hopscotch, running). Note some fit more than one.

- **Q:** Is jumping rope aerobic, bone-strengthening or both?
  **A:** Both — it gets your heart pumping and the impact strengthens bones.
- **Q:** Give a muscle-strengthening activity.
  **A:** E.g., climbing on playground equipment, push-ups, tug-of-war, gymnastics.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Heart rate
*Student:* Measure heart rate before and after 3 minutes of jumping.

*How to teach:* Measure heart rate (15 seconds × 4) before and after 3 minutes of jumping. Record and compare.

- **Q:** Before: 20 beats in 15 seconds; after: 35 beats. What are the heart rates per minute?
  **A:** Before 80 bpm; after 140 bpm.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mood check
*Student:* Rate your mood before and after a 15-minute walk.

*How to teach:* Rate mood 1–5 before and after a 15-minute walk, and write what changed.

- **Q:** Why might your mood improve after a walk?
  **A:** Activity releases brain chemicals (endorphins) that improve mood and lower stress; fresh air and a break help too.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on the 60-minute guideline, types of activity and three benefits of being active.

*How to teach:* Quiz (see key).

- **Q:** How many minutes of activity should kids get daily?
  **A:** At least 60 minutes.

**Assessment:** Activity benefits quiz.

**Answer key:** Activity benefits quiz key: 1) Recommended daily activity for kids — 60 minutes or more. 2) Three types of activity — aerobic, muscle-strengthening, bone-strengthening. 3) How many days a week for muscle- and bone-strengthening — at least 3 each. 4) Give an aerobic activity — running, biking, swimming, dancing. 5) Name 3 benefits — any physical, mental and social benefits (stronger heart, better mood, better sleep, friendships). 6) Why does heart rate rise during exercise — muscles need more oxygen, so the heart pumps more blood. Proficient = 5 of 6.
