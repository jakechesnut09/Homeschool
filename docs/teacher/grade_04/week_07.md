# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 7

## ELA 4: Coyote and trickster tales across cultures

**Prepare:** A library collection of California Native Coyote stories (e.g., a version of how Coyote stole/brought fire), an Anansi tale (e.g., 'Anansi and the Pot of Wisdom' or 'How Anansi Got the Stories'), Venn diagram. Read both stories beforehand so you can answer from your version.

**Watch for:**
- Treating all trickster tales as the same story. Compare details: the tricks, who is fooled, and the lesson differ.
- Changing events when rewriting in first person. Point of view changes what the narrator knows and feels, not what happens.

### Day 1 (Monday): Coyote tales
*Student:* Read a California Native Coyote story (e.g., how Coyote brought fire) and identify the trickster pattern.

*How to teach:* Read the Coyote story aloud. Identify the trickster pattern: a clever (often greedy or foolish) character uses tricks, which may help others or backfire.

- **Q:** What makes Coyote a trickster?
  **A:** He uses cleverness and deception rather than strength to get what he wants; in fire stories he steals fire from those who keep it and brings it to people (answers should cite your version).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Anansi
*Student:* Read an Anansi tale from West Africa and record its events and theme.

*How to teach:* Read the Anansi tale. The child records the sequence of events and the theme.

- **Q:** Who is Anansi and where do his stories come from?
  **A:** A spider trickster from West African (Akan/Ashanti) folklore.
- **Q:** State the theme of the tale you read with a detail.
  **A:** Answers depend on the version; e.g., in 'Anansi and the Pot of Wisdom,' no one person can hold all the wisdom — his small son shows him how to climb the tree, and the wisdom spills out to everyone.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compare
*Student:* Complete a Venn diagram comparing the two tricksters' themes and patterns of events.

*How to teach:* Complete a Venn diagram: both tricksters' traits, tricks, outcomes, lessons. Ask what is similar across cultures and why.

- **Q:** Name one way the two tricksters are alike.
  **A:** Both are animals who act like people and use clever tricks; both stories often explain how something came to be or teach a lesson.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Point of view
*Student:* Rewrite a short scene of the Coyote story in first person as Coyote and discuss what changes.

*How to teach:* The child rewrites a short scene in first person as Coyote ('I crept toward the fire...'). Discuss what we now learn (his thoughts) and lose (others' views).

- **Q:** What changes when the story is told by Coyote in first person?
  **A:** We hear Coyote's thoughts and feelings using 'I,' but we only know what he knows; the narrator can't see inside other characters.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a compare/contrast paragraph about the two tales citing details.

*How to teach:* The child writes a compare/contrast paragraph with a topic sentence, two similarities, two differences and details from each tale.

- **Q:** What words signal comparison and contrast?
  **A:** Comparison: both, similarly, also. Contrast: however, but, unlike, on the other hand.

**Assessment:** Compare/contrast paragraph.

**Answer key:** Compare/contrast paragraph rubric: 4 = clear topic sentence naming both tales, at least two similarities and two differences in theme or pattern of events, a specific detail from each story, transition words, concluding sentence; 3 = mostly complete but details general; 2 = only similarities or only differences; 1 = summary of one story only.

## Math 4: Multistep addition and subtraction problems

**Prepare:** Bar model (tape diagram) templates or blank paper. Look up or use sample state park visitor numbers for Day 3 (any realistic values). Prepare a Unit 2 test (key below).

**Watch for:**
- Choosing the operation by keywords ('more' means add) instead of the situation. Always draw the bar model first and ask: are we finding a whole or a part?
- Doing only the first step of a two-step problem. Have the child write a plan: 'First I find..., then I find...'

### Day 1 (Monday): Bar models
*Student:* Draw tape diagrams for 4 comparison and part-whole problems and write an equation with a letter for the unknown.

*How to teach:* Model a part-whole bar (whole on top, parts below) and a comparison bar (two bars, difference marked). The child draws 4 diagrams, labels the unknown with a letter and writes an equation.

- **Q:** Mia read 1,250 pages; Sam read 375 fewer. Draw a model and find Sam's pages.
  **A:** Two bars, Sam's shorter by 375: s = 1,250 - 375 = 875 pages.
- **Q:** A school has 846 students; 429 are girls. How many boys? Write an equation.
  **A:** 846 - 429 = b; b = 417 boys.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Two-step problems
*Student:* Solve 5 two-step problems (e.g., a library had 12,450 books, gave away 1,375, bought 2,060) and estimate first.

*How to teach:* Have the child estimate, then solve 5 two-step problems, showing each step. Ask 'Is your answer close to your estimate?'

- **Q:** A library had 12,450 books, gave away 1,375 and bought 2,060. How many now?
  **A:** 12,450 - 1,375 = 11,075; 11,075 + 2,060 = 13,135 books (estimate 12,000 - 1,000 + 2,000 = 13,000).
- **Q:** A farm picked 3,240 apples Monday and 2,875 Tuesday and sold 4,500. How many are left?
  **A:** 6,115 - 4,500 = 1,615 apples.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Write your own
*Student:* Write two multistep word problems using real data (state park visitors) and trade solutions with a parent.

*How to teach:* The child writes two multistep problems using park visitor data, solves them on a separate sheet, and trades with you. Check that each problem needs two steps and has one clear question.

- **Q:** If a park had 15,400 visitors in May and 22,980 in June, how many more in June?
  **A:** 7,580 more visitors.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit 2 review
*Student:* Mixed review of addition, subtraction and word problems with a self-check answer key.

*How to teach:* The child completes the mixed review, then self-checks with the key and redoes misses. Discuss which problem types felt hardest.

- **Q:** 70,000 - 36,452 = ?
  **A:** 33,548.
- **Q:** 25,678 + 49,385 = ?
  **A:** 75,063.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 2 test on algorithms and multistep problems.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test independently; require estimates on word problems. Score and correct together.

- **Q:** A theater has 2,500 seats. 1,368 adult and 947 child tickets were sold. How many seats are empty?
  **A:** 1,368 + 947 = 2,315; 2,500 - 2,315 = 185 empty seats.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test key: 1) 34,507 + 18,965 = 53,472 2) 60,000 - 27,384 = 32,616 3) 482,716 + 39,584 = 522,300 4) 8,103 - 4,567 = 3,536 5) A park had 45,320 visitors in June and 38,915 in July; how many more in June? 6,405. 6) Theater: 2,500 - (1,368 + 947) = 185 empty seats. 7) Bar model: Jo has 1,200 stamps, Lee has 350 more than Jo; total? Lee 1,550, total 2,750. Scoring: 1 point per computation, 2 points per word problem (model/equation + correct answer). Proficient = 80%.

## Science 4: Electric circuits

**Prepare:** D-cell batteries, small flashlight bulbs (or LEDs), insulated wires with stripped ends (or foil strips), paper clips, brass fasteners, a buzzer. Bookmark PhET 'Circuit Construction Kit: DC.' Safety: never use wall outlets; batteries only; don't leave a wire connected straight across a battery (it gets hot).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking one wire to a bulb is enough. A circuit must be a complete loop from one end of the battery, through the bulb, back to the other end.
- Thinking electricity is 'used up' by the bulb. Energy is transferred to light and heat; the charge flows around the loop.

### Day 1 (Monday): Light a bulb
*Student:* Use a battery, one wire and a bulb to find 4 ways to light the bulb and draw them.

*How to teach:* Challenge: light a bulb with one battery and one wire. The child finds and draws 4 arrangements that work.

- **Q:** What must touch the bulb to light it?
  **A:** The bulb's tip and its metal side (casing) must each connect to opposite ends of the battery, forming a complete loop.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Conductor test
*Student:* Build a circuit tester and test 12 household objects as conductors or insulators.

*How to teach:* Build a circuit tester with a gap; test 12 objects.

- **Q:** Which objects let the bulb light?
  **A:** Metal objects (paper clip, foil, coin, key) — conductors.
- **Q:** Which did not?
  **A:** Plastic, rubber, wood, paper, glass — insulators.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Switches
*Student:* Make a paper clip switch and add it to a circuit.

*How to teach:* Make a switch from a paper clip and two brass fasteners on cardboard and add it.

- **Q:** How does a switch work?
  **A:** Closing it completes the circuit so current flows; opening it breaks the loop.

### Day 4 (Thursday): PhET circuits
*Student:* Build series and parallel circuits in the Circuit Construction Kit.

*How to teach:* Build series and parallel circuits in the simulation.

- **Q:** If one bulb is removed in a series circuit, what happens?
  **A:** All bulbs go out — there is only one path.
- **Q:** In a parallel circuit?
  **A:** The other bulbs stay lit — each has its own path.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draw a circuit diagram and explain how energy travels.

*How to teach:* The child draws a circuit diagram and explains how energy travels.

- **Q:** Explain how energy gets from the battery to the light.
  **A:** Stored energy in the battery is carried by electric current through wires to the bulb, where it changes to light and heat.

**Assessment:** Circuit diagram and conductor data.

**Answer key:** Rubric: 4 = correct closed-circuit diagram with battery, wires, switch and bulb; explanation of energy transfer; conductor data table of 12 objects correctly sorted (metals conduct); 3 = minor error; 2 = open circuit drawn or many sorting errors; 1 = incomplete.

## California, A Changing State: Beliefs, legends and oral tradition

**Prepare:** A collection of California Indian stories (library), source cards (basket, photograph, textbook, oral story recording, diary, encyclopedia, mission record, painting, interview, map). Read the creation story ahead to answer from your version.

**Watch for:**
- Calling Native stories 'just myths.' They are sacred oral traditions that carry history, values and knowledge.
- Thinking an oral story recording is secondary. A recording of a tradition-bearer telling their people's story is a primary source.

### Day 1 (Monday): Legends
*Student:* Read a Chumash or Miwok creation story and identify what it teaches about the natural world.

*How to teach:* Read a Chumash or Miwok creation story; identify what it teaches about nature.

- **Q:** What does your story teach about the natural world?
  **A:** Answers depend on the story; e.g., it explains how animals or landforms came to be and teaches respect for animals and land.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Oral tradition
*Student:* Discuss how stories passed down orally preserve history and values.

*How to teach:* Discuss how oral tradition preserves history and values.

- **Q:** How is history passed on through oral tradition?
  **A:** Elders tell stories, songs and teachings to younger generations, who memorize and retell them.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Primary vs secondary
*Student:* Sort 10 sources (basket, photograph, textbook, oral story recording) as primary or secondary.

*How to teach:* Sort 10 sources as primary or secondary.

- **Q:** Is a basket made in 1850 primary or secondary?
  **A:** Primary — an object made at the time.
- **Q:** Is a textbook primary or secondary?
  **A:** Secondary — written later by someone not there.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Ceremonies
*Student:* Read about a ceremony such as the World Renewal ceremony of the Karuk and Hupa.

*How to teach:* Read about the World Renewal ceremony of the Karuk and Hupa.

- **Q:** What is the purpose of the World Renewal ceremony?
  **A:** To renew and balance the world, ensuring plentiful food (salmon, acorns) and well-being; it includes dances such as the White Deerskin Dance and Jump Dance.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a reflection on how legends connect people and place.

*How to teach:* The child writes a reflection on how legends connect people and place.

- **Q:** How do legends connect people to a place?
  **A:** They name and explain local landforms, animals and events, so the land itself holds the people's history and values.

**Assessment:** Source sort and reflection.

**Answer key:** Source sort key: primary — basket, photograph, oral story recording by a tradition bearer, diary, mission record, interview with an eyewitness, map made at the time; secondary — textbook, encyclopedia, modern painting of the past. Reflection rubric: 4 = explains with a specific story example how legends teach values and tie people to local places; 3 = general; 2 = vague; 1 = off-topic.

## Art & Music 4: Recorder songs with B-A-G and C, D

**Prepare:** Recorder, fingering chart and song sheets. High C = thumb + finger 2; high D = finger 2 only (thumb off). 'Merrily We Roll Along' (in G) = B A G A B B B, A A A, B B B, B A G A B B B, A A B A G. 'Mary Had a Little Lamb' is the same tune except the third phrase goes B D D, so it uses high D too.

**Watch for:**
- Leaving the thumb on for high D. High D needs only finger 2; the thumb hole must be open.
- Rushing half notes. Count 'one-two' for every half note so the beat stays steady.

### Day 1 (Monday): High C and D
*Student:* Learn high C and D fingerings and play scales B-C-D.

*How to teach:* Teach high C (thumb + 2) and high D (2 only). Play the 'scale' B–C–D–C–B slowly, checking for squeaks.

- **Q:** What is the fingering for high D?
  **A:** Left-hand finger 2 only, with the thumb hole uncovered.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mary Had a Little Lamb
*Student:* Play 'Mary Had a Little Lamb' using B, A and G from notation, keeping a steady beat.

*How to teach:* Your child plays 'Mary Had a Little Lamb' from notation while you tap a steady beat. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** Which phrase of 'Mary Had a Little Lamb' uses high D?
  **A:** The third phrase, 'little lamb' — B D D.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rehearse
*Student:* Rehearse 'Merrily We Roll Along' and 'Ode to Joy' excerpt.

*How to teach:* Rehearse 'Merrily We Roll Along' and the opening of 'Ode to Joy' (B B C D D C B A G G A B B A A), slow first.

- **Q:** 'Mary' and 'Merrily' share a melody. What's different?
  **A:** Only the third phrase: Mary goes B D D, Merrily goes B B B.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Record and evaluate
*Student:* Record your playing and evaluate tone and rhythm accuracy.

*How to teach:* Record both songs; your child rates tone (1–4) and rhythm accuracy (1–4) and names one fix.

- **Q:** What is a phrase in music?
  **A:** A musical 'sentence' — a group of notes that makes a complete idea, usually followed by a breath.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Play 'Mary Had a Little Lamb' and 'Merrily We Roll Along' for a parent, who checks tone and steady beat.

*How to teach:* Your child performs 'Mary' and 'Merrily' while you check tone and steady beat. Treat it like a real event: quiet audience, a short spoken introduction and a bow; then debrief together using the week's rubric, starting with what went well.

- **Q:** How can you make your tone smoother?
  **A:** Use gentle steady breath, cover holes fully with finger pads, and tongue each note softly with 'doo.'

**Assessment:** Recorded songs and self-evaluation.

**Answer key:** Recorded songs: 4 = correct notes, steady beat, clear tone with no squeaks; 3 = 1–2 errors; 2 = several errors or uneven beat; 1 = cannot complete. Self-evaluation proficient when ratings match the recording and one specific fix is named.

## PE 4: Throwing and catching games

**Prepare:** Soft balls, cones for a small ultimate field with end zones, flying disc, a timer. Sportsmanship list and self-rating sheet.

**Watch for:**
- Running with the disc in ultimate. Once you catch it, you must stop and pivot on one foot; pass within 10 seconds.
- Thinking sportsmanship only means shaking hands at the end. It includes honest calls, encouraging teammates, respecting opponents and controlling emotions throughout the game.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ultimate disc basics
*Student:* Learn ultimate disc rules (no running with the disc, pivot foot) and play a 2-on-2 game.

*How to teach:* Teach ultimate: move the disc by passing only; no running with the disc (pivot foot); defender counts a 10-second stall; drops, out-of-bounds or interceptions are turnovers; catch it in the end zone to score; no contact. Play 2-on-2.

- **Q:** What happens if a pass is dropped in ultimate?
  **A:** It is a turnover; the other team gets the disc.
- **Q:** How do you score in ultimate?
  **A:** Catch the disc in the end zone you're attacking.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sportsmanship talk
*Student:* List 5 sportsmanship behaviors and practice them in a game.

*How to teach:* List 5 sportsmanship behaviors (honest calls, encouraging teammates, shaking hands/saying good game, accepting calls calmly, including everyone). Practice them in a game.

- **Q:** Give 3 sportsmanship behaviors.
  **A:** Any 3: playing fair and honestly, encouraging teammates, respecting opponents and officials, accepting results calmly, saying 'good game.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hot potato relay
*Student:* Play a timed throwing and catching relay with family.

*How to teach:* Play a timed hot potato relay: throw and catch down a line and back; record times and try to beat them.

- **Q:** What should you do if a teammate drops the ball?
  **A:** Encourage them ('You've got this!') and keep going.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Skill review
*Student:* Review throwing and catching cues by teaching them to a sibling or parent.

*How to teach:* The child teaches the throwing and catching cues to a sibling or parent.

- **Q:** What is the most important catching cue to teach?
  **A:** Watch the ball into your hands and reach and give (with correct hand position).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit assessment
*Student:* Complete a throwing and catching skills test and a sportsmanship self-rating.

*How to teach:* Skills test (see key) and sportsmanship self-rating (1–4 on each behavior).

- **Q:** Rate yourself: what is one sportsmanship behavior you'll work on?
  **A:** Answers vary; should be a specific behavior with a plan.

**Assessment:** Skills test and sportsmanship rating.

**Answer key:** Skills test: 5 overhand throws to a target scored on the 5 cues (25 points) + 10 catches at mixed levels (10 points). Proficient = 20+ throw points and 8+ catches. Sportsmanship self-rating: 1–4 on honest calls, encouragement, respect, self-control; proficient = honest ratings with one specific goal.

## Health 4: Breakfast, fruits and vegetables

**Prepare:** Breakfast ingredients (e.g., whole-grain toast, eggs, fruit, yogurt, oatmeal), a 3-day rainbow tracker (red, orange, yellow, green, blue/purple, white/tan), and 3 new fruits or vegetables to taste (e.g., kiwi, jicama, purple cabbage).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking breakfast doesn't matter if you're not hungry. Breakfast refuels the brain and body after a night without food and helps focus and energy.
- Thinking all fruits and vegetables have the same nutrients. Different colors have different vitamins, minerals and plant nutrients, so eat a variety.

### Day 1 (Monday): Breakfast power
*Student:* Read about how breakfast fuels the brain and list 5 quick healthy breakfasts.

*How to teach:* Read about breakfast and the brain (KidsHealth). The child lists 5 quick healthy breakfasts with at least 2–3 food groups each.

- **Q:** Why is breakfast called 'brain fuel'?
  **A:** After sleeping all night, the brain and body need energy (glucose) from food to focus, think and have energy.
- **Q:** Give one quick healthy breakfast and its food groups.
  **A:** E.g., oatmeal with berries and milk = grains, fruit, dairy.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Make breakfast
*Student:* Prepare a healthy breakfast with 3 food groups.

*How to teach:* The child prepares a breakfast with 3 food groups (with adult help for the stove).

- **Q:** Name the food groups in your breakfast.
  **A:** Answers vary; must name 3 correct groups, e.g., egg (protein), toast (grains), orange slices (fruit).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Eat the rainbow
*Student:* Track the colors of fruits and vegetables you eat for 3 days.

*How to teach:* Start a 3-day rainbow tracker: color in a box for each color of fruit or vegetable eaten.

- **Q:** Why 'eat the rainbow'?
  **A:** Different colored fruits and vegetables give different vitamins, minerals and nutrients.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Taste test
*Student:* Taste-test 3 new fruits or vegetables and rate them.

*How to teach:* Taste 3 new fruits or vegetables. Rate each 1–5 and describe taste and texture.

- **Q:** Why try a new food more than once?
  **A:** Taste can change; it often takes several tries to like a new food.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph about the benefits of breakfast and fruits and vegetables.

*How to teach:* Write a paragraph about the benefits of breakfast and fruits and vegetables, using the tracker as evidence.

- **Q:** Name 2 benefits of eating fruits and vegetables.
  **A:** They give vitamins, minerals and fiber; they help digestion, help fight illness, and lower the risk of diseases later.

**Assessment:** Rainbow tracker and paragraph.

**Answer key:** Rainbow tracker and paragraph rubric: 4 = tracker completed 3 days with colors recorded; paragraph has a topic sentence, 2+ breakfast benefits (energy, focus) and 2+ fruit/vegetable benefits (vitamins, fiber, health), and evidence from the tracker; 3 = tracker mostly complete, paragraph with 3 benefits; 2 = 1–2 benefits; 1 = incomplete.
