# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 32

## ELA 4: Drafting the research report

**Prepare:** Notes and computer. Comma rules: commas separate items in a series (red, white, and blue); a comma follows an introductory phrase (In 1890, Yosemite became...).

**Watch for:**
- Plagiarizing by stringing notes into sentences that copy the source. Write from notes, not from the source.
- Inconsistent source list format. Choose one format: Title. Author/Organization. Website or publisher, year.

### Day 1 (Monday): Outline
*Student:* Outline the report with an introduction, 3-4 sections and conclusion.

*How to teach:* The child outlines intro, 3-4 sections and conclusion.

- **Q:** How many main ideas per section?
  **A:** One — each section has a heading and focuses on one subtopic.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Draft intro
*Student:* Draft an introduction that hooks the reader with a surprising fact, then draft the first headed section from note cards.

*How to teach:* Draft an introduction with a surprising fact; draft the first section.

- **Q:** Give a surprising-fact hook for Death Valley.
  **A:** 'Death Valley holds the record for the hottest air temperature on Earth: 134°F in 1913.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Draft body
*Student:* Draft the remaining 2-3 headed sections, paraphrasing notes and including one short quotation with its source.

*How to teach:* Draft the remaining sections with one short quotation and its source.

- **Q:** How do you include a quotation?
  **A:** Put exact words in quotation marks and name the source: According to the National Park Service, "..."

### Day 4 (Thursday): Commas
*Student:* Practice commas in series and introductory phrases; apply to the draft.

*How to teach:* Practice commas in series and after introductory phrases; apply to the draft.

- **Q:** Add commas: 'In 1864 President Lincoln signed the Yosemite Grant protecting the valley and the giant sequoias.'
  **A:** 'In 1864, President Lincoln signed the Yosemite Grant, protecting the valley and the giant sequoias.' (The comma after 1864 is the key correction.)

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write the conclusion and a source list (title, author, website or publisher) in a consistent format.

*How to teach:* Write the conclusion and a source list in a consistent format.

- **Q:** Show one source entry.
  **A:** 'Yosemite National Park. National Park Service. nps.gov/yose.'

**Assessment:** Complete draft with source list.

**Answer key:** Draft check (4-point): 4 = hook intro, 3-4 headed sections paraphrased from notes, one cited quotation, conclusion, consistent source list of 3+ sources; 3 = all parts, minor gaps; 2 = missing sections or sources; 1 = incomplete.

## Math 4: Points, lines, rays and angles

**Prepare:** Ruler, an index card (its corner is a right angle), colored pencils, a printed map of a San Francisco grid neighborhood (e.g., the Sunset District, where numbered avenues run north-south and named streets run east-west), a paper circle cut into fourths.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing a line (arrows both ends, goes on forever), a ray (one endpoint, one arrow) and a segment (two endpoints).
- Thinking an angle's size depends on the length of its sides. The size is the amount of turn between the rays.

### Day 1 (Monday): Geometry vocabulary
*Student:* Draw and label points, lines, segments and rays in a geometry dictionary.

*How to teach:* Model drawing and labeling a point A, line AB, segment CD and ray EF. The child makes a geometry dictionary page for each with a picture and definition.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a ray and a line segment?
  **A:** A ray has one endpoint and goes on forever in one direction; a segment has two endpoints.
- **Q:** How many endpoints does a line have?
  **A:** None — it goes on forever in both directions.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Angle hunt
*Student:* Find 10 angles at home, test with an index card corner and classify them.

*How to teach:* Hold the index card corner in an angle: if it matches, it is right; smaller is acute; bigger (but less than a straight line) is obtuse. The child finds and classifies 10 angles at home.

- **Q:** An angle is smaller than the corner of the card. What type is it?
  **A:** Acute (less than 90°).
- **Q:** What is the angle formed by the hands of a clock at 3:00?
  **A:** A right angle (90°).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Parallel and perpendicular
*Student:* Find parallel and perpendicular lines on a map of San Francisco streets and trace them.

*How to teach:* Explain parallel (never meet, always same distance) and perpendicular (meet at right angles). The child traces examples of each on the map.

- **Q:** On a street grid, are two numbered avenues parallel or perpendicular?
  **A:** Parallel — they run side by side and never meet.
- **Q:** An avenue crossing a street at a square corner is ___.
  **A:** Perpendicular.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Angles as turns
*Student:* Model 1/4, 1/2 and 3/4 turns with paper circle fractions and connect to 90, 180 and 270 degrees.

*How to teach:* Turn a paper circle-fourth around the center: 1/4 turn = 90°, 1/2 turn = 180°, 3/4 turn = 270°, full = 360°.

- **Q:** How many degrees is a 1/4 turn? A 3/4 turn?
  **A:** 90°; 270°.
- **Q:** How many degrees in a full turn?
  **A:** 360°.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on geometric figures and angle types.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz independently. Correct together.

- **Q:** Classify an angle of 135°.
  **A:** Obtuse.

**Assessment:** Geometry vocabulary quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Draw a ray: one endpoint with an arrow 2) Draw a segment: two endpoints 3) Draw parallel lines: never meet 4) Draw perpendicular lines: meet at a right angle 5) 40° = acute 6) 90° = right 7) 150° = obtuse 8) 180° = straight angle 9) 1/2 turn = 180° 10) Point = an exact location. Proficient: 8/10.

## Science 4: Defining a design problem: the wind-powered lifter

**Prepare:** Box fan, science notebook, photos/diagrams of historic windmills and modern turbines (EIA Energy Kids). Read the challenge yourself first: lift 10 pennies 30 cm using only wind from the fan.

**Watch for:**
- Writing criteria that can't be measured ('works well'). Use numbers: lifts 10 pennies 30 cm in under 60 seconds.
- Treating constraints as optional. They are limits the design must follow.

### Day 1 (Monday): The challenge
*Student:* Read the challenge: design a device that converts wind energy from a box fan into motion that lifts 10 pennies 30 cm; restate it in your own words.

*How to teach:* Read the challenge and have the child restate it in their own words.

- **Q:** Restate the challenge.
  **A:** Build a device that uses wind from a fan to turn something and wind up a string to lift 10 pennies 30 cm.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Criteria
*Student:* List measurable criteria for success (lift height, load, time to lift) in a table.

*How to teach:* List measurable criteria in a table.

- **Q:** Give three measurable criteria.
  **A:** Lifts at least 10 pennies; lifts them 30 cm; does so within a set time (e.g., 60 seconds).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Constraints
*Student:* List constraints: only household materials (cups, straws, skewers, cardstock, string, tape), 3 days of building, no batteries.

*How to teach:* List constraints.

- **Q:** What are the constraints?
  **A:** Only household materials listed, 3 days of building, no batteries (wind only).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Research
*Student:* Study photos and diagrams of historic windmills and modern turbines and note how blades and axles convert wind to rotation.

*How to teach:* Study windmill and turbine photos; note how blades and axles convert wind to rotation.

- **Q:** Why are turbine blades angled?
  **A:** Angled blades catch the wind so it pushes them sideways, making them rotate.

### Day 5 (Friday): Design brief
*Student:* Write a one-page design brief with problem, criteria, constraints and a first sketch idea.

*How to teach:* The child writes a one-page design brief with a first sketch.

- **Q:** What goes in a design brief?
  **A:** The problem, criteria, constraints and an initial idea/sketch.

**Assessment:** Design brief with criteria and constraints.

**Answer key:** Design brief rubric: 4 = problem clearly stated, 3+ measurable criteria, all constraints, labeled first sketch showing blades, axle, string and cup; 3 = criteria partly measurable; 2 = missing criteria or constraints; 1 = incomplete.

## California, A Changing State: Hard times: the Great Depression, Dust Bowl and World War II

**Prepare:** Calisphere sets 'The Great Depression: Hard Times,' 'Dust Bowl Migration,' 'Life on the Home Front During World War II' and 'Japanese American Internment'; Dorothea Lange's 'Migrant Mother' (1936, Nipomo, California). Facts: Executive Order 9066 (February 19, 1942) led to the forced removal of about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, most U.S. citizens, to camps such as Manzanar and Tule Lake; the U.S. formally apologized and paid reparations in 1988. Preview: discuss unfairness and fear sensitively.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Japanese American incarceration was about crimes. No one was charged; it was based on ancestry and prejudice.
- Thinking Dust Bowl migrants were welcomed. Many were called 'Okies,' faced prejudice and lived in poor camps.

### Day 1 (Monday): The Depression
*Student:* Read about the Great Depression in California and analyze images from the Calisphere set 'The Great Depression: Hard Times'.

*How to teach:* Read about the Depression in California; analyze images.

- **Q:** What was the Great Depression?
  **A:** A severe economic crisis in the 1930s when banks failed, businesses closed and many people lost jobs.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dust Bowl migrants
*Student:* Analyze Dorothea Lange's 'Migrant Mother' and photos from the Calisphere set 'Dust Bowl Migration'.

*How to teach:* Analyze 'Migrant Mother' and Dust Bowl photos.

- **Q:** What does 'Migrant Mother' show?
  **A:** A worried mother with her children in a pea-pickers' camp in 1936, showing the hardship of migrant farm workers.
- **Q:** Why did Dust Bowl families come to California?
  **A:** Drought and dust storms ruined farms in Oklahoma, Texas and nearby states; they hoped for farm work in California.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Home front
*Student:* Read about shipyards in Richmond, aircraft factories and women workers using the Calisphere set 'Life on the Home Front During World War II'.

*How to teach:* Read about the home front.

- **Q:** How did WWII change jobs in California?
  **A:** Shipyards (like Kaiser in Richmond) and aircraft factories hired huge numbers of workers, including women ('Rosie the Riveter') and African Americans who moved west.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Japanese American incarceration
*Student:* Read about Executive Order 9066 and analyze photos from the Calisphere set 'Japanese American Internment'.

*How to teach:* Read about EO 9066; analyze photos.

- **Q:** What did Executive Order 9066 lead to?
  **A:** The forced removal and incarceration of about 120,000 Japanese Americans in camps like Manzanar.
- **Q:** Was it justified?
  **A:** No — no one was charged with a crime; in 1988 the U.S. apologized and paid reparations.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on how one of these events changed California.

*How to teach:* Write a paragraph on how one event changed California.

- **Q:** Name one long-term change from WWII.
  **A:** Population and industry boomed; aerospace and defense industries grew; more diverse population.

**Assessment:** Historical paragraph.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric: 4 = names an event, explains 2-3 specific changes with dates or facts (e.g., Dust Bowl migration to the Central Valley; shipyards in Richmond; EO 9066 and Manzanar), conclusion; 3 = one change; 2 = summary; 1 = inaccurate.

## Art & Music 4: Music and connections

**Prepare:** Choose a short film clip (1–2 minutes, family-friendly) and open Chrome Music Lab Kandinsky. Gather recorder and percussion instruments. Pick a short story for the soundtrack.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking film music is just background. It shapes how viewers feel.
- Choosing random sounds for a soundtrack. Match tempo, dynamics and timbre to the mood.

### Day 1 (Monday): Film music
*Student:* Watch a film clip with and without sound and describe the mood change.

*How to teach:* Watch the clip with and without sound; describe the mood change. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** How does music change a film scene?
  **A:** It creates mood—tension, joy, sadness—and tells viewers how to feel.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Kandinsky
*Student:* Draw in Chrome Music Lab's Kandinsky to turn art into sound.

*How to teach:* Draw in Kandinsky to turn art into sound. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** Who was Wassily Kandinsky?
  **A:** A painter who connected colors and shapes with sounds and music.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Soundtrack
*Student:* Create a soundtrack for a short story using recorder and percussion.

*How to teach:* Create a soundtrack for a short story with recorder and percussion. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** What sounds could show suspense?
  **A:** Slow, soft, repeated low notes that build in volume.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Personal playlist
*Student:* Make a playlist of 5 songs that represent you and explain choices.

*How to teach:* Make a playlist of 5 songs that represent you; explain choices. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** Why might a song represent you?
  **A:** Its lyrics, mood or culture connects to your personality or experiences.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Unit test on musical traditions and connections.

*How to teach:* Unit test on musical traditions and connections. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** Name three musical traditions from this unit.
  **A:** Mariachi, taiko, gamelan, jazz/blues.

**Assessment:** Soundtrack and unit check.

**Answer key:** Unit check: 1) mariachi instruments (violin, trumpet, vihuela, guitarrón) 2) sesquialtera alternates 6/8 and 3/4 3) taiko from Japan, San Jose Taiko 4) gamelan from Indonesia 5) 12-bar blues 6) 'Take Five' in 5/4 7) film music creates mood. Soundtrack proficient if sounds match story events with clear tempo and dynamics choices.

## PE 4: Tag and invasion game strategy

**Prepare:** Flag belts or bandanas, cones for boundaries, foam ball, paper for the strategy board. Recruit family or neighbors for capture the flag.

**Watch for:**
- Running in straight lines away from taggers. Use fakes, pivots and changes of speed and direction.
- Everyone attacks at once in capture the flag. Assign defenders and attackers and communicate.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dodging
*Student:* Practice dodging, faking and pivoting in a 'shadow' drill with a partner.

*How to teach:* Shadow drill: leader dodges, fakes and pivots; partner mirrors. Switch.

- **Q:** Name 3 dodging skills.
  **A:** Fake (pretend to go one way), pivot (turn on one foot), change speed/direction.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Flag tag
*Student:* Play flag tag in a coned area, using fakes to keep your flags.

*How to teach:* Flag tag in a coned area; use fakes to keep flags.

- **Q:** Is it fair to guard your flag with your hands?
  **A:** No — protect it by dodging, not by holding or blocking.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Capture the flag
*Student:* Play capture the flag with family or neighbors, assigning defenders and attackers.

*How to teach:* Capture the flag: assign defenders and attackers; tagged players go to jail until freed.

- **Q:** Why do you need defenders?
  **A:** To protect your flag while attackers try to capture the other team's.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Strategy board
*Student:* Draw a strategy diagram for your team and test it in a rematch.

*How to teach:* Draw a strategy diagram (positions, arrows for runs, roles) and test it in a rematch.

- **Q:** Give an example strategy.
  **A:** E.g., two attackers run at the same time on opposite sides so defenders can't cover both.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write which strategy worked best and why, and how your team handled disagreements.

*How to teach:* Write which strategy worked best and why, and how the team handled disagreements.

- **Q:** How did your team settle disagreements?
  **A:** Answers vary; e.g., took turns choosing, voted, or used rock-paper-scissors.

**Assessment:** Strategy diagram and reflection.

**Answer key:** Strategy diagram and reflection rubric: 4 = diagram shows clear roles and planned movement; reflection names the best strategy, explains why it worked with evidence from play, and describes a fair way disagreements were handled; 3 = diagram and strategy with basic reason; 2 = vague; 1 = incomplete.

## Health 4: Refusal skills

**Prepare:** Make a refusal skills card: 1) Say no clearly, 2) Give a reason, 3) Suggest something else, 4) Walk away (and tell a trusted adult if needed). Prepare scenario cards; the parent plays the pressuring peer gently. Scoring sheet for Friday.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking you need a long excuse to say no. A firm 'No thanks' is enough; you don't owe an explanation.
- Confusing assertive with aggressive. Assertive is clear and respectful; aggressive is rude, yelling or threatening.

### Day 1 (Monday): Assertive voice
*Student:* Compare passive, aggressive and assertive responses and practice an assertive voice and body language.

*How to teach:* Act out passive (looking down, mumbling 'I don't know…'), aggressive (yelling, insulting) and assertive (eye contact, calm firm voice, standing tall, 'No, I don't want to') responses. Practice assertive voice and body language.

- **Q:** Describe an assertive response.
  **A:** Standing tall, making eye contact, using a calm, firm voice and saying clearly what you want.
- **Q:** What is the difference between assertive and aggressive?
  **A:** Assertive is firm and respectful; aggressive is hostile, loud or hurtful.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Refusal steps
*Student:* Learn refusal strategies: say no clearly, give a reason, suggest an alternative, walk away.

*How to teach:* Teach the 4 refusal steps and practice each with simple offers.

- **Q:** What are the 4 refusal steps?
  **A:** Say no clearly, give a reason, suggest an alternative, walk away (and tell a trusted adult).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Role-play 1
*Student:* Role-play refusing a vape offered by an older kid.

*How to teach:* Role-play: an older kid offers a vape. The child uses all 4 steps.

- **Q:** Example refusal to a vape offer?
  **A:** 'No thanks. Vaping is addictive and I play soccer. Want to shoot some hoops instead?' Then walk away if they keep pushing.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Role-play 2
*Student:* Role-play refusing alcohol at a party and leaving with help from a trusted adult.

*How to teach:* Role-play: someone offers alcohol at a party. The child refuses and calls a trusted adult to be picked up.

- **Q:** What do you do if friends keep pressuring you at a party?
  **A:** Repeat no, leave the situation and call a parent or trusted adult to pick you up.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Demonstrate refusal skills in a new scenario for a parent who scores the steps.

*How to teach:* New scenario (e.g., a friend offers a pill 'to help you stay awake'). The parent scores each step.

- **Q:** Why is walking away an important step?
  **A:** It gets you out of the risky situation and ends the pressure.

**Assessment:** Refusal skills performance check.

**Answer key:** Refusal skills performance check (score 1 point each, 6 total): says no clearly (1); gives a reason (1); suggests an alternative (1); walks away/leaves (1); assertive voice and eye contact (1); names a trusted adult to tell (1). 4 = 6; 3 = 5; 2 = 3–4; 1 = 0–2.
