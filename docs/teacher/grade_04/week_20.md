# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 20

## ELA 4: Drama: reading and performing a play

**Prepare:** Reader's theater script (an Aesop fable or 'The Emperor's New Clothes' — many free online), the prose version of the same story, simple props. Unit 5 test for Day 5 (key below).

**Watch for:**
- Reading stage directions aloud as lines. Stage directions (often in parentheses/italics) tell actors what to do.
- Thinking drama has a narrator like prose. Plays tell the story mostly through dialogue and action.

### Day 1 (Monday): Drama structure
*Student:* Read a reader's theater script and label cast list, scenes and stage directions.

*How to teach:* Read the script and label the cast list, scenes and stage directions.

- **Q:** What is a cast of characters?
  **A:** The list of characters in a play, usually at the beginning.
- **Q:** What are stage directions?
  **A:** Instructions for actors about movement, expression or setting, not spoken aloud.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Prose vs drama
*Student:* Compare the script to the prose version and list structural differences.

*How to teach:* Compare the script with the prose version and list structural differences.

- **Q:** Name two differences between prose and drama.
  **A:** Prose uses paragraphs and narration; drama uses a cast list, dialogue after character names, stage directions and scenes/acts.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Write a scene
*Student:* Convert a scene from Island of the Blue Dolphins into a short script.

*How to teach:* The child converts a scene from Island of the Blue Dolphins into a script.

- **Q:** How do you show what Karana feels in a script?
  **A:** Through her dialogue and stage directions, e.g., (Karana kneels, looking out to sea sadly).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rehearse
*Student:* Rehearse the script with family members using expression and blocking.

*How to teach:* Rehearse the script with family using expression and blocking.

- **Q:** What is blocking?
  **A:** Planning where and how actors move on stage.

### Day 5 (Friday): Perform & assess
*Student:* Perform the play; take the Unit 5 test on poetry and drama structure.

*How to teach:* Perform the play, then give the Unit 5 test.

- **Q:** In 'The Emperor's New Clothes,' who finally says the Emperor has no clothes?
  **A:** A small child in the crowd.

**Assessment:** Performance and Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test key: 1) Stanza = group of lines 2) Rhyme scheme of 'My Shadow' = AABB 3) 'Fog' compares fog to a cat (extended metaphor) 4) Repeated line in Frost's poem: 'And miles to go before I sleep' 5) Free verse = no regular rhyme/meter 6) Cast of characters = list of roles 7) Stage directions = instructions to actors 8) Two prose vs. drama differences (dialogue format, scenes vs. paragraphs, stage directions) 9) 'One if by land, two if by sea' refers to lantern signals 10) One fact Longfellow changed. Performance rubric: 4 = lines known, expressive, follows blocking; 3 = minor slips; 2 = reads flatly; 1 = incomplete. Proficient: 80%.

## Math 4: Fraction reasoning

**Prepare:** Paper circles of two sizes (one about 4 in across, one about 8 in). Write 4 student claims for Day 2, e.g., '1/8 > 1/4 because 8 > 4'; '2/3 = 3/4 because each is one piece away from a whole'; '4/6 = 2/3'; '1/2 of my pizza is more than 1/2 of yours'. Unit 5 test for Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Comparing fractions of different-sized wholes. Fraction comparisons only work when the wholes are the same size.
- 'Missing one piece' reasoning (2/3 = 3/4 because both are one piece short). The missing pieces are different sizes: 1/3 > 1/4, so 2/3 < 3/4.

### Day 1 (Monday): Same whole?
*Student:* Compare 1/2 of a small pizza and 1/4 of a large one with paper circles and write why the whole matters.

*How to teach:* Cut 1/2 of the small circle and 1/4 of the large circle and compare. Discuss: which is more? The child writes why the whole matters.

- **Q:** Can 1/4 of a large pizza be more than 1/2 of a small pizza?
  **A:** Yes — fractions of different wholes can't be compared by the fraction alone; a quarter of a big pizza can be more food.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Critique reasoning
*Student:* Analyze 4 student claims about fraction size and write agree/disagree arguments.

*How to teach:* Read each claim aloud; the child decides agree or disagree and writes an argument with a model.

- **Q:** Claim: 1/8 > 1/4 because 8 > 4. Agree or disagree?
  **A:** Disagree — eighths are smaller pieces; 1/8 < 1/4.
- **Q:** Claim: 2/3 = 3/4 because each is one piece away from a whole.
  **A:** Disagree — the missing 1/3 is bigger than the missing 1/4, so 2/3 < 3/4.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fraction word problems
*Student:* Solve 6 comparison word problems about who ran farther or ate more.

*How to teach:* The child solves 6 comparison stories, writing the comparison with a symbol and a sentence.

- **Q:** Lily ran 3/4 mile and Ben ran 5/8 mile. Who ran farther?
  **A:** Lily: 3/4 = 6/8 > 5/8.
- **Q:** Jo ate 2/6 of a pan of brownies and Max ate 1/3 of the same pan. Who ate more?
  **A:** They ate the same amount: 2/6 = 1/3.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit 5 review
*Student:* Review game covering equivalence and comparison.

*How to teach:* Review game with equivalence and comparison cards. The child explains each answer.

- **Q:** Write two fractions equivalent to 3/5.
  **A:** 6/10, 9/15 (also 60/100).
- **Q:** Compare 4/6 and 5/8.
  **A:** 4/6 = 16/24, 5/8 = 15/24, so 4/6 > 5/8.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 5 test on equivalent fractions and comparing fractions with explanations.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test independently. Correct together.

- **Q:** Compare 2/3 and 7/12.
  **A:** 2/3 = 8/12 > 7/12.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test key: 1) 2/3 = 8/12 2) 3/4 = 75/100 3) 10/12 = 5/6 (simplest form) 4) 3/8 < 1/2 5) 4/5 > 4/7 6) 5/6 > 3/4 (10/12 vs 9/12) 7) 2/10 < 25/100 8) Order 1/6, 2/3, 1/2 → 1/6, 1/2, 2/3 9) Explain: 1/3 of a big pizza vs 1/3 of a small pizza — not equal because the wholes are different sizes. 10) Ben: 3/8 mi, Ana 1/2 mi — Ana ran farther. Proficient: 8/10 with explanations.

## Science 4: Weathering

**Prepare:** Sugar cubes, a jar with lid, sidewalk chalk, white vinegar, cups, plastic straws and clay, freezer, a variety of rocks. List 10 weathering examples for the quiz (key below).

**Watch for:**
- Confusing weathering and erosion. Weathering breaks rock down in place; erosion moves the pieces away.
- Thinking weathering is always fast. Most weathering takes years to thousands of years; our models speed it up.

### Day 1 (Monday): Shake test
*Student:* Shake sugar cubes in a jar for 1, 2 and 3 minutes and observe rounding.

*How to teach:* Shake sugar cubes for 1, 2 and 3 minutes, comparing shapes after each.

- **Q:** What happened to the cubes' corners? What does this model?
  **A:** They wore down and became rounded — physical weathering, like rocks tumbling in a stream.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Chalk and vinegar
*Student:* Place chalk pieces in vinegar and in water overnight and compare them to model chemical weathering.

*How to teach:* Put chalk in vinegar and in water overnight; compare.

- **Q:** What happened to the chalk in vinegar? Why?
  **A:** It fizzed and dissolved/broke down — the acid reacted with the chalk (chemical weathering, like acid rain on limestone).
- **Q:** Why use water as a comparison?
  **A:** It is the control, showing the vinegar caused the change.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Freeze-thaw
*Student:* Freeze a water-filled straw sealed with clay and observe expansion.

*How to teach:* Fill a straw with water, seal both ends with clay and freeze overnight.

- **Q:** What happened to the clay plugs? Why?
  **A:** They were pushed out because water expands when it freezes — frost wedging cracks rocks the same way.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Plants weather rock
*Student:* Observe roots breaking sidewalks on a neighborhood walk.

*How to teach:* Walk the neighborhood looking for roots cracking sidewalks and lichen on rocks.

- **Q:** How do roots weather rock?
  **A:** Roots grow into cracks and widen them as they grow (physical weathering).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz sorting 10 examples into physical and chemical weathering.

*How to teach:* Give the sorting quiz.

- **Q:** Is rust on a rock physical or chemical weathering?
  **A:** Chemical — iron reacts with oxygen and water.

**Assessment:** Weathering quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key — Physical: 1) ice wedging 2) roots cracking rock 3) rocks tumbling in a river 4) wind-blown sand scraping rock 5) waves pounding cliffs 6) animals burrowing. Chemical: 7) acid rain dissolving limestone 8) rust forming on iron-rich rock 9) vinegar fizzing on chalk 10) cave formation by acidic water dissolving limestone. Proficient: 8/10.

## California, A Changing State: The Bear Flag Revolt

**Prepare:** Image of the original Bear Flag (search Calisphere 'Bear Flag'; the original was destroyed in the 1906 San Francisco fire, but photos exist), map of Sonoma. Facts: on June 14, 1846, American settlers seized Sonoma, arrested General Vallejo and declared the 'California Republic,' which lasted about 25 days until the U.S. Navy raised the U.S. flag at Monterey (July 7, 1846).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Bear Flag Revolt was a big war. It was a small uprising that lasted about 25 days and was absorbed into the Mexican-American War.
- Thinking Vallejo opposed the United States. He actually favored joining the U.S., yet was imprisoned by the rebels.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tensions
*Student:* Read about tensions between Mexican officials and American settlers in the 1840s.

*How to teach:* Read about tensions in the 1840s.

- **Q:** Why were Mexican officials worried about American settlers?
  **A:** Growing numbers of Americans were arriving without permission and some wanted California to join the U.S.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): John C. Frémont
*Student:* Read about Frémont's expeditions and his role in 1846.

*How to teach:* Read about Frémont.

- **Q:** Who was John C. Frémont?
  **A:** A U.S. Army explorer and mapmaker ('the Pathfinder') who was in California in 1846 and supported the Bear Flaggers, later leading the California Battalion.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): The revolt
*Student:* Read about the June 1846 capture of Sonoma and the arrest of Mariano Vallejo; sequence the events.

*How to teach:* Read about the capture of Sonoma; sequence events.

- **Q:** What happened on June 14, 1846?
  **A:** Americans seized Sonoma, took Vallejo prisoner and declared the California Republic.

### Day 4 (Thursday): The flag
*Student:* Analyze a photo of the original Bear Flag found on Calisphere: what symbols do you see and what do they mean?

*How to teach:* Analyze the Bear Flag photo.

- **Q:** What symbols are on the Bear Flag?
  **A:** A grizzly bear (strength), a red star (inspired by Texas's Lone Star Republic), a red stripe, and the words 'California Republic.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Perspectives
*Student:* Write two short journal entries about the revolt: one as a settler and one as Vallejo.

*How to teach:* Write two journal entries: settler and Vallejo.

- **Q:** How might Vallejo feel about being arrested?
  **A:** Shocked and betrayed — he had been friendly to Americans and favored joining the U.S.

**Assessment:** Perspective journal entries.

**Answer key:** Journal rubric: 4 = two entries in first person with accurate facts (date, Sonoma, Vallejo's arrest, the flag) and clearly different viewpoints (settler: freedom/land; Vallejo: betrayal, concern for family and property); 3 = accurate but similar viewpoints; 2 = few facts; 1 = incomplete.

## Art & Music 4: Community mural design

**Prepare:** Find photos of murals in Chicano Park (San Diego) and Balmy Alley (San Francisco). Get a large sheet of butcher paper, markers or paint, and invite family members to collaborate.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a mural is just a big painting. Murals usually share a public message about a community.
- Letting one person decide everything. Collaboration means combining ideas fairly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mural study
*Student:* Look at photos of murals in Chicano Park (San Diego) or Balmy Alley (San Francisco) and discuss their messages.

*How to teach:* Look at mural photos; discuss messages. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What messages do Chicano Park murals share?
  **A:** Pride in Mexican American/Chicano culture and history, community struggle and identity.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Set goals
*Student:* With family members, choose a theme about your community and set goals for the mural.

*How to teach:* With family, choose a community theme and set goals. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** Why set goals before drawing?
  **A:** So everyone agrees on the message and plan.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sketch
*Student:* Each person sketches ideas; combine the best into one plan.

*How to teach:* Each person sketches; combine the best into one plan. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** How can you combine different ideas fairly?
  **A:** Choose the strongest parts of each sketch and agree as a group.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Paint/draw
*Student:* Create the mural on a large sheet of paper together.

*How to teach:* Create the mural together. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What makes collaboration successful?
  **A:** Sharing tasks, listening, respecting ideas and working toward the goal.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & share
*Student:* Write an artist statement explaining the mural's message and display it.

*How to teach:* Write an artist statement explaining the message. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** What should the mural statement explain?
  **A:** The theme, symbols used and the message to the community.

**Assessment:** Collaborative mural and statement.

**Answer key:** Mural and statement: 4 = clear community theme, symbols support message, all contributed, statement explains message and symbols; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = unclear message; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 4: Stationary and moving hand dribble

**Prepare:** Basketball (size 5 or 6) or playground ball, flat surface, 6 cones, stopwatch.

**Watch for:**
- Slapping with the palm. Push the ball with finger pads, wrist flexing.
- Dribbling high and away from the body. Keep it at or below the waist and close to the body for control.

### Day 1 (Monday): Stationary
*Student:* Practice pound dribbles, low dribbles and figure-8s around the legs with each hand.

*How to teach:* Stationary: pound dribbles (hard, waist high), low dribbles (below knee), and figure-8s around the legs, each hand.

- **Q:** Which part of your hand controls the dribble?
  **A:** The finger pads.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Eyes up
*Student:* Dribble while calling out the number of fingers a parent holds up.

*How to teach:* Dribble while calling out the number of fingers held up by a parent.

- **Q:** Why dribble with eyes up in a game?
  **A:** To see teammates, defenders and the basket.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Moving dribble
*Student:* Dribble down and back on a line with right, left, then alternating hands.

*How to teach:* Dribble down and back on a line with right hand, then left, then alternating.

- **Q:** Why practice with your weaker hand?
  **A:** So you can dribble away from defenders on either side.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Crossover
*Student:* Learn the crossover dribble, switching hands low in front of the body around 6 cones.

*How to teach:* Crossover: low, quick bounce in front of the body from one hand to the other, weaving around 6 cones.

- **Q:** Where should the crossover bounce happen?
  **A:** Low and in front of your body, below the knees.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a timed cone dribble course using both hands.

*How to teach:* Time the cone course using both hands; record best of 3.

- **Q:** Best time 18.2 s; earlier best 21.0 s. Improvement?
  **A:** 2.8 seconds faster.

**Assessment:** Dribble course time.

**Answer key:** Dribble course time: record best of 3 times. Form rubric: 4 = uses both hands, ball stays low and controlled, crossovers at cones, eyes up much of the time, no lost balls; 3 = one lost ball or weak hand avoided; 2 = several losses; 1 = cannot complete.

## Health 4: Encouraging active families

**Prepare:** Poster paper and markers, outdoor equipment (balls, jump rope, chalk), the 3-week goal tracker. Prepare the Unit 5 test (see key).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking adults don't need activity. Adults need at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week plus muscle-strengthening; family activity helps everyone.
- Thinking an advocacy poster only needs a picture. It needs a clear message, accurate facts and a call to action.

### Day 1 (Monday): Family challenge
*Student:* Design a one-week family activity challenge with daily tasks (walk after dinner, dance party).

*How to teach:* Design a one-week family activity challenge with a daily task and a way to track (checklist on the fridge).

- **Q:** Give 2 family challenge ideas.
  **A:** E.g., a walk after dinner, a 10-minute dance party, a bike ride, a backyard relay.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Lead activity
*Student:* Lead the family in a 15-minute active game you choose.

*How to teach:* The child leads the family in a 15-minute active game, explaining rules and safety.

- **Q:** What makes a good activity leader?
  **A:** Clear rules, a safety reminder, encouraging everyone, and including all ages and abilities.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Advocacy poster
*Student:* Make a poster encouraging 60 minutes of play a day for kids in your neighborhood.

*How to teach:* Make a poster encouraging 60 minutes of play a day with a slogan, 2 facts and a call to action.

- **Q:** What is a call to action?
  **A:** A clear statement telling the audience what to do, e.g., 'Play 60 minutes every day!'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Goal check
*Student:* Review your 3-week goal tracker and reflect on progress.

*How to teach:* Review the 3-week tracker and reflect: Did you meet the goal? What helped? What got in the way?

- **Q:** How do you know if you met your goal?
  **A:** Compare the tracker data to the measurable target in the goal.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit assessment
*Student:* Take the Unit 5 test on the benefits, types and recommended amount of physical activity and on goal setting.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test (see key).

- **Q:** Name the three types of physical activity kids need.
  **A:** Aerobic, muscle-strengthening and bone-strengthening.

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test and family challenge.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test key: 1) Daily activity for kids — 60 minutes or more. 2) Three types — aerobic, muscle-strengthening, bone-strengthening. 3) Example of vigorous activity — running, soccer, jump rope, swimming laps. 4) Example of moderate activity — brisk walking, easy biking. 5) Two benefits for mental health — better mood, less stress, better sleep/focus. 6) One barrier and a solution — e.g., rain → indoor dance. 7) What does SMART stand for — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. 8) Rewrite 'be more active' as SMART — any goal with activity, amount, frequency and time frame. Proficient = 6 of 8; plus family challenge completed and tracked.
