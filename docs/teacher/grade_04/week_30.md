# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 30

## ELA 4: Research questions and sources

**Prepare:** Library access and computer. Sample credible sources: nps.gov pages for Yosemite, Death Valley, Redwood National and State Parks; Tahoe info from state agencies or university sites. Index cards.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking anything online is true. Check author, date and purpose; prefer .gov, .edu and museums.
- Writing questions with yes/no answers. Research questions start with how, why, what.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose a wonder
*Student:* Choose a California natural wonder (Yosemite, Death Valley, redwoods, Lake Tahoe) and write research questions.

*How to teach:* The child picks a natural wonder and writes 5-6 research questions.

- **Q:** Turn 'Is Death Valley hot?' into a research question.
  **A:** 'Why is Death Valley so hot, and how hot has it gotten?' (Record 134°F in 1913.)

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Credible sources
*Student:* Evaluate 4 websites about your topic (.gov, .edu, a museum, a personal blog) for author, date and purpose, and rate each.

*How to teach:* The child evaluates 4 websites for author, date and purpose and rates each.

- **Q:** Why is a personal blog less reliable than nps.gov?
  **A:** The blog may not have an expert author or fact-checking; the National Park Service is an official agency with experts.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Gather
*Student:* Find 3 sources including one with maps or charts.

*How to teach:* The child finds 3 sources, including one with maps or charts.

- **Q:** Why include a source with maps?
  **A:** It shows location and data that text alone can't, and can be cited or adapted for the report.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Notes
*Student:* Take paraphrased notes on note cards with source info.

*How to teach:* Paraphrased notes on note cards with source info.

- **Q:** Paraphrase: 'Coast redwoods are the tallest trees on Earth, reaching over 360 feet.'
  **A:** Example: 'The tallest trees in the world are coast redwoods; some grow taller than 360 feet.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Check sources and notes with a parent and fix gaps.

*How to teach:* Check sources and notes with your child; fill gaps.

- **Q:** What do you do if a question has no notes?
  **A:** Find another source or revise the question.

**Assessment:** Research questions and note check.

**Answer key:** Check (4-point): 4 = 5+ open-ended research questions, 3 credible sources recorded (title, author/organization, site), 15+ paraphrased notes linked to questions; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = copied notes or weak sources; 1 = incomplete.

## Math 4: Measurement word problems

**Prepare:** Number line templates and an analog clock. Prepare 6 elapsed-time problems and mixed-unit problems (samples below).

**Watch for:**
- Subtracting times like regular numbers (12:20 - 9:45 = 2:75). Use a number line jumping to the next hour.
- Regrouping 1 foot as 10 inches. 1 ft = 12 in, 1 hr = 60 min; regroup using the right number.

### Day 1 (Monday): Elapsed time
*Student:* Solve 6 elapsed time problems with number lines (e.g., a train from Sacramento leaves 9:45 and arrives 12:20).

*How to teach:* Draw a number line from 9:45: jump 15 min to 10:00, 2 hours to 12:00, 20 min to 12:20, and add the jumps. The child solves 6 problems.

- **Q:** A train leaves Sacramento at 9:45 and arrives at 12:20. How long is the trip?
  **A:** 2 hours 35 minutes.
- **Q:** A movie starts at 1:50 and lasts 1 hour 45 minutes. When does it end?
  **A:** 3:35.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mixed units
*Student:* Solve 5 problems with mixed units like 3 ft 4 in minus 1 ft 10 in.

*How to teach:* Model 3 ft 4 in - 1 ft 10 in by regrouping 1 ft as 12 in (2 ft 16 in - 1 ft 10 in). The child solves 5 problems.

- **Q:** 3 ft 4 in - 1 ft 10 in = ?
  **A:** 1 ft 6 in.
- **Q:** 2 hr 40 min + 1 hr 35 min = ?
  **A:** 4 hr 15 min.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fractions of units
*Student:* Solve problems like 3/4 of an hour in minutes and 1/2 kg in grams.

*How to teach:* Use the clock to show 3/4 of an hour and a scale or table to show 1/2 kg. The child solves several fractions-of-units problems.

- **Q:** How many minutes are in 3/4 of an hour?
  **A:** 45 minutes.
- **Q:** How many grams are in 1/2 kg?
  **A:** 500 g.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Planning a day
*Student:* Make a timed schedule for a weekend trip using elapsed time and distances.

*How to teach:* The child plans a weekend schedule with start/end times and drive times, checking each elapsed time.

- **Q:** You leave at 8:30 a.m. and drive 2 hours 50 minutes. When do you arrive?
  **A:** 11:20 a.m.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on measurement word problems.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz independently. Correct together.

- **Q:** 5 yd 1 ft - 2 yd 2 ft = ?
  **A:** 2 yd 2 ft.

**Assessment:** Measurement problem quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) 9:45 to 12:20 = 2 hr 35 min 2) Start 1:50, 1 hr 45 min later = 3:35 3) 3 ft 4 in - 1 ft 10 in = 1 ft 6 in 4) 2 hr 40 min + 1 hr 35 min = 4 hr 15 min 5) 3/4 hr = 45 min 6) 1/2 kg = 500 g 7) 2 L - 750 mL = 1,250 mL 8) 5 yd 1 ft - 2 yd 2 ft = 2 yd 2 ft. Proficient: 7/8.

## Science 4: Environmental effects of energy use

**Prepare:** Pan of water, vegetable oil, feathers, cotton balls, sponges, spoons, dish soap; chocolate chip cookies and toothpicks; index cards, petroleum jelly, magnifying glass.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking renewable sources have no environmental effects. Wind farms affect birds, dams change rivers and fish habitat, solar farms use land.
- Thinking oil spills are easy to clean. Even with tools, much oil remains.

### Day 1 (Monday): Oil spill
*Student:* Simulate an oil spill and test cleanup methods.

*How to teach:* Make an 'ocean' with a small oil spill; test cotton, sponge, spoon and soap.

- **Q:** What happened to the feather dipped in oil?
  **A:** It became matted and heavy — birds with oily feathers can't fly or stay warm.
- **Q:** Which cleanup worked best?
  **A:** Answer from data; usually absorbents (cotton/sponge) collect surface oil, but none removed all of it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mining
*Student:* Model mining by extracting chocolate chips from a cookie with toothpicks and observe the damage to the 'land'.

*How to teach:* Extract chips from a cookie with toothpicks; observe damage.

- **Q:** What does the broken cookie model?
  **A:** Mining disturbs and damages land and habitats even when you carefully remove resources.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Air pollution
*Student:* Smear petroleum jelly on index cards, place them near a road and in the yard for 3 days, and compare particles collected.

*How to teach:* Place jelly-coated cards near a road and in the yard for 3 days; compare particles.

- **Q:** Which card collected more particles? Why?
  **A:** Usually the one near the road — car exhaust and dust add particles to the air.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Trade-offs
*Student:* Discuss trade-offs of 4 energy sources (cost, pollution, reliability, land use) and rank them.

*How to teach:* Rank 4 sources by cost, pollution, reliability and land use.

- **Q:** What is a trade-off of natural gas?
  **A:** It is reliable and available day or night, but burning it releases carbon dioxide and air pollution.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write about how one energy source affects the environment.

*How to teach:* The child writes how one energy source affects the environment.

- **Q:** Give an example of an environmental effect of a dam.
  **A:** It blocks salmon migration and floods valleys to make reservoirs.

**Assessment:** Environmental effects writing.

**Answer key:** Writing rubric: 4 = names one source, explains at least two environmental effects accurately (e.g., oil: spills, air pollution), connects to evidence from a model, mentions a way to reduce impacts; 3 = one effect explained; 2 = vague; 1 = incorrect.

## California, A Changing State: Growth of cities: Los Angeles and beyond

**Prepare:** Calisphere set 'Everyday Life and People in Late 19th Century California,' citrus crate label images, population data: San Francisco 1850 ~35,000 (1852 state census), 1870 149,473, 1890 298,997, 1910 416,912, 1920 506,676; Los Angeles 1850 1,610, 1870 5,728, 1890 50,395, 1910 319,198, 1920 576,673. Unit 9 test for Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Los Angeles was always California's biggest city. San Francisco was larger until Los Angeles passed it by 1920.
- Thinking crate labels are just art. They were advertising that sold California as a sunny paradise to attract settlers.

### Day 1 (Monday): LA boom
*Student:* Read about Los Angeles's 1880s land boom after the railroads arrived.

*How to teach:* Read about the 1880s land boom.

- **Q:** What caused the 1880s Los Angeles land boom?
  **A:** Railroads (Southern Pacific, then the Santa Fe) reached LA, and a fare war made tickets very cheap, bringing thousands of newcomers.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Citrus and oil
*Student:* Read about orange groves and the discovery of oil in Los Angeles; analyze citrus crate label art.

*How to teach:* Read about citrus and oil; analyze crate labels.

- **Q:** Who discovered oil in Los Angeles in 1892?
  **A:** Edward Doheny.
- **Q:** What image did crate labels give of California?
  **A:** Sunny, healthy, abundant land — advertising to attract buyers and settlers.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Everyday life
*Student:* Analyze images from the Calisphere set 'Everyday Life and People in Late 19th Century California'.

*How to teach:* Analyze everyday life images.

- **Q:** Name one change in everyday life by the 1890s.
  **A:** Electric streetcars, telephones, larger cities, new schools.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Graph growth
*Student:* Graph the populations of San Francisco and Los Angeles from 1850 to 1920 and compare.

*How to teach:* Graph SF and LA populations 1850-1920.

- **Q:** When did Los Angeles pass San Francisco in population?
  **A:** By 1920 (576,673 vs. 506,676).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 9 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 9 test on transportation, immigration and urban growth.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 9 test.

- **Q:** Name the Big Four.
  **A:** Stanford, Huntington, Hopkins, Crocker.

**Assessment:** Unit 9 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 9 test key: 1) Overland Mail ~25 days 2) Pony Express 1860-61, ~10 days 3) Telegraph 1861 ended the Pony Express 4) Big Four and Central Pacific 5) Chinese workers' role and unequal pay 6) Golden Spike May 10, 1869 at Promontory Summit 7) Chinese Exclusion Act 1882 8) Immigrant origins 9) LA boom after railroads 10) LA passed SF by 1920. Proficient: 80%.

## Art & Music 4: Taiko, gamelan and Asian American music

**Prepare:** Find videos of San Jose Taiko and Balinese gamelan. Gather buckets, drumsticks or wooden spoons, a pot and a shaker.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking taiko is just drumming. Taiko includes choreographed movement, posture and kiai (shouts).
- Thinking gamelan parts all play the same rhythm. Players interlock different patterns that fit together.

### Day 1 (Monday): Taiko
*Student:* Watch San Jose Taiko and describe movement and sound.

*How to teach:* Watch San Jose Taiko; describe movement and sound. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What makes taiko performance visual as well as musical?
  **A:** Big, coordinated arm movements, strong stances and shouts.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Bucket taiko
*Student:* Learn a 4-beat taiko pattern on a bucket with kiai shouts.

*How to teach:* Learn a 4-beat taiko pattern on a bucket with kiai shouts. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** What is a kiai?
  **A:** A shout used to show energy and keep the group together.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Gamelan
*Student:* Listen to Balinese gamelan and identify layered patterns.

*How to teach:* Listen to gamelan; identify layered patterns. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** What instruments are in a gamelan?
  **A:** Mostly metal gongs and metallophones (bronze keys), plus drums.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compose
*Student:* Create a layered rhythm piece with 3 repeating parts on bucket, pot and shaker and notate it.

*How to teach:* Create a 3-part layered rhythm on bucket, pot and shaker; notate it. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What is a polyrhythm?
  **A:** Two or more different rhythms played at the same time.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform the taiko piece and compare taiko and gamelan.

*How to teach:* Perform and compare taiko and gamelan. Set up a simple 'stage' and audience, have your child introduce the work, and hold applause until the end; afterward ask them to name one strength and one thing to improve before you add one specific comment.

- **Q:** Where does gamelan come from?
  **A:** Indonesia (Bali and Java).

**Assessment:** Taiko performance and comparison.

**Answer key:** Taiko performance: steady beat, strong movement, kiai at planned points. Comparison: 4 = two similarities and two differences (instruments, origin, layering); 3 = three; 2 = two; 1 = one.

## PE 4: Striking and volleying games

**Prepare:** Paddles (pickleball paddles or ping-pong paddles), a whiffle or pickleball ball, a net (rope or low net); draw a small court with chalk including a 'kitchen' (non-volley zone) line about 7 feet from the net (adjust for a smaller court). Score sheet; volleyball for Newcomb.

**Watch for:**
- Volleying (hitting before the bounce) in the kitchen in pickleball. You can't volley while standing in the non-volley zone.
- Thinking anyone can score on any rally in pickleball. Only the serving side scores a point.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pickleball basics
*Student:* Learn pickleball serve and scoring with family.

*How to teach:* Teach pickleball basics: underhand serve, contact below the waist, diagonally into the opposite service box; the serve and return must each bounce once (two-bounce rule); only the serving side scores; games to 11, win by 2; no volleys in the kitchen.

- **Q:** What is the two-bounce rule?
  **A:** The serve must bounce once before it's returned, and the return must bounce once before the serving team hits it; after that, volleys are allowed (outside the kitchen).
- **Q:** Who can score a point in pickleball?
  **A:** Only the serving side.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mini-tournament
*Student:* Play a family pickleball or paddle tournament.

*How to teach:* Play a family round-robin mini-tournament (short games to 7 or 11).

- **Q:** What is a round-robin?
  **A:** Each player or team plays every other player or team once.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Newcomb ball
*Student:* Play Newcomb ball (catch-and-throw volleyball).

*How to teach:* Newcomb ball: catch and throw over the net; each side may pass up to 3 times before throwing over; the point goes to the other side if the ball hits the floor.

- **Q:** How is Newcomb ball like volleyball?
  **A:** Same court, net and goal (land the ball on the other side), but you catch and throw instead of hitting.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sportsmanship
*Student:* Write 3 sentences describing how you showed sportsmanship in the tournament.

*How to teach:* Write 3 sentences about how you showed sportsmanship in the tournament.

- **Q:** Give an example sentence.
  **A:** E.g., 'I called my own ball out when it landed past the line.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit assessment
*Student:* Complete a striking and volleying skills test.

*How to teach:* Striking and volleying skills test (see key).

- **Q:** Where must the serve land in pickleball?
  **A:** In the diagonal service box beyond the kitchen line.

**Assessment:** Striking skills test.

**Answer key:** Striking and volleying skills test: 5 underhand serves into the diagonal box (proficient = 3+); 10 paddle strikes to a partner or wall (forehand/backhand mix, proficient = 7 controlled); 10 volleys (sets/bumps) to a partner (proficient = 7 controlled). Rules check: two-bounce rule and kitchen rule explained correctly. 4 = all proficient; 3 = 2 of 3 skills; 2 = 1; 1 = none.

## Health 4: Media influences on ATOD

**Prepare:** Parent preview: select 3 alcohol or tobacco ads from health-education collections (e.g., Stanford Tobacco Prevention Toolkit, historical ads at tobacco.stanford.edu) and one short TV or movie scene showing drinking or smoking that is appropriate for your child. Poster paper for the counter-ad.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking ads show what really happens. Ads show glamour, fun and popularity and leave out health risks, addiction and cost.
- Thinking influencer posts are just personal opinions. Many are paid (sponsored) posts; look for '#ad' or 'sponsored.'

### Day 1 (Monday): Ad analysis
*Student:* Analyze 3 alcohol or tobacco ads for target audience and hidden messages.

*How to teach:* For each ad, identify the target audience and hidden messages (e.g., 'you'll be popular,' 'it's relaxing,' 'adults are cool'). Note what's missing (health effects, addiction).

- **Q:** Who is the target audience of an ad with bright colors and cartoons?
  **A:** Young people.
- **Q:** What do alcohol and tobacco ads usually leave out?
  **A:** Health risks, addiction, cost and real-life consequences.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Movies and TV
*Student:* Notice how alcohol or smoking is portrayed in a TV show or movie scene and whether consequences are shown.

*How to teach:* Watch the scene. Note how drinking or smoking is shown and whether consequences appear.

- **Q:** Did the scene show any consequences? Why might media leave them out?
  **A:** Usually not; showing only the fun or glamorous side makes the product look appealing and makes the story simpler.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Counter-ad
*Student:* Create a counter-ad that shows the truth about a product.

*How to teach:* Create a counter-ad that uses ad techniques to show the truth (e.g., 'Want yellow teeth, bad breath and a habit that costs thousands of dollars a year?').

- **Q:** What makes a counter-ad effective?
  **A:** It grabs attention, uses true facts about the product's real effects, and targets the same audience.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Social media
*Student:* Discuss influencers and sponsored posts that promote vaping.

*How to teach:* Discuss influencers and sponsored posts promoting vaping or products. Learn to look for '#ad,' 'sponsored,' or 'paid partnership.'

- **Q:** What does '#ad' on a post mean?
  **A:** The influencer was paid or rewarded to promote the product.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a media literacy checklist for evaluating ads.

*How to teach:* Write a media literacy checklist of questions for evaluating ads.

- **Q:** Name 3 questions for your checklist.
  **A:** Who made this and why? Who is the target audience? What techniques are used? What is left out? Is it true? Is it paid?

**Assessment:** Counter-ad and checklist.

**Answer key:** Counter-ad and checklist rubric: 4 = counter-ad uses at least 2 attention techniques to present accurate facts about a real harm, targeted to a clear audience; checklist has at least 5 critical questions (who made it, purpose, audience, techniques, what's missing, sponsorship, accuracy); 3 = 3–4 questions or counter-ad with one fact; 2 = vague; 1 = incomplete.
