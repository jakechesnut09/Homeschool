# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 5

## ELA 4: Aesop's fables and morals

**Prepare:** Aesop's Fables (Gutenberg). Choose 6 fables: The Ant and the Grasshopper, The Fox and the Grapes, The Tortoise and the Hare, The Boy Who Cried Wolf, The Lion and the Mouse, The Milkmaid and Her Pail. Write 12 adage cards with meanings.

**Watch for:**
- Restating what happened instead of the moral. The moral is a lesson for people, e.g., 'Slow and steady wins the race.'
- Not noticing that suffixes change part of speech: hope (noun/verb) → hopeful (adjective) → hopefulness (noun).

### Day 1 (Monday): Fable reading
*Student:* Read 6 Aesop fables (e.g., 'The Ant and the Grasshopper', 'The Fox and the Grapes') and state each moral in your own words.

*How to teach:* Read the six fables aloud with the child. After each, the child states the moral in their own words.

- **Q:** What is the moral of 'The Fox and the Grapes'?
  **A:** People sometimes pretend they don't want what they can't have ('sour grapes').
- **Q:** What is the moral of 'The Lion and the Mouse'?
  **A:** Even small or weak friends can help; kindness is rewarded.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Adages & proverbs
*Student:* Match 12 adages ('Don't count your chickens...') to meanings and to the fable that fits.

*How to teach:* Explain that adages and proverbs are short sayings with a lesson. The child matches 12 adages to meanings and fables.

- **Q:** What does 'Don't count your chickens before they hatch' mean, and which fable fits?
  **A:** Don't plan on something before it happens — 'The Milkmaid and Her Pail.'
- **Q:** Which fable matches 'Slow and steady wins the race'?
  **A:** 'The Tortoise and the Hare.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Suffixes -ful, -less, -ness
*Student:* Build and define 15 words with suffixes; note how suffixes change part of speech.

*How to teach:* Teach -ful (full of), -less (without), -ness (state of). The child builds 15 words and labels the part of speech.

- **Q:** What does 'fearless' mean and what part of speech is it?
  **A:** Without fear; adjective.
- **Q:** Change 'kind' into a noun using a suffix.
  **A:** Kindness.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Discussion
*Student:* Discuss with a parent whether the grasshopper deserved help, citing the text.

*How to teach:* Discuss: Did the grasshopper deserve help? The child must cite what the grasshopper and ants said or did.

- **Q:** What did the grasshopper do all summer, according to the fable?
  **A:** He sang (played) instead of storing food, while the ants worked to gather food for winter.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write an original 1-paragraph fable with a moral; quiz on suffixes.

*How to teach:* The child writes a 1-paragraph original fable with animal characters and a stated moral; give the suffix quiz.

- **Q:** What are the parts of a fable?
  **A:** A short story, usually with animal characters who act like people, and a moral (lesson) at the end.

**Assessment:** Original fable and suffix quiz.

**Answer key:** Original fable rubric: 4 = animal characters with human traits, a clear problem and outcome, and a moral that follows from the events; 3 = moral present but weakly connected; 2 = story without clear moral; 1 = incomplete. Suffix quiz key (sample): hopeful = full of hope (adj.); careless = without care (adj.); darkness = state of being dark (noun); thankful = full of thanks (adj.); helpless = without help (adj.); sadness = state of being sad (noun). Proficient: 80%.

## Math 4: Addition with the standard algorithm

**Prepare:** Base-ten blocks and grid paper (one digit per box). Write the 10 regrouping problems and 5 road-trip problems ahead of time, or use the samples below.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting to add the regrouped 1 (carried ten) in the next column. Have the child write the small 1 at the top of the next column immediately.
- Misaligning places when numbers have different lengths (e.g., 4,567 + 892). Use grid paper and line up the ones column on the right.

### Day 1 (Monday): Partial sums to algorithm
*Student:* Add 4-digit numbers using partial sums, then the standard algorithm, and compare the two recordings.

*How to teach:* Model 3,456 + 2,738 with partial sums (thousands, hundreds, tens, ones), then with the standard algorithm side by side. Ask the child where the regrouped 1s in the algorithm appear in the partial sums.

- **Q:** Solve 3,456 + 2,738 with partial sums.
  **A:** 5,000 + 1,100 + 80 + 14 = 6,194.
- **Q:** Solve 2,519 + 1,364 with the standard algorithm.
  **A:** 3,883.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Regrouping across places
*Student:* Solve 10 addition problems with multiple regroupings up to 6 digits using grid paper to align places.

*How to teach:* The child solves 10 problems on grid paper, one digit per square. Watch the first two and have the child say 'regroup' aloud every time a column is 10 or more.

- **Q:** 487,659 + 236,478 = ?
  **A:** 724,137.
- **Q:** 5,698 + 4,507 = ?
  **A:** 10,205.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Estimate first
*Student:* Estimate each sum by rounding before computing and circle any answer that is not reasonable.

*How to teach:* Before each problem, the child rounds both addends and writes the estimate; after solving, compares the answer to the estimate and circles any answer that is far off.

- **Q:** Estimate 3,892 + 4,156, then solve.
  **A:** Estimate 4,000 + 4,000 = 8,000; exact 8,048 — reasonable.
- **Q:** A student got 6,084 for 2,975 + 4,109. Is that reasonable?
  **A:** No — estimate 3,000 + 4,000 = 7,000; correct answer is 7,084.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Word problems
*Student:* Solve 5 two-step addition word problems about miles traveled on a California road trip.

*How to teach:* Read each road-trip problem together and have the child underline the numbers and the question. They solve, then write the answer in a full sentence with 'miles.'

- **Q:** A family drove 382 miles, then 245 miles, then 96 miles. How far in all?
  **A:** 723 miles (382 + 245 = 627; 627 + 96 = 723).
- **Q:** Los Angeles to San Francisco is about 383 miles and San Francisco to Sacramento about 88 miles. How far is LA to Sacramento by that route?
  **A:** About 471 miles.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on multi-digit addition and estimation.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz independently; the child estimates first on each problem. Score together and redo any misses.

- **Q:** Estimate, then solve: 6,812 + 2,950.
  **A:** Estimate 7,000 + 3,000 = 10,000; exact 9,762.

**Assessment:** Addition quiz with estimation check.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) 4,567 + 3,288 = 7,855 2) 25,906 + 18,497 = 44,403 3) 308,745 + 92,368 = 401,113 4) 6,812 + 2,950 = 9,762 (estimate 10,000) 5) 12,345 + 67,890 = 80,235 6) 999 + 4,001 = 5,000 7) Estimate 48,700 + 21,300 to nearest thousand: 49,000 + 21,000 = 70,000 8) Is 5,214 a reasonable answer for 2,689 + 3,525? No; estimate 6,000, actual 6,214. Proficient = 7/8 with estimates written.

## Science 4: Sound energy

**Prepare:** Plastic wrap, a bowl, rice grains, a metal pot and spoon to bang, a shoebox, rubber bands of different thicknesses, 2 paper cups and 3-5 m of string, a tuning fork if available.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking sound travels best (or only) through air. Sound travels through solids and liquids too — often faster.
- Confusing loudness and pitch. Pitch is high/low (vibration speed); loudness depends on how big the vibrations are.

### Day 1 (Monday): Vibrations
*Student:* Stretch plastic wrap over a bowl, sprinkle rice and make sounds nearby to see the rice move.

*How to teach:* Stretch plastic wrap tightly over the bowl, sprinkle rice, and bang a pot nearby. The rice jumps.

- **Q:** Why does the rice move when you bang the pot nearby?
  **A:** The pot vibrates, making the air vibrate; those vibrations travel to the plastic wrap and make it (and the rice) vibrate.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rubber band guitar
*Student:* Build a shoebox rubber band guitar and test how tightness changes pitch.

*How to teach:* Build the guitar and test how tightness and thickness change pitch.

- **Q:** How does tightening a rubber band change the sound?
  **A:** It makes the pitch higher because the band vibrates faster.
- **Q:** Which makes a lower pitch: a thick or thin band?
  **A:** A thick band (vibrates more slowly).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sound travels
*Student:* Test how sound travels through air, water and a table by tapping and listening.

*How to teach:* Tap a table with your ear on it and then lifted; tap two stones underwater in a tub while listening with an ear to the side.

- **Q:** Did the tap sound louder through the table or through air?
  **A:** Through the table — sound travels well through solids.

### Day 4 (Thursday): String telephone
*Student:* Build a cup telephone and test string length and tightness.

*How to teach:* Build a cup telephone; test with string tight vs. loose.

- **Q:** Why must the string be tight?
  **A:** A tight string carries the vibrations from one cup to the other; a loose string absorbs them.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write how sound energy moved from source to ear in each investigation.

*How to teach:* The child writes how sound energy moved from source to ear in each investigation.

- **Q:** Describe the path of sound in the cup telephone.
  **A:** Voice vibrates the cup bottom → string vibrates → other cup bottom vibrates → air → ear.

**Assessment:** Sound energy explanation.

**Answer key:** Explanation rubric: 4 = for each investigation names the vibrating source, the material it traveled through (air, string, wood, water) and the ear, using the word 'vibration'; correctly states tighter/thinner = higher pitch; 3 = most investigations explained; 2 = vague; 1 = incorrect.

## California, A Changing State: California Indian nations and regions

**Prepare:** Map of California Indian language groups/tribal territories (e.g., from a California Indian Library collection or textbook), Calisphere set 'Native Americans: Pre-Columbian.' Homelands: Chumash — Santa Barbara coast and Channel Islands; Tongva — Los Angeles Basin; Ohlone — San Francisco Bay to Monterey; Miwok — Sierra foothills, Central Valley and Marin coast; Pomo — north coast and Clear Lake; Yurok — Klamath River and northwest coast; Mojave — Colorado River; Kumeyaay — San Diego area.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking California Indians were one group. Over 100 distinct nations spoke many languages.
- Talking about Native peoples only in the past tense. Many nations are thriving communities today.

### Day 1 (Monday): Map of nations
*Student:* Label a map with Chumash, Tongva, Ohlone, Miwok, Pomo, Yurok, Mojave and Kumeyaay homelands.

*How to teach:* Label the 8 homelands on the map.

- **Q:** Where were the Chumash homelands?
  **A:** The Santa Barbara coast and the Channel Islands.
- **Q:** Which nation lived along the Colorado River?
  **A:** The Mojave.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Living with the land
*Student:* Compare how coastal, valley, mountain and desert peoples obtained food and shelter in a chart.

*How to teach:* Chart how coastal, valley, mountain and desert peoples got food and shelter.

- **Q:** How did coastal peoples like the Chumash get food?
  **A:** Fishing, gathering shellfish, hunting sea mammals, and gathering acorns and seeds.
- **Q:** How did the Mojave farm in the desert?
  **A:** They planted corn, beans and squash in soil left by the Colorado River's floods.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Primary sources
*Student:* Examine images from the Calisphere set 'Native Americans: Pre-Columbian' and describe tools and dwellings.

*How to teach:* Examine Calisphere images; describe tools and dwellings.

- **Q:** Name a type of California Indian dwelling.
  **A:** Tule-reed or brush houses (Chumash domed houses of willow and tule), redwood plank houses (Yurok), bark houses (Miwok).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Acorns
*Student:* Read about acorn processing and sequence the steps from gathering to cooking.

*How to teach:* Read about acorn processing and sequence the steps.

- **Q:** List the steps of acorn processing.
  **A:** Gather acorns → dry and store → crack and shell → grind into flour in a mortar → leach with water to remove bitter tannins → cook as mush or bread (often with hot stones in baskets).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on how one nation adapted to its environment.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph on one nation's adaptation.

- **Q:** Example: How did the Yurok adapt to their environment?
  **A:** They lived along the Klamath River, fished for salmon, built redwood plank houses and dugout redwood canoes.

**Assessment:** Adaptation paragraph.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric: 4 = names one nation and its region, explains 2-3 specific adaptations (food, shelter, tools, clothing) linked to the environment; 3 = two adaptations; 2 = general; 1 = inaccurate.

## Art & Music 4: Steady beat, rhythm and meter

**Prepare:** Open Chrome Music Lab Rhythm. Get a bucket or pot to drum on and make 8 rhythm cards (quarter = 'ta', two eighths = 'ti-ti', half = 'ta-a', quarter rest = silent beat). Pick a march (e.g., Sousa) and a waltz (e.g., Strauss 'Blue Danube').

**Watch for:**
- Confusing beat and rhythm. The beat is steady like a heartbeat; the rhythm follows the words.
- Skipping rests. A rest is a silent beat — keep counting.

### Day 1 (Monday): Beat vs rhythm
*Student:* Pat the steady beat while saying the rhythm of 'Old MacDonald' and explain the difference.

*How to teach:* Pat the beat while singing 'Old MacDonald,' then clap the words' rhythm. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** What is the difference between beat and rhythm?
  **A:** Beat is the steady pulse; rhythm is the pattern of long and short sounds (like the words).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rhythm notation
*Student:* Read and clap quarter notes, eighth notes, half notes and rests from 8 rhythm cards.

*How to teach:* Read and clap 8 cards, saying 'ta' and 'ti-ti.' Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** How many beats is a half note?
  **A:** Two beats.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Meter
*Student:* Conduct 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 patterns to recordings of a march and a waltz.

*How to teach:* Conduct 2/4 (down-up), 3/4 (down-out-up) and 4/4 (down-in-out-up) to a march and a waltz. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** Which meter fits a waltz?
  **A:** 3/4.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Improvise
*Student:* Improvise 4-beat rhythms on a bucket drum in a call-and-response with a parent.

*How to teach:* Call-and-response: you play 4 beats, your child answers with improvised 4 beats. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** What is improvising?
  **A:** Making up music on the spot.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Clap 4 rhythm patterns and identify the meter of 3 songs.

*How to teach:* Clap 4 patterns; identify the meter of 3 songs. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** What does a bar line do?
  **A:** Divides music into measures with equal numbers of beats.

**Assessment:** Rhythm reading and meter check.

**Answer key:** Rhythm reading: 4 patterns clapped accurately with steady beat (4 = all correct; 3 = 3; 2 = 2; 1 = 0–1). Meter check: march = 2 or 4; waltz = 3; third song as chosen.

## PE 4: Catching at different levels

**Prepare:** Tennis balls, a playground ball, open space, a partner. Score sheet for the 20-catch test (high, middle, low).

**Watch for:**
- Catching with stiff arms. Reach, then 'give' by bending elbows to absorb the ball.
- Using the same hand position at every level. Above the waist: thumbs together; below: pinkies together.

### Day 1 (Monday): Hand position
*Student:* Practice thumbs-together catches high and pinkies-together catches low (30 tosses).

*How to teach:* Toss 30 balls at high and low levels; the child uses thumbs-together for high and pinkies-together for low, calling out the position.

- **Q:** A ball is coming at your shins. Hand position?
  **A:** Pinkies together, palms up.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Partner catch
*Student:* Play partner catch, stepping back after every 3 successful catches.

*How to teach:* Partner catch; step back after every 3 successful catches in a row. Record the farthest distance.

- **Q:** Why step back only after 3 in a row?
  **A:** To build success before making the task harder.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Moving catch
*Student:* Run a pattern and catch a ball tossed ahead of you.

*How to teach:* Run a pattern (straight then cut); partner tosses ahead. Catch on the move with hands, eyes on the ball.

- **Q:** Where should the passer aim for a moving catcher?
  **A:** Ahead of them, into the space they are running to.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Bounce catch
*Student:* Play a reaction-bounce game with a playground ball.

*How to teach:* Play a reaction-bounce game: partner bounces the ball at different angles and speeds; catch it after one bounce.

- **Q:** What helps you react faster?
  **A:** A ready position (knees bent, weight on balls of feet, hands ready) and watching the ball.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a 20-catch test at three levels and record the score.

*How to teach:* 20-catch test: 7 high, 7 middle, 6 low tosses from about 15 feet. Score clean catches with correct hand position.

- **Q:** You caught 17 of 20. What fraction is that?
  **A:** 17/20 (85%).

**Assessment:** Catching test score.

**Answer key:** Catching test score: 1 point per clean catch with correct hand position (20 max). 4 = 18–20; 3 = 14–17; 2 = 10–13; 1 = below 10.

## Health 4: Vitamins, minerals and water

**Prepare:** Measuring teaspoons, clear cups, sugar, and labels from a 12-oz soda, a juice drink and a 20-oz sports drink. Key fact: 4 grams of sugar = about 1 teaspoon. Use KidsHealth or MyPlate for vitamin/mineral research.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking juice drinks and sports drinks are healthy because they have fruit pictures or are for athletes. Many have as much sugar as soda; water is best for everyday thirst.
- Thinking you only need water when you feel thirsty. Drink water throughout the day, especially when active or hot.

### Day 1 (Monday): Vitamins and minerals
*Student:* Research one vitamin and one mineral (e.g., vitamin C, calcium) and their sources and jobs.

*How to teach:* Research one vitamin and one mineral. Example: vitamin C (citrus, strawberries, peppers; helps heal wounds and fight infection) and calcium (milk, yogurt, cheese, fortified soy milk, leafy greens; builds strong bones and teeth).

- **Q:** What does vitamin C do, and where do you get it?
  **A:** It helps heal cuts, keeps gums healthy and helps fight infections; found in oranges, strawberries, kiwi, bell peppers and broccoli.
- **Q:** What does calcium do?
  **A:** It builds strong bones and teeth (and helps muscles and nerves work).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Water's job
*Student:* Learn how water regulates temperature and track your water intake for one day.

*How to teach:* Explain water's jobs: cools the body through sweat, carries nutrients, removes waste, cushions joints. Track water cups for one day.

- **Q:** How does water help control body temperature?
  **A:** When you get hot you sweat; as sweat evaporates from the skin it cools you down.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sugar in drinks
*Student:* Measure the teaspoons of sugar in a soda, juice drink and sports drink using labels and compare.

*How to teach:* Read grams of sugar on each label and divide by 4 to get teaspoons. Measure that many teaspoons into clear cups.

- **Q:** A 12-oz soda has 39 g of sugar. About how many teaspoons is that?
  **A:** 39 ÷ 4 ≈ 9¾, about 10 teaspoons.
- **Q:** A sports drink has 34 g of sugar. About how many teaspoons?
  **A:** 34 ÷ 4 = 8½ teaspoons.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rethink your drink
*Student:* Make a chart of 6 healthier drink swaps (sparkling water with fruit instead of soda) with teaspoons of sugar saved.

*How to teach:* Make a chart of 6 drink swaps with teaspoons of sugar saved (e.g., soda 10 tsp → sparkling water with lemon 0 tsp = 10 tsp saved; chocolate milk → plain milk saves the added sugar).

- **Q:** If you swap a 39 g soda for water, how many teaspoons of sugar do you save?
  **A:** About 10 teaspoons (39 ÷ 4 ≈ 9.75).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write 3 reasons water is the best drink choice.

*How to teach:* The child writes 3 reasons water is the best drink choice.

- **Q:** Give 3 reasons water is the best drink.
  **A:** It has no sugar or calories, it is what the body needs to cool down and carry nutrients, it doesn't harm teeth, and it's usually free.

**Assessment:** Sugar-in-drinks chart and paragraph.

**Answer key:** Sugar-in-drinks chart: proficient chart lists each drink, grams of sugar from the label, and teaspoons calculated by dividing by 4 (e.g., 39 g soda ≈ 10 tsp; 34 g sports drink ≈ 8½ tsp; juice drink: label g ÷ 4), with sugar measured into cups. Paragraph rubric: 4 = 3 accurate reasons water is best plus evidence from the chart; 3 = 2 reasons; 2 = 1 reason; 1 = none.
