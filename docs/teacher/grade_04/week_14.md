# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 14

## ELA 4: Island of the Blue Dolphins, chapters 7-14

**Prepare:** Novel (chapters 7-14). Preview: Karana's brother Ramo is killed by wild dogs in this section — prepare to discuss grief.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing first-person narration with the author's voice. Karana is the narrator; Scott O'Dell is the author.
- Labeling every comparison as a simile. A simile uses 'like' or 'as'; a metaphor says one thing is another.

### Day 1 (Monday): Alone
*Student:* Read chapters 7-9 and chart Karana's actions and feelings after Ramo's death.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 7-9. The child charts Karana's actions and feelings after Ramo's death.

- **Q:** Why does Karana jump from the ship?
  **A:** She sees that her little brother Ramo has been left behind on the island and won't leave him.
- **Q:** What happens to Ramo, and how does Karana respond?
  **A:** He is killed by the wild dogs; she is grief-stricken, vows to kill the dogs, and burns the village.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Point of view
*Student:* Discuss how first-person narration affects what we know; rewrite a paragraph in third person.

*How to teach:* Discuss how first person limits us to Karana's thoughts. The child rewrites a paragraph in third person.

- **Q:** Change 'I walked to the cliff and looked at the sea' to third person.
  **A:** 'Karana walked to the cliff and looked at the sea.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Building a home
*Student:* Read chapters 10-12 and list the survival decisions Karana makes.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 10-12. The child lists survival decisions Karana makes.

- **Q:** Why is making weapons a hard decision for Karana?
  **A:** Her tribe's law forbids women to make weapons; she fears the consequences but decides she must to survive.
- **Q:** Name two survival decisions she makes.
  **A:** Examples: making a bow, arrows and a spear; trying to leave by canoe (and turning back when it leaks); building a house and a fence of whale ribs on the headland; storing food.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Figurative language
*Student:* Find 5 similes/metaphors in chapters 13-14 and explain them.

*How to teach:* The child finds 5 similes or metaphors in chapters 13-14 and explains each.

- **Q:** What is the difference between a simile and a metaphor?
  **A:** A simile compares using 'like' or 'as'; a metaphor compares by saying one thing is another.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on how Karana has changed so far, with two quotes.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph on how Karana has changed, using two quotations.

- **Q:** How has Karana changed so far?
  **A:** She has gone from depending on her people to being self-reliant and brave — breaking the weapons law, building her own shelter and providing for herself.

**Assessment:** Character change paragraph.

**Answer key:** Character change paragraph rubric: 4 = clear claim about change from start to now, two accurately quoted and cited passages with explanation, conclusion; 3 = claim with one strong quote; 2 = retelling with little analysis; 1 = off-topic. Expected content: Karana moves from relying on her family and village to independence, courage and skill.

## Math 4: Division with place value and area models

**Prepare:** Base-ten blocks, grid paper. Prepare 4 area-model problems, 8 partial-quotient problems and 6 check problems (samples below).

**Watch for:**
- In partial quotients, forgetting to add the partial quotients at the end (writing only the last one). Keep a column on the right and add it.
- Checking division by multiplying but forgetting to add the remainder. Check: quotient × divisor + remainder = dividend.

### Day 1 (Monday): Base-ten division
*Student:* Divide 156 / 3 by sharing base-ten blocks into 3 groups and trading as needed.

*How to teach:* Build 156 with 1 flat, 5 rods, 6 units and share into 3 groups; trade the flat for 10 rods when it can't be shared. The child records each trade.

- **Q:** 156 ÷ 3 = ?
  **A:** 52.
- **Q:** Why did you trade the hundred flat?
  **A:** One hundred can't be split into 3 equal groups of hundreds, so trade it for 10 tens (15 tens total) and share those.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Area model division
*Student:* Use an area model with an unknown side length to solve 4 problems like 432 / 4.

*How to teach:* Draw a rectangle labeled area 432 with one side 4 and find the other side in chunks (4 × 100 = 400, 4 × 8 = 32). The child solves 3 more.

- **Q:** 432 ÷ 4 using an area model.
  **A:** 100 + 8 = 108.
- **Q:** 655 ÷ 5 = ?
  **A:** 131 (500 + 150 + 5 → 100 + 30 + 1).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Partial quotients
*Student:* Solve 8 problems with partial quotients, subtracting friendly multiples of the divisor.

*How to teach:* Model subtracting friendly multiples (×100, ×10, ×5, ×2) and listing them to the side. The child solves 8 problems.

- **Q:** 584 ÷ 4 with partial quotients.
  **A:** 100 + 40 + 6 = 146.
- **Q:** 297 ÷ 5 = ?
  **A:** 59 R2.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Check with multiplication
*Student:* Check 6 quotients by multiplying and adding the remainder.

*How to teach:* The child multiplies each quotient by the divisor and adds the remainder to check 6 problems; mark any that don't match and fix them.

- **Q:** Check 925 ÷ 6 = 154 R1.
  **A:** 154 × 6 = 924; 924 + 1 = 925. Correct.
- **Q:** Is 389 ÷ 4 = 96 R5 correct?
  **A:** No — the remainder 5 is bigger than 4. Correct answer 97 R1.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on area-model and partial-quotient division.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz; the child chooses area model or partial quotients but must show work and check two answers.

- **Q:** 738 ÷ 6 = ?
  **A:** 123.

**Assessment:** Division strategies quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) 156 ÷ 3 = 52 2) 432 ÷ 4 = 108 3) 655 ÷ 5 = 131 4) 738 ÷ 6 = 123 5) 847 ÷ 7 = 121 6) 389 ÷ 4 = 97 R1 7) 503 ÷ 8 = 62 R7 8) 925 ÷ 6 = 154 R1 (check 154 × 6 + 1 = 925). Proficient: 7/8 with work shown.

## Science 4: Seeing: light and the eye

**Prepare:** A shoebox with a lid, a small object, a flashlight, a diagram of the eye (cornea, pupil, iris, lens, retina, optic nerve), a hand mirror.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking eyes send out rays to see. Light from a source reflects off objects and enters the eye.
- Thinking we can see in total darkness after our eyes adjust. Without any light, nothing can reflect into the eye.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dark box
*Student:* Look into a dark shoebox with an object, then open a small light hole and observe.

*How to teach:* Place an object in a closed shoebox; peek through a small eye hole — nothing is visible. Then open a light hole.

- **Q:** Why could you see the object only after opening the light hole?
  **A:** Light entered, reflected off the object and traveled into your eye.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Eye model
*Student:* Draw and label the eye and how light enters the pupil.

*How to teach:* Draw and label the eye: light passes through the cornea and pupil, the lens focuses it on the retina, the optic nerve carries signals to the brain.

- **Q:** What does the retina do?
  **A:** Senses the light and sends signals through the optic nerve to the brain.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pupil changes
*Student:* Observe pupil size in dim and bright light with a mirror.

*How to teach:* In dim light, the child looks in a mirror; then turn on a bright light and watch the pupils.

- **Q:** What happens to the pupil in bright light? Why?
  **A:** It gets smaller to let in less light and protect the eye; in dim light it gets larger.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Seeing model
*Student:* Draw a model of light from a lamp reflecting off a book into the eye.

*How to teach:* Draw the model: lamp → light ray to book → reflected ray to eye → brain.

- **Q:** Is the book a light source?
  **A:** No — it reflects light from the lamp.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain in writing how light allows us to see.

*How to teach:* The child explains in writing how light allows us to see.

- **Q:** Explain how we see a red apple.
  **A:** Light from a source hits the apple, which reflects red light into our eyes; the eye sends signals to the brain, which makes sense of the image.

**Assessment:** Seeing model and explanation.

**Answer key:** Rubric: 4 = model shows light from a source reflecting off an object into the eye with arrows in the correct direction, and the explanation mentions the brain; 3 = arrows correct, brain missing; 2 = arrows from eye to object; 1 = incomplete.

## California, A Changing State: Presidios and pueblos

**Prepare:** Map, Calisphere images. Facts: four presidios — San Diego (1769), Monterey (1770), San Francisco (1776), Santa Barbara (1782). Pueblos — San José (1777, first civilian town) and Los Angeles (1781). Los Angeles was founded by 44 pobladores (11 families) of Spanish, Native American and African ancestry.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all Spanish settlers were from Spain. Many pobladores were mestizo, Native and of African ancestry from New Spain (Mexico).
- Mixing up purposes. Missions = religion; presidios = military; pueblos = towns and farms.

### Day 1 (Monday): Presidios
*Student:* Read about the four presidios and their purpose.

*How to teach:* Read about the four presidios.

- **Q:** What was a presidio's purpose?
  **A:** A fort where soldiers protected Spain's claim and the missions.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pueblos
*Student:* Read about the founding of San José and Los Angeles.

*How to teach:* Read about San José and Los Angeles.

- **Q:** Why were pueblos founded?
  **A:** To grow food and raise livestock for the presidios and to bring civilian settlers.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Los Pobladores
*Student:* Read about the 44 settlers who founded Los Angeles in 1781 and their diverse backgrounds.

*How to teach:* Read about Los Pobladores.

- **Q:** Who founded Los Angeles in 1781?
  **A:** 44 settlers (11 families) from Sonora and Sinaloa of mixed Spanish, Native and African heritage.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compare
*Student:* Compare mission, presidio and pueblo in a chart.

*How to teach:* Compare mission, presidio and pueblo.

- **Q:** Who lived in each?
  **A:** Mission: priests and Native neophytes; presidio: soldiers and families; pueblo: civilian settler families.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz comparing the purposes, people and locations of missions, presidios and pueblos.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz.

- **Q:** Name the four presidios.
  **A:** San Diego, Monterey, San Francisco, Santa Barbara.

**Assessment:** Settlement quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) Mission purpose: convert Native people to Catholicism; farming 2) Presidio: military protection 3) Pueblo: civilian town to grow food 4) Four presidios: San Diego, Monterey, San Francisco, Santa Barbara 5) Pueblos: San José 1777, Los Angeles 1781 6) Los Angeles's 44 pobladores were of diverse backgrounds. Proficient: 5/6.

## Art & Music 4: Gold Rush songs

**Prepare:** Print lyrics of 'Oh My Darling, Clementine' and 'Sweet Betsy from Pike,' and a California/US map. Find recordings. Clementine on recorder in G uses low D, G, A, B, C and high D — teach two phrases at a time.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking ballads are just silly songs. Ballads tell stories and often show real conditions (hard travel, mining life), sometimes with humor.
- Thinking Gold Rush songs were written for school. They were sung by miners and travelers for entertainment and comfort.

### Day 1 (Monday): Clementine
*Student:* Sing 'Oh My Darling, Clementine' and discuss what the lyrics tell about a forty-niner's family.

*How to teach:* Sing 'Clementine'; discuss: a miner (forty-niner) lives with his daughter in a canyon while 'excavating for a mine.'

- **Q:** What does 'forty-niner' mean in the song?
  **A:** A person who came to California in the 1849 Gold Rush to look for gold.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sweet Betsy
*Student:* Sing 'Sweet Betsy from Pike' and trace Betsy and Ike's overland journey on a map.

*How to teach:* Sing 'Sweet Betsy from Pike'; trace her journey from Pike County, Missouri, across the plains, deserts and mountains to California.

- **Q:** What hardships did Betsy and Ike face?
  **A:** Long overland travel, crossing deserts and mountains, losing animals, hunger and tiredness (told with humor).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Play Clementine
*Student:* Learn the 'Clementine' melody on recorder in G, two phrases at a time.

*How to teach:* Learn the 'Clementine' melody on recorder two phrases at a time. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** Why learn a song two phrases at a time?
  **A:** Short chunks are easier to master before putting them together.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Context writing
*Student:* Write 3 sentences on what these ballads reveal about Gold Rush life and why people sang them.

*How to teach:* Write 3 sentences on what the ballads reveal about Gold Rush life. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** Why did people sing during the Gold Rush?
  **A:** For entertainment, comfort, to share stories and pass long hours of travel and work.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform one Gold Rush song (sung or played) and explain why you chose it.

*How to teach:* Perform one Gold Rush song and explain why you chose it. Set up a simple 'stage' and audience, have your child introduce the work, and hold applause until the end; afterward ask them to name one strength and one thing to improve before you add one specific comment.

- **Q:** What is a refrain?
  **A:** A part of a song that repeats, like 'Oh my darling, oh my darling.'

**Assessment:** Song performance and explanation.

**Answer key:** Performance: 4 = accurate melody and lyrics, steady beat, expressive; 3 = minor errors; 2 = several errors; 1 = incomplete. Explanation proficient if it links the song to Gold Rush history (miners, overland travel).

## PE 4: Double Dutch and partner jumping

**Prepare:** Two long ropes, a post or second turner, one long single rope for partner jumping. Paper for the routine plan.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking double Dutch ropes turn together. They turn in opposite directions, alternating like an eggbeater: when one rope is at the top, the other is at the bottom.
- Rushing the entry. Rock in rhythm with the back rope first, then enter.

### Day 1 (Monday): Partner jumping
*Student:* Jump side-by-side with a partner sharing one short rope, each turning one handle.

*How to teach:* Partners stand side by side, each holding one handle with the outside hand, and turn together while jumping in unison.

- **Q:** What is the key to partner jumping?
  **A:** Turning and jumping at the same time — count aloud together.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Double Dutch turning
*Student:* Practice 'eggbeater' turning of two ropes in alternating circles with a family member and a post.

*How to teach:* Eggbeater turning: two ropes turned inward toward the center, alternating so one hits the ground as the other is at the top. Practice saying 'right, left' with each hit.

- **Q:** In double Dutch, how do the two ropes move?
  **A:** In opposite, alternating circles — one up while the other is down.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Double Dutch entry
*Student:* Rock in time with the back rope, then enter and try 3 jumps; count best streak.

*How to teach:* Entry: stand by a turner, rock in rhythm with the back rope, enter as it hits the ground, and jump each rope (quick small jumps). Count best streak.

- **Q:** Why are double Dutch jumps quicker?
  **A:** Two ropes pass under per full cycle, so you jump twice as often.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Routine design
*Student:* With a partner, design a 4-part routine mixing partner jumps and single-rope tricks.

*How to teach:* Design a 4-part partner routine mixing partner jumps and single-rope tricks; write it down.

- **Q:** Write your 4-part routine.
  **A:** Answers vary; e.g., partner bounce ×10, skier ×8, solo criss-cross ×4, partner turn-around ×4.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform the partner routine and give each other one compliment and one tip.

*How to teach:* Perform; give one compliment and one tip to each other.

- **Q:** Give an example of a helpful tip.
  **A:** E.g., 'Count out loud so we start together.'

**Assessment:** Partner routine performance.

**Answer key:** Partner routine performance rubric: 4 = 4 planned parts performed in order, partners stay in rhythm, at most 2 misses, specific compliment and tip given; 3 = 4 parts with 3–4 misses; 2 = 2–3 parts; 1 = cannot complete. Double Dutch best streak recorded (any number).

## Health 4: Cooking a healthy recipe

**Prepare:** The week 13 meal plan and shopping list, a simple recipe, measuring cups and spoons, cutting board, a child-appropriate knife (adult supervision always), colander. Review hot-surface and knife safety first.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking you need soap to wash produce. Rinse fruits and vegetables under cool running water (rub or scrub firm produce); do not use soap or bleach.
- Holding food with flat fingers while cutting. Use the 'claw grip' — fingertips curled under, knuckles guiding the knife.

### Day 1 (Monday): Kitchen safety
*Student:* Learn kitchen safety rules (handwashing, knife safety with adult, hot surfaces).

*How to teach:* Teach kitchen safety: wash hands 20 seconds, tie back hair, adult supervises knives and stove, turn pot handles inward, use oven mitts, wipe spills right away.

- **Q:** Why turn pot handles toward the back of the stove?
  **A:** So no one bumps them and spills hot food.
- **Q:** How long should you wash hands with soap before cooking?
  **A:** At least 20 seconds.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Shop
*Student:* Help shop for the meal planned last week, reading labels to choose items.

*How to teach:* Shop for the meal (in person or online) and read labels to choose items (e.g., whole-grain tortillas, low-sodium beans).

- **Q:** Two cans of beans: 480 mg sodium vs. 140 mg sodium per serving. Which do you choose?
  **A:** The 140 mg (low-sodium) can.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Prep
*Student:* Wash produce under running water and, with adult supervision, practice safe cutting with a claw grip.

*How to teach:* Wash produce under running water. With supervision, practice the claw grip on soft foods (cucumber, banana).

- **Q:** What is the claw grip?
  **A:** Curling your fingertips under and holding food with your knuckles forward so the knife can't cut your fingertips.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cook
*Student:* Cook the planned dinner with an adult and measure ingredients.

*How to teach:* Cook the dinner with an adult, measuring ingredients carefully.

- **Q:** How do you measure 1 cup of flour accurately?
  **A:** Spoon it into the cup and level it off with a straight edge.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a reflection: what food groups were in the meal and how did it taste?

*How to teach:* Write a reflection on the meal's food groups and taste.

- **Q:** What food groups were in your meal?
  **A:** Answers must correctly list the groups in the cooked meal.

**Assessment:** Cooked meal and reflection.

**Answer key:** Cooked meal and reflection rubric: 4 = follows all safety rules (handwashing, claw grip, adult supervision), helps shop using labels, measures correctly, cooks the planned meal, and reflection lists the correct food groups and an honest taste review with one change for next time; 3 = minor reminders needed; 2 = several reminders or reflection incomplete; 1 = minimal participation.
