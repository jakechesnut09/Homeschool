# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 27

## ELA 4: Oz chapters 13-18

**Prepare:** Novel chapters 13-18 (13 The Rescue, 14 The Winged Monkeys, 15 The Discovery of Oz the Terrible, 16 The Magic Art of the Great Humbug, 17 How the Balloon Was Launched, 18 Away to the South).

**Watch for:**
- Quoting without explaining. Each quote needs a sentence connecting it to the theme.
- Thinking the Wizard's gifts were magic. The Wizard is a 'humbug'; the gifts work because the friends believe in them.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rescue
*Student:* Read chapters 13-14 and note each friend's contribution.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 13-14. The child notes each friend's contribution to the rescue.

- **Q:** How do the Winkies help the Tin Woodman and Scarecrow?
  **A:** They find and mend the battered Tin Woodman (tinsmiths) and restuff the Scarecrow with straw.
- **Q:** What does the Golden Cap do?
  **A:** Its wearer can command the Winged Monkeys three times.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): The Wizard revealed
*Student:* Read chapters 15-16 and discuss what the Wizard really is.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 15-16. Discuss who the Wizard really is.

- **Q:** Who is the Wizard of Oz really?
  **A:** An ordinary man from Omaha, a former balloonist/ventriloquist, whose balloon drifted to Oz; he is a 'humbug' who tricks people.
- **Q:** How does Toto expose him?
  **A:** Toto knocks over the screen in the corner, revealing the little old man.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Gifts
*Student:* Read chapters 17-18 and analyze whether the friends already had what they wanted.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 17-18. Analyze whether the friends already had what they wanted.

- **Q:** What does the Wizard give each friend?
  **A:** Scarecrow: brains of bran mixed with pins and needles; Tin Woodman: a silk heart stuffed with sawdust; Lion: a drink he calls courage.
- **Q:** Did they already have these qualities? Give evidence.
  **A:** Yes — the Scarecrow made clever plans, the Tin Woodman wept over hurting a beetle and cared for everyone, and the Lion faced danger to protect his friends.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Theme
*Student:* Write a paragraph stating a theme (e.g., 'what you seek may already be inside you') supported by two quotations.

*How to teach:* The child writes a theme paragraph with two quotations.

- **Q:** State a theme.
  **A:** 'What you seek may already be inside you' — the friends showed brains, heart and courage before receiving the Wizard's gifts.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Discuss the theme with a parent and revise the paragraph.

*How to teach:* Discuss the theme; the child revises the paragraph based on the conversation.

- **Q:** Why does the Wizard leave without Dorothy?
  **A:** The balloon's rope breaks/it floats away while Dorothy is chasing Toto, so she is left behind.

**Assessment:** Theme paragraph.

**Answer key:** Theme paragraph rubric: 4 = clear theme sentence, two accurately quoted examples from chapters 13-18 (or earlier) with explanation, concluding sentence; 3 = theme with one strong example; 2 = summary with little analysis; 1 = off-topic.

## Math 4: Comparing decimals

**Prepare:** Hundred grids, a meter stick marked in centimeters, a list of race times for Day 4 (e.g., Ana 12.45 s, Ben 12.5 s, Cal 12.09 s, Dee 12.4 s).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 0.38 > 0.4 because 38 > 4. Write 0.4 as 0.40 or shade both on grids.
- In race times, thinking the larger number wins. In races, the smallest time is fastest.

### Day 1 (Monday): Grid comparisons
*Student:* Shade grids to compare 0.4 and 0.38 and explain which is greater.

*How to teach:* Shade 0.4 on one grid and 0.38 on another and compare. Teach writing 0.4 as 0.40 to compare hundredths.

- **Q:** Which is greater, 0.4 or 0.38? Why?
  **A:** 0.4 — it is 40 hundredths, and 40 > 38.
- **Q:** Compare 0.7 and 0.71.
  **A:** 0.7 < 0.71 (70 hundredths < 71).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Meter stick decimals
*Student:* Measure 6 objects in meters to hundredths using a meter stick and order them.

*How to teach:* Measure a table: 1 meter and 23 centimeters = 1.23 m. The child measures 6 objects, writes meters with hundredths, and orders them.

- **Q:** An object is 85 cm long. Write it in meters.
  **A:** 0.85 m.
- **Q:** Order 1.23 m, 0.9 m, 1.3 m.
  **A:** 0.9 m, 1.23 m, 1.3 m.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compare and order
*Student:* Compare 12 decimal pairs and order 3 sets of decimals.

*How to teach:* The child compares 12 pairs and orders 3 sets, lining up decimal points.

- **Q:** Compare 2.06 and 2.6.
  **A:** 2.06 < 2.6 (6 hundredths < 60 hundredths).
- **Q:** Order 0.5, 0.05, 0.55.
  **A:** 0.05, 0.5, 0.55.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sports times
*Student:* Compare race times from a track meet (e.g., 12.45 vs 12.5 seconds) and rank runners.

*How to teach:* Rank the runners by time and discuss why the smallest time wins.

- **Q:** Who ran faster: 12.45 seconds or 12.5 seconds?
  **A:** 12.45 seconds — it is less time than 12.50.
- **Q:** Rank 12.45, 12.5, 12.09, 12.4 from fastest to slowest.
  **A:** 12.09, 12.4, 12.45, 12.5.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz comparing 10 decimal pairs to hundredths and explaining one with a grid model.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz; one comparison must be explained with a shaded grid.

- **Q:** Compare 0.6 and 0.59 with a grid model.
  **A:** 0.6 > 0.59: 60 squares shaded vs 59 squares.

**Assessment:** Comparing decimals quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) 0.4 > 0.38 2) 0.7 < 0.71 3) 0.25 < 0.3 4) 0.09 < 0.1 5) 0.5 = 0.50 6) 1.2 > 1.02 7) 0.83 > 0.8 8) 0.6 > 0.59 9) 3.45 < 3.5 10) 0.99 < 1.0. Grid explanation: proficient shows both grids correctly shaded and says which has more hundredths. Proficient: 8/10.

## Science 4: Other hazards: tsunamis, landslides, floods

**Prepare:** Tub, sand, water, craft sticks, clay, small plants or toothpicks, a NOAA tsunami page (tsunami.gov) and a photo of a California 'Tsunami Hazard Zone' sign. Unit 7 test for Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking engineering can eliminate hazards. Solutions reduce impacts; they can't stop natural events.
- Thinking a tsunami is one big wave. It is a series of waves; the first may not be the largest.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tsunami model
*Student:* Make a wave in a tub and test sea wall designs.

*How to teach:* Build a sand shore with toy houses; make a big wave with a board; then test a craft-stick sea wall.

- **Q:** Did the sea wall reduce damage? What are its trade-offs?
  **A:** Usually yes for small waves; trade-offs: cost, blocked beach access, and very large waves can still overtop it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Landslides
*Student:* Model a hillside and test plants/stakes to reduce slides.

*How to teach:* Model a hillside; pour water to cause a slide; then add plant roots/stakes and retest.

- **Q:** Why do plants reduce landslides?
  **A:** Roots hold soil together and absorb water.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Floods
*Student:* Build a clay or sand levee along a sand river in a pan and test how well it holds back a flood of poured water.

*How to teach:* Build a sand river and a clay/sand levee; pour a flood.

- **Q:** Which held better, clay or sand?
  **A:** Usually clay — it absorbs less water and resists erosion; use your data.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Warning systems
*Student:* Read about the NOAA tsunami warning system and tsunami evacuation signs on the California coast.

*How to teach:* Read about the NOAA tsunami warning system and evacuation signs.

- **Q:** If you feel a strong earthquake at the beach, what should you do?
  **A:** Move quickly inland or to high ground right away; don't wait for an official warning.
- **Q:** How do tsunami warnings work?
  **A:** Sensors (seismometers and deep-ocean buoys) detect earthquakes and wave changes; warning centers send alerts to coastal areas.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 assessment
*Student:* Take the Unit 7 test on reading maps of Earth's features and comparing solutions to natural hazards.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test.

- **Q:** On a topo map, what do close lines mean?
  **A:** Steep slope.

**Assessment:** Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 7 test key: 1) Contour lines and steepness 2) Trench and ridge definitions 3) Ring of Fire pattern 4) San Andreas Fault — plates slide past each other 5) Cross-bracing vs. base isolation compared 6) Drop, Cover, Hold On 7) Sea walls reduce wave damage but have trade-offs 8) Plants reduce landslides 9) Levees hold back floods 10) Warning systems save lives. Proficient: 80%.

## California, A Changing State: Pony Express, telegraph and mail

**Prepare:** U.S. map, Morse code chart, flashlight, stopwatch. Facts: Butterfield Overland Mail stagecoaches (1858-61) carried mail from Missouri to San Francisco in about 25 days; the Pony Express (April 1860-October 1861) carried mail about 1,900 miles from St. Joseph, Missouri to Sacramento in about 10 days, with riders changing horses every 10-15 miles; the transcontinental telegraph was completed October 24, 1861.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Pony Express lasted for decades. It lasted only about 18 months.
- Thinking one rider rode the whole way. Riders relayed the mail, each covering roughly 75-100 miles.

### Day 1 (Monday): Overland Mail
*Student:* Read about the Butterfield Overland Mail stagecoach route and its 25-day trip.

*How to teach:* Read about Butterfield Overland Mail.

- **Q:** How long did the stagecoach mail take?
  **A:** About 25 days.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pony Express
*Student:* Read about the 1860-61 Pony Express and calculate how long a letter took from Missouri to Sacramento.

*How to teach:* Read about the Pony Express; calculate travel time.

- **Q:** If the Pony Express covered about 1,900 miles in 10 days, about how far per day?
  **A:** About 190 miles per day.
- **Q:** Where did the route start and end?
  **A:** St. Joseph, Missouri to Sacramento, California.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Telegraph
*Student:* Read about the 1861 transcontinental telegraph and send a Morse code message with a flashlight.

*How to teach:* Read about the 1861 telegraph; send a Morse message.

- **Q:** How long did a telegraph message take?
  **A:** Minutes (almost instantly).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compare speeds
*Student:* Make a chart comparing message times by ship, stagecoach, Pony Express and telegraph.

*How to teach:* Chart message times by ship, stagecoach, Pony Express and telegraph.

- **Q:** Order the methods from slowest to fastest.
  **A:** Ship (weeks to months), stagecoach (about 25 days), Pony Express (about 10 days), telegraph (minutes).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on why the Pony Express ended after only 18 months.

*How to teach:* Write why the Pony Express ended.

- **Q:** Why did the Pony Express end?
  **A:** The transcontinental telegraph (October 1861) sent messages almost instantly, and the Pony Express lost money.

**Assessment:** Speed chart and paragraph.

**Answer key:** Speed chart: ship around Cape Horn months / via Panama weeks; stagecoach ~25 days; Pony Express ~10 days; telegraph minutes. Paragraph rubric: 4 = explains the telegraph's speed made it obsolete and that it was costly, with dates (1860-61, ~18 months); 3 = main reason only; 2 = vague; 1 = incorrect.

## Art & Music 4: Built environments: California architecture

**Prepare:** Find images of a California mission adobe (e.g., Mission Santa Barbara), a San Francisco Victorian ('Painted Ladies'), a Craftsman bungalow (e.g., Gamble House, Pasadena) and a mid-century modern house (e.g., Palm Springs). Plan a neighborhood walk with a camera and sketchbook.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking buildings are just boxes. Architecture uses form, materials and details to fit climate and culture.
- Thinking style is only decoration. Materials and shapes often respond to climate (thick adobe walls keep interiors cool).

### Day 1 (Monday): Styles
*Student:* Compare images of a mission adobe, a San Francisco Victorian, a Craftsman bungalow and a mid-century modern house.

*How to teach:* Compare the four styles. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** Why do adobe buildings have thick walls?
  **A:** Thick earth walls keep interiors cool in hot weather and warm at night.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Neighborhood walk
*Student:* Photograph and sketch 4 buildings in your neighborhood.

*How to teach:* Photograph and sketch 4 neighborhood buildings. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** What is a facade?
  **A:** The front face of a building.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Details
*Student:* Draw close-up details (windows, roofs, doors) of one building.

*How to teach:* Draw close-up details of one building. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** Name two architectural details you drew.
  **A:** Answer varies; e.g., arched windows, shingles, porch columns.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Design a building
*Student:* Design a building for your community and draw its facade.

*How to teach:* Design a building for your community and draw its facade. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** How can a design fit California's climate?
  **A:** Shade for sun, large windows for light, materials that stay cool, drought-tolerant landscaping.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & share
*Student:* Explain how your design fits California's climate and community.

*How to teach:* Explain how the design fits climate and community. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** What makes mid-century modern homes distinctive?
  **A:** Flat or low roofs, large glass walls and open plans connecting indoors and outdoors.

**Assessment:** Building design.

**Answer key:** Building design: 4 = detailed facade with clear features explained for climate and community; 3 = most; 2 = little explanation; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 4: Striking with paddles

**Prepare:** Paddle (ping-pong or paddleball), foam or tennis ball, a smooth wall, string between two chairs for a low net.

**Watch for:**
- Swinging with the wrist only. Swing from the shoulder with a firm wrist and turn the body.
- Facing the wall for a backhand. Turn the paddle-side shoulder toward the wall before swinging.

### Day 1 (Monday): Grip & bumps
*Student:* Learn the shake-hands grip and bump a ball upward 20 times.

*How to teach:* Shake-hands grip. Bump the ball upward 20 times with a flat paddle face.

- **Q:** Describe the shake-hands grip.
  **A:** Hold the handle like shaking hands, with the paddle face perpendicular to the ground.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Forehand
*Student:* Practice forehand strikes against a wall from a sideways stance.

*How to teach:* Forehand against a wall from a side stance (non-paddle shoulder toward the wall), swing low to high, follow through.

- **Q:** What are the forehand cues?
  **A:** Side to the target, swing low to high, follow through toward the target.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Backhand
*Student:* Practice 30 backhand strikes against a wall, turning the shoulder toward the wall before each swing.

*How to teach:* Backhand: turn so the paddle-side shoulder faces the wall, swing across the body from low to high with the back of the hand leading. 30 strikes.

- **Q:** For a right-hander's backhand, which shoulder points to the wall?
  **A:** The right shoulder.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rally
*Student:* Rally with a partner over a low net (string between chairs).

*How to teach:* Rally with a partner over the string net, counting consecutive hits.

- **Q:** Why should you return to a ready position between hits?
  **A:** So you can move quickly to the next ball.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Count your longest wall rally with forehand and backhand.

*How to teach:* Count the longest wall rally using forehand and backhand.

- **Q:** Tries: 12, 17, 15. Best?
  **A:** 17.

**Assessment:** Wall rally count.

**Answer key:** Wall rally count: record the longest rally (any mix of forehands and backhands). Benchmarks: 4 = 15+ with both strokes and correct side stance; 3 = 10–14; 2 = 5–9; 1 = fewer than 5.

## Health 4: Alcohol and the body

**Prepare:** Parent preview: alcohol unit. Body outline (trace the child on butcher paper or print one), a tape line on the floor. Clear the area for the spinning activity and spot the child; skip spinning if the child gets motion sick.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking alcohol is a stimulant because people may act silly. Alcohol is a depressant; it slows down the brain and body.
- Thinking alcohol only causes problems for adults who drink a lot. Even small amounts affect judgment and coordination, and drinking is especially harmful for young people.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is alcohol
*Student:* Learn what alcohol is and why it's illegal for people under 21.

*How to teach:* Explain alcohol (ethanol) is a drug found in beer, wine and liquor. It is a depressant that slows the brain. It's illegal for people under 21 to buy or drink alcohol, partly because the brain is still developing.

- **Q:** What kind of drug is alcohol?
  **A:** A depressant — it slows down the brain and body.
- **Q:** What is the legal drinking age in the U.S.?
  **A:** 21.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Short-term effects
*Student:* List short-term effects (slowed reaction time, poor balance, poor judgment).

*How to teach:* List short-term effects: slowed reaction time, poor balance and coordination, slurred speech, blurred vision, poor judgment, nausea/vomiting; too much can cause alcohol poisoning.

- **Q:** Name 3 short-term effects of alcohol.
  **A:** Any 3: slowed reactions, poor balance, slurred speech, blurred vision, poor judgment, vomiting, alcohol poisoning.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Coordination demo
*Student:* Walk a tape line while spinning first to feel how impaired balance makes tasks hard; discuss.

*How to teach:* Have the child walk the tape line normally, then spin 5 times (with spotting) and try again. Discuss how hard it was and connect to how alcohol impairs balance, reaction time and driving.

- **Q:** How is this activity like the effects of alcohol?
  **A:** Alcohol affects the cerebellum and makes balance and coordination poor, so simple tasks like walking a line become hard.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Long-term effects
*Student:* Learn long-term effects on liver, heart and brain.

*How to teach:* Learn long-term effects of heavy drinking: liver damage (fatty liver, cirrhosis), heart disease and high blood pressure, brain damage and memory problems, stomach irritation and ulcers, some cancers, addiction (alcohol use disorder).

- **Q:** Which organ breaks down (filters) alcohol?
  **A:** The liver.
- **Q:** Name a long-term effect on the heart.
  **A:** High blood pressure or heart disease (weakened heart muscle).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Label a body outline with 6 short- and long-term effects of alcohol on the brain, liver, heart and stomach.

*How to teach:* Label the body outline with 6 short- and long-term effects on the brain, liver, heart and stomach.

- **Q:** What effect does alcohol have on the stomach?
  **A:** It irritates the stomach lining, which can cause nausea, vomiting and ulcers.

**Assessment:** Body outline.

**Answer key:** Body outline key (6 accurate labels needed): Brain — slowed reactions, poor judgment and coordination (short-term); memory problems, brain damage, addiction (long-term). Liver — must break down alcohol; fatty liver, cirrhosis (long-term). Heart — high blood pressure, heart disease. Stomach — irritation, nausea, vomiting, ulcers. Also eyes — blurred vision; mouth — slurred speech. 4 = 6+ accurate, both short- and long-term included; 3 = 4–5; 2 = 2–3; 1 = 0–1.
