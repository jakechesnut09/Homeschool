# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 31

## ELA 4: Firsthand and secondhand accounts

**Prepare:** A John Muir excerpt from 'My First Summer in the Sierra' or 'The Yosemite' (Gutenberg) and an encyclopedia article on Yosemite. Muir (1838-1914) spent the summer of 1869 in the Sierra and helped inspire the creation of Yosemite National Park (1890).

**Watch for:**
- Confusing firsthand (written by someone who was there) with 'first person.' A secondhand account can quote people.
- Copying both texts instead of combining. Integrate: 'Muir describes..., and the encyclopedia adds...'

### Day 1 (Monday): Firsthand
*Student:* Read a John Muir excerpt about Yosemite and note what he saw and felt.

*How to teach:* Read the Muir excerpt. The child notes what he saw and felt.

- **Q:** How can you tell Muir's account is firsthand?
  **A:** He writes as 'I' about what he personally saw and felt in the mountains, with vivid emotional descriptions.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Secondhand
*Student:* Read an encyclopedia article on Yosemite and note its focus.

*How to teach:* Read the encyclopedia article. Note its focus (facts, dates, geography).

- **Q:** What is the focus of the encyclopedia article?
  **A:** Facts — location, size, geology (glaciers carved the valley), history and features like Half Dome and El Capitan.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compare
*Student:* Complete a chart comparing the two accounts' focus and information.

*How to teach:* Complete a chart comparing focus and information.

- **Q:** How are the two accounts different?
  **A:** Muir's is personal, emotional and descriptive; the encyclopedia is objective and gives facts and dates.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Integrate
*Student:* Write a paragraph integrating information from both.

*How to teach:* The child writes a paragraph integrating information from both.

- **Q:** Write an integrating sentence.
  **A:** Example: 'Muir describes Yosemite's waterfalls with wonder, and the encyclopedia explains that glaciers carved the valley that the waterfalls plunge into.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz identifying firsthand vs secondhand accounts.

*How to teach:* Give the firsthand vs. secondhand quiz.

- **Q:** A diary written by a gold miner in 1849 is ___.
  **A:** Firsthand.
- **Q:** A textbook chapter on the Gold Rush is ___.
  **A:** Secondhand.

**Assessment:** Comparison chart and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: firsthand — diary, letter, autobiography, interview with an eyewitness, photograph taken at the event; secondhand — encyclopedia, textbook, biography, news article written later by a non-witness. Chart rubric: 4 = notes the focus and type of information in each account and 2+ differences with examples; 3 = one difference; 2 = summary only; 1 = incomplete.

## Math 4: Area and perimeter

**Prepare:** Tape measure, grid paper. Formulas: area = length × width; perimeter = 2 × length + 2 × width. Unit 8 test for Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up area and perimeter. Perimeter is the distance around (a fence, in feet); area is the space inside (carpet, in square feet).
- Forgetting to label units, or using 'feet' for area. Area is in square units.

### Day 1 (Monday): Measure rooms
*Student:* Measure two rooms at home and compute area and perimeter with formulas.

*How to teach:* Measure one room together to the nearest foot and compute area and perimeter; the child measures a second room alone.

- **Q:** A room is 12 ft by 10 ft. Find the area and perimeter.
  **A:** Area 120 sq ft; perimeter 44 ft.
- **Q:** Which would you need to know to buy carpet?
  **A:** Area.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Unknown sides
*Student:* Find unknown side lengths given area or perimeter (10 problems).

*How to teach:* Model: area 48 sq ft and length 8 ft → 8 × w = 48, w = 6. The child solves 10 problems.

- **Q:** A rectangle has area 48 sq ft and length 8 ft. What is the width?
  **A:** 6 ft.
- **Q:** A rectangle has perimeter 30 ft and length 9 ft. What is the width?
  **A:** 6 ft (30 - 18 = 12; 12 ÷ 2 = 6).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Garden design
*Student:* Design a garden with 24 ft of fencing and find which rectangle gives the largest area.

*How to teach:* The child draws every whole-foot rectangle with perimeter 24 ft on grid paper, finds each area, and picks the largest.

- **Q:** Which rectangles have perimeter 24 ft and what are their areas?
  **A:** 1×11 = 11, 2×10 = 20, 3×9 = 27, 4×8 = 32, 5×7 = 35, 6×6 = 36 sq ft.
- **Q:** Which gives the largest garden?
  **A:** The 6 ft by 6 ft square, 36 sq ft.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit 8 review
*Student:* Mixed review of conversions, measurement problems, area and perimeter.

*How to teach:* Mixed review of conversions, measurement problems, area and perimeter.

- **Q:** How many inches are in 4 feet?
  **A:** 48 in.
- **Q:** A square has sides of 7 cm. Area and perimeter?
  **A:** Area 49 sq cm; perimeter 28 cm.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 8 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 8 test on conversions, measurement problems, area and perimeter.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test and collect the garden design as the task.

- **Q:** A patio is 15 ft by 6 ft. What is its area?
  **A:** 90 sq ft.

**Assessment:** Unit 8 test plus garden design task.

**Answer key:** Unit 8 test key: 1) 6 m = 600 cm 2) 5 lb = 80 oz 3) 3 hr = 180 min 4) 10:40 to 1:15 = 2 hr 35 min 5) 4 ft 2 in - 1 ft 8 in = 2 ft 6 in 6) 12 ft by 10 ft room: area 120 sq ft, perimeter 44 ft 7) Area 56 sq m, length 8 m: width 7 m 8) Perimeter 40 in, width 5 in: length 15 in 9) 1/4 hr = 15 min. Garden task rubric: 4 = lists all 6 whole-number rectangles with perimeter 24 ft, areas correct, identifies 6×6 (36 sq ft) as largest and explains; 3 = most rectangles with correct conclusion; 2 = some areas wrong or conclusion unsupported; 1 = incomplete.

## Science 4: Energy research and presentation

**Prepare:** Library books and websites (California Energy Commission, U.S. Energy Information Administration kids' site 'EIA Energy Kids'), poster board. Facts: Diablo Canyon is California's last operating nuclear plant (near San Luis Obispo); Altamont Pass has one of the earliest large U.S. wind farms; Hoover Dam on the Colorado River supplies hydroelectric power to Southern California; Kern County is California's main oil-producing county. Unit 8 test for Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Listing only pros or only cons. Every source has trade-offs.
- Copying text onto the poster. Use diagrams and short phrases.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose a source
*Student:* Choose one energy source used in California (Diablo Canyon nuclear, Altamont Pass wind, Central Valley solar, Hoover Dam hydro, oil from Kern County) and write 3 research questions.

*How to teach:* The child chooses one California energy source and writes 3 research questions.

- **Q:** Give a good research question.
  **A:** Example: 'How does Altamont Pass wind farm turn wind into electricity, and what are its effects on birds?'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Research notes
*Student:* Take notes from two sources on how the energy is made, where the resource comes from and its environmental effects.

*How to teach:* The child takes notes from two sources on how energy is made, resource origin and effects.

- **Q:** How does a nuclear plant make electricity?
  **A:** Splitting uranium atoms releases heat that boils water into steam, which spins turbines connected to generators.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Poster
*Student:* Create a poster with a labeled diagram of how the resource becomes electricity and a pros/cons chart.

*How to teach:* Create the poster: labeled diagram from resource to electricity and a pros/cons chart.

- **Q:** What steps are common to most power plants?
  **A:** An energy source turns a turbine; the turbine spins a generator that makes electricity sent over power lines.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Present
*Student:* Present the poster to family in 3 minutes and answer questions about trade-offs.

*How to teach:* The child presents in 3 minutes and answers trade-off questions.

- **Q:** What is a con of solar farms?
  **A:** They need a lot of land and produce power only when the sun shines.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 8 assessment
*Student:* Take the Unit 8 test on natural resources, renewable vs nonrenewable energy and environmental effects.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 8 test.

- **Q:** Is uranium renewable?
  **A:** No — it is a mined, nonrenewable resource.

**Assessment:** Poster presentation and Unit 8 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 8 test key: 1) Renewable vs. nonrenewable definitions 2) Classify: solar R, wind R, coal N, oil N, natural gas N, uranium N, hydro R 3) Fossil fuels formed from ancient organisms over millions of years 4) Turbines + generators make electricity 5) One environmental effect each of oil, dams, wind 6) Top California sources from the chart 7) Trade-off explained. Poster rubric: 4 = accurate diagram of resource → electricity, balanced pros/cons, clear 3-minute presentation, accurate answers; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = inaccurate or one-sided; 1 = incomplete.

## California, A Changing State: Water: dams, aqueducts and reservoirs

**Prepare:** California water project map (California Department of Water Resources), rainfall and population maps, Calisphere set 'Building Bridges, Dams and Power Plants.' Facts: most rain and snow fall in the north and the Sierra, while most people live in the south; William Mulholland's Los Angeles Aqueduct (1913) carried Owens Valley water about 230 miles, leading to conflict ('California Water Wars') as the valley dried; Hetch Hetchy (O'Shaughnessy Dam, 1923) supplies San Francisco despite John Muir's opposition; Shasta Dam (completed 1945) anchors the federal Central Valley Project; the State Water Project (Oroville Dam, California Aqueduct) carries water south.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking water is evenly spread. About two-thirds of precipitation falls in the northern part of the state, but most people live in the south.
- Thinking water projects had no downsides. They flooded valleys, harmed fish, and took water from other communities.

### Day 1 (Monday): Where water is
*Student:* Compare maps of where rain falls and where people live in California.

*How to teach:* Compare rainfall and population maps.

- **Q:** What problem do the maps show?
  **A:** Most water falls in the north and mountains, but most people and farms are in the south and Central Valley.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): LA Aqueduct
*Student:* Read about William Mulholland and the 1913 Los Angeles Aqueduct from Owens Valley, including the conflict with Owens Valley residents.

*How to teach:* Read about Mulholland and the LA Aqueduct.

- **Q:** Why were Owens Valley residents angry?
  **A:** Los Angeles bought land and water rights and diverted the river, drying up Owens Lake and harming farms; some residents dynamited the aqueduct.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Big projects
*Student:* Map Hetch Hetchy, Shasta Dam, the Central Valley Project and the State Water Project.

*How to teach:* Map Hetch Hetchy, Shasta Dam, CVP and SWP.

- **Q:** Which city gets water from Hetch Hetchy?
  **A:** San Francisco.
- **Q:** Why did John Muir oppose Hetch Hetchy Dam?
  **A:** It flooded a beautiful valley in Yosemite National Park.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Primary sources
*Student:* Analyze photos from the Calisphere set 'Building Bridges, Dams and Power Plants'.

*How to teach:* Analyze photos of dams and power plants.

- **Q:** Besides storing water, what do dams provide?
  **A:** Hydroelectric power and flood control.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draw a flow diagram showing water's path from Sierra snow to your faucet.

*How to teach:* Draw water's path from Sierra snow to your faucet.

- **Q:** Describe the path.
  **A:** Snow melts in the Sierra → flows into rivers → stored behind dams in reservoirs → carried by aqueducts/canals → treated at a plant → pipes → your faucet.

**Assessment:** Water flow diagram.

**Answer key:** Diagram rubric: 4 = labeled steps from snowpack, rivers, reservoir/dam, aqueduct, treatment, pipes to faucet, naming at least one real project; 3 = steps correct without real project; 2 = missing steps; 1 = incomplete.

## Art & Music 4: Jazz, blues and California sounds

**Prepare:** Find recordings of Duke Ellington's 'C-Jam Blues,' Dave Brubeck Quartet's 'Take Five' (1959, composed by Paul Desmond; Brubeck grew up in Concord, California), and Ella Fitzgerald scat singing (e.g., 'How High the Moon'). Find a free blues backing track in G.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking improvisation is random. Jazz musicians improvise using scales, rhythms and the song's form.
- Playing swung eighths evenly. Swing makes the first of each pair longer: 'doo-ba.'

### Day 1 (Monday): Blues form
*Student:* Listen to a 12-bar blues (e.g., 'C-Jam Blues') and clap on the first beat of each of the 12 bars.

*How to teach:* Listen to a 12-bar blues; clap on beat 1 of each bar, counting bars 1–12. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** How many bars are in one chorus of a 12-bar blues?
  **A:** Twelve.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Swing
*Student:* Practice swung eighth notes ('doo-ba doo-ba') on recorder on B, A and G.

*How to teach:* Practice swung eighths ('doo-ba doo-ba') on B, A and G. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** How are swung eighths different from straight eighths?
  **A:** Swung eighths are uneven — long-short; straight eighths are even.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Improvise
*Student:* Improvise with B, A, G over a blues backing track.

*How to teach:* Improvise with B, A, G over a blues backing track. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** What is improvisation in jazz?
  **A:** Making up melodies on the spot that fit the song's chords and rhythm.

### Day 4 (Thursday): California jazz
*Student:* Listen to Dave Brubeck's 'Take Five' and identify the 5/4 meter.

*How to teach:* Listen to 'Take Five'; count the 5 beats per measure (1-2-3, 4-5). Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** What meter is 'Take Five' in?
  **A:** 5/4 — five beats per measure.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Evaluate a jazz recording using criteria (steady groove, creative improvisation, expression) and write a short review.

*How to teach:* Evaluate a jazz recording with criteria; write a short review. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** What is scat singing?
  **A:** Improvised singing with nonsense syllables instead of words, like an instrument.

**Assessment:** Improvisation and evaluation.

**Answer key:** Improvisation: 4 = stays with the beat and blues form, uses swing rhythm and B-A-G creatively; 3 = mostly; 2 = loses beat; 1 = none. Review: addresses groove, improvisation and expression with specific examples.

## PE 4: Cooperative challenges

**Prepare:** Hula hoop, a rope or tape for the 'lava river' (10 feet wide), 3 paper plates, blindfold, cones. Gather 3+ family members. Timer.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the loudest person should lead every time. Good groups listen to every idea and share roles.
- Thinking frustration means failure. Frustration is normal; name it, take a breath and try a new plan.

### Day 1 (Monday): Hula hoop pass
*Student:* Pass a hula hoop around a circle of family members without letting go of hands; time three tries and improve.

*How to teach:* Hula hoop pass around a hand-holding circle without letting go; time 3 tries and discuss improvements.

- **Q:** What strategy improved your time?
  **A:** Answers vary; e.g., the next person preparing early and teammates helping guide the hoop.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Blindfold course
*Student:* Guide a blindfolded partner through a cone course using words only, then switch roles.

*How to teach:* Guide a blindfolded partner through cones using words only; switch roles.

- **Q:** What makes directions clear?
  **A:** Specific words: number of steps, direction (left/right from the walker's view), and 'stop.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Lava river
*Student:* Cross a 10-foot 'lava river' as a group using only 3 paper-plate stepping stones.

*How to teach:* Lava river: the group crosses 10 feet using only 3 paper plates as stepping stones (plates must always have a foot on them or they're lost).

- **Q:** Why must the group plan before starting?
  **A:** With only 3 plates, everyone needs to know the order and how to pass plates forward.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Debrief
*Student:* List the words and actions that helped the group succeed and one thing that caused frustration.

*How to teach:* Debrief: list words and actions that helped, and one thing that caused frustration and how to handle it next time.

- **Q:** Name an encouraging phrase that helped your team.
  **A:** E.g., 'Good idea!', 'We can do this,' 'Take your time.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a cooperation self-rating (listening, encouraging, sharing ideas) and set one improvement goal.

*How to teach:* Self-rate cooperation (listening, encouraging, sharing ideas) 1–4 and set one improvement goal.

- **Q:** Write a cooperation goal.
  **A:** E.g., 'Next time I will listen to everyone's idea before suggesting mine.'

**Assessment:** Cooperation self-rating and goal.

**Answer key:** Cooperation self-rating and goal: child rates listening, encouraging and sharing ideas 1–4 with an example for each and writes one specific improvement goal. Proficient = honest ratings matching parent observation, examples given, and a specific, actionable goal.

## Health 4: Peer pressure

**Prepare:** Make 10 scenario cards (e.g., 'Friends say "Just try it, everyone does"' = spoken negative; 'Everyone at the table is on their phone so you get yours out' = unspoken; 'Your friend says "Come run with me"' = spoken positive). Health journal.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking peer pressure is always spoken. Unspoken pressure — wanting to fit in because of what others are doing — is common too.
- Thinking peer pressure is always bad. Positive peer pressure encourages healthy choices like studying, trying out for a team or being kind.

### Day 1 (Monday): Types of pressure
*Student:* Learn spoken, unspoken, positive and negative peer pressure with examples.

*How to teach:* Teach 4 kinds: spoken (direct asking, teasing, daring), unspoken (seeing others do something and wanting to fit in), positive (encourages healthy choices), negative (encourages unhealthy or unsafe choices). Give examples of each.

- **Q:** What is unspoken peer pressure?
  **A:** Feeling you should do something because others are doing it, even though no one asked you.
- **Q:** Give an example of positive peer pressure.
  **A:** E.g., a friend encourages you to join a sports team, try a new healthy food, or study for a test.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Scenario sort
*Student:* Sort 10 scenario cards by type of pressure (spoken, unspoken, positive, negative) and explain two choices.

*How to teach:* Sort the 10 scenario cards by type and explain two choices.

- **Q:** 'Your friends all wear a brand and you feel left out.' Which type?
  **A:** Unspoken (negative if it pushes you to do something you don't want).
- **Q:** 'Come on, just one puff, don't be a baby.' Which type?
  **A:** Spoken, negative.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Good friends
*Student:* Make a list of qualities of friends who support healthy choices.

*How to teach:* List qualities of friends who support healthy choices (respect your no, encourage you, honest, kind, include you).

- **Q:** How does a true friend respond when you say no?
  **A:** They respect your decision and don't keep pushing or tease you.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Positive pressure
*Student:* Plan a way to use positive peer pressure to encourage a healthy habit.

*How to teach:* Plan a way to use positive peer pressure for a healthy habit (e.g., invite friends to a bike ride instead of video games).

- **Q:** How could you positively influence a friend?
  **A:** Model the healthy behavior and invite them to join, e.g., 'Let's play soccer at recess.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write about a time you resisted pressure or helped someone else.

*How to teach:* Write about a time you resisted pressure or helped someone else make a good choice.

- **Q:** What strategies helped you resist?
  **A:** Answers vary, e.g., saying no firmly, suggesting something else, leaving, being with supportive friends.

**Assessment:** Peer pressure reflection.

**Answer key:** Peer pressure reflection rubric: 4 = describes a real situation, identifies the type of pressure (spoken/unspoken, positive/negative), explains the strategy used and the outcome, and what they learned; 3 = describes the situation and strategy; 2 = vague or no strategy; 1 = incomplete.
