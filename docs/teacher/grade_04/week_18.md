# Teacher's Guide — 4th Grade, Week 18

## ELA 4: Poetry elements

**Prepare:** A Child's Garden of Verses (Stevenson): 'My Shadow' and 'The Land of Counterpane'; Carl Sandburg's 'Fog'; Robert Frost's 'Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening' (check copyright — it is in the public domain in the U.S. since 2019). Pick one short poem for memorization.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking every poem must rhyme. 'Fog' is free verse; rhyme is one tool among many.
- Confusing mood (the feeling the reader gets) with tone or with the poem's topic.

### Day 1 (Monday): Stevenson
*Student:* Read 'My Shadow' and 'The Land of Counterpane'; mark rhyme scheme and count beats.

*How to teach:* Read both Stevenson poems aloud. The child marks rhyme scheme with letters and taps the beats.

- **Q:** What is the rhyme scheme of 'My Shadow'?
  **A:** Rhyming couplets: AABB.
- **Q:** What is the 'land of counterpane'?
  **A:** The child's bedspread (counterpane) while sick in bed, where he plays with toy soldiers, ships and cities among the hills of the blankets.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Imagery
*Student:* Read Sandburg's 'Fog' and explain the extended metaphor.

*How to teach:* Read 'Fog' twice. Explain extended metaphor: the fog is compared to a cat throughout.

- **Q:** What is fog compared to and what details show it?
  **A:** A cat — it comes 'on little cat feet,' sits 'on silent haunches' looking over the harbor and city, and then moves on.
- **Q:** Is 'Fog' rhyming or free verse?
  **A:** Free verse — it doesn't rhyme or keep a steady beat.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Frost
*Student:* Read 'Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening' and discuss mood and repetition.

*How to teach:* Read Frost's poem and discuss the mood (quiet, peaceful, a little lonely) and the repeated last line.

- **Q:** What line repeats at the end and what might it mean?
  **A:** 'And miles to go before I sleep' — the speaker has duties and a long way to go (it may also suggest a long life ahead).
- **Q:** What does the horse think about stopping?
  **A:** The little horse 'must think it queer' to stop with no farmhouse near; he shakes his harness bells.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Write poems
*Student:* Write a metaphor poem and a rhyming quatrain about a California place.

*How to teach:* The child writes a metaphor poem and a rhyming quatrain (ABAB or AABB) about a California place.

- **Q:** What is a quatrain?
  **A:** A four-line stanza.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Memorize and recite a poem; quiz on poetry terms.

*How to teach:* The child recites a memorized poem with expression. Give the poetry terms quiz.

- **Q:** What is a stanza?
  **A:** A group of lines in a poem, like a paragraph in prose.

**Assessment:** Recitation and poetry terms quiz.

**Answer key:** Poetry terms quiz key: stanza = group of lines; rhyme scheme = pattern of end rhymes shown with letters; rhythm/meter = pattern of beats; free verse = poetry without regular rhyme or meter; simile = comparison with like/as; metaphor = direct comparison; imagery = words that appeal to the senses; repetition = repeating words/lines for effect; mood = feeling the poem creates. Recitation rubric: 4 = accurate, fluent, expressive, eye contact; 3 = 1-2 slips; 2 = several prompts needed; 1 = read from paper.

## Math 4: Simplifying and finding common denominators

**Prepare:** Fraction strips. Make 20 memory cards (10 pairs: e.g., 1/2 & 4/8, 2/3 & 8/12, 3/4 & 9/12, 1/5 & 2/10, 3/10 & 30/100, 2/6 & 1/3, 4/10 & 2/5, 6/8 & 3/4, 10/12 & 5/6, 50/100 & 1/2 drawn as a model).

**Watch for:**
- Dividing the numerator and denominator by different numbers. Find a common factor and divide both by it.
- Choosing a common denominator that isn't a multiple of both (rewriting 1/3 and 1/4 as tenths). List multiples of both denominators and find one in both lists.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dividing by common factors
*Student:* Use factor knowledge to rename 6/8, 9/12, 10/100 in simpler form and check with fraction strips.

*How to teach:* Model 6/8: both 6 and 8 have factor 2, so 6/8 = 3/4; check by laying fraction strips side by side. The child simplifies the rest and checks with strips.

- **Q:** Rename 6/8 and 9/12 in simplest form.
  **A:** 6/8 = 3/4 (÷2); 9/12 = 3/4 (÷3).
- **Q:** Rename 10/100 in simpler form.
  **A:** 1/10 (÷10).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Common denominators
*Student:* Rename pairs like 1/3 and 1/4 as twelfths using multiples.

*How to teach:* List multiples of 3 and 4 to find 12; rewrite 1/3 = 4/12 and 1/4 = 3/12. The child renames several pairs.

- **Q:** Rename 1/3 and 1/4 with denominator 12.
  **A:** 4/12 and 3/12.
- **Q:** Rename 3/4 and 5/8 with a common denominator.
  **A:** 6/8 and 5/8.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fraction match game
*Student:* Play a memory match game with 20 cards of equivalent fraction pairs and models.

*How to teach:* Lay cards face down and play memory; a match counts only if the child explains why the two are equal.

- **Q:** Is 4/10 equal to 2/5? How do you know?
  **A:** Yes — divide 4 and 10 by 2 to get 2/5.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Real-world equivalents
*Student:* Solve recipe problems: is 2/4 cup of flour the same as 1/2 cup? Explain with a drawing.

*How to teach:* Pose the flour question and have the child draw measuring cups. Then solve 2-3 more recipe equivalence problems.

- **Q:** Is 2/4 cup of flour the same as 1/2 cup?
  **A:** Yes — a cup split into 4 parts with 2 filled is the same amount as half the cup.
- **Q:** A recipe needs 3/4 cup of milk but you only have a 1/8 cup measure. How many scoops?
  **A:** 6 scoops (3/4 = 6/8).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on simplest form and common denominators.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz independently; allow strips for checking. Correct together.

- **Q:** Simplify 8/12.
  **A:** 2/3.

**Assessment:** Quiz on renaming fractions.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) 4/8 = 1/2 2) 6/9 = 2/3 3) 8/12 = 2/3 4) 20/100 = 1/5 5) 1/2 and 1/3 as sixths: 3/6 and 2/6 6) 3/4 and 5/8 as eighths: 6/8 and 5/8 7) 2/5 and 3/10 as tenths: 4/10 and 3/10 8) 6/10 = 3/5. Proficient: 7/8.

## Science 4: Animal internal structures

**Prepare:** Cleaned, boiled chicken bones (thigh, wing), a plastic bottle with bottom cut off, 2 small balloons, 1 large balloon, a straw, clay; a zip bag, crackers, water; a timer for pulse. Safety: wash hands after handling bones.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking lungs pull air in by themselves. The diaphragm muscle moves down to make room, and air rushes in.
- Thinking digestion only happens in the stomach. It starts in the mouth and continues in the intestines.

### Day 1 (Monday): Skeleton
*Student:* Examine cleaned chicken bones and compare to human bones.

*How to teach:* Compare chicken bones with a human skeleton diagram: hollow bird bones vs. our bones.

- **Q:** What are three jobs of the skeleton?
  **A:** Support the body, protect organs (skull protects brain, ribs protect heart/lungs), and help movement with muscles.
- **Q:** Why are bird bones light/hollow?
  **A:** Lighter bones help birds fly.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Lung model
*Student:* Build a lung model from a plastic bottle, two balloons and a straw and pull the diaphragm balloon to model breathing.

*How to teach:* Build the lung model; pull the bottom balloon (diaphragm) down and watch inner balloons inflate.

- **Q:** What happens when the diaphragm moves down?
  **A:** Space in the chest gets bigger and air rushes into the lungs.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Heart
*Student:* Measure pulse at rest and after exercise and explain the heart's job.

*How to teach:* Measure pulse for 15 seconds × 4 at rest and after 1 minute of jumping jacks.

- **Q:** Why does your heart beat faster after exercise?
  **A:** Muscles need more oxygen and energy, so the heart pumps blood faster to deliver it.
- **Q:** What is the heart's job?
  **A:** Pumping blood carrying oxygen and nutrients through the body.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Digestion
*Student:* Model digestion with a plastic bag, crackers and water.

*How to teach:* Mash crackers with water in a zip bag to model chewing and stomach churning.

- **Q:** What order does food travel through the digestive system?
  **A:** Mouth → esophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Label a diagram of internal structures and functions.

*How to teach:* The child labels a diagram of brain, heart, lungs, stomach, intestines, skeleton and states each function.

- **Q:** What is the brain's job?
  **A:** To control the body, process information from the senses and send signals for responses.

**Assessment:** Labeled diagram.

**Answer key:** Diagram key: brain — controls body and processes senses; heart — pumps blood; lungs — take in oxygen, release carbon dioxide; stomach — breaks down food; small intestine — absorbs nutrients; large intestine — absorbs water; skeleton — support and protection; muscles — movement. Proficient: 7/8 correct labels and functions.

## California, A Changing State: Life on the ranchos

**Prepare:** Calisphere set 'Californio Society,' biographies of Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo and Pío Pico, map. Unit 5 test for Day 5.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking cowboy culture started in Texas. Vaqueros in Mexico and California developed roping, riding and gear later used by American cowboys.
- Thinking rancho life was the same for everyone. Rancho owners lived well; Native workers did much of the hard labor.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rancho life
*Student:* Read about daily life on a rancho (hide and tallow trade, fiestas, household work) and list jobs of each family member.

*How to teach:* Read about rancho life; list jobs of each family member.

- **Q:** What were rancho products?
  **A:** Cattle hides and tallow (fat for candles and soap).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Vaqueros
*Student:* Read about vaqueros, many of them Native Californians, and their roping and riding skills that shaped the American cowboy.

*How to teach:* Read about vaqueros.

- **Q:** Name two vaquero contributions to American cowboy culture.
  **A:** The lariat/lasso (la reata), the rodeo, the saddle horn, chaps (chaparreras) and spurs.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Vallejo and Pico
*Student:* Read biographies of Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo and Pío Pico.

*How to teach:* Read biographies of Vallejo and Pío Pico.

- **Q:** Who was Pío Pico?
  **A:** The last governor of Mexican California, of mixed African, Native and Spanish ancestry.
- **Q:** Who was Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo?
  **A:** A Californio military commander at Sonoma with large ranchos; he later supported joining the U.S. and helped write California's first constitution.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Settlements map
*Student:* Map Mexican settlements, Fort Ross and Sutter's Fort.

*How to teach:* Map Mexican settlements, Fort Ross and Sutter's Fort.

- **Q:** Where was Sutter's Fort?
  **A:** In the Sacramento Valley, at present-day Sacramento (New Helvetia).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 5 test on Mexican independence, secularization and rancho life.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test.

- **Q:** What were hides called in the trade?
  **A:** 'California bank notes.'

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test key: 1) Mexico independent 1821 2) Foreign trade opened; hide and tallow trade 3) Secularization 1834 4) Diseño and land grants 5) Rancho products: hides and tallow 6) Vaqueros' skills shaped the cowboy 7) Vallejo — Sonoma commander, later helped write the constitution 8) Pío Pico — last Mexican governor 9) Sutter's Fort location. Proficient: 80%.

## Art & Music 4: Papel picado and Mexican folk art

**Prepare:** Gather tissue paper, scissors, string, glue stick; find images of papel picado, alebrijes and Talavera pottery.

**Watch for:**
- Cutting without folding. Folding first makes symmetrical designs.
- Thinking negative space is 'empty.' The cut-out holes are part of the design.

### Day 1 (Monday): Folk art tour
*Student:* Look at images of papel picado, alebrijes and Talavera pottery and discuss when and why they are made.

*How to teach:* Show images of papel picado (cut tissue banners), alebrijes (brightly painted fantasy creatures) and Talavera (tin-glazed pottery from Puebla). Your child describes colors and patterns and you discuss when each is made and used.

- **Q:** When is papel picado used?
  **A:** At celebrations such as Día de los Muertos, Christmas, weddings, birthdays and festivals.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Positive/negative space
*Student:* Practice folding and cutting symmetrical shapes and identify positive and negative space.

*How to teach:* Fold a square of scrap paper in half (then in quarters), cut small shapes along the folds, and open it. Your child points to positive space (paper left) and negative space (holes).

- **Q:** What is negative space in papel picado?
  **A:** The cut-out holes where the paper was removed.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Design
*Student:* Sketch a papel picado design with a theme (celebration, nature, family).

*How to teach:* Your child picks a theme (celebration, nature, family) and sketches a design on folded paper, shading the parts to cut out and keeping a solid border at the top for the string.

- **Q:** What is symmetry?
  **A:** When both halves of a design are mirror images.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cut
*Student:* Cut 3 tissue paper banners and string them together.

*How to teach:* Stack two sheets of tissue, fold and cut carefully, then unfold. Make 3 banners and glue each over a string along the top edge.

- **Q:** Why fold paper before cutting?
  **A:** To make symmetrical repeating shapes with one cut.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & share
*Student:* Hang the banner and explain its symbols and the tradition it comes from.

*How to teach:* Hang the banner and have your child explain its symbols and the tradition to a family member.

- **Q:** Where does papel picado come from?
  **A:** Mexico.

**Assessment:** Papel picado banner.

**Answer key:** Banner: 3 cut panels with symmetry, clear theme, balance of positive/negative space, neat cuts. Explanation: identifies Mexican origin and meaning of symbols.

## PE 4: Partner and group balance stunts

**Prepare:** Mat, family members to partner and spot. Discuss rules: stand only on hips and thighs (never the lower back), communicate before moving, come down slowly together.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking partner stunts are about one strong person. Both partners must communicate, lean together and keep their bodies tight.
- Climbing on the back or knees. Base supports on strong points (hips, thighs over the knees, shoulders over hands).

### Day 1 (Monday): Counterbalance
*Student:* Practice partner sit-ups-to-stand back-to-back and lean-away holds with hands joined.

*How to teach:* Practice back-to-back sit-to-stand (push backs together, walk feet in, rise together) and lean-away holds with hands joined.

- **Q:** Why must both partners push equally?
  **A:** If one pushes harder or lets go, both lose balance and fall.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Partner stunts
*Student:* Try partner balances such as the 'thigh stand' with a spotter, holding for 3 seconds.

*How to teach:* With a spotter, try a thigh stand: base in a deep squat, flyer steps onto the base's thighs near the hips, both lean back holding wrists. Hold 3 seconds and dismount forward.

- **Q:** Where does the flyer stand in a thigh stand?
  **A:** On the base's thighs close to the hips (never on the knees).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Strength builders
*Student:* Do planks, superman holds and wall sits to build the strength stunts need.

*How to teach:* Do planks (30 s), superman holds (20 s) and wall sits (30 s) ×2.

- **Q:** Which muscles does a superman hold strengthen?
  **A:** The back muscles (and glutes).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Group shape
*Student:* With 2-3 family members, create a group balance that has at least 4 points of contact with the floor.

*How to teach:* With 2–3 family members, design a group balance with at least 4 points of contact with the floor.

- **Q:** What does '4 points of contact' mean?
  **A:** Four body parts total (hands, feet, knees, etc.) touching the floor.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform 2 partner stunts and the group balance; discuss what communication made them work.

*How to teach:* Perform 2 partner stunts and the group balance; discuss what communication helped.

- **Q:** Give an example of good communication in a stunt.
  **A:** E.g., counting '1, 2, 3, up' together and saying 'down' before releasing.

**Assessment:** Group balance performance.

**Answer key:** Group balance performance rubric: 4 = 2 partner stunts held 3+ seconds with safe base points and controlled dismounts, group balance with 4+ points of contact held still, clear communication; 3 = all performed with a wobble; 2 = one stunt unsafe or not held; 1 = incomplete.

## Health 4: Tracking activity and screen time

**Prepare:** Print a 3-day activity log (activity, start/end time, minutes, light/moderate/vigorous) and screen-time log. Discuss the family's screen-time limits ahead. Timer.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking any movement counts as moderate or vigorous. Moderate makes you breathe harder (can talk but not sing); vigorous makes it hard to say more than a few words.
- Counting homework on a computer as recreational screen time. Log recreational screens (TV, games, videos) separately.

### Day 1 (Monday): Activity log
*Student:* Log activity minutes for 3 days, labeling each activity as light, moderate or vigorous.

*How to teach:* Teach intensity levels: light (slow walk, stretching), moderate (brisk walk, bike ride — can talk, not sing), vigorous (running, soccer, jump rope — hard to talk). Log activity for 3 days.

- **Q:** How can you tell if an activity is vigorous?
  **A:** You breathe hard and fast and can only say a few words without stopping for breath.
- **Q:** Is a brisk walk light, moderate or vigorous?
  **A:** Moderate.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Screen time log
*Student:* Log recreational screen time (TV, games, videos) for the same 3 days with start and end times.

*How to teach:* Log recreational screen time with start and end times for the same 3 days.

- **Q:** You watched TV from 3:45 to 5:15. How many minutes?
  **A:** 90 minutes (1 hour 30 minutes).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compare
*Student:* Compare activity to the 60-minute goal and screen time to family limits.

*How to teach:* Total each day's activity and compare to 60 minutes; total screen time and compare to family limits.

- **Q:** Day 1: 25 + 20 + 10 minutes of activity. Did you meet 60?
  **A:** No — 55 minutes, 5 minutes short.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Swap
*Student:* List 5 active alternatives to screen time that work indoors and outdoors and try one today.

*How to teach:* List 5 active alternatives to screen time (indoor and outdoor) and try one today.

- **Q:** Give 2 indoor active alternatives to screens.
  **A:** E.g., a dance party, an indoor obstacle course, a kids' yoga video, balloon volleyball or a jumping-jacks challenge.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph about patterns in your logs and one change you will make next week.

*How to teach:* Write a paragraph about patterns (e.g., more screens on weekends) and one specific change for next week.

- **Q:** What makes a good change statement?
  **A:** It is specific and measurable, e.g., 'I will play outside for 30 minutes before any screen time on school days.'

**Assessment:** Activity and screen time analysis.

**Answer key:** Activity and screen time analysis rubric: 4 = 3 complete days of both logs with correct minute totals and intensity labels; compares to the 60-minute goal and family limits; paragraph describes a pattern with evidence and one specific, measurable change; 3 = minor math errors or general change; 2 = logs incomplete; 1 = missing.
