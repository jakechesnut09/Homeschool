# Teacher's Guide — 7th Grade, Week 33

## English Language Arts 7: Comparing authors on one issue

**Prepare:** Two articles on lowering the voting age to 16 (pro/con — e.g., Newsela, ProCon); index cards for evidence.

**Watch for:**
- Treating a debate as a fight. Model respectful disagreement.
- Preparing only your side.

### Day 1 (Monday): Paired reading
*Student:* Read two articles on lowering the voting age to 16 and identify each author's claim and key evidence.

*How to teach:* Read both articles on lowering the voting age. Your child identifies each author's claim and key evidence. Check before moving on: ask "A common pro reason?" Look for: 16-year-olds work, pay taxes and are affected by laws.

- **Q:** A common pro reason?
  **A:** 16-year-olds work, pay taxes and are affected by laws.
- **Q:** A common con reason?
  **A:** Concerns about maturity and that most states set adult rights at 18.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Comparison
*Student:* Analyze how each author emphasizes different evidence and write a comparison paragraph.

*How to teach:* Your child analyzes how each author emphasizes different evidence and writes a comparison paragraph. Check before moving on: ask "How might authors emphasize different evidence?" Look for: One cites studies on teen civic knowledge; the other cites brain development research.

- **Q:** How might authors emphasize different evidence?
  **A:** One cites studies on teen civic knowledge; the other cites brain development research.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Debate prep
*Student:* Assigned a side, build a case with three reasons and evidence cards.

*How to teach:* Assign a side. Your child builds a case with three reasons and evidence cards. Check before moving on: ask "What goes on an evidence card?" Look for: Quote or fact, source, and how it supports the reason.

- **Q:** What goes on an evidence card?
  **A:** Quote or fact, source, and how it supports the reason.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Anticipating rebuttals
*Student:* List likely opposing arguments and draft rebuttals for each.

*How to teach:* Your child lists likely opposing arguments and drafts rebuttals for each. Check before moving on: ask "Why anticipate rebuttals?" Look for: To prepare responses and strengthen your case.

- **Q:** Why anticipate rebuttals?
  **A:** To prepare responses and strengthen your case.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write and rehearse a 2-minute opening statement.

*How to teach:* Your child writes and rehearses a 2-minute opening statement. Check before moving on: ask "What should an opening include?" Look for: Claim, roadmap of reasons, a hook.

- **Q:** What should an opening include?
  **A:** Claim, roadmap of reasons, a hook.

**Assessment:** Comparison paragraph and debate case.

**Answer key:** Comparison paragraph rubric: names both claims, contrasts evidence, explains effect. Debate case: three reasons with cited evidence, rebuttals for 2+ opposing arguments.

## Mathematics 7: Chance experiments and probability

**Prepare:** Coins, dice, paper bag with colored tiles (e.g., 5 red, 3 blue, 2 green).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking past results affect future independent events (gambler's fallacy).
- Expecting experimental results to exactly match theory in few trials.

### Day 1 (Monday): Likelihood
*Student:* Place 10 events on a 0-to-1 likelihood line from impossible to certain and justify placement.

*How to teach:* Your child places 10 events on a 0–1 line and justifies. Check before moving on: ask "Rolling a 7 on one die?" Look for: 0, impossible.

- **Q:** Rolling a 7 on one die?
  **A:** 0, impossible.
- **Q:** Flipping heads?
  **A:** 1/2.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sample spaces
*Student:* List sample spaces for rolling a die, flipping two coins and spinning a 4-color spinner.

*How to teach:* Your child lists sample spaces. Check before moving on: ask "Two coins?" Look for: HH, HT, TH, TT (4).

- **Q:** Two coins?
  **A:** HH, HT, TH, TT (4).
- **Q:** One die?
  **A:** 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Experimental probability
*Student:* Draw from a bag of colored tiles 50 times with replacement and compute relative frequencies.

*How to teach:* Your child draws 50 times with replacement and records relative frequencies. Check before moving on: ask "Red drawn 22 of 50 times. Relative frequency?" Look for: 0.44.

- **Q:** Red drawn 22 of 50 times. Relative frequency?
  **A:** 0.44.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Theory vs experiment
*Student:* Compare results to theoretical probability and explain how more trials bring them closer.

*How to teach:* Your child compares to theory (red = 5/10 = 0.5) and explains. Check before moving on: ask "Why do more trials help?" Look for: Experimental probability tends to get closer to theoretical (law of large numbers).

- **Q:** Why do more trials help?
  **A:** Experimental probability tends to get closer to theoretical (law of large numbers).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on probability language and models.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (see key). Check before moving on: ask "P(even on a die)?" Look for: 3/6 = 1/2.

- **Q:** P(even on a die)?
  **A:** 3/6 = 1/2.

**Assessment:** Probability quiz and experiment record.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) P(5 on a die) = 1/6. 2) P(blue) from 5R 3B 2G = 3/10. 3) Sample space of a 4-color spinner: 4 outcomes. 4) 'Likely' is between 0.5 and 1. 5) 30 heads in 50 flips: 0.6 experimental vs 0.5 theoretical.

## Science 7 (Integrated): Human population and resource consumption

**Prepare:** World population data (1800–today); online ecological footprint calculator; household water and electric bills.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking population has always grown fast. Growth was slow until the 1800s, then accelerated.
- Thinking one person's use doesn't matter. Multiply by millions.

### Day 1 (Monday): Population growth
*Student:* Graph world population from 1800 to today and describe the pattern.

*How to teach:* Your child graphs world population. Check before moving on: ask "Approximate values?" Look for: ~1 billion (1800), 2 billion (1927), 6 billion (1999), 8 billion (2022).

- **Q:** Approximate values?
  **A:** ~1 billion (1800), 2 billion (1927), 6 billion (1999), 8 billion (2022).
- **Q:** Shape?
  **A:** Slow then steep (exponential-like), recently slowing.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ecological footprint
*Student:* Use an ecological footprint calculator for the household and compare with world averages.

*How to teach:* Use a footprint calculator. Your child compares with world averages. Check before moving on: ask "What does a footprint measure?" Look for: Land/resources needed to support one person's lifestyle.

- **Q:** What does a footprint measure?
  **A:** Land/resources needed to support one person's lifestyle.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Household audit
*Student:* Audit household water and electricity use from bills for one month and calculate per-person use.

*How to teach:* Your child calculates per-person water and electricity use from bills. Check before moving on: ask "300 kWh per month for 4 people?" Look for: 75 kWh per person per month.

- **Q:** 300 kWh per month for 4 people?
  **A:** 75 kWh per person per month.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Argument
*Student:* Write an argument with evidence on how increased consumption affects one Earth system.

*How to teach:* Your child writes an argument on consumption affecting one Earth system. Check before moving on: ask "Example?" Look for: Increased energy use burns fossil fuels, adding CO2 to the atmosphere.

- **Q:** Example?
  **A:** Increased energy use burns fossil fuels, adding CO2 to the atmosphere.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on population and consumption impacts.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Check before moving on: ask "Why does population matter?" Look for: More people means more resource use.

- **Q:** Why does population matter?
  **A:** More people means more resource use.

**Assessment:** Household audit and argument.

**Answer key:** Household audit: per-person calculations correct. Argument rubric: claim, evidence, reasoning on Earth system impact. Quiz: population trend, footprint, consumption impacts.

## World History & Geography: Medieval and Early Modern Times: The Columbian Exchange and mercantilism

**Prepare:** Foods from the Columbian Exchange; Equiano excerpt (preview: describes the Middle Passage's brutality); trade simulation cards.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the exchange was equal. Disease devastated Indigenous Americans.
- Thinking mercantilism means free trade.

### Day 1 (Monday): Columbian Exchange
*Student:* Sort foods, animals and diseases into Old World and New World origins and cook a dish using both.

*How to teach:* Sort foods, animals and diseases. Check before moving on: ask "From the Americas?" Look for: Maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cacao, tobacco.

- **Q:** From the Americas?
  **A:** Maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cacao, tobacco.
- **Q:** To the Americas?
  **A:** Horses, cattle, wheat, sugar cane, smallpox.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Effects
*Student:* Analyze population data for the Americas after 1492 and explain the impact of disease.

*How to teach:* Analyze population data. Check before moving on: ask "Impact of disease?" Look for: Native population fell by as much as 90% in some areas.

- **Q:** Impact of disease?
  **A:** Native population fell by as much as 90% in some areas.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Atlantic slave trade
*Student:* Read Olaudah Equiano's narrative excerpt and describe the Middle Passage.

*How to teach:* Read Equiano. Check before moving on: ask "Middle Passage?" Look for: Voyage from Africa to Americas: crowded, chained, disease, high death rates.

- **Q:** Middle Passage?
  **A:** Voyage from Africa to Americas: crowded, chained, disease, high death rates.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mercantilism and markets
*Student:* Play a mercantilism trade simulation and explain joint-stock companies and market economies.

*How to teach:* Play the mercantilism simulation. Your child explains joint-stock companies and market economies. Check before moving on: ask "Mercantilism?" Look for: Colonies supply raw materials and buy finished goods to enrich the mother country.

- **Q:** Mercantilism?
  **A:** Colonies supply raw materials and buy finished goods to enrich the mother country.
- **Q:** Joint-stock company?
  **A:** Investors share costs and profits (Dutch East India Company).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on the most significant effect of the Columbian Exchange.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a paragraph on the most significant effect of the Columbian Exchange. Check before moving on: ask "Candidate answer?" Look for: Disease — largest population effect.

- **Q:** Candidate answer?
  **A:** Disease — largest population effect.

**Assessment:** Paragraph and simulation reflection.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric: 4 = clear topic sentence answering the prompt, at least two accurate specific facts or document references, explanation of how they support the claim, concluding sentence; 3 = accurate but thin explanation; 2 = general statements, few facts; 1 = off-topic or inaccurate. Simulation reflection: explains mercantilism and joint-stock companies accurately.

## Spanish 1A: Los deportes e ir a + infinitive

**Prepare:** Sports schedule; read about Real Madrid–Barcelona (El Clásico) or Boca Juniors–River Plate (Superclásico). Sports: el fútbol, el béisbol, el básquetbol, el vóleibol, el tenis, el fútbol americano, el golf, el hockey, la natación, el ciclismo, el atletismo, el boliche.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting 'a' in ir a + infinitive: 'Voy jugar.' Say Voy a jugar.
- Saying 'jugar fútbol' is common in Latin America, but jugar al fútbol is standard; accept both and note jugar a + el = al.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sports
*Student:* Learn 12 sports and jugar a and describe which sports family members play.

*How to teach:* Learn 12 sports with jugar a; describe who plays what.

- **Q:** Write: 'My sister plays soccer.'
  **A:** Mi hermana juega al fútbol.
- **Q:** Conjugate jugar for yo and nosotros.
  **A:** juego; jugamos.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ir a + infinitive
*Student:* Learn voy a jugar, vas a ver... and write 10 sentences about plans for next weekend.

*How to teach:* Teach ir a + infinitive; write 10 sentences about next weekend.

- **Q:** Write: 'I'm going to read a book this weekend.'
  **A:** Voy a leer un libro este fin de semana.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Culture: fútbol
*Student:* Read about a famous soccer rivalry (Real Madrid–Barcelona or Boca–River) and summarize it in five Spanish sentences.

*How to teach:* Read about a rivalry; summarize in five Spanish sentences.

- **Q:** What is 'El Clásico'?
  **A:** The famous soccer match between Real Madrid and FC Barcelona.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Plans calendar
*Student:* Make a two-week plans calendar and tell a parent your plans using time expressions.

*How to teach:* Make a two-week plans calendar; tell a parent your plans.

- **Q:** Say: 'Tomorrow I'm going to go to the park.'
  **A:** Mañana voy a ir al parque.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take an ir a + infinitive quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the ir a + infinitive quiz.

- **Q:** Translate: 'What are you going to do tonight?'
  **A:** ¿Qué vas a hacer esta noche?

**Assessment:** Ir a + infinitive quiz.

**Answer key:** Ir a + infinitive quiz samples: 'I'm going to play basketball' → Voy a jugar al básquetbol; 'We are going to watch the game tonight' → Vamos a ver el partido esta noche; 'Are you going to swim tomorrow?' → ¿Vas a nadar mañana?; 'They are going to visit grandma next weekend' → Van a visitar a la abuela el próximo fin de semana. 80% proficient.

## Visual & Performing Arts 7: Subtractive sculpture and finishing

**Prepare:** Bar of soap (Ivory carves well), plastic knife, paper clip/toothpick for detail, paint and sealer (Mod Podge or clear acrylic) for the clay piece.

**Watch for:**
- Carving details too early. Remove big chunks first, then refine.
- Carving thin projections that break. Keep forms compact.

### Day 1 (Monday): Plan
*Student:* Sketch a design for a soap carving from three views.

*How to teach:* Sketch the design from front, side and top.

- **Q:** Why sketch three views before carving?
  **A:** So you know how the form looks from all sides before removing material.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Carve
*Student:* Rough-carve the soap block with a plastic knife.

*How to teach:* Rough-carve the soap, removing big chunks.

- **Q:** Is soap carving additive or subtractive?
  **A:** Subtractive — material is removed.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Refine
*Student:* Refine details and smooth surfaces.

*How to teach:* Refine details and smooth surfaces.

- **Q:** Why can't you easily fix a mistake in subtractive sculpture?
  **A:** Material once removed can't be added back.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Finish clay piece
*Student:* Paint and seal the clay sculpture.

*How to teach:* Paint and seal the clay sculpture.

- **Q:** What is a patina?
  **A:** A surface finish or coloring, like the green on aged bronze, or a painted imitation.

### Day 5 (Friday): Artist statement
*Student:* Write an artist statement for one sculpture.

*How to teach:* Write an artist statement for one sculpture.

- **Q:** Compare your carving process to Michelangelo's.
  **A:** Both are subtractive — removing material to reveal the form; he carved marble with chisels, you carved soap with a plastic knife.

**Assessment:** Soap carving and finished clay sculpture.

**Answer key:** Soap carving rubric (1–4): planned from three views, recognizable compact form, smooth surfaces, details refined. Clay finish: painted thoughtfully, sealed. Artist statement: subject, method (additive vs subtractive), choices, challenges, connection to studied sculptors.

## Physical Education 7: Hiking and orienteering

**Prepare:** Baseplate compass, park map, water, daypack, snacks, sunscreen, first aid kit. Set up 6 orienteering checkpoints in yard/park with bearings and distances. Choose a 3–5 mile trail and check weather and trail conditions.

**Watch for:**
- Reading a bearing with the compass needle not boxed. Turn the bezel/your body until the red needle sits in the orienting arrow ('red in the shed').
- Taking shortcuts off trail. Stay on durable trails to protect plants and prevent erosion (Leave No Trace).

### Day 1 (Monday): Compass skills
*Student:* Learn to take a bearing with a compass in the yard.

*How to teach:* Teach: point the direction-of-travel arrow at a target, rotate the housing so the orienting arrow lines up under the red needle, read the bearing at the index line. To follow a bearing, set it, then turn your body until 'red is in the shed' and walk where the arrow points. Measure pace count (steps per 10 m).

- **Q:** What bearing is due east?
  **A:** 90°.
- **Q:** What bearing is due south?
  **A:** 180°.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Orienteering course
*Student:* Complete a homemade orienteering course with 6 checkpoints.

*How to teach:* Complete the 6-checkpoint course using bearings and pace counts.

- **Q:** Your pace count is 16 steps per 10 m. How many steps for 50 m?
  **A:** 80 steps.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Trail prep
*Student:* Learn hiking safety, Leave No Trace and pack a day bag.

*How to teach:* Teach hiking safety (tell someone your plan, stay on trail, carry the 10 essentials, water) and Leave No Trace; pack a day bag.

- **Q:** Name three Leave No Trace principles.
  **A:** Plan ahead; travel on durable surfaces; dispose of waste properly; leave what you find; minimize fire impacts; respect wildlife; be considerate of others.
- **Q:** Who has the right-of-way on a trail: hikers going uphill or downhill?
  **A:** Hikers going uphill.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Hike
*Student:* Complete a 3–5 mile hike at a local or state park.

*How to teach:* Complete the hike; note distance, elevation and how it felt.

- **Q:** Name two signs of dehydration on a hike.
  **A:** Thirst, headache, dizziness, dark urine, fatigue.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Journal on the hike's distance, elevation and how it felt.

*How to teach:* Journal the hike log.

- **Q:** What does elevation gain mean?
  **A:** The total vertical distance climbed during the hike.

**Assessment:** Orienteering completion and hike log.

**Answer key:** Orienteering: all 6 checkpoints found using bearings and paces. Hike log proficient: date, trail, distance, elevation gain, time, weather, how it felt, and one Leave No Trace action. Leave No Trace 7 principles: plan ahead and prepare; travel and camp on durable surfaces; dispose of waste properly; leave what you find; minimize campfire impacts; respect wildlife; be considerate of other visitors.

## Health Education 7: Health care and checkups

**Prepare:** Gather your child's immunization record and recent checkup info. Prepare a list of local clinics, pharmacies, public health department. Personal health record template (name, allergies, medications, vaccinations, doctor/dentist contacts, insurance).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking you only need a doctor when sick. Yearly well checkups and twice-yearly dental cleanings catch problems early.
- Being afraid to ask doctors questions. Doctors expect questions; it's your body and your right to understand.

### Day 1 (Monday): Checkups
*Student:* Learn what happens at medical, dental and vision checkups.

*How to teach:* Describe what happens at well checkups (height/weight, BP, vaccines, questions), dental (cleaning, X-rays, exam) and vision exams.

- **Q:** How often should teens see a dentist?
  **A:** Usually every 6 months.
- **Q:** Name two things a doctor checks at a well visit.
  **A:** Height and weight, blood pressure, vision/hearing, vaccines, heart and lungs, development, mental health.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Screenings
*Student:* Learn about scoliosis, vision and hearing screenings.

*How to teach:* Explain screenings: scoliosis (spine curvature, bending forward test), vision (eye chart), hearing (beeps at different pitches).

- **Q:** What is scoliosis?
  **A:** A sideways curve of the spine, often checked during growth spurts by having the person bend forward.
- **Q:** Why screen people who feel fine?
  **A:** Screening finds problems early, before symptoms, when they're easier to treat.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Community services
*Student:* Map local clinics, pharmacies and public health services.

*How to teach:* Map local health services on paper or online.

- **Q:** What can a pharmacist help you with?
  **A:** Explaining how to take medicines, side effects, interactions, choosing OTC products, some vaccines.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Advocating for yourself
*Student:* Practice asking a doctor questions about your health.

*How to teach:* Role-play a doctor visit; your child prepares 3 questions and practices describing symptoms (when, where, how bad).

- **Q:** Give one good question to ask your doctor.
  **A:** 'What does this medicine do, and what side effects should I watch for?' or 'Is my growth normal?'

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Create a personal health record.

*How to teach:* Your child completes the personal health record.

- **Q:** Why keep a record of allergies and medications?
  **A:** So any doctor or emergency responder can treat you safely.

**Assessment:** Personal health record.

**Answer key:** Personal health record: includes full name, birth date, emergency contacts, doctor/dentist names and phones, allergies, current medications, immunization dates, chronic conditions, and blood type if known. Proficient: complete and accurate, stored safely.
