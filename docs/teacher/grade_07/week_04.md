# Teacher's Guide — 7th Grade, Week 4

## English Language Arts 7: Personal narrative drafting

**Prepare:** Writing notebook, colored pens; reread 'Thank You, Ma'am' as a mentor text for dialogue and a quick, vivid opening.

**Watch for:**
- Summarizing the whole day instead of one moment. Have your child zoom in on a 10-minute 'seed' moment rather than a 'watermelon' topic like 'my vacation'.
- Rushing the climax in one sentence. Teach 'slow down the hot moment' with sensory details, internal thoughts and short sentences.
- Ending with 'and then I went home.' Ending should reflect on what the moment taught or changed.

### Day 1 (Monday): Finding a story
*Student:* Brainstorm a 'heart map' of memorable moments and choose one with a clear conflict or turning point.

*How to teach:* Model your own heart map (people, places, firsts, fears). Your child brainstorms at least 10 moments, circles three with a clear conflict or turning point, and tells you each in 1 minute; pick the one with the most feeling. Check before moving on: ask "Why is 'the day I froze on the high dive' better than 'summer camp'?" Look for: It is one focused moment with a conflict (fear) and a turning point, so it can be told in detail.

- **Q:** Why is 'the day I froze on the high dive' better than 'summer camp'?
  **A:** It is one focused moment with a conflict (fear) and a turning point, so it can be told in detail.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Planning
*Student:* Plan the narrative on a story mountain with exposition, rising action, climax and reflection.

*How to teach:* Draw a story mountain. Your child fills in exposition (who/where), 3+ rising-action events, the climax, falling action and reflection. Check before moving on: ask "Where does the reflection go and what does it do?" Look for: At the end; it explains what the narrator learned or how they changed.

- **Q:** Where does the reflection go and what does it do?
  **A:** At the end; it explains what the narrator learned or how they changed.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Drafting the opening
*Student:* Draft an opening that starts with dialogue, action or a vivid setting detail, trying two versions.

*How to teach:* Show how Hughes starts in the action ('She was a large woman with a large purse…' and the snatch happens at once). Your child drafts two openings (dialogue, action, or setting) and chooses the stronger. Check before moving on: ask "Rewrite 'I am going to tell you about my first race' as an action lead." Look for: Example: 'My spikes bit into the track as the starter raised the gun.'

- **Q:** Rewrite 'I am going to tell you about my first race' as an action lead.
  **A:** Example: 'My spikes bit into the track as the starter raised the gun.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Drafting the body
*Student:* Finish the draft (600–900 words), slowing down the climax with sensory details and internal thoughts.

*How to teach:* Your child finishes the draft (600–900 words). Check that the climax is at least a full paragraph with senses and thoughts. Check before moving on: ask "Name three ways to slow down a climax." Look for: Sensory details, internal thoughts/feelings, dialogue, and short sentences or fragments for pace.

- **Q:** Name three ways to slow down a climax.
  **A:** Sensory details, internal thoughts/feelings, dialogue, and short sentences or fragments for pace.

### Day 5 (Friday): Peer conference
*Student:* Read the draft aloud to a parent who gives two 'glows' and one 'grow'; record revision goals.

*How to teach:* Your child reads aloud; you give two specific glows ('Your dialogue sounds real') and one grow ('Show what you were thinking at the top'). Your child writes 2–3 revision goals. Check before moving on: ask "What makes feedback useful?" Look for: It is specific and points to a place in the draft, not just 'good job'.

- **Q:** What makes feedback useful?
  **A:** It is specific and points to a place in the draft, not just 'good job'.

**Assessment:** Complete narrative draft and revision goals.

**Answer key:** Completion check (not graded for polish): 600–900 words; identifiable exposition, conflict, climax and reflection; at least one dialogue or sensory passage; 2–3 written revision goals. Mark 'complete' only if all parts are present.

## Mathematics 7: Scale drawing project & unit test

**Prepare:** Poster paper, ruler, colored pencils, calculator. Let your child choose a dream classroom or park and a scale (suggest 1 cm = 1 m or 1 in = 5 ft).

**Watch for:**
- Halving the scale and assuming the drawing's area halves. Halving lengths makes area 1/4 as big.
- Computing area from drawing measurements without converting. Convert each side to actual size first, then multiply.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dream space design
*Student:* Design a dream classroom or park at a chosen scale, listing at least six objects with actual and scaled dimensions.

*How to teach:* Help your child choose a scale that fits the poster. They list at least six objects with actual and scaled dimensions in a table before drawing. Check before moving on: ask "At 1 cm = 2 m, a 14 m by 8 m pool is drawn how big?" Look for: 7 cm by 4 cm.

- **Q:** At 1 cm = 2 m, a 14 m by 8 m pool is drawn how big?
  **A:** 7 cm by 4 cm.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Actual area
*Student:* Compute actual area of each region in the design using the scale and record in a table.

*How to teach:* Model one region: convert drawing sides to actual sides, then multiply. Your child completes the area table for every region. Check before moving on: ask "A garden is drawn 3 cm by 5 cm at 1 cm = 2 m. Actual area?" Look for: 6 m × 10 m = 60 m².

- **Q:** A garden is drawn 3 cm by 5 cm at 1 cm = 2 m. Actual area?
  **A:** 6 m × 10 m = 60 m².

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Redraw at new scale
*Student:* Redraw one part of the design at half the original scale and compare the two drawings' areas.

*How to teach:* Your child redraws one part at half the scale (each length half as long on paper) and compares drawing areas. Check before moving on: ask "A drawing region is 8 cm by 6 cm (48 cm²). At half the scale, its drawn area?" Look for: 4 cm × 3 cm = 12 cm², one-fourth as much. The actual area is unchanged.

- **Q:** A drawing region is 8 cm by 6 cm (48 cm²). At half the scale, its drawn area?
  **A:** 4 cm × 3 cm = 12 cm², one-fourth as much. The actual area is unchanged.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit review
*Student:* Work a mixed review set of 12 problems on scaled copies, scale factor, maps and area.

*How to teach:* Work the first two review problems together, then your child does the rest independently. Mark and re-teach errors. Check before moving on: ask "Scale 1 in = 8 ft. A wall is 3.5 in on the drawing. Actual?" Look for: 28 ft.

- **Q:** Scale 1 in = 8 ft. A wall is 3.5 in on the drawing. Actual?
  **A:** 28 ft.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 test
*Student:* Take the unit 1 test and present the design poster aloud, explaining how the scale was used.

*How to teach:* Give the unit test (see key) and then have your child present the poster for 3 minutes, explaining the scale with one example. Check before moving on: ask "Why must a scale drawing include its scale?" Look for: Without it, no one can convert drawing lengths to actual lengths.

- **Q:** Why must a scale drawing include its scale?
  **A:** Without it, no one can convert drawing lengths to actual lengths.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test and scale design project.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test (sample items + answers): 1) Factor from 6 to 15: 2.5. 2) Copy of 4x10 at factor 0.5: 2x5. 3) Area 9 scaled by 4: 144. 4) 1 cm = 25 km, 7 cm: 175 km. 5) 1 in to 6 ft as unitless: 1:72. 6) 30 ft wall at 1 in = 5 ft: 6 in. 7) Angle 115° in copy: 115°. Project rubric: 4 = scale stated, six+ objects with correct actual/scaled dimensions, correct area table, clear explanation; 3 = minor errors; 2 = several errors; 1 = incomplete.

## Science 7 (Integrated): Properties of substances

**Prepare:** Kitchen scale (grams), measuring cup with mL, water, vegetable oil, corn syrup, grape, cork, coin; salt, sugar, baking soda, teaspoon; vinegar, iodine (tincture, adult handles); cornstarch, baking soda and sugar as 'mystery powders' in labeled cups A–C.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking heavy objects always sink. Sinking depends on density compared with the liquid.
- Forgetting to subtract the container's mass. Tare the scale or subtract.

### Day 1 (Monday): Density
*Student:* Measure mass and volume of water, oil and corn syrup and calculate density for each.

*How to teach:* Model density = mass ÷ volume with 100 mL of water (≈100 g → 1.0 g/mL). Your child measures oil and corn syrup and calculates. Check before moving on: ask "50 mL of oil has a mass of 46 g. Density?" Look for: 0.92 g/mL — less than water, so oil floats on water.

- **Q:** 50 mL of oil has a mass of 46 g. Density?
  **A:** 0.92 g/mL — less than water, so oil floats on water.
- **Q:** Typical densities ranking?
  **A:** Corn syrup (~1.3–1.4 g/mL) > water (1.0) > oil (~0.92).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Density column
*Student:* Build a density column with corn syrup, water and oil and predict where a grape, cork and coin will settle.

*How to teach:* Your child pours corn syrup, then water, then oil slowly down the side and predicts where each object settles before dropping it. Check before moving on: ask "Where do the coin, grape and cork settle?" Look for: Coin on the bottom; grape usually sinks through water and rests on the corn syrup; cork floats on the oil.

- **Q:** Where do the coin, grape and cork settle?
  **A:** Coin on the bottom; grape usually sinks through water and rests on the corn syrup; cork floats on the oil.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Solubility
*Student:* Test how many teaspoons of salt, sugar and baking soda dissolve in 100 mL of water and graph results.

*How to teach:* Your child adds one level teaspoon at a time to 100 mL room-temperature water, stirring until no more dissolves, and graphs teaspoons dissolved. Check before moving on: ask "Which dissolves the most?" Look for: Sugar dissolves by far the most, salt a moderate amount (~36 g per 100 mL), baking soda the least (~10 g).

- **Q:** Which dissolves the most?
  **A:** Sugar dissolves by far the most, salt a moderate amount (~36 g per 100 mL), baking soda the least (~10 g).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mystery powders
*Student:* Identify three unlabeled white powders using solubility, texture and reaction with vinegar or iodine.

*How to teach:* Your child tests each powder (dissolves? feel? vinegar? iodine?) and uses a key. Check before moving on: ask "Which powder turns blue-black with iodine?" Look for: Cornstarch — iodine detects starch.

- **Q:** Which powder turns blue-black with iodine?
  **A:** Cornstarch — iodine detects starch.
- **Q:** Which fizzes with vinegar?
  **A:** Baking soda — it reacts to produce carbon dioxide gas.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a lab report on the mystery-powder investigation with a data table and conclusion.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a lab report: question, hypothesis, materials, procedure, data table, conclusion. Check before moving on: ask "Why test with several properties?" Look for: One property alone may match more than one substance; several properties identify it with confidence.

- **Q:** Why test with several properties?
  **A:** One property alone may match more than one substance; several properties identify it with confidence.

**Assessment:** Density calculations and mystery-powder lab report.

**Answer key:** Density calculations correct to 2 decimal places with units g/mL. Lab report rubric: 4 = complete data table for all tests, correct IDs (fizz = baking soda, blue-black with iodine = cornstarch, dissolves with no reaction = sugar), conclusion cites data; 3 = one ID weakly supported; 2 = missing data; 1 = incomplete.

## World History & Geography: Medieval and Early Modern Times: Arabia and the origins of Islam

**Prepare:** Physical map of Arabia; Khan Academy video on the origins of Islam; timeline paper; three-religion comparison chart.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Arabia was entirely empty desert. It had oases, towns and busy trade routes.
- Confusing the hijra with the hajj. The hijra (622) was Muhammad's migration to Medina; the hajj is the pilgrimage to Mecca.

### Day 1 (Monday): Geography of Arabia
*Student:* Map the Arabian Peninsula's deserts, oases and trade routes and compare nomadic Bedouin and town life.

*How to teach:* Your child maps the deserts, oases, Red Sea coast and caravan routes and compares Bedouin and town life. Check before moving on: ask "How did Bedouin live?" Look for: As nomadic herders moving with camels, sheep and goats between oases and pastures.

- **Q:** How did Bedouin live?
  **A:** As nomadic herders moving with camels, sheep and goats between oases and pastures.
- **Q:** Town life?
  **A:** Settled merchants and craftspeople in oasis towns like Mecca and Yathrib.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mecca as a trade center
*Student:* Explain why Mecca was a crossroads of caravans and a religious center before Islam.

*How to teach:* Your child explains why Mecca was a crossroads. Check before moving on: ask "Why was Mecca important before Islam?" Look for: It was on caravan routes between Yemen and Syria and had the Kaaba, a shrine pilgrims visited.

- **Q:** Why was Mecca important before Islam?
  **A:** It was on caravan routes between Yemen and Syria and had the Kaaba, a shrine pilgrims visited.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Life of Muhammad
*Student:* Create an illustrated timeline of Muhammad's life from 570 CE to 632 CE, including the hijra.

*How to teach:* Your child makes an illustrated timeline. Check before moving on: ask "Key dates?" Look for: 570 born in Mecca; 610 first revelation (Muslims believe from the angel Gabriel); 622 hijra to Medina (year 1 of the Islamic calendar); 630 returns to Mecca; 632 dies.

- **Q:** Key dates?
  **A:** 570 born in Mecca; 610 first revelation (Muslims believe from the angel Gabriel); 622 hijra to Medina (year 1 of the Islamic calendar); 630 returns to Mecca; 632 dies.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Connections to Judaism and Christianity
*Student:* Compare monotheism, prophets and holy books across Judaism, Christianity and Islam in a chart.

*How to teach:* Your child compares the three faiths. Check before moving on: ask "Shared beliefs?" Look for: Monotheism; many shared prophets (Abraham, Moses); holy books; Abraham as an ancestor of faith.

- **Q:** Shared beliefs?
  **A:** Monotheism; many shared prophets (Abraham, Moses); holy books; Abraham as an ancestor of faith.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on geography and the origins of Islam.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Check before moving on: ask "What is the hijra?" Look for: Muhammad's 622 migration from Mecca to Medina.

- **Q:** What is the hijra?
  **A:** Muhammad's 622 migration from Mecca to Medina.

**Assessment:** Timeline and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Arabian geography: deserts, oases, trade routes. 2) Mecca as trade and religious center. 3) Muhammad born c. 570 in Mecca. 4) Hijra 622. 5) Returned to Mecca 630, died 632. 6) Islam, Judaism and Christianity are monotheistic. Timeline: all dates correct with brief captions.

## Spanish 1A: El mundo hispanohablante

**Prepare:** Blank world map, colored pencils, an online map game (e.g., Seterra 'Spanish-speaking countries'). Phone to record. List of California places: Los Ángeles, San Francisco, Sacramento, Fresno, Sierra Nevada, Monterey, etc.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting Equatorial Guinea. It is the one Spanish-speaking country in Africa.
- Saying 'Soy en México' for 'I'm from Mexico.' Use 'Soy de México.'

### Day 1 (Monday): Mapa
*Student:* Label the 21 Spanish-speaking countries and their capitals on a blank map, color-coding Spain, Mexico/Central America, the Caribbean, South America, and Equatorial Guinea.

*How to teach:* Label all 21 countries and capitals, color-coded by region.

- **Q:** What is the capital of Argentina?
  **A:** Buenos Aires.
- **Q:** Which Spanish-speaking country is in Africa, and what is its capital?
  **A:** Guinea Ecuatorial (Equatorial Guinea); capital Malabo.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): ¿De dónde eres?
*Student:* Practice ¿De dónde eres? / Soy de... and learn that Spanish is spoken widely in California and the U.S.; list five California place names of Spanish origin.

*How to teach:* Practice —¿De dónde eres? —Soy de ___. List five California place names of Spanish origin.

- **Q:** Give five California places with Spanish names.
  **A:** Los Ángeles, San Francisco, Sacramento, San Diego, Fresno, Sierra Nevada, Monterey, Santa Cruz (any five).
- **Q:** Answer in Spanish: ¿De dónde eres? (from California)
  **A:** Soy de California.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Map quiz practice
*Student:* Use an online map game or flashcards to drill countries and capitals until you can place all 21 in under five minutes.

*How to teach:* Drill the map game until all 21 can be placed in under 5 minutes.

- **Q:** What is the capital of Peru? Of Colombia?
  **A:** Lima; Bogotá.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Presentación
*Student:* Write and record a self-introduction: greeting, name, how you are, age in numbers, where you are from, and farewell.

*How to teach:* Write and record a self-introduction.

- **Q:** How do you say 'I am 12 years old'?
  **A:** Tengo doce años.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 assessment
*Student:* Take the unit test (greetings listening, numbers, alphabet, map of 21 countries) and submit the recorded introduction.

*How to teach:* Give the unit 1 test and collect the recording.

- **Q:** Name the capital of Spain and of Mexico.
  **A:** Madrid; Ciudad de México.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test and presentational recording rubric.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test: greetings listening (match greeting to time of day/feelings), numbers 0–31, alphabet spelling, and map. 21 countries and capitals: España–Madrid; México–Ciudad de México; Guatemala–Ciudad de Guatemala; Honduras–Tegucigalpa; El Salvador–San Salvador; Nicaragua–Managua; Costa Rica–San José; Panamá–Ciudad de Panamá; Cuba–La Habana; República Dominicana–Santo Domingo; Puerto Rico–San Juan; Colombia–Bogotá; Venezuela–Caracas; Ecuador–Quito; Perú–Lima; Bolivia–La Paz (seat of government) and Sucre (constitutional capital); Chile–Santiago; Argentina–Buenos Aires; Uruguay–Montevideo; Paraguay–Asunción; Guinea Ecuatorial–Malabo. Recording rubric: greeting, name, feeling, age (Tengo __ años), origin (Soy de…), farewell; 6/6 proficient with intelligible pronunciation.

## Visual & Performing Arts 7: Proportion and portrait drawing

**Prepare:** Mirror, pencils, eraser, a picture of a face with proportion guidelines (search 'Loomis' or 'basic face proportions'). Keep an early-year sketch for comparison.

**Watch for:**
- Drawing the eyes near the top of the head. Eyes are about halfway between the top of the head and the chin.
- Drawing symbols (almond eyes, a 'nose triangle') instead of observing. Look in the mirror and draw shapes and values you actually see.

### Day 1 (Monday): Face proportions
*Student:* Learn standard face proportions and draw a guideline face.

*How to teach:* Teach guidelines: oval; eyes halfway down; the face is about five eye-widths across; bottom of nose halfway from eyes to chin; mouth about one-third down from nose to chin; ears from eye line to nose line.

- **Q:** Where are the eyes on the head?
  **A:** About halfway between the top of the skull and the chin.
- **Q:** How far apart are the eyes?
  **A:** About one eye-width apart.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Features
*Student:* Practice drawing eyes, nose and mouth from a mirror, one page each.

*How to teach:* Practice eyes, nose and mouth from the mirror, one page each.

- **Q:** Why does the iris look partly covered by the upper eyelid?
  **A:** The upper lid naturally overlaps the top of the iris; showing the whole circle makes eyes look startled.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Self-portrait sketch
*Student:* Sketch a self-portrait from a mirror using guidelines.

*How to teach:* Sketch the self-portrait lightly with guidelines.

- **Q:** What is symmetry, and is a real face perfectly symmetrical?
  **A:** Symmetry is balance where both sides match; real faces are nearly but not perfectly symmetrical.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Self-portrait shading
*Student:* Add value to the self-portrait using the light source.

*How to teach:* Add value based on a single light source; shade eye sockets, under nose, under lower lip, neck.

- **Q:** Which parts of the face usually fall into shadow with light from above?
  **A:** Eye sockets, under the nose, under the lower lip, under the chin and the neck.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Compare the self-portrait to an early-year sketch and list improvements.

*How to teach:* Compare with an earlier sketch and list improvements.

- **Q:** Name one specific improvement.
  **A:** Open answer — e.g., eyes placed at the halfway line now.

**Assessment:** Self-portrait.

**Answer key:** Self-portrait rubric (1–4): proportions (eyes at halfway line, about one eye-width apart, nose bottom halfway between eyes and chin, mouth one-third from nose to chin, ears between eye and nose lines), observational detail, value showing a consistent light source, expression/personality. Proficient = proportions mostly correct and at least 4 values.

## Physical Education 7: Cooperative challenges

**Prepare:** Hula hoops, a blindfold or scarf, soft obstacles (pillows, cones), 3–5 soft balls, a rope circle for 'toxic waste', and at least 3 people. Clear the area of sharp edges for the blind walk.

**Watch for:**
- Shouting over each other to solve challenges. Teams improve when one person speaks at a time and the group listens and tries ideas.
- Blaming a teammate after a failed attempt. Focus on the plan, not the person: 'What can we change next try?'

### Day 1 (Monday): Hula-hoop pass
*Student:* Pass a hoop around a hand-held circle without letting go and improve time over 5 tries.

*How to teach:* Group holds hands in a circle and passes a hoop around without letting go. Time 5 tries and discuss strategies between tries.

- **Q:** What strategy helped improve your time?
  **A:** Example: bending low, coordinating movements, verbally guiding the next person.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Blind walk
*Student:* Guide a blindfolded partner through an obstacle course using only verbal cues.

*How to teach:* Guide a blindfolded partner through obstacles with only voice cues; switch roles. Coach specific cues: 'stop', 'two small steps right'.

- **Q:** What makes a verbal cue effective?
  **A:** It's specific, short and calm — direction plus distance (e.g., 'three steps forward').

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Group juggle
*Student:* Keep several balls moving in a set pattern with family members, adding balls each round.

*How to teach:* Establish a throwing pattern (say name, toss underhand); add balls each round.

- **Q:** Why say the person's name before throwing?
  **A:** It gets their attention and prepares them to catch, avoiding drops.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Human knot
*Student:* Untangle a human knot or complete 'toxic waste' transfer with rope.

*How to teach:* Untangle a human knot or move 'toxic waste' with ropes without touching it.

- **Q:** What role does a leader play in group challenges?
  **A:** Organizes ideas, keeps everyone involved, keeps the group calm and focused.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Discuss which communication strategies worked and write a reflection.

*How to teach:* Discuss and write a reflection using the rubric.

- **Q:** Name one communication strategy that worked this week.
  **A:** Open answer — e.g., one person speaks at a time, using names, specific directional cues.

**Assessment:** Teamwork rubric and reflection.

**Answer key:** Teamwork rubric (score 1–4 in each): communication (clear, specific cues), listening (lets others speak, builds on ideas), encouragement (positive words), problem-solving (suggests and tests strategies). Reflection: proficient answer names 2 strategies that worked (e.g., counting out loud, assigning roles, giving step-by-step cues like 'two steps left') and one personal goal.

## Health Education 7: Valid nutrition information and fad diets

**Prepare:** Find 4 nutrition sources: e.g., a .gov site (MyPlate), a hospital site (KidsHealth), a supplement-selling blog, and a diet influencer post. Parent preview: Day 3–4 discuss body image and eating disorders — keep tone supportive and nonjudgmental, and avoid weight/calorie talk about your child's body.

**Watch for:**
- Believing a source is valid because it looks professional or has many followers. Check the author's credentials, evidence and whether they are selling something.
- Thinking eating disorders only affect girls or underweight people. They affect all genders and body sizes; they are serious illnesses that need professional help.

### Day 1 (Monday): Source check
*Student:* Evaluate 4 nutrition websites/posts for author, evidence and purpose.

*How to teach:* Teach the checklist: Who wrote it? What evidence? Why was it written (sell or inform)? When updated? Your child evaluates the four sources and ranks them.

- **Q:** Which ending usually means a government source: .com, .gov or .org?
  **A:** .gov (.org can be anyone; .com is commercial).
- **Q:** A post says 'Doctors hate this one trick!' and links to a product. Valid?
  **A:** No — no evidence, sensational claim, and the purpose is to sell.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Fad diets
*Student:* Compare two fad diet claims with MyPlate guidance and explain the risks.

*How to teach:* Read two fad diet claims (e.g., 'no carbs ever', 'juice cleanse'). Your child compares each to MyPlate and lists risks.

- **Q:** Why is cutting out all carbohydrates risky for a growing teen?
  **A:** Carbs are the main energy source for the brain and muscles; cutting them causes fatigue and poor focus and removes fiber, vitamins and minerals.
- **Q:** What is the healthiest way to maintain weight?
  **A:** Balanced meals following MyPlate, regular physical activity, adequate sleep — not extreme restriction.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Body image
*Student:* Discuss how media images affect body image and list healthy self-talk statements.

*How to teach:* Discuss how edited and filtered images create unrealistic standards. Your child rewrites three negative self-talk statements as healthy ones.

- **Q:** Rewrite: 'I look terrible compared to everyone online.'
  **A:** Example: 'Online photos are edited. My body is strong and lets me do things I enjoy.'
- **Q:** What is body image?
  **A:** How you see, think and feel about your own body.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Eating disorders
*Student:* Learn warning signs of eating disorders and how to help a friend get support.

*How to teach:* Teach warning signs calmly and practice what to say to a friend: 'I'm worried about you. Can we talk to an adult?' Emphasize these are medical illnesses with treatment.

- **Q:** Name two warning signs of an eating disorder.
  **A:** Skipping meals, extreme dieting or calorie counting, hiding food, excessive exercise, frequent comments about being fat, rapid weight change.
- **Q:** What is the most helpful thing to do if you are worried about a friend?
  **A:** Tell a trusted adult (parent, school counselor, doctor) — don't keep it secret.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz on valid information and healthy weight practices.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz from the assessment key orally or written. Review any missed items.

- **Q:** List two questions to ask to decide if health information is valid.
  **A:** Who is the author and are they an expert? Is there evidence? Are they trying to sell something? Is it current?

**Assessment:** Source evaluation and quiz.

**Answer key:** Source evaluation: for each source child lists author/credentials, evidence (studies, experts), purpose (inform vs sell), date, and gives a valid/not valid verdict with reason — e.g., MyPlate = valid (USDA, evidence-based, no sales); influencer selling detox tea = not valid (no credentials, selling product). Quiz (suggested): 1) Name 3 signs of a fad diet — promises fast weight loss, cuts out whole food groups, sells products/pills, sounds too good to be true. 2) Why are fad diets risky? — missing nutrients, fatigue, weight regain, can lead to disordered eating. 3) Healthy weight is maintained by balanced eating and regular activity. 4) Name 2 warning signs of an eating disorder — skipping meals, obsessive calorie counting, secret eating, excessive exercise, big weight changes. 5) What to do if worried about a friend — tell a trusted adult.
