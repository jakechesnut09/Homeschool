# Teacher's Guide — 7th Grade, Week 1

## English Language Arts 7: Launching the year; 'Thank You, Ma'am' by Langston Hughes

**Prepare:** Print 'Thank You, Ma'am' from CommonLit; sticky notes; a reading-interest survey (make one: favorite genres, last book loved, reading habits). Read the story yourself first (about 10 minutes).

**Watch for:**
- Treating characterization as only what the narrator says directly. Show that Mrs. Jones's actions (dragging Roger home, feeding him) reveal more than any adjective.
- Calling any group of words with a noun a clause. A clause has a subject AND a verb; a phrase lacks one of them.

### Day 1 (Monday): Reader inventory
*Student:* Complete a reading interest survey, set a goal of 25 independent books for the year, and write a journal entry about a memorable story.

*How to teach:* Your child completes the survey, sets a 25-book goal and writes a journal entry about a memorable story. Help pick the first independent book and set a daily reading time. Check before moving on: ask "What makes a reading goal realistic?" Look for: It fits your reading speed and time: e.g., 25 books ≈ one book every 1½ weeks with 20–30 minutes a day.

- **Q:** What makes a reading goal realistic?
  **A:** It fits your reading speed and time: e.g., 25 books ≈ one book every 1½ weeks with 20–30 minutes a day.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Read 'Thank You, Ma'am'
*Student:* Read Hughes' 'Thank You, Ma'am' aloud and annotate Mrs. Jones's actions and words with sticky notes labeled 'trait' and 'evidence'.

*How to teach:* Read the story aloud (alternate paragraphs). Model one sticky note: 'Trait: strong/bold — Evidence: she kicks him and drags him home.' Your child adds at least five more. Check before moving on: ask "What does Roger try to do at the start, and what happens?" Look for: He tries to snatch Mrs. Jones's purse; the strap breaks, he falls, and she grabs him and drags him to her home.

- **Q:** What does Roger try to do at the start, and what happens?
  **A:** He tries to snatch Mrs. Jones's purse; the strap breaks, he falls, and she grabs him and drags him to her home.
- **Q:** Why does Roger say he wanted the money?
  **A:** To buy a pair of blue suede shoes.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Direct vs. indirect characterization
*Student:* Build a two-column chart of direct and indirect characterization for Roger and Mrs. Jones and write a paragraph on how she changes him, citing two quotes.

*How to teach:* Explain direct (narrator states the trait) vs indirect (speech, thoughts, actions, effect on others, looks). Your child fills the chart and writes a paragraph on how she changes Roger, citing two quotes. Check before moving on: ask "Give an example of indirect characterization of Mrs. Jones." Look for: She leaves her purse on the daybed where Roger could take it, showing trust and kindness.

- **Q:** Give an example of indirect characterization of Mrs. Jones.
  **A:** She leaves her purse on the daybed where Roger could take it, showing trust and kindness.
- **Q:** How does Roger change?
  **A:** He stops wanting to run and wants her to trust him; he sits where she can see him and can barely get out 'Thank you, ma'am' at the end.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: phrases vs. clauses
*Student:* Learn phrases (prepositional, participial) versus independent and dependent clauses and label 15 sentences from the story.

*How to teach:* Teach: phrase = no subject-verb pair (prepositional: 'in the dark'; participial: 'carrying a purse'); clause = subject + verb; independent stands alone, dependent begins with words like because, when, although. Your child labels 15 sentences. Check before moving on: ask "Label: 'When she got him home'" Look for: Dependent clause (subject 'she', verb 'got', begins with 'when').

- **Q:** Label: 'When she got him home'
  **A:** Dependent clause (subject 'she', verb 'got', begins with 'when').
- **Q:** Label: 'into the street'
  **A:** Prepositional phrase.

### Day 5 (Friday): Discussion & check
*Student:* Discuss with a parent whether Mrs. Jones's choice was wise, building on each other's points, then take a short quiz on characterization and clauses.

*How to teach:* Discuss: was Mrs. Jones's choice wise? Model building on a point ('I agree, and also…'). Then give the quiz (see key). Check before moving on: ask "What does Mrs. Jones give Roger at the end?" Look for: Ten dollars to buy the blue suede shoes (after feeding him).

- **Q:** What does Mrs. Jones give Roger at the end?
  **A:** Ten dollars to buy the blue suede shoes (after feeding him).

**Assessment:** Evidence paragraph, grammar labeling and discussion checklist.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Direct characterization = narrator states a trait; 2) Indirect = shown through words, actions, thoughts, looks, effects; 3) Example: she makes him wash his face (caring/firm); 4) 'Because he was hungry' = dependent clause; 5) 'with blue suede shoes' = prepositional phrase. Paragraph rubric: 4 = clear claim, two or more accurately quoted and cited pieces of evidence, explanation that connects each quote to the claim; 3 = claim and evidence but thin explanation; 2 = evidence summarized rather than quoted, or weak link to claim; 1 = opinion without evidence. Discussion checklist: listens, builds on others, cites the text, stays respectful.

## Mathematics 7: What are scaled copies?

**Prepare:** Print or write a 20-question grade 6 readiness check (Khan Academy 6th-grade unit tests work), graph paper, ruler, colored pencils. Draw five letter-F versions on grid paper: original 2 wide x 4 tall, a copy doubled in both directions, one stretched only taller, one with an extra unit added to every side, and one halved.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 'add the same amount to every side' makes a scaled copy. Show a 2x4 rectangle grown to 4x6 (+2) — its shape changes; a scaled copy multiplies every length by the same factor (4x8).
- Believing angles get bigger in a bigger copy. Measure corresponding angles with a protractor: they are always equal in scaled copies.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pre-assessment & math autobiography
*Student:* Take a 20-question grade 6 readiness check (ratios, fractions, decimals, area) and write a half-page math autobiography describing strengths and goals.

*How to teach:* Give the readiness check untimed and without help, then have your child write a half-page math autobiography (favorite topics, what feels hard, a goal for the year). Grade the check together and list topics missed below 70% for warm-up review. Check before moving on: ask "What is 3/4 of 20?" Look for: 15 (20 ÷ 4 = 5, 5 × 3 = 15).

- **Q:** What is 3/4 of 20?
  **A:** 15 (20 ÷ 4 = 5, 5 × 3 = 15).
- **Q:** Area of a triangle with base 10 cm and height 6 cm?
  **A:** 30 cm² (½ × 10 × 6).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Scaled copies
*Student:* Compare five versions of a letter 'F' drawn on grid paper and decide which are scaled copies, explaining which lengths changed and how.

*How to teach:* Show the five F's and ask which 'look right' as smaller or bigger versions. Model measuring each segment of the original and a copy, and check whether every length was multiplied by the same number. Your child sorts the rest and writes how lengths changed.

- **Q:** An F has segments 2, 4 and 1 units. A copy has 4, 8 and 2. Scaled copy?
  **A:** Yes — every length is multiplied by 2.
- **Q:** A copy has 3, 5 and 2 (each +1). Scaled copy?
  **A:** No — adding 1 multiplies the lengths by different amounts (2→3 is ×1.5, 4→5 is ×1.25).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Corresponding parts
*Student:* Label corresponding points, segments and angles on pairs of polygons; measure angles with a protractor to see that angles stay the same.

*How to teach:* Model labeling a polygon ABCD and its copy A'B'C'D', matching corresponding points and sides. Your child labels several pairs and measures corresponding angles with a protractor, recording them in a table. Ask what they notice about the angles.

- **Q:** Side AB = 3 and its corresponding side A'B' = 7.5. What is the scale factor?
  **A:** 2.5 (7.5 ÷ 3).
- **Q:** Angle A is 70°. What is angle A' in a scaled copy?
  **A:** 70° — angles do not change in a scaled copy.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Making scaled copies
*Student:* Draw a scaled copy of a grid polygon with scale factor 3, then 1/2, and write how each side length changed.

*How to teach:* Model copying a grid polygon at scale factor 3 by multiplying each horizontal and vertical run by 3 and connecting points. Then your child draws a copy at scale factor 1/2 and writes each original and new side length. Check before moving on: ask "A rectangle is 4 by 6. Draw it at scale factor 1/2 — what are the new sides?" Look for: 2 by 3.

- **Q:** A rectangle is 4 by 6. Draw it at scale factor 1/2 — what are the new sides?
  **A:** 2 by 3.
- **Q:** A side of 5 units at scale factor 3?
  **A:** 15 units.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a 6-problem quiz identifying scale factors and drawing a scaled copy; correct mistakes in a different color.

*How to teach:* Give the 6-problem quiz (see key) without notes. Have your child correct mistakes in a different color and explain each fix aloud. Check before moving on: ask "Is a scale factor of 1 a 'scaled copy'?" Look for: Yes — it is an identical (same size) copy.

- **Q:** Is a scale factor of 1 a 'scaled copy'?
  **A:** Yes — it is an identical (same size) copy.

**Assessment:** Quiz on scale factor and scaled-copy drawing; readiness check used to plan review.

**Answer key:** Quiz (6): 1) Original side 4, copy side 12: scale factor 3. 2) Original 10, copy 5: scale factor 1/2. 3) Copy of a 6-by-8 rectangle at factor 1.5: 9 by 12. 4) Is a 3x5 → 5x7 rectangle a scaled copy? No (+2 is not a constant multiple). 5) Angle 40° in original → 40° in copy. 6) Drawing at factor 2 of a given grid triangle: every side doubled, angles match (full credit if all vertices correct). Readiness check: score by topic; plan 5-minute warm-ups on any topic under 70%.

## Science 7 (Integrated): What is everything made of? Particles and atoms

**Prepare:** Notebook, goggles, home lab safety contract (write one: goggles on, no tasting, adult for heat/chemicals, wash hands, clean up). Food coloring, hot and cold water, clear cups, balloon, vanilla extract. Bookmark PhET 'Build an Atom'.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking particles stop moving in cold water. All particles are always moving; they move more slowly when colder.
- Thinking the balloon must have a hole for the smell to escape. Vanilla molecules are small enough to pass through the gaps between the rubber's molecules.

### Day 1 (Monday): Safety & notebook
*Student:* Set up a science notebook with a table of contents and sign a home lab safety contract after reviewing goggles, heat and chemical rules.

*How to teach:* Review each safety rule and why it matters, then sign the contract together. Your child sets up a table of contents and numbers the notebook pages. Check before moving on: ask "What do you do if a chemical splashes into your eye?" Look for: Tell an adult immediately and rinse the eye with running water for at least 15 minutes.

- **Q:** What do you do if a chemical splashes into your eye?
  **A:** Tell an adult immediately and rinse the eye with running water for at least 15 minutes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Phenomenon: food coloring
*Student:* Drop food coloring into hot and cold water, time how long it takes to spread, and sketch a particle model of what is happening.

*How to teach:* Have your child predict which cup will spread color faster, then drop one drop in each at the same time and time the spreading. They sketch particle models of each cup (dots with motion lines). Check before moving on: ask "Why does the color spread faster in hot water?" Look for: Water particles move faster at higher temperature, so they bump and spread the coloring particles faster.

- **Q:** Why does the color spread faster in hot water?
  **A:** Water particles move faster at higher temperature, so they bump and spread the coloring particles faster.
- **Q:** Why does it spread at all without stirring?
  **A:** Water particles are constantly moving and colliding with the dye particles (diffusion).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Scent in a balloon
*Student:* Put vanilla extract inside a balloon, inflate and tie it, then smell the outside and explain using particles.

*How to teach:* Put a few drops of vanilla in a balloon, inflate and tie it, and wait 10 minutes. Your child smells the outside and explains with a particle drawing. Check before moving on: ask "How can you smell vanilla through a sealed balloon?" Look for: Vanilla molecules are tiny and moving; they pass through microscopic gaps between the rubber molecules and reach your nose.

- **Q:** How can you smell vanilla through a sealed balloon?
  **A:** Vanilla molecules are tiny and moving; they pass through microscopic gaps between the rubber molecules and reach your nose.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Atoms and elements
*Student:* Use the PhET 'Build an Atom' simulation to build hydrogen, carbon and oxygen atoms and record protons, neutrons and electrons.

*How to teach:* Model building hydrogen in PhET (1 proton, 1 electron). Your child builds carbon and oxygen and records protons, neutrons and electrons in a table. Check before moving on: ask "How many protons does carbon have? Oxygen?" Look for: Carbon 6; oxygen 8 (the atomic number).

- **Q:** How many protons does carbon have? Oxygen?
  **A:** Carbon 6; oxygen 8 (the atomic number).
- **Q:** What determines which element an atom is?
  **A:** The number of protons.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph answering 'Is matter continuous or made of particles?' and take a vocabulary quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a CER on 'Is matter continuous or made of particles?' using the food coloring and balloon evidence, then takes the vocabulary quiz. Check before moving on: ask "Give one piece of evidence that matter is made of particles." Look for: Vanilla scent passed through the balloon wall, which only makes sense if both are made of particles with spaces between them.

- **Q:** Give one piece of evidence that matter is made of particles.
  **A:** Vanilla scent passed through the balloon wall, which only makes sense if both are made of particles with spaces between them.

**Assessment:** CER paragraph and particle model sketches.

**Answer key:** Vocabulary quiz: matter (anything with mass and volume), particle, atom (smallest unit of an element), element, proton (+), neutron (no charge), electron (−), diffusion. CER rubric: 4 = accurate claim, at least two pieces of specific evidence from the investigation (numbers/observations), reasoning that links evidence to the scientific principle; 3 = claim and evidence, reasoning thin; 2 = claim with vague evidence; 1 = claim only or incorrect. Sketches: show particles in both cups, faster motion in hot water.

## World History & Geography: Medieval and Early Modern Times: Rome at its height: strengths and legacies

**Prepare:** Long strip of paper for a 300–1800 CE wall timeline; blank Mediterranean map; Twelve Tables excerpt (Fordham Ancient History Sourcebook); cardboard, scissors and tape for the arch.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Rome 'fell' all at once and the whole empire ended. The western half fell in 476 CE; the eastern (Byzantine) half lasted until 1453.
- Confusing the Roman Republic and Empire. The Twelve Tables come from the Republic (c. 450 BCE); the Empire began with Augustus (27 BCE).

### Day 1 (Monday): Course launch & timeline
*Student:* Build a wall timeline from 300 to 1800 CE and preview the nine regions studied this year on a world map.

*How to teach:* Lay out the timeline (1 cm or 1 inch per 10 years) and mark 476, 1054, 1215, 1492 and 1776 as anchors. On a world map your child locates the year's regions (Rome/Byzantium, Arabia, China, West Africa, Japan, Europe, Mesoamerica/Andes). Check before moving on: ask "How many years does the course timeline cover?" Look for: 1,500 years (300 to 1800 CE).

- **Q:** How many years does the course timeline cover?
  **A:** 1,500 years (300 to 1800 CE).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Mapping the empire
*Student:* Map the Roman Empire in 117 CE, labeling Rome, Constantinople, the Rhine and Danube frontiers and major provinces.

*How to teach:* Show a map of the empire under Trajan (117 CE, its greatest extent). Your child labels Rome, Constantinople, the Rhine and Danube frontiers, and provinces such as Gaul, Hispania, Britannia and Egypt. Check before moving on: ask "Why were the Rhine and Danube important?" Look for: They formed natural river frontiers that the army defended against Germanic peoples.

- **Q:** Why were the Rhine and Danube important?
  **A:** They formed natural river frontiers that the army defended against Germanic peoples.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Roman law and citizenship
*Student:* Read excerpts of the Twelve Tables (primary source) and list three laws still reflected in modern legal ideas.

*How to teach:* Read selected laws aloud. Your child lists three ideas still in modern law (laws written and posted publicly, the right of the accused to appear in court, penalties for false testimony, property rights). Check before moving on: ask "Why did plebeians want laws written down?" Look for: So patricians (judges) could not interpret unwritten laws unfairly; written laws apply to everyone.

- **Q:** Why did plebeians want laws written down?
  **A:** So patricians (judges) could not interpret unwritten laws unfairly; written laws apply to everyone.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Engineering
*Student:* Study Roman roads, aqueducts and the Pantheon dome, and build a cardboard arch to test why arches carry weight.

*How to teach:* Show images of roads, aqueducts and the Pantheon. Your child builds a cardboard arch with a keystone and tests how much weight it holds. Check before moving on: ask "Why does an arch carry heavy loads?" Look for: The stones press against each other (compression), spreading the weight outward and down to the supports; the keystone locks them.

- **Q:** Why does an arch carry heavy loads?
  **A:** The stones press against each other (compression), spreading the weight outward and down to the supports; the keystone locks them.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on Rome's most important legacy and take a map and vocabulary quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a paragraph on Rome's most important legacy, then takes the map and vocabulary quiz. Check before moving on: ask "Name three Roman legacies." Look for: Written law/legal ideas, Latin (root of Romance languages), engineering (roads, aqueducts, arches, concrete), republican government ideas.

- **Q:** Name three Roman legacies.
  **A:** Written law/legal ideas, Latin (root of Romance languages), engineering (roads, aqueducts, arches, concrete), republican government ideas.

**Assessment:** Map quiz and legacy paragraph.

**Answer key:** Map quiz: Rome, Constantinople, Rhine, Danube, Mediterranean, Gaul, Hispania, Egypt, Britannia. Vocabulary: republic, empire, citizen, aqueduct, legacy. Paragraph rubric: 4 = clear topic sentence answering the prompt, at least two accurate specific facts or document references, explanation of how they support the claim, concluding sentence; 3 = accurate but thin explanation; 2 = general statements, few facts; 1 = off-topic or inaccurate.

## Spanish 1A: ¡Hola! Greetings, farewells, and names

**Prepare:** Spanish notebook, a mirror for pronunciation, a free Duolingo account. Find a short greeting dialogue video (e.g., a Spanish Proficiency Exercises or Señor Jordan clip). Practice the vowel sounds a, e, i, o, u yourself.

**Watch for:**
- Using 'buenos días' all day. Buenos días = morning (until about noon/lunch), buenas tardes = afternoon/early evening, buenas noches = night (also a goodnight).
- Answering 'Mucho gusto' with 'Mucho gusto' only. That's fine, but 'Igualmente' (likewise) or 'El gusto es mío' are natural replies.

### Day 1 (Monday): Saludos
*Student:* Watch a short greeting dialogue, then practice hola, buenos días/tardes/noches aloud and label a time-of-day chart with the right greeting.

*How to teach:* Watch the dialogue, then say each greeting aloud and label a chart: morning → buenos días, afternoon → buenas tardes, night → buenas noches.

- **Q:** Which greeting do you use at 3:00 p.m.?
  **A:** Buenas tardes.
- **Q:** Which greeting do you use at 9:00 p.m.?
  **A:** Buenas noches.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): ¿Cómo te llamas?
*Student:* Practice the exchange ¿Cómo te llamas? / Me llamo... / Mucho gusto / Igualmente with a parent playing a new classmate; record it twice.

*How to teach:* You play a new classmate. Practice: —¿Cómo te llamas? —Me llamo ___. ¿Y tú? —Me llamo ___. —Mucho gusto. —Igualmente. Record twice.

- **Q:** How do you say 'My name is Ana'?
  **A:** Me llamo Ana.
- **Q:** Someone says 'Mucho gusto.' What can you answer?
  **A:** Igualmente (or Mucho gusto / El gusto es mío).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Despedidas
*Student:* Learn adiós, hasta luego, hasta mañana, nos vemos; write and act out three 4-line mini-dialogues that open with a greeting and close with a farewell.

*How to teach:* Learn farewells; your child writes and acts three 4-line dialogues that start with a greeting and end with a farewell.

- **Q:** How do you say 'See you tomorrow'?
  **A:** Hasta mañana.
- **Q:** What does 'Nos vemos' mean?
  **A:** See you (later).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Culture: el saludo
*Student:* Read about greeting customs (handshake, cheek kiss in Spain and much of Latin America) and compare them with greetings in your family in a two-column chart.

*How to teach:* Read about greeting customs; make a two-column chart comparing them with your family's.

- **Q:** How do many people greet friends in Spain and much of Latin America?
  **A:** With a kiss on the cheek (one cheek in most of Latin America, two in Spain), or a handshake in formal settings.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete Duolingo's first unit and perform an unscripted greet-introduce-farewell exchange with a parent.

*How to teach:* Finish Duolingo unit 1; do an unscripted greet–introduce–farewell exchange scored with the checklist.

- **Q:** Translate: 'Good morning. My name is Luis. See you later.'
  **A:** Buenos días. Me llamo Luis. Hasta luego.

**Assessment:** Oral exchange checklist: greets, gives name, responds to mucho gusto, uses a farewell.

**Answer key:** Oral exchange checklist (1 point each): greets appropriately for the time of day (Hola / Buenos días / Buenas tardes), asks or answers '¿Cómo te llamas?' with 'Me llamo…', responds to 'Mucho gusto' with 'Igualmente' or 'Mucho gusto', uses a farewell (Adiós / Hasta luego / Hasta mañana / Nos vemos). 4/4 = proficient; pronunciation intelligible.

## Visual & Performing Arts 7: Sketchbook habits and contour drawing

**Prepare:** Sketchbook, pencils (2H, HB, 2B, 4B, 6B), a fine felt-tip pen, a shoe, a timer. Draw a few blind contours yourself so you can model laughing at the wobbly results.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking blind contour drawings should look 'right.' The goal is training eye–hand coordination and careful looking; distorted results are expected.
- Using one uniform line everywhere. Vary line weight: heavier for edges in shadow or nearer, lighter for distant or lit edges.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sketchbook setup
*Student:* Design a sketchbook cover and fill one page with 20 different kinds of lines.

*How to teach:* Your child designs a cover and fills a page with 20 line types; label a few (contour, gestural, implied, hatched).

- **Q:** What is line quality?
  **A:** The character of a line — its thickness, darkness, smoothness or roughness, and how it varies.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Blind contour
*Student:* Draw your non-dominant hand in blind contour three times for 3 minutes each.

*How to teach:* Explain blind contour: eyes on the hand, never on the paper, pencil moving slowly as eyes trace edges. Three 3-minute drawings.

- **Q:** What is a contour line?
  **A:** A line that follows the edges (outer and inner) of a form.
- **Q:** Why draw without looking at the paper?
  **A:** It forces you to really observe the object and trains eye–hand coordination.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Modified contour
*Student:* Draw a shoe in modified contour, glancing at the paper only at corners.

*How to teach:* Modified contour: look mostly at the shoe, glance at the paper only at corners or turns. Include laces, stitching and inner edges.

- **Q:** How is modified contour different from blind contour?
  **A:** You can glance at the paper occasionally (at key points) to keep proportions, while still looking mostly at the object.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Beating creative block
*Student:* Try three idea-starters (random object, word prompt, 'draw it 5 ways') and fill a page.

*How to teach:* Try three idea starters: draw a random object, a word prompt, and one object 5 different ways.

- **Q:** Name one strategy to beat creative block.
  **A:** Use a prompt, change the medium, set a timer, draw one object many ways, or look at artists for inspiration.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Choose your best contour drawing and write three sentences about line quality.

*How to teach:* Your child picks the best contour and writes three sentences about line quality.

- **Q:** Point to a place where you varied line weight. Why?
  **A:** Open answer — e.g., darker line where the shoe touches the floor to show weight and shadow.

**Assessment:** Sketchbook pages and reflection.

**Answer key:** Sketchbook rubric (1–4): cover design shows personal identity; line page has 20 distinct line types (thick, thin, dashed, zigzag, spiral, wavy, broken, implied, hatched, etc.); three blind contours of 3 minutes each with continuous line; modified contour shoe with accurate shapes and varied line; idea-starter page full. Reflection proficient: three sentences using 'line quality' vocabulary (weight, continuity, expressiveness) about a specific drawing.

## Physical Education 7: Warm-ups and aerobic capacity baseline

**Prepare:** Mark two lines 20 meters apart with cones on a flat, non-slip surface. Download the free FITNESSGRAM PACER audio (or plan a measured mile on a track). Stopwatch, fitness journal, and a printout of the FITNESSGRAM Healthy Fitness Zone (HFZ) chart for your child's age and sex.

**Watch for:**
- Starting the PACER too fast. The early levels are slow on purpose; the goal is to reach the line just as the beep sounds.
- Doing static stretches before vigorous exercise as the warm-up. Warm up with dynamic movement; save holding stretches for the cool-down.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dynamic warm-up
*Student:* Learn a 10-move dynamic warm-up (high knees, butt kicks, carioca, leg swings) and a static cool-down stretch routine.

*How to teach:* Teach a 10-move dynamic warm-up (jog, high knees, butt kicks, carioca, leg swings, arm circles, walking lunges, skips, side shuffles, inchworms) and a 5-stretch static cool-down held 20–30 seconds. Your child leads it back to you.

- **Q:** Why warm up dynamically before exercise?
  **A:** It raises heart rate, body temperature and blood flow to muscles and moves joints through their range, preparing the body and reducing injury risk.
- **Q:** When should static stretches be done?
  **A:** During the cool-down after activity, holding each 15–30 seconds without bouncing.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): PACER practice
*Student:* Practice the 20-meter PACER pacing for 5 minutes with the audio cadence.

*How to teach:* Explain the PACER: run 20 m before each beep; the pace gets faster each level (about each minute). Practice 5 minutes, turning at the line with one foot over it.

- **Q:** What happens if you reach the line before the beep?
  **A:** Wait at the line for the beep, then run back.
- **Q:** When does the PACER end?
  **A:** The second time you don't reach the line before the beep.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): PACER test
*Student:* Complete the 20-meter PACER (or timed mile) and record laps or time.

*How to teach:* Warm up, then run the PACER (or timed mile). Record laps or time immediately. Walk to cool down.

- **Q:** What does the PACER measure?
  **A:** Aerobic capacity — how well the heart and lungs deliver oxygen to working muscles.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Active recovery
*Student:* Take a 30-minute brisk walk or bike ride and log it in a fitness journal.

*How to teach:* Your child takes a 30-minute brisk walk or bike ride and logs date, activity, minutes and effort.

- **Q:** Why include an easy day after a hard test?
  **A:** Active recovery promotes blood flow and lets muscles recover while still building the habit.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Compare your PACER score to the Healthy Fitness Zone chart and write one observation.

*How to teach:* Compare the score to the HFZ chart and write one observation and one goal idea.

- **Q:** What is the Healthy Fitness Zone?
  **A:** The FITNESSGRAM score range linked to good health for your age and sex; scores below it are 'Needs Improvement.'

**Assessment:** Recorded PACER/mile baseline.

**Answer key:** Baseline record is complete when it shows the date, test (PACER laps or mile time), the score and the HFZ category (Needs Improvement / Healthy Fitness Zone). For the PACER, the score is the number of laps completed (the test ends the second time the runner fails to reach the line before the beep). Proficient: test done with honest effort and correct pacing, result recorded with one written observation.

## Health Education 7: Wellness and the six nutrients

**Prepare:** Gather a health journal, 5–6 packaged foods with Nutrition Facts labels and a measuring cup. Draw a simple wellness wheel (circle cut into physical, mental/emotional, social sections) or print one.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 'fat' is always bad. Explain that fat is an essential nutrient (energy, cell membranes, absorbing vitamins A, D, E, K); the goal is mostly unsaturated fats and less saturated/trans fat.
- Forgetting water is a nutrient. List all six: carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals and water.

### Day 1 (Monday): Wellness wheel
*Student:* Complete a wellness wheel rating physical, mental, social and emotional health and write one goal.

*How to teach:* Explain wellness as balance across physical, mental/emotional and social health. Your child rates each area 1–10 on the wheel, shades it, and writes one goal for the lowest area. Ask them to give a reason for each rating.

- **Q:** What are three dimensions of wellness?
  **A:** Physical, mental/emotional and social health (some models add spiritual, environmental or intellectual).
- **Q:** Why can a problem in one area affect another?
  **A:** They are connected: e.g., poor sleep (physical) makes you irritable (emotional), which can cause arguments with friends (social).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Six nutrients
*Student:* Make a chart of the six nutrients with functions and two food sources each.

*How to teach:* Introduce the six nutrients, then your child builds a chart with function and two food sources each. Check the chart against the key in the assessment section.

- **Q:** Name the six nutrients.
  **A:** Carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, water.
- **Q:** Which nutrient is the body's main source of quick energy?
  **A:** Carbohydrates.
- **Q:** Which nutrient builds and repairs muscle and other tissues?
  **A:** Protein.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Water
*Student:* Track water intake for a day and calculate how much a teen needs.

*How to teach:* Your child logs every drink for one day in cups. Explain that most 12–13-year-olds need roughly 7–8 cups of fluids a day (more for larger or very active teens and in heat). Compare their total and list ways to drink more.

- **Q:** Give two jobs of water in the body.
  **A:** Regulating body temperature (sweat), carrying nutrients and oxygen, removing wastes, cushioning joints, aiding digestion — any two.
- **Q:** Name an early sign of dehydration.
  **A:** Thirst, dark yellow urine, headache, tiredness or dizziness.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Nutrient detective
*Student:* Find nutrients in five pantry foods using their labels.

*How to teach:* Model reading one label: find calories, total fat, carbohydrates, fiber, sugars, protein, and the vitamins/minerals at the bottom. Your child records the main nutrients in five pantry foods and names the food richest in each.

- **Q:** On a Nutrition Facts label, where do you find vitamins and minerals?
  **A:** At the bottom: vitamin D, calcium, iron and potassium are required, listed with % Daily Value.
- **Q:** A label says 5 g protein per serving and there are 2 servings in the package. How much protein in the whole package?
  **A:** 10 g.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a nutrient quiz and journal about one change to try.

*How to teach:* Give a short oral or written quiz (see key). Then your child journals one specific change to try, such as adding a fruit at breakfast.

- **Q:** Which vitamins are fat-soluble?
  **A:** A, D, E and K.
- **Q:** Which mineral is most important for strong bones and teeth?
  **A:** Calcium (with vitamin D helping absorb it).

**Assessment:** Nutrient chart and quiz.

**Answer key:** Nutrient chart: six rows — carbohydrates (main energy; bread, rice, fruit), proteins (build/repair tissue; beans, eggs, meat), fats (stored energy, absorb vitamins A, D, E, K; nuts, olive oil), vitamins (regulate body processes; citrus = vitamin C, carrots = vitamin A), minerals (bones, blood; milk = calcium, spinach/beans = iron), water (temperature, transport, digestion; water, fruits). Quiz key (suggested): 1) carbohydrates 2) protein 3) water 4) calcium 5) vitamins A, D, E, K 6) fats and carbohydrates/protein give energy; vitamins and minerals do not provide calories. Score 5–6 proficient.
