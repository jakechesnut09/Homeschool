# Teacher's Guide — 7th Grade, Week 3

## English Language Arts 7: Point of view: 'The Tell-Tale Heart' by Edgar Allan Poe

**Prepare:** Print 'The Tell-Tale Heart' and find the LibriVox audio. Parental preview: a murder and dismemberment, told calmly; suitable for most 7th graders, but read first.

**Watch for:**
- Believing the narrator because he says he is sane. Point to his actions: the obsession with the eye, the murder, hearing a heart.
- Putting commas between cumulative adjectives ('three old men'). Use the 'and' test.

### Day 1 (Monday): Listen and read
*Student:* Read along with a LibriVox recording of 'The Tell-Tale Heart' and mark every time the narrator insists he is not mad.

*How to teach:* Play the LibriVox recording while your child follows the printed text, pausing after each paragraph. Your child marks every place the narrator insists he is not mad (e.g., the opening 'why will you say that I am mad?'). Ask: why would a sane person need to keep saying so?

- **Q:** Why does the narrator kill the old man?
  **A:** Because of the old man's 'vulture' eye — not for money or anger.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Unreliable narrator
*Student:* Make a T-chart of what the narrator claims versus what his actions show and explain why he is unreliable.

*How to teach:* Draw a T-chart labeled 'What he claims' and 'What his actions show.' Model one row ('I am calm' vs. he shrieks and confesses), then your child adds at least five rows with quotes. Finish with a 3–4 sentence explanation of why he is unreliable.

- **Q:** What makes the narrator unreliable?
  **A:** He insists he is calm and sane while describing an obsessive murder and hearing a dead man's heart.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mood and word choice
*Student:* Identify repetition, dashes and sound words that build tension and explain their effect on mood in a paragraph.

*How to teach:* Read the climax aloud twice, once flatly and once with the dashes and repetition emphasized. Model how 'louder! louder! louder!' speeds the pace, then your child finds two more examples (dashes, the repeated heartbeat, sound words) and writes a paragraph explaining how they create a tense, frantic mood.

- **Q:** Effect of 'louder! louder! louder!'?
  **A:** The repetition speeds the pace and shows rising panic, building tension.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Grammar: coordinate adjectives
*Student:* Learn the comma rule for coordinate adjectives (the 'and' test) and edit 12 sentences describing the old man's room.

*How to teach:* Teach the rule: if 'and' between the adjectives sounds right and order can switch, use a comma. Your child edits 12 sentences. Check before moving on: ask "'The dark, silent room'—comma correct?" Look for: Yes: 'dark and silent' works.

- **Q:** 'The dark, silent room'—comma correct?
  **A:** Yes: 'dark and silent' works.
- **Q:** 'the old wooden bed'?
  **A:** No comma: 'old and wooden' sounds odd; they are cumulative.

### Day 5 (Friday): Rewrite & check
*Student:* Rewrite the climax from the police officers' point of view in 300 words and take a point-of-view quiz.

*How to teach:* Review the officers' visit in the story, then your child rewrites the climax in about 300 words from an officer's point of view (third person or first person 'we'), including what the officers notice about the narrator. Then give the point-of-view quiz. Check before moving on: ask "What does the narrator do at the end?" Look for: He confesses, shrieking to tear up the planks to find the heart.

- **Q:** What does the narrator do at the end?
  **A:** He confesses, shrieking to tear up the planks to find the heart.

**Assessment:** Point-of-view rewrite and quiz.

**Answer key:** POV quiz: 1) First person. 2) Unreliable narrator = one whose account can't be trusted. 3) Evidence: insists he is not mad. 4) Officers came because a neighbor heard a shriek. Rewrite rubric: 4 = consistent third-person/officer POV, accurate events, shows officers' growing suspicion, ~300 words; 3 = minor POV slips; 2 = retells same POV; 1 = incomplete.

## Mathematics 7: Scale drawings and maps

**Prepare:** Printed California road map with a scale bar (or set your own: 1 cm = 50 km), ruler, tape measure; clear a room to measure. Preview a basketball court diagram (94 ft × 50 ft).

**Watch for:**
- Measuring map distance and forgetting to multiply by the scale. Have your child write 'map cm × 50 = actual km' at the top of the page.
- Treating '1 in to 10 ft' as 1:10. Convert to the same units first: 10 ft = 120 in, so 1:120.

### Day 1 (Monday): Reading a scale
*Student:* Use the scale 1 cm to 50 km on a California map to find road distances between five cities.

*How to teach:* Model measuring between two cities in cm and multiplying by 50. Your child finds five distances, rounding map measurements to the nearest 0.1 cm, and records them in a table. Check before moving on: ask "Two cities are 4.2 cm apart at 1 cm = 50 km. Actual distance?" Look for: 210 km.

- **Q:** Two cities are 4.2 cm apart at 1 cm = 50 km. Actual distance?
  **A:** 210 km.
- **Q:** Two cities are 375 km apart. Map distance?
  **A:** 7.5 cm (375 ÷ 50).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Creating a scale drawing
*Student:* Measure a bedroom or kitchen with a tape measure and plan a scale drawing using 1 inch to 2 feet.

*How to teach:* Measure the room together and record length and width in feet. Your child converts each measurement at 1 inch = 2 feet and sketches the plan lightly. Check before moving on: ask "A room is 12 ft by 10 ft. Size on the drawing at 1 in = 2 ft?" Look for: 6 in by 5 in.

- **Q:** A room is 12 ft by 10 ft. Size on the drawing at 1 in = 2 ft?
  **A:** 6 in by 5 in.
- **Q:** A bed is 6.5 ft long. Drawing length?
  **A:** 3.25 in.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Scales without units
*Student:* Rewrite scales like 1 in to 10 ft as 1 to 120 and compare which of two map scales shows more detail.

*How to teach:* Model converting 1 in to 10 ft into 1:120 by changing feet to inches. Your child converts several scales and decides which shows more detail (the one whose second number is smaller). Check before moving on: ask "Write 1 cm to 1 m without units." Look for: 1:100.

- **Q:** Write 1 cm to 1 m without units.
  **A:** 1:100.
- **Q:** Which shows more detail: 1:500 or 1:5,000?
  **A:** 1:500 — each unit on the drawing represents less real distance, so things are drawn larger.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Equivalent scales
*Student:* Decide which of six scales are equivalent and redraw a basketball court at a new scale.

*How to teach:* Show how 1 in to 4 ft and 1/2 in to 2 ft are equivalent (both 1:48). Your child sorts the six scales and redraws the court at 1 in = 10 ft. Check before moving on: ask "Basketball court 94 ft by 50 ft at 1 in = 10 ft?" Look for: 9.4 in by 5 in.

- **Q:** Basketball court 94 ft by 50 ft at 1 in = 10 ft?
  **A:** 9.4 in by 5 in.
- **Q:** Are 1 cm : 2 m and 5 cm : 10 m equivalent?
  **A:** Yes, both are 1:200.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Finish the room floor plan in scale with labeled actual dimensions and answer 5 map distance questions.

*How to teach:* Your child finishes the floor plan with a title, scale and labeled actual dimensions, then answers the 5 map questions (see key). Check before moving on: ask "On a 1:24 drawing a desk is 2.5 in long. Actual length?" Look for: 60 in = 5 ft.

- **Q:** On a 1:24 drawing a desk is 2.5 in long. Actual length?
  **A:** 60 in = 5 ft.

**Assessment:** Floor-plan scale drawing scored with a rubric (accuracy, labels, scale) plus map quiz.

**Answer key:** Floor-plan rubric: 4 = all measurements converted correctly, scale stated, every object labeled with actual dimensions, neat and ruled; 3 = one conversion error; 2 = several errors or missing scale; 1 = not to scale. Map quiz at 1 cm = 50 km: 1) 3 cm → 150 km 2) 6.4 cm → 320 km 3) 0.8 cm → 40 km 4) 500 km → 10 cm 5) 125 km → 2.5 cm.

## Science 7 (Integrated): Modeling molecules and extended structures

**Prepare:** Gumdrops or mini marshmallows in 4 colors, toothpicks; salt and sugar crystals, magnifier; PhET 'Build a Molecule'.

**Watch for:**
- Reading the subscript as applying to the element before AND after it. In CO2 the 2 belongs only to O.
- Thinking salt is made of separate 'NaCl molecules'. It is an extended repeating lattice of Na and Cl ions.

### Day 1 (Monday): Chemical formulas
*Student:* Interpret formulas H2O, CO2, O2, CH4 and C6H12O6 by counting atoms of each element.

*How to teach:* Model counting atoms in H2O. Your child counts atoms in CO2, O2, CH4 and C6H12O6. Check before moving on: ask "Atoms in C6H12O6?" Look for: 6 C, 12 H, 6 O = 24 atoms.

- **Q:** Atoms in C6H12O6?
  **A:** 6 C, 12 H, 6 O = 24 atoms.
- **Q:** Atoms in CH4?
  **A:** 1 carbon and 4 hydrogen = 5.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Gumdrop molecules
*Student:* Build gumdrop-and-toothpick models of water, carbon dioxide, methane and ammonia using a color key.

*How to teach:* Set a color key (e.g., black = C, white = H, red = O, blue = N). Your child builds H2O, CO2, CH4 and NH3. Check before moving on: ask "Draw ammonia." Look for: One nitrogen bonded to three hydrogens (NH3).

- **Q:** Draw ammonia.
  **A:** One nitrogen bonded to three hydrogens (NH3).
- **Q:** How many toothpicks connect atoms in CH4?
  **A:** 4 — each H bonds to the central C.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): PhET Build a Molecule
*Student:* Use PhET 'Build a Molecule' to assemble 8 molecules and record formulas and names.

*How to teach:* Your child assembles 8 molecules in PhET and records formula and name. Check before moving on: ask "What does the formula O2 show?" Look for: A molecule of two oxygen atoms bonded together (the oxygen we breathe).

- **Q:** What does the formula O2 show?
  **A:** A molecule of two oxygen atoms bonded together (the oxygen we breathe).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Crystals
*Student:* Examine salt and sugar crystals with a magnifier and build a repeating NaCl lattice model to show an extended structure.

*How to teach:* Look at salt crystals (cubes) and sugar crystals under a magnifier. Your child builds a 3×3×3 alternating NaCl lattice. Check before moving on: ask "Why are salt crystals cube-shaped?" Look for: Sodium and chloride ions repeat in a cubic pattern, so the crystal grows as cubes.

- **Q:** Why are salt crystals cube-shaped?
  **A:** Sodium and chloride ions repeat in a cubic pattern, so the crystal grows as cubes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draw labeled models of three molecules and one extended structure and explain the difference.

*How to teach:* Your child draws and labels three molecules and one extended structure and explains the difference. Check before moving on: ask "Molecule vs. extended structure?" Look for: A molecule is a small group of a fixed number of atoms; an extended structure is a repeating pattern that continues in all directions (salt, diamond, metals).

- **Q:** Molecule vs. extended structure?
  **A:** A molecule is a small group of a fixed number of atoms; an extended structure is a repeating pattern that continues in all directions (salt, diamond, metals).

**Assessment:** Molecule models and labeled drawings.

**Answer key:** Check drawings: H2O (bent, O center with 2 H), CO2 (O=C=O, linear), CH4 (C with 4 H), plus NaCl lattice with alternating ions. Explanation proficient: molecules have a set number of atoms; extended structures repeat with no set size.

## World History & Geography: Medieval and Early Modern Times: The Byzantine Empire

**Prepare:** Map of Constantinople; Procopius excerpt on Theodora and the Nika riots (532); selections from Justinian's Code; Smarthistory video on Hagia Sophia; Venn diagram.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Byzantines called themselves Byzantines. They called themselves Romans.
- Thinking the Great Schism was only about religion. Politics and language (Greek vs. Latin) mattered too.

### Day 1 (Monday): Constantinople's location
*Student:* Analyze a map of Constantinople and explain three geographic advantages for trade and defense.

*How to teach:* Analyze the map. Your child explains three advantages of the location. Check before moving on: ask "Three advantages?" Look for: Water on three sides for defense, the Golden Horn harbor, control of the Bosporus trade route between the Black Sea and Mediterranean, and position between Europe and Asia.

- **Q:** Three advantages?
  **A:** Water on three sides for defense, the Golden Horn harbor, control of the Bosporus trade route between the Black Sea and Mediterranean, and position between Europe and Asia.
- **Q:** Who founded the city in 330?
  **A:** Emperor Constantine.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Justinian and Theodora
*Student:* Read Procopius's account of Theodora during the Nika riots (primary source) and evaluate his reliability.

*How to teach:* Read the Procopius passage. Your child evaluates his reliability. Check before moving on: ask "What does Theodora urge?" Look for: That Justinian not flee; she says royalty makes a good burial shroud, meaning she'd rather die an empress.

- **Q:** What does Theodora urge?
  **A:** That Justinian not flee; she says royalty makes a good burial shroud, meaning she'd rather die an empress.
- **Q:** Why question Procopius?
  **A:** He was a court historian who also wrote the 'Secret History' attacking Justinian and Theodora, so his accounts may be biased in different directions.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Justinian's Code
*Student:* Read selected laws from Justinian's Code and compare them with the Twelve Tables.

*How to teach:* Read selected laws. Your child compares them with the Twelve Tables. Check before moving on: ask "What was Justinian's Code?" Look for: A collection and organization of Roman law (529–534 CE) that became the basis of many European legal systems.

- **Q:** What was Justinian's Code?
  **A:** A collection and organization of Roman law (529–534 CE) that became the basis of many European legal systems.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Great Schism
*Student:* Compare Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic practices in a Venn diagram and explain the 1054 split.

*How to teach:* Fill the Venn diagram. Your child explains the 1054 split. Check before moving on: ask "Two differences?" Look for: The pope claimed authority over all Christians, while Orthodox recognized patriarchs; Latin vs. Greek in worship; Orthodox priests may marry.

- **Q:** Two differences?
  **A:** The pope claimed authority over all Christians, while Orthodox recognized patriarchs; Latin vs. Greek in worship; Orthodox priests may marry.
- **Q:** What happened in 1054?
  **A:** The pope's representative and the patriarch of Constantinople excommunicated each other, splitting Christianity into Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 assessment
*Student:* Take the unit 1 test and examine the Hagia Sophia mosaics, writing how art reflected Byzantine power.

*How to teach:* Give the unit 1 test. Then view Hagia Sophia mosaics and have your child write how art showed Byzantine power. Check before moving on: ask "How did mosaics show power?" Look for: Gold mosaics showing emperors beside Christ or Mary connected the emperor's power to God.

- **Q:** How did mosaics show power?
  **A:** Gold mosaics showing emperors beside Christ or Mary connected the emperor's power to God.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test: 1) 117 CE greatest extent. 2) Twelve Tables = written laws. 3) Arches/aqueducts. 4) 410 Visigoths, 476 fall of West. 5) Constantinople founded 330 on the Bosporus. 6) Justinian's Code organized Roman law. 7) Theodora's role in the Nika riots. 8) 1054 Great Schism. Short response on art proficient with a specific mosaic and its message.

## Spanish 1A: El alfabeto y los números 0–31

**Prepare:** Alphabet chart, an alphabet song video, a deck of playing cards (remove face cards), dice. Write 8 dictation words (e.g., perro, llamar, niño, jugo, hola, gato, cero, quince).

**Watch for:**
- Pronouncing h. Spanish h is silent: hola = 'OH-la'.
- Mixing up 'veinte' style numbers: 16–19 and 21–29 are usually one word (dieciséis, veintiuno); 31 is three words (treinta y uno).

### Day 1 (Monday): El alfabeto
*Student:* Learn the Spanish alphabet with a song, then practice the five pure vowel sounds and the letters ñ, ll, rr, j, and h (silent).

*How to teach:* Sing the alphabet; drill vowels a (ah), e (eh), i (ee), o (oh), u (oo); ñ (ny), ll (y), rr (trilled), j (h sound), h (silent).

- **Q:** How is 'j' pronounced in 'jugo'?
  **A:** Like a strong English h: 'HOO-go'.
- **Q:** Is the h in 'hola' pronounced?
  **A:** No — h is silent.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): ¿Cómo se escribe?
*Student:* Spell your full name, your town, and five family members' names aloud; a parent writes what they hear to check accuracy.

*How to teach:* Spell names aloud letter by letter; you write what you hear.

- **Q:** How do you ask 'How do you spell it?'
  **A:** ¿Cómo se escribe?
- **Q:** Spell 'Ana' in Spanish letter names.
  **A:** a, ene, a.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Números 0–15
*Student:* Learn numbers 0–15 and play a card game (draw two cards, say the sum in Spanish) for 15 minutes.

*How to teach:* Learn 0–15; draw two cards and say the sum.

- **Q:** Say 7 + 8.
  **A:** Siete más ocho son quince.
- **Q:** How do you say 12 and 14?
  **A:** Doce, catorce.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Números 16–31
*Student:* Learn 16–31 (dieciséis, veintiuno), then do a listening dictation of 15 phone-style number strings.

*How to teach:* Learn 16–31; dictate 15 phone-style strings (e.g., 'veinticinco–diecisiete–treinta').

- **Q:** Write 22 and 26 in words.
  **A:** Veintidós, veintiséis.
- **Q:** Write 31 in words.
  **A:** Treinta y uno.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a spelling dictation of 8 Spanish words and a numbers 0–31 listening quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the spelling dictation and number listening quiz.

- **Q:** What number is 'diecinueve'?
  **A:** 19.

**Assessment:** Spelling dictation and number listening quiz.

**Answer key:** Spelling dictation: 8 words spelled correctly including ñ, ll, rr and accents (7/8 proficient). Number quiz 0–31: cero, uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez, once, doce, trece, catorce, quince, dieciséis, diecisiete, dieciocho, diecinueve, veinte, veintiuno, veintidós, veintitrés, veinticuatro, veinticinco, veintiséis, veintisiete, veintiocho, veintinueve, treinta, treinta y uno. 90% proficient.

## Visual & Performing Arts 7: Linear perspective

**Prepare:** Ruler, pencil, eraser, print or screen image of Raphael's The School of Athens (1509–1511, Vatican). Try a one-point box yourself first.

**Watch for:**
- Placing the horizon line at the top of the page by default. The horizon line is eye level; it changes depending on where the viewer stands.
- Drawing vertical lines toward the vanishing point. In one- and two-point perspective, vertical edges stay vertical; only receding edges go to vanishing points.

### Day 1 (Monday): One-point
*Student:* Draw block letters of your initials in one-point perspective.

*How to teach:* Draw a horizon line and one vanishing point; draw initials as flat block letters, then connect corners to the VP to make them 3-D.

- **Q:** What is a vanishing point?
  **A:** The point on the horizon line where parallel receding lines appear to meet.
- **Q:** What does the horizon line represent?
  **A:** The viewer's eye level.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Finding vanishing points
*Student:* Find and trace orthogonals in The School of Athens to locate its vanishing point.

*How to teach:* Trace the floor tiles and architecture lines in The School of Athens until they meet.

- **Q:** Where is the vanishing point in The School of Athens?
  **A:** At the center, near Plato and Aristotle, the two central figures — drawing the viewer's eye to them.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): One-point room
*Student:* Draw your bedroom or a hallway in one-point perspective.

*How to teach:* Draw a room or hallway in one-point: back wall rectangle, orthogonals from its corners to the edges, add doors and furniture.

- **Q:** What are orthogonals?
  **A:** Diagonal lines that recede toward the vanishing point.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Two-point
*Student:* Draw a street corner of buildings in two-point perspective.

*How to teach:* Two-point: horizon line with VPs near the page edges; draw the nearest vertical building edge, connect top and bottom to both VPs.

- **Q:** When do you use two-point perspective?
  **A:** When you view an object from a corner, so two sides recede to two vanishing points.

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Explain in writing how perspective changed art in the Renaissance.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a paragraph on perspective in the Renaissance.

- **Q:** Who is credited with first demonstrating linear perspective?
  **A:** Filippo Brunelleschi, in Florence in the early 1400s (Leon Battista Alberti later explained it in writing).

**Assessment:** Perspective drawings.

**Answer key:** Perspective drawings rubric (1–4): horizon line and vanishing point(s) clearly marked; all receding lines (orthogonals) converge accurately; verticals vertical and horizontals horizontal (one-point); objects shrink with distance; neat ruler work. Reflection proficient: explains that Renaissance artists (Brunelleschi demonstrated it ~1415–20; Alberti wrote it up in 1435) used linear perspective to create the illusion of 3-D depth on a flat surface, making scenes look realistic and drawing the eye to a focal point (e.g., Plato and Aristotle in The School of Athens).

## Physical Education 7: SMART goals and circuit training

**Prepare:** Jump rope, cones, 8 task cards (jumping jacks, squats, plank, mountain climbers, jump rope, wall sit, lunges, push-ups), interval timer app set to 40 s on / 20 s off, calendar. Print a 6–20 Borg RPE scale or 1–10 scale.

**Watch for:**
- Goals like 'get fitter.' SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound — e.g., 'add 8 PACER laps by January 15.'
- Racing through reps with poor form to boost count. Quality reps first; count only good-form reps.

### Day 1 (Monday): SMART goals
*Student:* Write two SMART goals based on baseline scores (e.g., 'add 8 PACER laps by January').

*How to teach:* Model writing a SMART goal from a baseline score. Your child writes two (one cardio, one strength/flexibility).

- **Q:** Is 'I will do more push-ups' SMART? Fix it.
  **A:** No. Fixed: 'I will increase from 10 to 16 push-ups by the January re-test by doing push-ups 3 days a week.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Circuit 1
*Student:* Complete an 8-station circuit (jumping jacks, squats, planks, mountain climbers) 40 seconds on, 20 off, 2 rounds.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate each station, then run 40 s on / 20 s rest, 2 rounds. Your child records reps.

- **Q:** What is a circuit?
  **A:** A series of exercise stations done one after another with short rests, working different muscle groups.
- **Q:** Name the main muscles used in mountain climbers.
  **A:** Core (abdominals), shoulders, hip flexors and legs.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Goal action plan
*Student:* Plan weekly activities to reach each goal and post them on a calendar.

*How to teach:* Your child plans weekly activities that support each goal and posts them on a calendar.

- **Q:** How often should you train push-ups to improve muscular endurance?
  **A:** About 2–3 non-consecutive days per week.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Circuit 2
*Student:* Repeat the circuit and try to increase reps at each station.

*How to teach:* Repeat the circuit; challenge to beat day-2 reps with good form.

- **Q:** Why might reps improve after only a few days?
  **A:** Better technique, pacing and familiarity (nervous system learning) more than big muscle changes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Journal on how the circuit felt and rate perceived exertion.

*How to teach:* Your child journals and rates perceived exertion (RPE).

- **Q:** What is RPE?
  **A:** Rating of Perceived Exertion — a self-rating of how hard exercise feels (e.g., 6–20 Borg scale or 1–10).

**Assessment:** SMART goals and circuit log.

**Answer key:** SMART goals (2): each names a fitness test, a measurable target based on baseline, a realistic increase and a date, plus 2 action steps. Circuit log: reps at each of 8 stations for 2 rounds on both days and RPE. Proficient: both goals meet all SMART criteria and day-4 log shows attempt to increase reps.

## Health Education 7: Influences on food choices

**Prepare:** Collect 3 food ads aimed at teens (cereal boxes, snack/drink commercials, social media posts), poster paper and markers. Ask a grandparent or relative ahead to be interviewed about a traditional family food.

**Watch for:**
- Believing ads don't affect them. Point out repeated logos, jingles and influencers; ask why companies spend billions if ads didn't work.
- Thinking cultural or traditional foods are automatically unhealthy. Most cuisines include vegetables, beans and whole grains; focus on balance and portion, not dropping family foods.

### Day 1 (Monday): Influence map
*Student:* Map internal (hunger, emotions) and external (family, culture, media) influences on your food choices.

*How to teach:* Explain internal influences (hunger, emotions, taste, habits) vs external (family, friends, culture, media, cost, availability). Your child draws a web of their own influences and stars the strongest two.

- **Q:** Is boredom an internal or external influence on eating?
  **A:** Internal — it is a feeling inside you.
- **Q:** Give two external influences on food choice.
  **A:** Family meals, culture, friends, advertising, cost, what's available at home or school — any two.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ad analysis
*Student:* Analyze three food ads aimed at teens for techniques like celebrity endorsement and bright packaging.

*How to teach:* Look at the three ads together. Your child fills a chart: product, target audience, techniques used, missing information.

- **Q:** What is a 'celebrity endorsement'?
  **A:** A famous person or influencer promoting a product so fans want to copy them.
- **Q:** Why do cereal boxes for kids put cartoon characters at kids' eye level?
  **A:** To catch children's attention so they ask parents to buy it — marketing to a target audience.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cultural foods
*Student:* Interview a family member about a traditional family food and its meaning.

*How to teach:* Your child interviews a family member about a traditional food: what it is, when it is eaten, who taught them, what it means. Write a short summary.

- **Q:** How can culture influence what a family eats?
  **A:** Through traditional recipes, holiday foods, spices and ingredients, and rules about food passed down through generations.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Counter-ad
*Student:* Design a counter-ad promoting a healthy food using the same persuasive techniques.

*How to teach:* Your child designs a counter-ad for a healthy food (e.g., carrots, water) using at least two of the same techniques. Check the claim is accurate.

- **Q:** Name two persuasive techniques you used and how.
  **A:** Example: bandwagon ('Everyone's grabbing water'), bright colors, a famous athlete, catchy slogan — answer should name and explain two.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on which influence affects you most.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a paragraph naming the strongest influence on their eating, with an example and a plan to manage it. Check for a clear topic sentence and specific example.

- **Q:** How can you reduce the effect of ads on your choices?
  **A:** Notice the technique, ask what is missing, read the label, decide based on your needs rather than the ad.

**Assessment:** Ad analysis and counter-ad.

**Answer key:** Ad analysis (per ad): names the target audience, at least two techniques (celebrity/influencer endorsement, bandwagon, bright packaging/cartoon characters, free prizes, 'fun' or 'cool' appeal, health halo words like 'natural'), and what the ad leaves out (sugar, sodium, cost). Counter-ad rubric: 4 = promotes a healthy food with two clearly named persuasive techniques, accurate claim, clear audience, eye-catching design; 3 = one technique, accurate; 2 = vague message; 1 = incomplete.
