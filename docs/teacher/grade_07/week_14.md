# Teacher's Guide — 7th Grade, Week 14

## English Language Arts 7: Drafting the argumentative essay

**Prepare:** Evidence organizer from week 13; an argument essay template (intro, 2 body paragraphs, counterclaim/rebuttal, conclusion); 12 wordy practice sentences.

**Watch for:**
- Stating evidence without explaining it. After every quote, require one or two sentences beginning 'This shows…'.
- A rebuttal that only repeats the claim. It must answer the opposing reason with evidence.

### Day 1 (Monday): Introduction
*Student:* Draft an introduction with background and a clear claim.

*How to teach:* Model an introduction with a hook, two sentences of background on zoos, and the claim. Your child drafts their own and reads it aloud; check that the claim is arguable and appears at the end. Check before moving on: ask "Is 'Zoos are interesting' a claim?" Look for: No — it isn't arguable. A claim: 'Accredited zoos should exist because they protect endangered species.'

- **Q:** Is 'Zoos are interesting' a claim?
  **A:** No — it isn't arguable. A claim: 'Accredited zoos should exist because they protect endangered species.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Reasons and evidence
*Student:* Draft two body paragraphs, each with a reason, two pieces of cited evidence and explanation.

*How to teach:* Your child drafts two body paragraphs, each with a topic sentence (reason), two cited pieces of evidence and explanation after each. Use the question 'So what does this prove?' to push for explanation. Check before moving on: ask "How do you cite a source in-text?" Look for: Name the author or source and add a parenthetical, e.g., (Smith) or (Association of Zoos and Aquariums).

- **Q:** How do you cite a source in-text?
  **A:** Name the author or source and add a parenthetical, e.g., (Smith) or (Association of Zoos and Aquariums).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Counterclaim
*Student:* Draft a counterclaim and rebuttal paragraph using phrases like 'Some argue… however…'.

*How to teach:* Model the 'Some argue… However…' structure. Your child drafts a paragraph that fairly states the opposing view, then rebuts it with evidence. Check that the rebuttal uses evidence, not just repetition.

- **Q:** Model rebuttal?
  **A:** 'Some argue that animals suffer in zoos. However, modern accredited zoos design habitats around natural behaviors and track animal welfare.'

### Day 4 (Thursday): Concise language
*Student:* Cut wordiness and redundancy from 12 practice sentences, then from your own draft.

*How to teach:* Show a wordy sentence and its concise version. Your child fixes 12 practice sentences, then cuts at least five wordy phrases ('in my opinion', 'due to the fact that') from the draft. Check before moving on: ask "Make concise: 'In my personal opinion I think that zoos are basically good.'" Look for: 'Zoos are beneficial.'

- **Q:** Make concise: 'In my personal opinion I think that zoos are basically good.'
  **A:** 'Zoos are beneficial.'
- **Q:** 'Due to the fact that'?
  **A:** 'Because'.

### Day 5 (Friday): Conclusion
*Student:* Draft a conclusion that restates the claim and leaves a call to action.

*How to teach:* Your child drafts a conclusion that restates the claim in new words, sums up the reasons, and ends with a call to action. Read the whole draft aloud together. Check before moving on: ask "Example call to action?" Look for: 'Visit and support accredited zoos that fund conservation.'

- **Q:** Example call to action?
  **A:** 'Visit and support accredited zoos that fund conservation.'

**Assessment:** Complete argument draft.

**Answer key:** Draft completion: introduction with claim; two body paragraphs each with reason + 2 cited evidence + explanation; counterclaim and rebuttal; conclusion with call to action. Check each element present; return with comments.

## Mathematics 7: Tax, tips, commission and markup

**Prepare:** A restaurant menu (online is fine), receipts showing tax, calculator. Look up your local sales tax rate (California base is 7.25%; most areas are higher).

**Watch for:**
- Applying markup and discount percents to the same base. Markup applies to cost; the discount applies to the new retail price.
- Computing tip on the total after tax when your family uses the pre-tax amount (either is acceptable — decide and be consistent).

### Day 1 (Monday): Sales tax
*Student:* Compute California sales tax (use local rate) on five purchases and find the tax rate from a receipt.

*How to teach:* Model tax = rate × price. Your child computes tax and totals for five purchases and finds a rate from a receipt (tax ÷ subtotal). Check before moving on: ask "$40 item at 7.25%?" Look for: Tax $2.90; total $42.90.

- **Q:** $40 item at 7.25%?
  **A:** Tax $2.90; total $42.90.
- **Q:** Receipt: subtotal $20, tax $1.75. Rate?
  **A:** 8.75%.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tips
*Student:* Compute 15%, 18% and 20% tips on menu orders using mental math (10% then adjust).

*How to teach:* Model 10%-then-adjust mental math. Your child computes 15%, 18% and 20% tips on menu orders. Check before moving on: ask "18% tip on $45?" Look for: $8.10 (10% = 4.50, 5% = 2.25, 3% = 1.35).

- **Q:** 18% tip on $45?
  **A:** $8.10 (10% = 4.50, 5% = 2.25, 3% = 1.35).
- **Q:** 20% of $45?
  **A:** $9.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Markup and markdown
*Student:* Solve problems where a store marks up wholesale cost by 40% then discounts the retail price by 25%.

*How to teach:* Model: cost $50, markup 40% → $70, then 25% off → $52.50. Your child solves several and compares the final price to cost. Check before moving on: ask "Did the store still profit?" Look for: Yes, $2.50 above the $50 cost (5%).

- **Q:** Did the store still profit?
  **A:** Yes, $2.50 above the $50 cost (5%).
- **Q:** Cost $30, 50% markup, 20% off. Final price?
  **A:** $36.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Commission and fees
*Student:* Calculate salesperson commission and service fees, then solve 'which deal is better' comparisons.

*How to teach:* Model commission as a percent of sales. Your child compares deals. Check before moving on: ask "5% commission on $3,000?" Look for: $150.

- **Q:** 5% commission on $3,000?
  **A:** $150.
- **Q:** Better deal: $200 with 15% off or $190 with $10 off?
  **A:** 15% off: $170 vs $180 — the 15% off deal.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Plan a restaurant meal for a family within $60 including tax and tip and show all calculations.

*How to teach:* Your child plans a family meal within $60 including tax and tip, showing every calculation. Check before moving on: ask "A $48 subtotal with 9% tax and 18% tip on the subtotal—under $60?" Look for: Tax $4.32, tip $8.64, total $60.96 — just over.

- **Q:** A $48 subtotal with 9% tax and 18% tip on the subtotal—under $60?
  **A:** Tax $4.32, tip $8.64, total $60.96 — just over.

**Assessment:** Restaurant budget task and short quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) $25 at 8% tax = $27. 2) 20% tip on $35 = $7. 3) Cost $80, 25% markup = $100. 4) $100 marked 30% down = $70. 5) 6% commission on $1,500 = $90. Meal rubric: 4 = items listed, correct tax and tip, total ≤ $60, all work shown; 3 = one arithmetic error; 2 = over budget or missing tax/tip; 1 = incomplete.

## Science 7 (Integrated): Matter and energy in organisms: synthesis

**Prepare:** A sealed jar with a sprig of an aquatic plant (elodea) or small potted plant (optional snail), or use data from a video of a sealed bottle garden; poster paper, markers; flashcards.

**Watch for:**
- Saying energy cycles like matter. Matter (atoms) cycles; energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat — it flows one way.
- Thinking plants photosynthesize but don't respire. Plants respire all the time; in light, photosynthesis is faster than respiration.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sealed ecosystem
*Student:* Analyze data from a sealed jar containing a plant (and optionally a snail) and explain how both survive.

*How to teach:* Show the sealed-jar data (e.g., O2 and CO2 levels by day and night). Your child explains how plant and snail both survive, and what would happen in total darkness. Check before moving on: ask "How do the plant and snail survive in a sealed jar?" Look for: The plant uses light, CO2 and water to make sugar and O2; the snail uses O2 and releases CO2; decomposers recycle waste.

- **Q:** How do the plant and snail survive in a sealed jar?
  **A:** The plant uses light, CO2 and water to make sugar and O2; the snail uses O2 and releases CO2; decomposers recycle waste.
- **Q:** What if the jar were kept dark?
  **A:** Photosynthesis stops; oxygen runs out and both eventually die.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Carbon atom journey
*Student:* Write a story following one carbon atom from the air into a leaf, a rabbit and back into the air.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm the journey together, then your child writes a first-person story of a carbon atom traveling from the air into a leaf, a rabbit and back to the air, naming each process. Check before moving on: ask "Name the process at each step." Look for: Air → leaf: photosynthesis (into glucose); leaf → rabbit: eating/digestion; rabbit → air: cellular respiration releases CO2.

- **Q:** Name the process at each step.
  **A:** Air → leaf: photosynthesis (into glucose); leaf → rabbit: eating/digestion; rabbit → air: cellular respiration releases CO2.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Model poster
*Student:* Create a poster model linking photosynthesis and respiration with matter and energy arrows.

*How to teach:* Your child draws a poster linking photosynthesis and respiration, with one color for matter arrows (CO2, H2O, O2, sugar) and another for energy (light in, heat out). Check before moving on: ask "Which arrows make a loop and which don't?" Look for: Matter arrows make a loop; energy arrows go in one direction (sunlight → chemical energy → heat).

- **Q:** Which arrows make a loop and which don't?
  **A:** Matter arrows make a loop; energy arrows go in one direction (sunlight → chemical energy → heat).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit review
*Student:* Review with flashcards and a practice set on unit 3 concepts.

*How to teach:* Quiz with flashcards, then your child completes a practice set on unit 3; re-teach any missed term. Check before moving on: ask "Reactants and products of photosynthesis?" Look for: Reactants: CO2 and H2O (with light energy); products: glucose (C6H12O6) and O2.

- **Q:** Reactants and products of photosynthesis?
  **A:** Reactants: CO2 and H2O (with light energy); products: glucose (C6H12O6) and O2.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 assessment
*Student:* Take the unit 3 test.

*How to teach:* Give the unit 3 test independently. Check before moving on: ask "Where does most of a plant's mass come from?" Look for: Carbon dioxide from the air.

- **Q:** Where does most of a plant's mass come from?
  **A:** Carbon dioxide from the air.

**Assessment:** Carbon story, model poster and unit 3 test.

**Answer key:** Carbon story rubric: correct path and process names at each step (photosynthesis, digestion, respiration) = proficient. Poster: matter loops, energy flows one way, both equations shown. Unit 3 test: 1) photosynthesis equation; 2) respiration equation; 3) mass mostly from CO2; 4) leaf disks float from O2; 5) yeast CO2 increases with sugar; 6) fat 9 Cal/g, carbs/protein 4 Cal/g; 7) food molecules broken down and rebuilt.

## World History & Geography: Medieval and Early Modern Times: Japan's geography, Shotoku and Heian culture

**Prepare:** Map of Japan; Seventeen-Article Constitution excerpt (604); excerpt from The Tale of Genji or The Pillow Book; shoebox, sand, rocks, fork for rock garden.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Japan copied China completely. It borrowed selectively (writing, Buddhism, Confucianism) and adapted.
- Thinking Zen and Pure Land are the same. Pure Land emphasizes faith in Amida Buddha; Zen emphasizes meditation.

### Day 1 (Monday): Geography
*Student:* Map Japan's islands, mountains and proximity to Korea and China and explain how geography shaped settlement.

*How to teach:* Your child maps Japan's four main islands, mountains and distance to Korea and China. Check before moving on: ask "Four main islands?" Look for: Honshu, Hokkaido, Kyushu, Shikoku.

- **Q:** Four main islands?
  **A:** Honshu, Hokkaido, Kyushu, Shikoku.
- **Q:** How did geography shape settlement?
  **A:** Mountains cover most land, so people lived on coastal plains; the sea gave food and some protection.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Shotoku's constitution
*Student:* Read Prince Shotoku's Seventeen-Article Constitution and identify Confucian and Buddhist ideas.

*How to teach:* Read the constitution. Your child marks Confucian and Buddhist ideas. Check before moving on: ask "Example of Confucian idea?" Look for: Harmony; obeying superiors; officials act with propriety.

- **Q:** Example of Confucian idea?
  **A:** Harmony; obeying superiors; officials act with propriety.
- **Q:** Buddhist idea?
  **A:** Revere the three treasures: Buddha, the Law, the monks.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Heian court
*Student:* Read an excerpt from The Tale of Genji or The Pillow Book and describe court life.

*How to teach:* Read the excerpt. Your child describes Heian court life. Check before moving on: ask "Who wrote The Tale of Genji?" Look for: Murasaki Shikibu, a court lady, around 1000.

- **Q:** Who wrote The Tale of Genji?
  **A:** Murasaki Shikibu, a court lady, around 1000.
- **Q:** Court life focus?
  **A:** Beauty, poetry, elegant dress and manners.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Japanese Buddhism
*Student:* Compare Pure Land and Zen Buddhism and create a simple Zen rock garden in a shoebox.

*How to teach:* Compare Pure Land and Zen; build a rock garden. Check before moving on: ask "Why did Zen appeal to samurai?" Look for: Its discipline and meditation built focus and self-control.

- **Q:** Why did Zen appeal to samurai?
  **A:** Its discipline and meditation built focus and self-control.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on early Japan.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Check before moving on: ask "Prince Shotoku's goals?" Look for: Strengthen central government and spread Buddhism.

- **Q:** Prince Shotoku's goals?
  **A:** Strengthen central government and spread Buddhism.

**Assessment:** Quiz and source analysis.

**Answer key:** Quiz: islands, Shotoku and Chinese influence, Heian period (794–1185), Murasaki Shikibu, Pure Land vs Zen. Source analysis: identifies Confucian and Buddhist ideas with quotes.

- Thinking all Spanish-speaking countries share the same favorite sport. Fútbol dominates most, but béisbol is huge in the Dominican Republic, Cuba, Venezuela and Puerto Rico.

*How to teach:* Read about fútbol in Argentina, béisbol in the Dominican Republic, el paseo in Spain; list products and practices.

*How to teach:* Watch a Señor Wooly video; list activity words; summarize in English.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pen-pal email

*How to teach:* Write a 10-sentence pen-pal email.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 assessment
*Student:* Take the unit test: listening to likes, reading a pen-pal letter, and writing a reply.

## Visual & Performing Arts 7: Chords and harmony

**Prepare:** Keyboard or ukulele, Chrome Music Lab 'Chords,' a 12-bar blues recording (e.g., 'Johnny B. Goode' or a classic blues), chords for 'This Land Is Your Land' (in C: C, F, G).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking major means loud and minor means soft. Major/minor describe the third of the chord (bright vs darker sound), not volume.
- Building triads by playing every other key including black keys randomly. A triad stacks every other note of the scale: root, 3rd, 5th.

### Day 1 (Monday): Triads
*Student:* Build C, F and G major triads on keyboard or ukulele.

*How to teach:* Build C, F and G triads by stacking every other note.

- **Q:** What notes are in a C major triad?
  **A:** C, E, G.
- **Q:** What notes are in a G major triad?
  **A:** G, B, D.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Major vs minor
*Student:* Play major and minor triads and describe the difference in sound.

*How to teach:* Play major and minor triads (C vs Cm: C-E♭-G); describe the sound.

- **Q:** What changes between C major and C minor?
  **A:** The middle note (the 3rd) is lowered a half step: E becomes E-flat.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): I-IV-V
*Student:* Play a I-IV-V-I progression and identify it in a 12-bar blues recording.

*How to teach:* Play I-IV-V-I in C; listen for the changes in a 12-bar blues.

- **Q:** What are I, IV and V in the key of G?
  **A:** G, C and D.
- **Q:** Write the 12-bar blues pattern.
  **A:** I I I I | IV IV I I | V IV I I.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Song chords
*Student:* Strum or play chords to a simple folk song like 'This Land Is Your Land'.

*How to teach:* Strum or play C, F, G to 'This Land Is Your Land.'

- **Q:** What is harmony?
  **A:** Two or more notes sounding together, such as chords supporting a melody.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a chord identification quiz by ear and on paper.

*How to teach:* Give the chord quiz by ear and on paper.

- **Q:** Spell an A minor triad.
  **A:** A, C, E.

**Assessment:** Chord quiz and play-along.

**Answer key:** Chord quiz key: C major = C-E-G; F major = F-A-C; G major = G-B-D; A minor = A-C-E; D minor = D-F-A; E minor = E-G-B. Major triad = root + major 3rd (4 half steps) + perfect 5th (7 half steps); minor = root + minor 3rd (3 half steps) + perfect 5th. In C major: I = C, IV = F, V = G. 12-bar blues: I-I-I-I / IV-IV-I-I / V-IV-I-I. By-ear: identify major (bright) vs minor (darker) — 80% proficient. Play-along: changes on time with steady strum.

## Physical Education 7: Pickleball

**Prepare:** Pickleball paddles, perforated plastic balls, a wall or local court (36-inch net at center). Phone for video. Learn the rules from USA Pickleball's summary.

**Watch for:**
- Volleying while standing in the kitchen. You may not volley (hit before the bounce) while touching the non-volley zone, including the line.
- Rushing the net after serving. The two-bounce rule means the serving team must let the return bounce, so they stay back first.

### Day 1 (Monday): Groundstrokes
*Student:* Practice forehand and backhand groundstrokes against a wall.

*How to teach:* Forehand and backhand groundstrokes against a wall: ready position, turn shoulders, contact in front, follow through low to high.

- **Q:** Where should you contact the ball?
  **A:** Out in front of your body, about waist height.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dinks and volleys
*Student:* Practice dinking at the kitchen line and volleys with a partner.

*How to teach:* Teach the dink: soft shot from the kitchen line that arcs just over the net into the opponent's kitchen. Volley with a firm, short punch.

- **Q:** What is a dink?
  **A:** A soft shot that lands in the opponent's non-volley zone (kitchen).
- **Q:** What is a volley?
  **A:** Hitting the ball before it bounces.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rules
*Student:* Learn the serve, two-bounce rule and kitchen rule, then play doubles.

*How to teach:* Teach serve (underhand, diagonal), the two-bounce rule and kitchen rule; play doubles.

- **Q:** Explain the two-bounce rule.
  **A:** The serve must bounce once and the return must bounce once before either team may volley.
- **Q:** What is the kitchen?
  **A:** The 7-foot non-volley zone on each side of the net; you can't volley while standing in it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Game day
*Student:* Play a pickleball round robin at a local court.

*How to teach:* Play a round robin at a local court, practicing court etiquette.

- **Q:** To how many points is a standard game played?
  **A:** 11, win by 2; only the serving team scores.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Analyze your follow-through using a phone video.

*How to teach:* Film a few strokes, watch in slow motion and analyze follow-through.

- **Q:** Why is follow-through important?
  **A:** It keeps the paddle on the path toward the target, improving accuracy and control and reducing strain.

**Assessment:** Video self-analysis.

**Answer key:** Video self-analysis rubric: 4 = identifies ready position, paddle face angle, contact point in front of body, weight transfer and full follow-through toward target, and writes one specific correction; 3 = identifies 3 elements; 2 = describes generally; 1 = incomplete. Rules key: underhand serve diagonally, contact below the waist; two-bounce rule; kitchen = 7 ft non-volley zone; games to 11, win by 2; only the serving team scores.

## Health Education 7: Online safety and digital citizenship

**Prepare:** Do a test search of your child's name first. Pick one app/device to review privacy settings on together. Collect or write 6 sample messages (3 phishing, 3 legitimate). Preview Common Sense lesson on online predators.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking deleted posts are gone. Screenshots, archives and servers can keep them; assume anything posted is permanent.
- Believing online predators are easy to spot. Groomers often pretend to be peers, give compliments and gifts, and ask for secrecy.

### Day 1 (Monday): Digital footprint
*Student:* Search your name online and discuss what a digital footprint is.

*How to teach:* Search your child's name together. Discuss what a digital footprint is and how colleges and employers may see it.

- **Q:** What is a digital footprint?
  **A:** The trail of information you leave online — posts, photos, comments, searches — that can be permanent.
- **Q:** Name two things to never share publicly.
  **A:** Home address, phone number, school name, passwords, real-time location, birth date.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Privacy settings
*Student:* Review privacy settings on a device or app with a parent.

*How to teach:* Sit together and review privacy settings on one app: private account, location off, who can message you.

- **Q:** Why turn off location sharing in apps?
  **A:** It can reveal where you live or are, which strangers could use.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Online predators
*Student:* Learn grooming warning signs and how to report.

*How to teach:* Teach grooming warning signs: flattery, gifts, asking for secrecy, moving to private chat, asking for photos or to meet. Teach: stop, block, screenshot, tell an adult; report to CyberTipline.

- **Q:** Name two grooming warning signs.
  **A:** Asking to keep the relationship secret, giving gifts or excessive compliments, asking for personal photos, wanting to meet in person, isolating you from family.
- **Q:** An online 'friend' asks you to keep your chats secret. What do you do?
  **A:** Stop chatting, don't send anything, save the messages and tell a trusted adult.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Scams
*Student:* Identify phishing attempts in 6 sample messages.

*How to teach:* Your child examines 6 messages and marks phishing ones, citing clues.

- **Q:** List three clues of a phishing message.
  **A:** Urgent threats, requests for passwords or personal info, misspellings, odd sender address, links that don't match the real site, prizes that are too good to be true.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Create a family media agreement.

*How to teach:* Write the family media agreement together and sign it.

- **Q:** What should you do if you see something online that makes you uncomfortable?
  **A:** Don't respond, take a screenshot, close it, and tell a trusted adult.

**Assessment:** Family media agreement.

**Answer key:** Family media agreement rubric: 4 = includes screen-time limits and device-free times/places, privacy rules (no sharing address, school, location, passwords), who you can chat with, what to do with uncomfortable messages (don't reply, screenshot, tell an adult), consequences and signatures; 3 = most items; 2 = only time limits; 1 = incomplete.
