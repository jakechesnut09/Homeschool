# Teacher's Guide — 7th Grade, Week 11

## English Language Arts 7: Reading informational text: central ideas and structure

**Prepare:** Three ReadWorks articles (free account) on technology and teens, including one on teen screen time; a two-central-ideas graphic organizer.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing topic ('screen time') with central idea ('Too much screen time can hurt sleep').
- Choosing structure by one signal word. Look at how the whole article is organized.

### Day 1 (Monday): Text features & structure
*Student:* Preview three ReadWorks articles and identify each one's structure (chronological, compare-contrast, cause-effect, problem-solution).

*How to teach:* Preview headings and first lines of three articles. Your child labels each structure and names the signal words. Check before moving on: ask "Which signal words suggest cause-effect?" Look for: Because, as a result, therefore, led to, consequently.

- **Q:** Which signal words suggest cause-effect?
  **A:** Because, as a result, therefore, led to, consequently.
- **Q:** Problem-solution signals?
  **A:** Problem, issue, one solution, to fix this.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Two central ideas
*Student:* Read an article on teen screen time and trace two central ideas through the text with a graphic organizer.

*How to teach:* Read the screen-time article together. Your child uses the organizer to trace two central ideas, noting in each paragraph which idea it develops and the supporting detail. Ask: what does the author want us to understand about screen time?

- **Q:** What's the difference between a topic and a central idea?
  **A:** A topic is the subject (screen time); a central idea is the point the author makes about it (e.g., heavy screen use is linked to poorer sleep).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Objective summary
*Student:* Write an objective summary of the article stating both central ideas and key details.

*How to teach:* Model the first sentence of a summary (title, author, central ideas). Your child writes a 5–7 sentence objective summary including both central ideas and key details. Check for and remove any opinion words.

- **Q:** What should not appear?
  **A:** Personal opinions, 'I think', and minor details.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Academic vocabulary
*Student:* Learn 10 academic words (analyze, significant, evidence, impact, contrast…) and use each in a sentence about the article.

*How to teach:* Introduce the 10 words with student-friendly definitions and an example. Your child writes a sentence about the article using each word correctly. Quiz orally by giving a definition and asking for the word.

- **Q:** Use 'significant' correctly.
  **A:** 'The article reports a significant drop in sleep among heavy users.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on an unseen article identifying structure and central ideas.

*How to teach:* Give an unseen ReadWorks article with its questions, plus two short prompts: name the structure with a signal word, and state two central ideas. Score with the ReadWorks key and the rubric below. Check before moving on: ask "How do you find a central idea?" Look for: Look at the title, introduction, topic sentences and conclusion for the repeated point.

- **Q:** How do you find a central idea?
  **A:** Look at the title, introduction, topic sentences and conclusion for the repeated point.

**Assessment:** Summary and informational reading quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: use ReadWorks key for multiple choice; for structure, correct label + one signal word cited; central ideas stated as full sentences with two supporting details each. Summary rubric: 4 = both central ideas, key details, objective, concise.

## Mathematics 7: Area of a circle

**Prepare:** Paper plates, scissors, grid paper, compass, calculator.

**Watch for:**
- Using 2πr (circumference) for area. Area is square units: πr².
- Squaring the diameter. If given d, halve first: d = 10 → r = 5 → A = 25π.

### Day 1 (Monday): Estimating area
*Student:* Trace a circle on grid paper and estimate its area by counting squares, comparing to r squared.

*How to teach:* Trace a circle of radius 5 grid units and count squares (whole and combined partials). Your child compares the count to r² = 25. Check before moving on: ask "About how many times r² is the area?" Look for: A little more than 3 times (≈ 78.5 = 3.14 × 25).

- **Q:** About how many times r² is the area?
  **A:** A little more than 3 times (≈ 78.5 = 3.14 × 25).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Cutting the circle
*Student:* Cut a paper plate into 16 sectors and rearrange them into a near-parallelogram to show area is pi r times r.

*How to teach:* Cut the plate into 16 sectors, rearrange alternately into a 'parallelogram'. Your child identifies base ≈ half the circumference (πr) and height ≈ r. Check before moving on: ask "Why is the area πr²?" Look for: The rearranged shape is about πr long and r tall, so area = πr × r = πr².

- **Q:** Why is the area πr²?
  **A:** The rearranged shape is about πr long and r tall, so area = πr × r = πr².

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Using the formula
*Student:* Find areas of pizzas, sprinkler regions and circular gardens given radius or diameter.

*How to teach:* Work a pizza example given diameter. Your child solves the pizza, sprinkler and garden problems. Check before moving on: ask "12-inch pizza (diameter). Area?" Look for: r = 6, A = 36π ≈ 113.04 in².

- **Q:** 12-inch pizza (diameter). Area?
  **A:** r = 6, A = 36π ≈ 113.04 in².
- **Q:** Sprinkler reaches 7 m. Area watered?
  **A:** 49π ≈ 153.86 m².

### Day 4 (Thursday): Area vs circumference
*Student:* Decide whether 8 situations need area or circumference (fencing vs sod, crust vs cheese) and solve each.

*How to teach:* Your child labels each of the 8 situations 'around' (circumference) or 'covering' (area), then solves. Check before moving on: ask "Fencing a circular garden of radius 4 m?" Look for: Circumference: 8π ≈ 25.12 m.

- **Q:** Fencing a circular garden of radius 4 m?
  **A:** Circumference: 8π ≈ 25.12 m.
- **Q:** Sod to cover it?
  **A:** Area: 16π ≈ 50.24 m².

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on area of circles and choosing the correct measure.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (see key) and the written derivation. Check before moving on: ask "Area of a circle with diameter 20 cm?" Look for: 100π ≈ 314 cm².

- **Q:** Area of a circle with diameter 20 cm?
  **A:** 100π ≈ 314 cm².

**Assessment:** Quiz and written derivation explaining the plate rearrangement.

**Answer key:** Quiz (π ≈ 3.14): 1) r = 3 → A ≈ 28.26. 2) d = 8 → A ≈ 50.24. 3) r = 10 → A = 314. 4) Edging a round table r = 2 ft → circumference ≈ 12.56 ft. 5) Cheese on a 14-in pizza → area ≈ 153.86 in². Derivation rubric: proficient explanation names the base (half the circumference, πr) and height (r) and concludes A = πr².

## Science 7 (Integrated): Photosynthesis: where does a plant's mass come from?

**Prepare:** Fresh spinach, baking soda, dish soap (1 drop), plastic syringe (no needle), hole punch or straw, clear cups, desk lamp, timer; short reading on van Helmont's willow experiment; gumdrops and toothpicks.

**Watch for:**
- Believing plant mass comes from soil. Van Helmont's soil lost only a few ounces; most mass comes from carbon dioxide in the air (plus water).
- Thinking plants don't need oxygen or don't respire. Plants do both photosynthesis and cellular respiration.

### Day 1 (Monday): Phenomenon: a growing tree
*Student:* Read about van Helmont's willow tree experiment and predict where the tree's mass came from.

*How to teach:* Read about van Helmont: a 5-lb willow grew to about 169 lb in 5 years while the soil lost only about 2 ounces. Your child predicts where the mass came from and records the prediction. Check before moving on: ask "What did van Helmont conclude, and what was he missing?" Look for: He concluded the mass came from water; he missed that carbon dioxide from the air supplies most of the mass (carbon).

- **Q:** What did van Helmont conclude, and what was he missing?
  **A:** He concluded the mass came from water; he missed that carbon dioxide from the air supplies most of the mass (carbon).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Floating leaf disks
*Student:* Use a syringe to infiltrate spinach leaf disks with baking-soda water and count how many float under a lamp every minute.

*How to teach:* Punch 10 leaf disks, pull air out with the syringe in baking-soda water until they sink, place under the lamp and count floating disks each minute. Your child records in a table. Check before moving on: ask "Why do the disks float?" Look for: Photosynthesis makes oxygen gas that collects in the leaf, making disks less dense.

- **Q:** Why do the disks float?
  **A:** Photosynthesis makes oxygen gas that collects in the leaf, making disks less dense.
- **Q:** Why add baking soda?
  **A:** It supplies carbon dioxide (as bicarbonate) for photosynthesis.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Light variable
*Student:* Repeat the leaf-disk test at different lamp distances and graph floating rate versus light.

*How to teach:* Repeat at two or three lamp distances. Your child graphs minutes-to-float versus distance. Check before moving on: ask "Prediction for a farther lamp?" Look for: Disks float more slowly because less light means less photosynthesis.

- **Q:** Prediction for a farther lamp?
  **A:** Disks float more slowly because less light means less photosynthesis.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Photosynthesis equation
*Student:* Build a gumdrop model of 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 and explain how atoms are rearranged.

*How to teach:* Build 6CO2 + 6H2O with gumdrops, rearrange into C6H12O6 + 6O2. Your child counts atoms. Check before moving on: ask "Atom count each side?" Look for: 6 C, 12 H, 18 O.

- **Q:** Atom count each side?
  **A:** 6 C, 12 H, 18 O.
- **Q:** Where does energy come from?
  **A:** Light energy is stored in the sugar's chemical bonds.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a CER answering 'Where does a plant's mass come from?' using leaf-disk evidence.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a CER. Check before moving on: ask "Claim?" Look for: Most of a plant's mass comes from carbon dioxide in the air, built into sugar during photosynthesis.

- **Q:** Claim?
  **A:** Most of a plant's mass comes from carbon dioxide in the air, built into sugar during photosynthesis.

**Assessment:** Leaf-disk data and CER.

**Answer key:** Leaf-disk data: table and graph showing faster floating with more light. CER rubric: 4 = accurate claim, at least two pieces of specific evidence from the investigation (numbers/observations), reasoning that links evidence to the scientific principle; 3 = claim and evidence, reasoning thin; 2 = claim with vague evidence; 1 = claim only or incorrect.

## World History & Geography: Medieval and Early Modern Times: Geography and the gold-salt trade

**Prepare:** West Africa vegetation-zone map, colored pencils; a bowl of salt and gold-painted rocks; al-Bakri's description of Ghana (1068, in Fordham or Levtzion & Hopkins).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Africa is all jungle or desert. West Africa has bands: Sahara desert, Sahel, savanna and forest.
- Thinking salt was cheap everywhere. In the hot south it was scarce and needed to preserve food and replace salt lost by sweating.

### Day 1 (Monday): Vegetation zones
*Student:* Color a map of West Africa's desert, Sahel, savanna and forest zones and list resources from each.

*How to teach:* Your child colors the four zones and lists resources from each. Check before moving on: ask "Resource from the Sahara?" Look for: Salt (e.g., mines at Taghaza).

- **Q:** Resource from the Sahara?
  **A:** Salt (e.g., mines at Taghaza).
- **Q:** From the forest/savanna south?
  **A:** Gold (Bambuk, Bure), kola nuts, ivory.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Gold and salt
*Student:* Explain why salt was as valuable as gold and simulate silent barter with a family member.

*How to teach:* Explain silent barter, then simulate it with your child. Check before moving on: ask "Why was salt worth gold?" Look for: People in the south needed salt to survive and preserve food but had none; northerners wanted gold.

- **Q:** Why was salt worth gold?
  **A:** People in the south needed salt to survive and preserve food but had none; northerners wanted gold.
- **Q:** How did silent barter work?
  **A:** Traders left salt, withdrew; gold miners left gold beside it; trading continued until both were satisfied, without speaking.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ghana Empire
*Student:* Read al-Bakri's description of Ghana's king and court (primary source) and list signs of wealth and power.

*How to teach:* Read al-Bakri. Your child lists signs of wealth and power. Check before moving on: ask "Signs of the king's wealth?" Look for: Gold ornaments, horses with gold trappings, dogs with gold collars, and the king keeping all gold nuggets (people could trade gold dust).

- **Q:** Signs of the king's wealth?
  **A:** Gold ornaments, horses with gold trappings, dogs with gold collars, and the king keeping all gold nuggets (people could trade gold dust).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Family and specialization
*Student:* Explain how family clans and specialized crafts like blacksmithing supported cities.

*How to teach:* Your child explains clans and crafts like blacksmithing. Check before moving on: ask "Why were blacksmiths important?" Look for: They made iron tools and weapons for farming and war, giving Ghana military strength.

- **Q:** Why were blacksmiths important?
  **A:** They made iron tools and weapons for farming and war, giving Ghana military strength.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on geography and Ghana.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Check before moving on: ask "How did Ghana grow rich?" Look for: By taxing the gold-salt trade passing through its land.

- **Q:** How did Ghana grow rich?
  **A:** By taxing the gold-salt trade passing through its land.

**Assessment:** Map and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: four vegetation zones; salt from the north, gold from the south; silent barter; Ghana taxed trade; king kept nuggets; capital Koumbi Saleh; iron weapons. Map: zones colored with resources.

## Spanish 1A: Proyecto: Una persona famosa del mundo hispano

**Prepare:** Poster board or slides, library books or reliable sites, SpanishDict.com. Help choose a figure (Frida Kahlo, Lionel Messi, Sonia Sotomayor, Ellen Ochoa, Gabriel García Márquez, Selena, etc.).

**Watch for:**
- Translating long English sentences word for word. Use structures you know: Es…, Tiene…, Es de…, Le gusta…
- Reading the poster aloud. Present from key words to sound natural.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose and research
*Student:* Choose a figure (e.g., Frida Kahlo, Lionel Messi, Sonia Sotomayor, Ellen Ochoa) and collect five facts in English, then key Spanish words.

*How to teach:* Choose a figure; collect 5 facts and key Spanish words.

- **Q:** How do you say 'She is a famous artist'?
  **A:** Es una artista famosa.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Write
*Student:* Write an 8–10 sentence profile in Spanish using ser, nationalities, personality, and physical description.

*How to teach:* Write an 8–10 sentence profile.

- **Q:** Write: 'Ellen Ochoa is an American astronaut. She is intelligent.'
  **A:** Ellen Ochoa es una astronauta estadounidense. Es inteligente.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Revise
*Student:* Check adjective agreement and articles with a checklist; look up three new words on SpanishDict.

*How to teach:* Revise with checklist; look up three words.

- **Q:** Find the error: 'Sonia Sotomayor es una juez importanta.'
  **A:** 'importanta' should be 'importante' (and 'juez' → 'jueza' is also accepted): Sonia Sotomayor es una jueza importante.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Present
*Student:* Create a poster or 3-slide presentation and practice presenting it without reading word for word.

*How to teach:* Make the poster/slides; practice without reading.

- **Q:** Why is 'el artista' / 'la artista' the same word?
  **A:** Nouns ending in -ista keep the same form; only the article shows gender.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 assessment
*Student:* Present the profile (1–2 minutes) and take the unit 3 written test on ser and adjective agreement.

*How to teach:* Present (1–2 minutes); give unit 3 test.

- **Q:** Translate: 'Lionel Messi is Argentine, short and very hardworking.'
  **A:** Lionel Messi es argentino, bajo y muy trabajador.

**Assessment:** Presentational rubric and unit 3 test.

**Answer key:** Presentational rubric (1–4 each): content (8–10 sentences: name, nationality, origin, profession, personality, appearance, why important), language (ser, tener, agreement, articles), pronunciation/fluency, visuals. Unit 3 test: ser conjugation, pronouns, adjective agreement, nationalities, articles, physical description. Model sentence: Frida Kahlo es una artista mexicana. Es de Coyoacán, México. Es talentosa y fuerte. Tiene el pelo negro y los ojos castaños.

## Visual & Performing Arts 7: Pitch, staff and keyboard

**Prepare:** Keyboard (app or real piano), Chrome Music Lab, musictheory.net note-identification exercises.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing treble and bass clef notes. Treble lines E-G-B-D-F, spaces F-A-C-E; bass lines G-B-D-F-A, spaces A-C-E-G.
- Thinking every pair of neighboring white keys is a whole step. E–F and B–C are half steps (no black key between).

### Day 1 (Monday): Treble clef
*Student:* Learn treble clef note names and complete a note-naming drill.

*How to teach:* Teach treble clef: lines Every Good Boy Does Fine, spaces F-A-C-E. Drill on musictheory.net.

- **Q:** Name the treble clef lines from bottom to top.
  **A:** E, G, B, D, F.
- **Q:** Name the treble clef spaces.
  **A:** F, A, C, E.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Bass clef
*Student:* Learn bass clef note names and complete a drill.

*How to teach:* Teach bass clef: lines Good Boys Do Fine Always, spaces All Cows Eat Grass. Drill.

- **Q:** Name the bass clef spaces from bottom to top.
  **A:** A, C, E, G.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Keyboard
*Student:* Locate notes on a keyboard and play C, G and F major scales.

*How to teach:* Find C (left of two black keys), then play C, G and F major scales.

- **Q:** Which black key is used in G major?
  **A:** F-sharp.
- **Q:** Which black key is used in F major?
  **A:** B-flat.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Steps
*Student:* Build major scales using the whole-whole-half pattern.

*How to teach:* Build major scales using W-W-H-W-W-W-H; try D major.

- **Q:** What is a half step?
  **A:** The distance from one key to the very next key (white or black).
- **Q:** Build D major.
  **A:** D E F# G A B C# D.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a note-reading and scale quiz.

*How to teach:* Take the note-reading and scale quiz.

- **Q:** What are ledger lines?
  **A:** Short lines added above or below the staff for notes outside its range.

**Assessment:** Note-reading quiz.

**Answer key:** Note-reading quiz: name 10 treble and 10 bass notes (80% proficient). Scales: C major = C D E F G A B C (no sharps/flats); G major = G A B C D E F# G (one sharp); F major = F G A Bb C D E F (one flat). Major scale pattern: W-W-H-W-W-W-H.

## Physical Education 7: Volleyball: forearm pass and set

**Prepare:** Volleyball (or beach ball for beginners), a wall, a net or rope tied about 7 ft high.

**Watch for:**
- Swinging the arms to bump. Keep the platform still and angled; legs and shoulders provide lift.
- Hitting the ball with hands or thumbs separated. Form a flat platform with forearms together and thumbs parallel.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ready position & bump
*Student:* Practice ready position and forearm pass to self 20 times, then against a wall.

*How to teach:* Ready position: feet shoulder-width, knees bent, weight forward, arms out. Platform: hands clasped, thumbs parallel, elbows locked, contact on forearms above wrists. 20 bumps to self, then against a wall.

- **Q:** Where should the ball contact your arms in a forearm pass?
  **A:** On the flat part of the forearms, just above the wrists.
- **Q:** What creates the lift in a bump?
  **A:** Bending and extending the legs, not swinging the arms.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Set
*Student:* Practice overhead sets to self and to a wall target.

*How to teach:* Set: hands above forehead forming a triangle window, contact with finger pads, extend arms and legs. Set to self and to a wall target.

- **Q:** Which part of the hands contacts the ball in a set?
  **A:** The finger pads (not palms), with hands shaped in a triangle above the forehead.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Partner passing
*Student:* Bump and set with a partner, counting consecutive contacts.

*How to teach:* Bump and set with a partner counting consecutive contacts.

- **Q:** When do you use a set vs a bump?
  **A:** Set for high balls you can get under, usually the second contact; bump for low or hard-driven balls.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Movement to ball
*Student:* Practice shuffling to the ball before passing.

*How to teach:* Toss to the sides; your child shuffles (no crossing feet) to get behind the ball before passing.

- **Q:** Why move your feet before passing?
  **A:** Getting behind the ball lets you face the target and keep the platform angled correctly.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Complete a consecutive-pass skill test (target 15).

*How to teach:* Run the consecutive-pass test.

- **Q:** What is the platform?
  **A:** The flat surface formed by both forearms held together to pass the ball.

**Assessment:** Volleyball pass test.

**Answer key:** Consecutive-pass test: count consecutive controlled bumps/sets (to self or with a wall/partner) without the ball dropping or being caught. 15+ proficient, 25+ advanced, 8–14 developing. Note cues: ready position, platform, contact on forearms, legs lift.

## Health Education 7: Bullying, cyberbullying and conflict resolution

**Prepare:** Preview a Common Sense Education cyberbullying lesson. Write three upstander scenarios (in person, group chat, online game). Think of a recent sibling conflict to use.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking teasing that 'everyone does' isn't bullying. Bullying is repeated, intentional and involves a power imbalance; it hurts regardless of intent claims.
- Responding to cyberbullies or deleting the evidence. Don't reply; screenshot/save, block, report and tell an adult.

### Day 1 (Monday): Types of bullying
*Student:* Define physical, verbal, social and cyberbullying with examples.

*How to teach:* Define bullying (repeated, intentional, power imbalance) and the four types. Your child gives an example of each.

- **Q:** Give an example of social bullying.
  **A:** Excluding someone on purpose, spreading rumors, or getting others to ignore someone.
- **Q:** What three features make something bullying rather than a single conflict?
  **A:** It is intentional, repeated and involves a power imbalance.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Upstander
*Student:* Learn upstander strategies and write responses to three scenarios.

*How to teach:* Teach upstander strategies: don't laugh, speak up if safe, support the target privately, get an adult, report. Your child writes responses to three scenarios.

- **Q:** Someone posts a mean meme about a classmate in your group chat. What can an upstander do?
  **A:** Don't like or share it, say 'Not cool,' reach out to the classmate, screenshot and report it to an adult.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Conflict resolution
*Student:* Learn a six-step conflict resolution model and apply it to a sibling conflict.

*How to teach:* Teach: 1) cool off 2) each tells their side with I-messages 3) listen and restate 4) brainstorm solutions 5) choose a fair solution 6) follow up. Apply to a real sibling conflict.

- **Q:** Why is cooling off the first step?
  **A:** Strong emotions make it hard to think and listen; calming down prevents saying hurtful things.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Decision making
*Student:* Use the DECIDE model on a social situation.

*How to teach:* Teach DECIDE: Define the problem, Explore options, Consider consequences, Identify values, Decide and act, Evaluate. Your child walks through a social dilemma.

- **Q:** What do the letters in DECIDE stand for?
  **A:** Define the problem, Explore alternatives, Consider consequences, Identify values, Decide and act, Evaluate the result.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write an action plan for responding to cyberbullying.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a step-by-step cyberbullying action plan; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Should you reply to a cyberbully?
  **A:** No — don't respond; save evidence, block, report and tell an adult.

**Assessment:** Action plan.

**Answer key:** Cyberbullying action plan rubric: 4 = includes don't respond/retaliate, save evidence (screenshots), block the person, report on the platform, tell a trusted adult (and school if a classmate), and support the target; 3 = most steps; 2 = only 1–2 steps; 1 = incomplete or suggests retaliating.
