# Teacher's Guide — 7th Grade, Week 2

## English Language Arts 7: Suspense: 'The Most Dangerous Game' by Richard Connell

**Prepare:** Print 'The Most Dangerous Game' (public domain), plot diagram template. Preview: the villain hunts humans; violence is mostly implied but tense.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing setting description with plot events. Setting = time, place, atmosphere (dark, 'Ship-Trap Island').
- Thinking foreshadowing is the same as a prediction the reader makes. Foreshadowing is a clue the author plants.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ship-Trap Island
*Student:* Read the first third of 'The Most Dangerous Game' and list details of setting that create a sense of danger.

*How to teach:* Read the first third aloud. Your child lists setting details that create danger (the island's name, sailors' dread, the dark, the rocks, the 'moist black velvet' night). Check before moving on: ask "Why do sailors fear Ship-Trap Island?" Look for: It has a bad reputation; Whitney says sailors have a 'curious dread' of the place.

- **Q:** Why do sailors fear Ship-Trap Island?
  **A:** It has a bad reputation; Whitney says sailors have a 'curious dread' of the place.
- **Q:** What happens to Rainsford early in the story?
  **A:** He falls off the yacht at night and swims to the island.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rising action
*Student:* Read the middle section and chart Rainsford's three tricks on a plot diagram, noting foreshadowing clues.

*How to teach:* Read the middle. Your child charts the three tricks on a plot diagram and notes foreshadowing (e.g., Whitney's talk of 'hunters and huntees'). Check before moving on: ask "Name Rainsford's three tricks." Look for: Malay man-catcher (injures Zaroff's shoulder), Burmese tiger pit (kills one of the hounds), Ugandan knife trap (kills Ivan).

- **Q:** Name Rainsford's three tricks.
  **A:** Malay man-catcher (injures Zaroff's shoulder), Burmese tiger pit (kills one of the hounds), Ugandan knife trap (kills Ivan).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Climax and ending
*Student:* Finish the story and write a paragraph explaining how the jungle setting drives the conflict, with three cited details.

*How to teach:* Finish the story. Your child writes a paragraph on how the jungle setting drives conflict with three cited details. Check before moving on: ask "How does Rainsford escape the final chase?" Look for: He leaps off a cliff into the sea, then sneaks into Zaroff's bedroom.

- **Q:** How does Rainsford escape the final chase?
  **A:** He leaps off a cliff into the sea, then sneaks into Zaroff's bedroom.
- **Q:** What does the last line imply?
  **A:** 'He had never slept in a better bed' implies Rainsford killed Zaroff.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Word study: roots
*Student:* Study roots 'mal', 'bene', 'vis/vid', 'dict' and 'port'; find story words and build a 15-word root web.

*How to teach:* Teach each root with two examples on the board (mal: malfunction, malicious; bene: benefit, benevolent; vis/vid: vision, evident; dict: predict, dictionary; port: transport, portable). Your child skims the story for words with these roots, then builds a 15-word web with a definition for each. Check by covering the definitions and having your child explain three words using the root.

- **Q:** Meaning of 'mal' and an example?
  **A:** Bad — malicious, malfunction.
- **Q:** 'Port'?
  **A:** Carry — transport, portable.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a reading quiz with two constructed responses and a 10-item root quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the reading quiz with two constructed responses, then the 10-item root quiz, without notes. Grade together using the key, and have your child rewrite one constructed response to add a quotation if it lacked one. Check before moving on: ask "What does Zaroff hunt and why?" Look for: Humans, because animals no longer challenged him; he wants prey that can reason.

- **Q:** What does Zaroff hunt and why?
  **A:** Humans, because animals no longer challenged him; he wants prey that can reason.

**Assessment:** Plot diagram, setting paragraph, reading and root quizzes.

**Answer key:** Reading quiz: CR1 (setting builds suspense) proficient answers cite the island's name, the dark jungle, the sounds of the hunt. CR2 (Rainsford changes) proficient: he begins saying animals have no feelings and ends understanding the hunted's fear. Root quiz: mal = bad, bene = good, vis/vid = see, dict = say, port = carry (plus five word meanings: benefit = good result, dictate = say aloud to be written, visible = can be seen, malnutrition = bad nourishment, export = carry out).

## Mathematics 7: Scale factor, distance and area

**Prepare:** Graph paper, pattern blocks (or cut 1-inch paper squares and equilateral triangles), calculator.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking area scales by the same factor as length. Build it: a 1x1 square at factor 2 needs 4 squares, not 2. Area scales by the factor squared.
- Confusing the direction of the scale factor (original→copy vs copy→original). Always say 'from ___ to ___' and divide copy by original.

### Day 1 (Monday): Finding scale factors
*Student:* Find the scale factor between pairs of figures, including factors less than 1, and use them to compute missing side lengths.

*How to teach:* Model: scale factor = copy length ÷ original length. Work one pair with a factor less than 1 (10 → 4 gives 0.4). Your child finds factors for several pairs and uses them to find missing sides.

- **Q:** A triangle has sides 5, 8 and 10. In a scaled copy the side of 8 becomes 10. Scale factor?
  **A:** 10 ÷ 8 = 1.25 (so 5 → 6.25 and 10 → 12.5).
- **Q:** A side of 12 at factor 2/3?
  **A:** 8.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Reversing a scale
*Student:* Scale a triangle up by 4 then back down by 1/4 and explain why the reciprocal undoes the scaling.

*How to teach:* Draw a triangle, scale it by 4, then ask what factor gets back to the original. Your child performs both scalings on graph paper and writes why 1/4 undoes 4 (4 × 1/4 = 1). Check before moving on: ask "A copy was made at scale factor 5/2. What factor takes the copy back to the original?" Look for: 2/5, the reciprocal.

- **Q:** A copy was made at scale factor 5/2. What factor takes the copy back to the original?
  **A:** 2/5, the reciprocal.
- **Q:** Side 6 scaled by 4 then by 1/4?
  **A:** 6 → 24 → 6.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Scaling area
*Student:* Build squares and triangles from pattern blocks at scale factors 2 and 3, count pieces, and record that area grows by 4 and 9.

*How to teach:* Build a square from blocks, then build it at factor 2 and 3 and count pieces. Your child records side factor vs number of pieces in a table and states the pattern. Check before moving on: ask "A shape made of 5 blocks is scaled by factor 3. How many blocks?" Look for: 45 (5 × 3² = 5 × 9).

- **Q:** A shape made of 5 blocks is scaled by factor 3. How many blocks?
  **A:** 45 (5 × 3² = 5 × 9).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Area problems
*Student:* Solve 8 problems finding the area of scaled rectangles and triangles from the original area and scale factor.

*How to teach:* Model: new area = original area × (scale factor)². Work one example together, then your child solves the 8 problems, showing the squared factor each time. Check before moving on: ask "Rectangle area 15 cm², scale factor 4. New area?" Look for: 15 × 16 = 240 cm².

- **Q:** Rectangle area 15 cm², scale factor 4. New area?
  **A:** 15 × 16 = 240 cm².
- **Q:** Triangle area 36 in², scale factor 1/3. New area?
  **A:** 36 × 1/9 = 4 in².

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on scale factor, reciprocal scaling and area scaling with one written explanation.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz below, then the written explanation prompt: 'Why does area grow by the square of the scale factor?' Check before moving on: ask "A copy has area 50 and the original had area 2. What scale factor?" Look for: 5 (area grew ×25 = 5²).

- **Q:** A copy has area 50 and the original had area 2. What scale factor?
  **A:** 5 (area grew ×25 = 5²).

**Assessment:** Quiz and written explanation of why area scales by the square of the factor.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) 8 → 20: factor 2.5. 2) 9 → 6: factor 2/3. 3) Factor to undo 3/5: 5/3. 4) Area 12, factor 3: 108. 5) Area 80, factor 1/2: 20. Explanation rubric (4 = proficient): states that both length AND width are multiplied by the factor, so area is multiplied by factor × factor; gives an example (2x3 rectangle, factor 2 → 4x6, area 6 → 24 = 6 × 4). 3 = correct rule, weak example; 2 = rule only; 1 = says area scales by the factor.

## Science 7 (Integrated): Elements and the periodic table

**Prepare:** Periodic table printout, colored pencils; salt, sand, paper clip bits (or iron filings), magnet, coffee filter, funnel, pan for evaporating (adult for stovetop or leave in the sun).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking salt disappears when it dissolves. It is still there — evaporate the water to get it back.
- Calling any pure-looking substance an element. Water and salt are compounds (two or more elements chemically bonded).

### Day 1 (Monday): Element hunt
*Student:* Find 10 elements in household items (aluminum foil, copper wire, carbon pencil lead) and locate them on a periodic table.

*How to teach:* Your child hunts for 10 elements at home (aluminum foil — Al, copper wire — Cu, pencil 'lead' — C as graphite, iron nail — Fe, helium balloon — He, etc.) and finds each on the table. Check before moving on: ask "Symbol for aluminum? For iron?" Look for: Al; Fe.

- **Q:** Symbol for aluminum? For iron?
  **A:** Al; Fe.
- **Q:** Is pencil 'lead' really lead?
  **A:** No — it is graphite, a form of carbon, mixed with clay.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Reading the table
*Student:* Identify atomic number, symbol and mass for 15 elements and color-code metals, nonmetals and metalloids.

*How to teach:* Model reading the box for carbon (atomic number 6, symbol C, mass ≈ 12.01). Your child records 15 elements and colors metals, nonmetals and metalloids along the staircase line. Check before moving on: ask "What does atomic number tell you?" Look for: The number of protons in each atom of that element.

- **Q:** What does atomic number tell you?
  **A:** The number of protons in each atom of that element.
- **Q:** Is silicon a metal, nonmetal or metalloid?
  **A:** Metalloid.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Mixtures lab
*Student:* Separate a mixture of salt, sand and paper-clip bits using a magnet, filtering and evaporation.

*How to teach:* Your child plans the separation order, then does it: magnet pulls out the clips, add water to dissolve salt, filter out sand, evaporate the water to recover salt. Check before moving on: ask "Why must you filter before evaporating?" Look for: Filtering removes the sand; evaporating then leaves only the salt that was dissolved.

- **Q:** Why must you filter before evaporating?
  **A:** Filtering removes the sand; evaporating then leaves only the salt that was dissolved.
- **Q:** Which property does each step use?
  **A:** Magnetism (iron), solubility (salt dissolves, sand doesn't), particle size (filter), boiling point (water evaporates).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compounds vs. mixtures
*Student:* Sort 12 substances (water, air, salt, trail mix, sugar) as element, compound or mixture with justification.

*How to teach:* Define element, compound and mixture. Your child sorts 12 substances with one-sentence justifications. Check before moving on: ask "Classify water, air, salt, trail mix, sugar, gold." Look for: Water — compound; air — mixture; salt — compound; trail mix — mixture; sugar — compound; gold — element.

- **Q:** Classify water, air, salt, trail mix, sugar, gold.
  **A:** Water — compound; air — mixture; salt — compound; trail mix — mixture; sugar — compound; gold — element.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on elements, compounds, mixtures and periodic-table reading.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (see key). Check before moving on: ask "How is a compound different from a mixture?" Look for: A compound has elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio and can't be separated physically; a mixture's parts keep their own properties and can be separated physically.

- **Q:** How is a compound different from a mixture?
  **A:** A compound has elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio and can't be separated physically; a mixture's parts keep their own properties and can be separated physically.

**Assessment:** Separation lab write-up and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz: 1) Element = one kind of atom. 2) Compound = two or more elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio (H2O). 3) Mixture = physically combined, separable (sand + salt). 4) Na = sodium, atomic number 11. 5) O has 8 protons. 6) Metals are on the left/center, nonmetals upper right. Lab write-up: correct order (magnet → dissolve → filter → evaporate) with the property used at each step.

## World History & Geography: Medieval and Early Modern Times: Why did Rome fall?

**Prepare:** Cause-effect organizer; map of barbarian invasion routes; St. Jerome's letter (Letter 127 or 128, Fordham Sourcebook); five short documents on Rome's decline.

**Watch for:**
- Picking one single cause. Historians see many interacting causes: political, economic, military and outside pressure.
- Thinking 'barbarian' means uncivilized savages. Romans used it for non-Romans; many Goths were Christian and had served in Rome's army.

### Day 1 (Monday): Internal problems
*Student:* Read about political corruption, inflation and reliance on mercenaries and sort them into political, economic and military causes.

*How to teach:* Read a summary of 3rd–5th-century problems. Your child sorts causes into political, economic and military columns. Check before moving on: ask "Sort: inflation from debased coins; frequent civil wars; hiring Germanic mercenaries." Look for: Economic; political; military.

- **Q:** Sort: inflation from debased coins; frequent civil wars; hiring Germanic mercenaries.
  **A:** Economic; political; military.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Invasions
*Student:* Map the routes of the Huns, Visigoths and Vandals and the sack of Rome in 410 CE.

*How to teach:* Your child maps the routes of the Huns, Visigoths and Vandals and marks the sack of Rome. Check before moving on: ask "Who sacked Rome in 410?" Look for: The Visigoths under Alaric.

- **Q:** Who sacked Rome in 410?
  **A:** The Visigoths under Alaric.
- **Q:** When did the Western Empire end?
  **A:** 476 CE, when Odoacer deposed Emperor Romulus Augustulus.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Primary source: Jerome
*Student:* Read St. Jerome's letter reacting to the sack of Rome and analyze his point of view and tone.

*How to teach:* Read Jerome's reaction. Your child identifies his tone (shock, grief) and point of view (a Christian scholar living far away in Bethlehem). Check before moving on: ask "What does Jerome mean by 'the city which had taken the whole world was itself taken'?" Look for: Rome, which conquered so much, was itself conquered — showing his shock that the unthinkable had happened.

- **Q:** What does Jerome mean by 'the city which had taken the whole world was itself taken'?
  **A:** Rome, which conquered so much, was itself conquered — showing his shock that the unthinkable had happened.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mini-DBQ
*Student:* Analyze 5 short documents and rank the causes of Rome's fall from most to least important.

*How to teach:* Your child reads five documents, notes the cause each supports, and ranks causes with reasons. Check before moving on: ask "Example reasoning for ranking economic decline first?" Look for: Without money, emperors couldn't pay soldiers, so they relied on mercenaries and couldn't defend the borders.

- **Q:** Example reasoning for ranking economic decline first?
  **A:** Without money, emperors couldn't pay soldiers, so they relied on mercenaries and couldn't defend the borders.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a 5-paragraph essay explaining the three most important causes of Rome's fall with evidence.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a 5-paragraph essay on the three most important causes with document evidence. Check before moving on: ask "What must the thesis do?" Look for: Name the three causes and argue they were the most important.

- **Q:** What must the thesis do?
  **A:** Name the three causes and argue they were the most important.

**Assessment:** Mini-DBQ essay.

**Answer key:** Mini-DBQ essay rubric (4–1): Thesis names three causes; each body paragraph uses at least one document plus explanation; accurate facts (410 sack, 476 end, inflation, mercenaries, divided empire under Diocletian); conclusion; organization. Proficient = 3+ in each area.

## Spanish 1A: ¿Cómo estás? Feelings and formal vs. informal address

**Prepare:** Draw emoji feeling cards (happy, tired, sick, so-so) and 12 index cards with people (best friend, grandmother, doctor, teacher, cousin, little brother, store clerk, principal, neighbor, pet, police officer, classmate). Find a UT Austin 'Spanish Proficiency Exercises' greeting clip.

**Watch for:**
- Saying 'Estoy bueno' for 'I'm good.' Say 'Estoy bien' (bien is an adverb and doesn't change).
- Using tú with adults you don't know. Use usted with elders, strangers, doctors and teachers; tú with friends, family your age and children.

### Day 1 (Monday): ¿Cómo estás?
*Student:* Learn bien, muy bien, regular, mal, así así and practice responding to ¿Cómo estás? with emoji cards drawn at random.

*How to teach:* Draw a card; your child answers '¿Cómo estás?' to match: Muy bien / Bien / Regular / Así así / Mal / Estoy cansado(a).

- **Q:** How do you say 'I'm tired' (girl)?
  **A:** Estoy cansada.
- **Q:** What does 'así así' mean?
  **A:** So-so.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tú vs. usted
*Student:* Sort 12 people (best friend, grandmother, doctor, teacher, cousin) into tú or usted and write the matching greeting for each.

*How to teach:* Sort the 12 people into tú or usted and write a greeting for each (¿Cómo estás? vs ¿Cómo está usted?).

- **Q:** Which form do you use with a doctor?
  **A:** Usted: ¿Cómo está usted?
- **Q:** Which form do you use with your best friend?
  **A:** Tú: ¿Cómo estás?

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Listening
*Student:* Watch a Spanish Proficiency Exercises (UT Austin) greeting clip twice and note each speaker's name and how they feel.

*How to teach:* Watch the clip twice; note each speaker's name and feeling.

- **Q:** If a speaker says 'Estoy un poco cansado,' how does he feel?
  **A:** A little tired.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Role-play
*Student:* Role-play meeting a neighbor (usted) and then a new teammate (tú), switching the forms correctly.

*How to teach:* Role-play meeting an adult neighbor (usted) and a teammate (tú).

- **Q:** Greet an older neighbor in the afternoon and ask how she is.
  **A:** Buenas tardes, señora. ¿Cómo está usted?

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a 15-item quiz matching questions to responses and choosing tú/usted, then do a 1-minute oral check-in with a parent.

*How to teach:* Give the 15-item quiz and a 1-minute oral check-in.

- **Q:** Answer: ¿Qué tal?
  **A:** Bien, gracias. ¿Y tú? (or another feeling).

**Assessment:** Quiz (80%+) and oral check-in rubric.

**Answer key:** 15-item quiz: matching items like ¿Cómo estás? → Bien, gracias, ¿y tú?; ¿Cómo está usted? → Muy bien, gracias, ¿y usted?; ¿Cómo te llamas? → Me llamo…; ¿Qué tal? → Regular. Tú/usted: best friend tú, cousin tú, sibling tú, classmate tú, pet tú; grandmother usted (or tú in many families — accept either with reason), doctor usted, teacher usted, principal usted, store clerk usted, police officer usted, neighbor (adult) usted. 12/15 = 80% passing. Oral check-in: answers ¿Cómo estás? with a feeling and asks back with correct form.

## Visual & Performing Arts 7: Value and shading

**Prepare:** Pencils 2H–6B, a blending stump or tissue, an egg and a ball, a desk lamp (single strong light from one side). Find a Renaissance drawing (e.g., a Leonardo or Dürer study) online.

**Watch for:**
- Outlining shapes with a dark line and filling in. Real objects have edges defined by value changes, not outlines.
- Forgetting reflected light, making shadows flat. Light bounces off the table into the shadow side, so it's slightly lighter near the bottom edge.

### Day 1 (Monday): Value scale
*Student:* Create a 9-step value scale with pencil and a second using hatching and cross-hatching.

*How to teach:* Make a 9-step pencil value scale (light pressure/2H to heavy/6B), then a hatching and cross-hatching scale.

- **Q:** What is value?
  **A:** How light or dark a color or tone is.
- **Q:** How does cross-hatching make a darker value?
  **A:** Layers of crossing lines placed closer together cover more paper.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Shading spheres
*Student:* Draw and shade three spheres with highlight, core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow.

*How to teach:* Draw three spheres and shade with highlight, midtone, core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow.

- **Q:** Put these in order from lightest to darkest on a sphere: core shadow, highlight, reflected light, midtone.
  **A:** Highlight, midtone, reflected light, core shadow (the core shadow is darkest on the object; cast shadow nearest the object is darkest overall).
- **Q:** Which direction does the cast shadow fall?
  **A:** Away from the light source.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Egg study
*Student:* Light an egg with a lamp and draw it realistically for 25 minutes.

*How to teach:* Set up the egg under the lamp; 25-minute realistic study, building value gradually.

- **Q:** Why start shading lightly?
  **A:** You can always darken, but heavy graphite is hard to erase.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Texture shading
*Student:* Shade a crumpled paper bag using varied pencil grades.

*How to teach:* Shade a crumpled paper bag using different pencil grades for sharp folds and soft planes.

- **Q:** Which pencil is softer and darker: 2H or 6B?
  **A:** 6B (B pencils are softer/darker; H pencils are harder/lighter).

### Day 5 (Friday): Reflect
*Student:* Compare your egg study to a Renaissance drawing and note one technique to improve.

*How to teach:* Compare the egg to a Renaissance drawing (e.g., Leonardo's studies, which use hatching and soft modeling called sfumato).

- **Q:** What technique did Leonardo use for soft transitions between light and shadow?
  **A:** Sfumato (smoky, gradual blending); he also used hatching in drawings.

**Assessment:** Value scale and shaded egg study.

**Answer key:** Value scale: 9 even steps from white to black with no jumps; hatching/cross-hatching scale also shows 9 steps. Egg study rubric (1–4): accurate oval shape, full value range, highlight, core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow placed correctly for the light source, smooth gradation, no outline. Proficient = all 4 shadow parts present and consistent with the lamp.

## Physical Education 7: Strength and flexibility baseline

**Prepare:** Mat, measuring strip for curl-ups (3 inches/7.6 cm wide for ages 10+), yardstick or sit-and-reach box, 12-inch ruler for trunk lift, FITNESSGRAM cadence audio (one rep every 3 seconds). Weigh/measure privately; body composition is optional.

**Watch for:**
- Pulling on the neck or lifting the whole back off the mat during curl-ups. Keep arms straight, palms sliding along the strip, head returns to mat each rep.
- Lifting the trunk as high as possible in the trunk lift. The test stops at 12 inches; arching higher strains the back.

### Day 1 (Monday): Curl-up form
*Student:* Practice cadence curl-ups with correct form, then test to maximum.

*How to teach:* Teach curl-up form: knees bent ~140°, feet flat, arms straight with fingers on the strip, curl up until fingertips slide across, back down until head touches. Practice at cadence, then test.

- **Q:** What is the difference between muscular strength and muscular endurance?
  **A:** Strength is the most force a muscle can produce once; endurance is how many times or how long it can keep working.
- **Q:** What does the curl-up test measure?
  **A:** Abdominal muscular strength and endurance.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Push-up form
*Student:* Practice 90-degree push-ups at cadence, then test to maximum.

*How to teach:* Teach 90° push-up: body straight like a plank, lower until elbows reach 90°, push up to straight arms, at the 3-second cadence. Test to max.

- **Q:** What are two common push-up form errors?
  **A:** Sagging hips or piking, not lowering to 90° at the elbow, not fully extending arms, not keeping cadence.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Trunk lift & sit-and-reach
*Student:* Test trunk lift and back-saver sit-and-reach and record results.

*How to teach:* Trunk lift: lie face down, lift upper body slowly up to 12 inches max, hold to measure, two tries. Sit-and-reach: one leg straight, the other bent, reach forward four times and hold the fourth; test each side.

- **Q:** Why is the sit-and-reach called 'back-saver'?
  **A:** Only one leg is straight at a time, reducing strain on the lower back.
- **Q:** What does the trunk lift measure?
  **A:** Back extensor strength and flexibility.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Body composition (optional)
*Student:* Record height and weight privately with a parent and learn what the BMI/Healthy Fitness Zone measures.

*How to teach:* Optional: record height and weight privately; explain BMI is a screening estimate of body composition and doesn't measure muscle vs fat directly.

- **Q:** What is body composition?
  **A:** The proportion of fat mass to lean mass (muscle, bone, organs) in the body.

### Day 5 (Friday): Record & reflect
*Student:* Enter all scores in a fitness portfolio and highlight strengths and areas to improve.

*How to teach:* Your child enters all scores in the portfolio and highlights strengths and one area to improve.

- **Q:** Name the five health-related fitness components.
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility and body composition.

**Assessment:** Complete FITNESSGRAM baseline record.

**Answer key:** FITNESSGRAM baseline record: PACER/mile, curl-ups (count to max 75, stops at second form error or when cadence is missed), 90° push-ups (max 75, stops at second form error), trunk lift (inches, max 12), back-saver sit-and-reach (inches each side, max 12), optional BMI/body composition kept private; each with HFZ status. Proficient = all tests completed with correct form and recorded with dates.

## Health Education 7: MyPlate and reading labels

**Prepare:** Bookmark MyPlate.gov (MyPlate Plan tool needs age, sex, height, weight, activity). Gather two cereal boxes, five drink containers with labels, a teaspoon and a clear cup and sugar to measure. Body measurements are private — enter them yourself if preferred.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking % Daily Value is the percent of the food that is that nutrient. It is the percent of a whole day's recommended amount in ONE serving; 5% DV or less is low, 20% or more is high.
- Comparing labels without checking serving size. Two cereals with different serving sizes must be compared per equal amount (e.g., per cup or per 100 g).

### Day 1 (Monday): MyPlate
*Student:* Explore MyPlate.gov and get a personalized MyPlate Plan for your age and activity level.

*How to teach:* Explore MyPlate.gov together and run the MyPlate Plan for your child's age and activity level. Your child copies the daily targets for each food group into the journal.

- **Q:** What are the five MyPlate food groups?
  **A:** Fruits, vegetables, grains, protein foods and dairy (or fortified soy alternatives).
- **Q:** What fraction of the plate should be fruits and vegetables?
  **A:** About half.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Label reading
*Student:* Compare labels of two cereals on serving size, added sugar and fiber.

*How to teach:* Model comparing two cereal labels side by side: serving size, added sugars and dietary fiber. Your child decides which is the healthier everyday choice and explains using numbers.

- **Q:** Cereal A has 12 g added sugar (24% DV) per serving; cereal B has 3 g (6% DV). Which is high and which is low in added sugar?
  **A:** A is high (20% DV or more); B is close to low (5% DV or less is low, so 6% is moderate-low). B is the better everyday choice.
- **Q:** Why look at fiber?
  **A:** Fiber helps digestion and keeps you full; higher fiber (3 g+ per serving) usually means more whole grain.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sugar math
*Student:* Convert grams of sugar in five drinks to teaspoons and measure them out.

*How to teach:* Teach: 4 grams of sugar ≈ 1 teaspoon. Your child divides grams of sugar by 4 for five drinks, then measures that many teaspoons into a cup to see it.

- **Q:** A 20-oz soda has 65 g sugar. About how many teaspoons is that?
  **A:** 65 ÷ 4 ≈ 16 teaspoons.
- **Q:** A sports drink has 34 g sugar. How many teaspoons?
  **A:** 34 ÷ 4 = 8.5 teaspoons.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Meal plan
*Student:* Plan a full day of meals and snacks that meets MyPlate recommendations.

*How to teach:* Your child plans breakfast, lunch, dinner and two snacks using their MyPlate targets, listing amounts (cups/ounce-equivalents). Check that every food group appears.

- **Q:** Give an example of one ounce-equivalent of grains.
  **A:** One slice of bread, 1 cup of dry cereal, or ½ cup cooked rice or pasta.
- **Q:** Name a snack that includes two food groups.
  **A:** Apple slices with peanut butter (fruit + protein), yogurt with berries (dairy + fruit), or whole-grain crackers with cheese (grain + dairy).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Present the meal plan and evaluate it against MyPlate.

*How to teach:* Your child presents the plan and scores it against MyPlate using the rubric, noting one change. Ask follow-up questions about drinks and added sugar.

- **Q:** Why should at least half your grains be whole grains?
  **A:** Whole grains keep the bran and germ, giving more fiber, B vitamins and minerals than refined grains.
- **Q:** What is the daily limit for added sugars in the Dietary Guidelines?
