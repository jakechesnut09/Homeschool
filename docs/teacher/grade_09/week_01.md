# Teacher's Guide — 9th Grade, Week 1

## English 9: Plot, conflict, and suspense in "The Most Dangerous Game"

**Prepare:** Print or download "The Most Dangerous Game" (Project Gutenberg/CommonLit). Read it yourself first (about 30 minutes). Set up a notebook with tabs: Vocabulary, Quotes, Quick-writes. Note: the story involves hunting humans and some violence — fine for most 9th graders.

**Watch for:**
- Calling every problem 'man vs. man': the story has several conflicts — Rainsford vs. Zaroff (person vs. person), vs. the jungle and sea (person vs. nature), and his own fear (person vs. self).
- Treating the plot diagram's climax as 'the most exciting part' anywhere: the climax is the turning point where the main conflict is decided (the final confrontation in Zaroff's bedroom).
- Summarizing instead of explaining quotations: after each quote, ask 'what does this show and why does it matter?'

### Day 1 (Monday): Course launch & reading survey
*Student:* Set up a reader's notebook with sections for vocabulary, quotes, and quick-writes; write a one-page reading autobiography describing three books that shaped you.

*How to teach:* Explain how the reader's notebook will be used all year. Share a book that shaped you to model the reading autobiography, then have your child write one page about three books. Check that each book gets a 'why it mattered' sentence.

- **Q:** What three sections will your reader's notebook have, and what goes in each?
  **A:** Vocabulary (new words with definitions and sentences), Quotes (memorable lines with page numbers and a note), Quick-writes (short timed responses).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plot and conflict
*Student:* Learn the plot diagram and four conflict types; read the first half of "The Most Dangerous Game" and annotate every moment that raises suspense.

*How to teach:* Draw the plot diagram (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution) and name the four conflict types. Read the first half together or independently; your child highlights suspense moments (e.g., the gunshots heard at night, the name Ship-Trap Island) and explains one.

- **Q:** Early on, Rainsford says the world is made up of 'the hunters and the huntables.' Why is this ironic later?
  **A:** Rainsford, who doesn't care about the jaguar's feelings, becomes the 'huntable' — hunted by Zaroff — and learns what the prey feels.
- **Q:** What type of conflict is introduced when Rainsford falls off the yacht?
  **A:** Person vs. nature (he must survive the sea and reach the island).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Close reading Rainsford
*Student:* Finish the story; record three quotations that show Rainsford's attitude toward hunting changing and explain each in two sentences.

*How to teach:* Your child finishes the story and records three quotations showing Rainsford's attitude toward hunting, with two sentences of explanation each. Discuss whether his attitude really changes by the end.

- **Q:** How do Rainsford's views of hunting change during the story?
  **A:** At first he dismisses the feelings of hunted animals; after being hunted himself he understands fear — he even says he now knows how an animal feels, though the ending (sleeping in Zaroff's bed) leaves his change ambiguous.
- **Q:** What is the final line, and what does it suggest?
  **A:** 'He had never slept in a better bed, Rainsford decided.' It implies he defeated (killed) Zaroff without describing it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Suspense techniques
*Student:* Chart how Connell uses foreshadowing, setting (Ship-Trap Island), and pacing; write a paragraph explaining which technique creates the most tension.

*How to teach:* Make a three-column chart: foreshadowing, setting, pacing. Model one entry (the island's name and sailors' superstition foreshadow danger). Your child completes the chart and writes a paragraph arguing which technique creates the most tension.

- **Q:** How does the name 'Ship-Trap Island' create suspense?
  **A:** It foreshadows danger — sailors fear it — so readers expect something sinister before Rainsford arrives.
- **Q:** How does Connell's pacing change during the three-day hunt?
  **A:** Short sentences and quick action speed the pace and raise tension.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a plot diagram for the story and take a 10-question reading quiz; discuss aloud whether Rainsford is a hero.

*How to teach:* Your child completes the plot diagram and the quiz (key). Then discuss: Is Rainsford a hero? Push for evidence on both sides.

- **Q:** What is the climax of the story?
  **A:** Rainsford's surprise appearance in Zaroff's bedroom and their final confrontation, which decides the conflict.

**Assessment:** Plot diagram accuracy and reading quiz (80%+); paragraph scored for evidence and explanation.

**Answer key:** Plot diagram: exposition — Rainsford and Whitney on the yacht discuss hunting and Ship-Trap Island; rising action — Rainsford falls overboard, reaches the island, meets Zaroff and Ivan, learns Zaroff hunts men, the hunt begins and Rainsford sets traps (Malay man-catcher, Burmese tiger pit, Uganda knife trap) and leaps into the sea; climax — he confronts Zaroff in the bedroom; resolution — 'He had never slept in a better bed.' Quiz answers (sample): 1) Ship-Trap Island. 2) General Zaroff. 3) Ivan, Zaroff's servant. 4) Zaroff got bored hunting animals because they cannot reason. 5) Rainsford refuses to hunt and is forced to be the prey. 6) He gets a three-day head start. 7) He creates traps from his hunting knowledge. 8) He jumps into the sea. 9) He hides in Zaroff's bedroom. 10) Implied: Rainsford kills Zaroff. 80%+ = proficient. Paragraph rubric: 4 = arguable claim, two well-chosen quotations embedded and cited, commentary that explains HOW the evidence proves the claim; 3 = claim and evidence with thinner commentary; 2 = summary instead of analysis or only one piece of evidence; 1 = off-topic or no evidence.

## Algebra I: Real numbers and properties

**Prepare:** Print or draw a large nested diagram (natural ⊂ whole ⊂ integers ⊂ rationals; irrationals beside rationals, all inside reals). Write the 30 numbers on slips. Have a calculator and a number line ready; log in to Khan Academy.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking any decimal is irrational: show that repeating decimals like 0.333... equal fractions (1/3), so they are rational; only non-terminating, non-repeating decimals are irrational.
- Classifying √49 as irrational because it has a root sign: simplify first — √49 = 7, an integer.
- Believing irrational × irrational is always irrational: √2·√8 = √16 = 4 is rational.

### Day 1 (Monday): Number system
*Student:* Sort 30 numbers (√2, −3/4, π, 0.333..., √49) into a nested real-number diagram and justify each placement.

*How to teach:* Model placing two numbers in the diagram aloud (e.g., −3/4 goes in rationals but not integers because it is a ratio of integers that is not whole). Your child sorts the rest and writes a one-line reason for each; check by asking 'can it be written as a fraction of integers?'

- **Q:** Classify √49, −3/4, π, and 0.333... as specifically as possible.
  **A:** √49 = 7: natural, whole, integer, rational, real. −3/4: rational, real. π: irrational, real. 0.333... = 1/3: rational, real.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Properties
*Student:* Use commutative, associative, and distributive properties to compute 12 mental-math problems and name the property used.

*How to teach:* Show one example of each property: 4·(25·17) regrouped as (4·25)·17 = 1700 (associative); 7·98 = 7(100 − 2) = 686 (distributive). Your child does the 12 problems mentally, writing the property used, then explains one aloud.

- **Q:** Compute 25·13·4 mentally. Which properties make it easy?
  **A:** 25·4·13 = 100·13 = 1300 — commutative (swap order) then associative (regroup).
- **Q:** Compute 6·(103) mentally and name the property.
  **A:** 6(100 + 3) = 600 + 18 = 618 — distributive property.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rational vs. irrational
*Student:* Explore sums and products like 3+√2 and √2·√8; write an explanation of when results are rational.

*How to teach:* Have your child use a calculator to explore 3 + √2, 2·√3, √2·√8, √2 + (−√2), noting which results are rational. Lead them to the rules: rational + irrational is always irrational; rational (nonzero) × irrational is irrational; two irrationals can give either. Then they write the explanation.

- **Q:** Is √2·√8 rational or irrational?
  **A:** Rational: √2·√8 = √16 = 4.
- **Q:** Is 3 + √2 rational or irrational? Why?
  **A:** Irrational. If 3 + √2 = q were rational, then √2 = q − 3 would be rational (rationals are closed under subtraction) — a contradiction.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Order and estimate
*Student:* Estimate square roots between integers and order 15 real numbers on a number line.

*How to teach:* Model estimating √50: 49 < 50 < 64, so 7 < √50 < 8, and it is very close to 7 (≈7.07). Your child estimates the given roots, converts all 15 numbers to decimals, and places them on a number line; check order with a calculator.

- **Q:** Between which two integers is √50, and about what is it?
  **A:** Between 7 and 8 (49 < 50 < 64); about 7.07.
- **Q:** Order from least to greatest: 10/3, π, √10, 3.2
  **A:** π (3.14) < √10 (3.16) < 3.2 < 10/3 (3.33).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on number classification, properties, and the rational/irrational argument.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz (see key) without notes; then have your child write the rational + irrational argument in full sentences. Review any missed items together right away.

- **Q:** In one sentence, why is the real-number set closed under addition but the irrationals are not?
  **A:** The sum of any two reals is real, but two irrationals can sum to a rational, e.g., √2 + (−√2) = 0.

**Assessment:** Quiz plus written explanation of why rational + irrational is irrational.

**Answer key:** Suggested quiz and answers: 1) √64 → natural, whole, integer, rational, real. 2) −2.5 → rational. 3) √5 → irrational. 4) 0.1212... → rational (12/99 = 4/33). 5) 5(x + 2) = 5x + 10 → distributive. 6) 3 + 8 = 8 + 3 → commutative. 7) (2·5)·9 = 2·(5·9) → associative. 8) Is π + 1 rational? No, irrational. Written explanation (proficient): assume r is rational, x irrational, and r + x = q is rational; then x = q − r is a difference of rationals, so it is rational — contradicting that x is irrational; therefore r + x is irrational. Rubric: 4 = assumption, closure step, contradiction stated; 3 = correct idea with a gap; 2 = examples only; 1 = incorrect.

## Biology – The Living Earth: Science practices and ecosystem components

**Prepare:** Notebook, meter stick, 4 m of string, 4 stakes or rocks, hand lens. Pick two survey spots (one sunny, one shady). Home lab safety: goggles for chemicals, never taste lab materials, wash hands, adult supervision for heat or chemicals.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking abiotic means 'dead things': abiotic factors are nonliving parts of the environment (light, temperature, water, soil); a dead leaf came from a living thing (biotic origin).
- Confusing population and community: a population is one species; a community is all populations in an area.
- CER claims without data: evidence must cite actual measurements or counts.

### Day 1 (Monday): Lab safety & notebooks
*Student:* Set up a lab notebook (claim–evidence–reasoning format) and review home lab safety rules with a parent.

*How to teach:* Set up the notebook in claim–evidence–reasoning format and go over safety rules together; your child writes and signs a safety contract.

- **Q:** Why should you never taste or smell lab materials directly?
  **A:** They may be toxic or irritating; waft odors toward you instead and never taste anything in a lab.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ecosystem levels
*Student:* Read about organization from organism to biosphere and build a concept map with local examples.

*How to teach:* Read about levels of organization; your child builds a concept map with local examples.

- **Q:** Put in order from smallest to largest: community, organism, biosphere, population, ecosystem.
  **A:** Organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere.
- **Q:** What is the difference between a community and an ecosystem?
  **A:** A community is all the living populations in an area; an ecosystem includes the community plus the abiotic factors.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Quadrat survey
*Student:* Mark a 1 m² quadrat in a yard or park with string; record every organism and abiotic factor you observe.

*How to teach:* Mark a 1 m² quadrat; your child records every organism and abiotic factor (light, moisture, temperature, soil).

- **Q:** Name two biotic and two abiotic factors you might find in a yard quadrat.
  **A:** Biotic: grass, ants, clover, earthworms. Abiotic: sunlight, soil moisture, temperature, rocks.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Data sharing
*Student:* Graph quadrat species counts and compare to a second quadrat in a different spot (sun vs. shade).

*How to teach:* Repeat in a second spot; graph species counts side by side.

- **Q:** Predict: will a sunny or shady quadrat have more grass? Why?
  **A:** Usually the sunny one, because grass needs high light for photosynthesis; shade favors moss or shade-tolerant plants.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a CER paragraph explaining one abiotic factor that differed between quadrats; vocabulary quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a CER on one abiotic factor that differed; give the vocabulary quiz.

- **Q:** Define 'abiotic factor'.
  **A:** A nonliving physical or chemical part of an ecosystem, such as light, temperature, water, or soil.

**Assessment:** CER paragraph and vocabulary quiz.

**Answer key:** CER rubric: 4 = claim answers the question; evidence uses specific data (numbers, units, observations); reasoning connects the evidence to a scientific principle; 3 = claim and evidence with partial reasoning; 2 = claim with vague evidence; 1 = claim only or incorrect. Vocabulary quiz: biotic = living factors; abiotic = nonliving factors; population = one species in an area; community = all populations in an area; ecosystem = community + abiotic environment; biosphere = all ecosystems on Earth; quadrat = square sampling area; species = organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.

## World Geography (S1) & Ethnic Studies (S2): Thinking like a geographer: the five themes

**Prepare:** World atlas (or online), printed blank world map with latitude/longitude lines, list of 15 cities with coordinates (e.g., Los Angeles 34°N 118°W; London 51.5°N 0°; Cairo 30°N 31°E; Tokyo 35.7°N 139.7°E; Sydney 34°S 151°E; Rio de Janeiro 23°S 43°W).

**Watch for:**
- Reversing latitude and longitude: latitude (N/S of the equator) comes first; longitude (E/W of the prime meridian) second.
- Confusing location and place: location is where (absolute or relative); place is what it's like (physical and human characteristics).
- Forgetting the hemisphere letter: 34°N 118°W and 34°S 118°E are on opposite sides of the world.

### Day 1 (Monday): Five themes
*Student:* Learn the five themes of geography and apply each to your own neighborhood in a one-page chart.

*How to teach:* Teach the five themes (location, place, human-environment interaction, movement, region) with neighborhood examples; your child fills a one-page chart.

- **Q:** Name the five themes of geography.
  **A:** Location, place, human-environment interaction, movement, and region.
- **Q:** Which theme is a freeway bringing commuters to your town an example of?
  **A:** Movement (also human-environment interaction for building it).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Absolute location
*Student:* Locate 15 world cities by latitude and longitude on a blank map.

*How to teach:* Model plotting one city; your child plots 15.

- **Q:** Which city is near 34°S, 151°E?
  **A:** Sydney, Australia.
- **Q:** What lines measure distance north or south of the equator?
  **A:** Lines of latitude (parallels).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Relative location
*Student:* Describe the relative location of your home and three world capitals.

*How to teach:* Describe relative location using directions and landmarks.

- **Q:** Describe the relative location of Paris.
  **A:** Example: in northern France on the Seine River, about 340 km (210 mi) southeast of London across the English Channel.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mental maps
*Student:* Draw a world map from memory, then compare to an atlas and list three surprises.

*How to teach:* Draw a world map from memory; compare to an atlas.

- **Q:** Why are mental maps often distorted?
  **A:** We exaggerate places we know or care about and shrink unfamiliar regions; many people also learned from Mercator maps.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on five themes and coordinates.

*How to teach:* Quiz on themes and coordinates.

- **Q:** What are the latitude and longitude of the point where the equator meets the prime meridian?
  **A:** 0°, 0° (in the Gulf of Guinea off West Africa).

**Assessment:** Coordinates quiz and five-themes chart.

**Answer key:** Coordinates quiz answers: 1) Los Angeles ≈ 34°N, 118°W. 2) Cairo ≈ 30°N, 31°E. 3) Tokyo ≈ 36°N, 140°E. 4) Rio ≈ 23°S, 43°W. 5) Equator = 0° latitude; prime meridian = 0° longitude through Greenwich, England. 6) The five themes (above). 7) Example of place: Sacramento's hot, dry summers and capitol buildings. Five-themes chart: proficient = an accurate, specific local example for each theme.

## Spanish II: ¿Quién soy yo? Reintroductions and present tense review

**Prepare:** Set up the Spanish notebook with sections (vocabulario, gramática, escritura) and create the Conjuguemos account. Make a present-tense chart for -ar/-er/-ir endings plus ser, estar, tener, ir, and the yo-go verbs. Have a phone ready to record the self-introduction.

**Watch for:**
- Using 'estar' for personality (Estoy simpático): personality and traits use ser (Soy simpático); estar is for location and temporary states (Estoy cansado).
- Saying 'Yo gusto la pizza': gustar works backward — the thing liked is the subject: Me gusta la pizza; Me gustan los perros.
- Applying yo-go to every form (*nosotros tengamos): only the yo form adds -go (tengo); tener is also e→ie in other forms (tienes, tiene, tenemos).

### Day 1 (Monday): Presentaciones
*Student:* Write and record a 1-minute self-introduction (name, age, personality, likes) using ser, tener, and gustar.

*How to teach:* Model a short introduction aloud: 'Me llamo Ana. Tengo quince años. Soy creativa y un poco tímida. Me gusta leer y me gustan los gatos.' Your child writes a script with ser, tener, and gustar, practices it twice, then records about one minute; check for 'tengo ... años' (not 'soy ... años').

- **Q:** How do you say 'I am 15 years old' in Spanish?
  **A:** Tengo quince años. (Age uses tener, not ser.)
- **Q:** Say 'I like dogs' and 'I like to read.'
  **A:** Me gustan los perros. Me gusta leer. (gustan with a plural noun; gusta with an infinitive.)

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Present tense review
*Student:* Conjugate 30 regular -ar/-er/-ir verbs on Conjuguemos and write 10 sentences about your week.

*How to teach:* Review the endings: -ar (o, as, a, amos, áis, an), -er (o, es, e, emos, éis, en), -ir (o, es, e, imos, ís, en). Your child completes the Conjuguemos set, then writes 10 sentences about the week; check that each verb matches its subject.

- **Q:** Conjugate vivir for nosotros and comer for ellos.
  **A:** vivimos; comen
- **Q:** Translate: 'We study Spanish on Mondays.'
  **A:** Estudiamos español los lunes.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Irregular verbs
*Student:* Review yo-go verbs (tengo, hago, salgo, pongo) and stem-changers; complete a timed drill.

*How to teach:* Chant the yo-go forms (tengo, hago, salgo, pongo, vengo, digo, traigo) and show stem-changers as 'boot verbs' (all forms change except nosotros/vosotros). Your child does the timed drill twice and tries to beat the first time.

- **Q:** Give the yo form of hacer, salir, and poner.
  **A:** hago, salgo, pongo
- **Q:** Conjugate poder for tú and nosotros.
  **A:** puedes; podemos (nosotros does not change: o→ue only inside the 'boot').

### Day 4 (Thursday): Entrevista
*Student:* Interview a family member (or Spanish-speaking pen pal) in Spanish with 10 questions and record answers.

*How to teach:* Help your child write 10 questions with question words (¿Cómo te llamas? ¿Qué te gusta hacer? ¿Dónde vives? ¿Cuántos hermanos tienes?). They interview you or a pen pal in Spanish and write the answers in third person (Mi mamá se llama...).

- **Q:** Write the question 'Where do you live?' and answer it.
  **A:** ¿Dónde vives? — Vivo en (ciudad).
- **Q:** Change 'Me gusta cocinar' to report what your mother said.
  **A:** A mi mamá le gusta cocinar.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a present-tense quiz and submit your recorded self-introduction.

*How to teach:* Give the present-tense quiz with notes closed. Grade it together, have your child correct each error with the rule, and score the recording with the rubric.

- **Q:** Correct the error: 'Yo soy cansado hoy.'
  **A:** Yo estoy cansado hoy — a temporary condition uses estar.

**Assessment:** Present-tense quiz (80%+) and speaking recording rubric.

**Answer key:** Quiz (aim 80%+): sample key — hablar/yo: hablo; comer/tú: comes; vivir/nosotros: vivimos; tener/ella: tiene; hacer/yo: hago; salir/yo: salgo; poner/yo: pongo; querer/ellos: quieren; poder/tú: puedes; pedir/nosotros: pedimos. Recording rubric (4 pts each): 4 = 1 full minute, name/age/personality/likes all included, correct ser/tener/gustar, clear pronunciation; 3 = all content, 1–2 errors; 2 = missing a part or frequent errors; 1 = very short or mostly English.

## Visual Art I: Sketchbook habits and contour line

**Prepare:** 9×12 sketchbook, graphite pencils 2H–6B, kneaded eraser, mirror, shoe, plant, kitchen utensils, banana, paper bag. Explain the pencil scale: H = harder/lighter, B = softer/darker.

**Watch for:**
- Drawing symbols ('what an eye looks like') instead of observing. Correct: look at the subject most of the time.
- Starting with 6B. Correct: begin lightly with 2H/HB and build darks with B pencils.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sketchbook launch
*Student:* Decorate a sketchbook cover with your name in three lettering styles and draw your non-dominant hand for 10 minutes.

*How to teach:* Cover lettering in three styles; draw the non-dominant hand for 10 minutes.

- **Q:** What does 'H' and 'B' mean on a pencil?
  **A:** H = hard (lighter lines), B = black/soft (darker lines); higher numbers mean more of that quality.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Blind contour
*Student:* Do five 3-minute blind contour drawings of a shoe, a plant, and your face in a mirror without looking at the paper.

*How to teach:* Five 3-minute blind contours (shoe, plant, face in mirror).

- **Q:** What is blind contour drawing?
  **A:** Drawing the edges of a subject while looking only at the subject, never the paper.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Modified contour
*Student:* Draw a kitchen utensil grouping with modified contour, glancing at the paper only at corners.

*How to teach:* Modified contour of utensils, glancing at the paper only at corners.

- **Q:** How is modified contour different from blind contour?
  **A:** You may glance at the paper occasionally (e.g., at corners or junctions) to keep proportions, but mostly look at the subject.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cross-contour
*Student:* Draw a banana and a crumpled paper bag using cross-contour lines to show form.

*How to teach:* Cross-contour banana and crumpled bag.

- **Q:** What does cross-contour show that outline doesn't?
  **A:** The surface form and volume, using lines that wrap around the object.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Select your best contour drawing and write a 5-sentence reflection on what improved during the week.

*How to teach:* Choose the best contour drawing; write a 5-sentence reflection.

- **Q:** What is a contour?
  **A:** The edge of a form, including outer edges and interior edges like folds or overlaps.

**Assessment:** Sketchbook check for 10+ contour drawings and written reflection.

**Answer key:** Sketchbook check: 4 = 10+ contour drawings (hand, 5 blind, modified, 2 cross-contour, more) showing careful observation, varied line weight, and a 5-sentence reflection naming a specific improvement; 3 = 10 drawings, general reflection; 2 = 6–9 drawings; 1 = fewer than 6.

## PE 9 (Course 1): Components of fitness and safe warm-ups

**Prepare:** Athletic shoes, water bottle, activity-log template. Set up a 5-station circuit (one per health-related component). Write a 10-question components quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing muscular strength and muscular endurance. Correct: strength is maximum force in one effort; endurance is repeating or sustaining a contraction over time.
- Thinking body composition is just weight. Correct: it's the proportion of fat mass to lean mass (muscle, bone, water).

### Day 1 (Monday): Fitness components
*Student:* Learn the five health-related fitness components, then do a 20-minute circuit with one station for each.

*How to teach:* Teach the five components (cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, body composition). Circuit: jumping jacks (cardio), wall sit or heavy carry (strength), push-ups (endurance), sit-and-reach stretch (flexibility), discussion card (body composition).

- **Q:** Classify: a 1-mile run, one maximal push of a heavy object, 40 curl-ups.
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance; muscular strength; muscular endurance.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dynamic warm-up
*Student:* Learn and perform a 10-minute dynamic warm-up (high knees, butt kicks, leg swings, inchworms) and a 15-minute brisk walk/jog.

*How to teach:* Teach a 10-minute dynamic warm-up and do a 15-minute walk/jog.

- **Q:** What is the purpose of a dynamic warm-up?
  **A:** To gradually raise heart rate, body temperature and range of motion so muscles and joints are ready, reducing injury risk.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Activity log
*Student:* Set up a weekly activity log and complete a 30-minute bike ride, hike, or jog at moderate intensity.

*How to teach:* Set up the weekly log; complete a 30-minute moderate activity.

- **Q:** What does moderate intensity feel like?
  **A:** Breathing harder, can talk but not sing (about 5–6 on a 10-point RPE).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Static stretching
*Student:* Learn when to use static stretching; complete a 20-minute circuit and a cool-down stretch routine.

*How to teach:* Teach static stretching after activity (hold 15–30 s, no bouncing). 20-minute circuit and cool-down stretches.

- **Q:** When is static stretching best used?
  **A:** After activity during the cool-down, when muscles are warm.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Lead a family member through your warm-up and answer a 10-question fitness components quiz.

*How to teach:* Child leads a family member through the warm-up; give the quiz.

- **Q:** Name the five health-related fitness components.
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, body composition.

**Assessment:** Warm-up demonstration and fitness components quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) cardiorespiratory endurance – heart/lungs supply oxygen over time 2) muscular strength – max force once 3) muscular endurance – repeated contractions 4) flexibility – range of motion at a joint 5) body composition – fat vs lean mass 6) dynamic warm-up before, static stretching after 7) example of cardio: running/swimming 8) example of strength: heavy lift 9) example of endurance: push-ups test 10) hold static stretches 15–30 s. Warm-up demo: proficient = 8+ moves, correct form, clear counts, gradual intensity.

## Health & Wellness: Wellness and the health triangle

**Prepare:** Health journal and colored pencils. Print 10 wellness scenario cards and a 20-item risk/protective list. Find or make a 30-question wellness self-inventory (5 questions each for physical, mental/emotional, social, environmental, intellectual, spiritual or use your own areas). Save the inventory — it is retaken in week 36.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking health just means not being sick. Correct: WHO defines health as complete physical, mental and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease.
- Believing you can't influence your health. Correct: many risk factors (behaviors) are controllable.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is health?
*Student:* Read the WHO definition of health, then draw and label your own health triangle with three current strengths and one weakness on each side.

*How to teach:* Read the WHO definition (1948): 'a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.' The child draws a triangle and lists three strengths and one weakness per side.

- **Q:** What are the three sides of the health triangle?
  **A:** Physical, mental/emotional and social health.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Wellness continuum
*Student:* Place 10 scenario cards on a wellness continuum from premature death to high-level wellness and explain each placement in writing.

*How to teach:* Draw a line from premature death to high-level wellness with neutral in the middle. The child places each card and writes a reason.

- **Q:** Where does someone who exercises, sleeps well and has close friends fall?
  **A:** Toward high-level wellness, because healthy behaviors in several areas move them beyond simply not being sick.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Risk and protective factors
*Student:* Sort a list of 20 behaviors and conditions into risk factors and protective factors, then identify which you can control.

*How to teach:* Sort the 20 items into risk vs. protective, then mark which are controllable.

- **Q:** Classify: vaping, family history of diabetes, supportive friends, 9 hours of sleep.
  **A:** Vaping – controllable risk; family history of diabetes – uncontrollable risk; supportive friends – protective; 9 hours of sleep – controllable protective.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Self-assessment
*Student:* Complete a 30-question wellness self-inventory and graph your scores by area in your journal.

*How to teach:* The child completes the inventory and makes a bar graph of scores by area.

- **Q:** Which area scored lowest and what might explain it?
  **A:** Answers vary; the child should cite specific inventory items.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & reflect
*Student:* Write a one-page reflection naming your strongest and weakest areas of health and one reason each matters; take a 10-question vocabulary quiz.

*How to teach:* Child writes a one-page reflection; then give the vocabulary quiz.

- **Q:** Define wellness.
  **A:** An active process of making choices toward a healthy and fulfilling life across all areas of health.

**Assessment:** Vocabulary quiz and wellness reflection scored on a 4-point rubric.

**Answer key:** Vocabulary quiz key: 1) health – complete physical, mental, social well-being 2) wellness – active, ongoing state of choosing healthy behaviors 3) health triangle – physical, mental/emotional, social 4) wellness continuum – range from premature death to high-level wellness 5) risk factor – increases chance of harm 6) protective factor – decreases chance of harm 7) controllable risk – e.g., diet, activity, tobacco 8) uncontrollable risk – e.g., age, genetics 9) prevention – actions to stop problems before they start 10) health literacy – ability to find, understand and use health information. Reflection rubric (4): names strongest and weakest areas with evidence from the inventory and explains why each matters; 3 = both areas, thin explanation; 2 = one area; 1 = incomplete.
