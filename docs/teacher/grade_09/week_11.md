# Teacher's Guide — 9th Grade, Week 11

## English 9: Seminal U.S. documents and evaluating arguments

**Prepare:** Print the Declaration's preamble, the Gettysburg Address, and an excerpt of Douglass's 'What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?' (1852). Make a list of 8 fallacies (ad hominem, straw man, false dilemma, slippery slope, bandwagon, hasty generalization, appeal to authority, red herring) with examples.

**Watch for:**
- Using 'all men are created equal' only as a quote: Lincoln and Douglass both use it as a standard to judge the nation.
- Calling any weak argument 'ad hominem': ad hominem specifically attacks the person instead of the argument.
- Choosing a research issue that isn't debatable (e.g., 'pollution is bad').

### Day 1 (Monday): Founding ideals
*Student:* Read the Declaration's preamble and the Gettysburg Address; list ideas Lincoln echoes and how he redefines them.

*How to teach:* Read the preamble and Gettysburg Address; your child lists ideas Lincoln echoes and how he redefines them.

- **Q:** Which idea from the Declaration does Lincoln echo in his first sentence?
  **A:** That the nation was 'dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal.'
- **Q:** How does Lincoln redefine the war's purpose?
  **A:** As a test of whether a nation founded on equality can endure, calling for 'a new birth of freedom' and government 'of the people, by the people, for the people.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Douglass's challenge
*Student:* Read the Douglass excerpt; analyze how he uses the Declaration's ideals to indict slavery.

*How to teach:* Read the Douglass excerpt; analyze how he uses the Declaration's ideals to indict slavery.

- **Q:** What does Douglass say the Fourth of July reveals to the American slave?
  **A:** 'More than all other days in the year, the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim.'
- **Q:** How does Douglass use the nation's ideals against it?
  **A:** He praises the founders' principles, then shows that slavery betrays them, making the celebration hypocritical ('This Fourth of July is yours, not mine').

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fallacies
*Student:* Learn eight common fallacies and identify them in 12 sample arguments from letters to the editor and ads.

*How to teach:* Teach 8 fallacies; your child identifies them in 12 samples.

- **Q:** 'Either we ban phones in school or students will never learn anything.' Name the fallacy.
  **A:** False dilemma (either/or).
- **Q:** 'Everyone is buying these shoes, so you should too.' Name the fallacy.
  **A:** Bandwagon.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Choose your issue
*Student:* Pick a debatable issue (e.g., later school start times); find two credible sources and record claims and evidence in a research log.

*How to teach:* Your child picks an issue, finds two credible sources, and logs claims and evidence.

- **Q:** What makes a source credible for a research log?
  **A:** Known, accountable author or organization; evidence and citations; current; purpose to inform rather than sell; confirmed by other sources.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a fallacy quiz and write a claim plus three reasons for your argument essay.

*How to teach:* Fallacy quiz; your child writes a claim plus three reasons.

- **Q:** 'My opponent didn't even go to college, so his tax plan is wrong.' Fallacy?
  **A:** Ad hominem.

**Assessment:** Document comparison chart; fallacy quiz; approved claim and research log.

**Answer key:** Document comparison chart: proficient = notes 'all men are created equal' and liberty in all three; Lincoln reframes equality as the nation's unfinished task; Douglass uses it to expose slavery's hypocrisy. Fallacy quiz answers (sample items): 1) 'If we allow one late assignment, soon no one will turn anything in' — slippery slope. 2) 'You think we should fund schools more? So you want to waste money' — straw man. 3) 'A famous actor says this vitamin works' — false appeal to authority. 4) 'Two teens I know are rude, so all teens are rude' — hasty generalization. 5) Changing the subject to avoid the question — red herring. Claim approved if debatable, specific, and supported by three distinct reasons.

## Algebra I: Slope and rate of change

**Prepare:** Tape measure, graph paper, access to household stairs and a ramp (or board propped on books).

**Watch for:**
- Computing run over rise: slope = rise/run = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁).
- Subtracting coordinates in mixed order: keep the same point first in both numerator and denominator.
- Thinking a horizontal line has 'no slope': it has slope 0; a vertical line's slope is undefined.

### Day 1 (Monday): Stair slope lab
*Student:* Measure rise and run of household stairs and a ramp; compute and compare slopes.

*How to teach:* Measure rise and run of one stair together and compute rise/run. Your child measures more stairs and a ramp and compares their steepness.

- **Q:** A stair has a 7-inch rise and an 11-inch run. What is its slope?
  **A:** 7/11 ≈ 0.64.
- **Q:** Which is steeper, a 1:12 ramp or that stair?
  **A:** The stair (0.64 vs. 1/12 ≈ 0.083).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Slope from two points
*Student:* Calculate slope from points, tables, and graphs for 15 problems.

*How to teach:* Model the slope formula with (2, 3) and (6, 11). Your child computes slope for 15 problems from points, tables, and graphs.

- **Q:** Find the slope through (2, 3) and (6, 11).
  **A:** (11 − 3)/(6 − 2) = 8/4 = 2.
- **Q:** Find the slope through (−1, 4) and (3, −4).
  **A:** (−4 − 4)/(3 − (−1)) = −8/4 = −2.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Rate of change in context
*Student:* Interpret slopes like −2.5 gallons/hour and $12/ticket.

*How to teach:* Read a slope as a rate with units: −2.5 gallons/hour means the tank loses 2.5 gallons each hour. Your child interprets each rate in a sentence.

- **Q:** A tank's volume changes at −2.5 gallons per hour. Interpret.
  **A:** The tank loses 2.5 gallons every hour.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Average rate of change
*Student:* Find average rate of change of nonlinear data over intervals (e.g., population table).

*How to teach:* For nonlinear data, the average rate of change over an interval is (change in output)/(change in input). Your child computes average rates for intervals of a population table and compares them.

- **Q:** Population was 50,000 in 2000 and 62,000 in 2010. Average rate of change?
  **A:** 12,000/10 = 1,200 people per year.
- **Q:** Average rate of change of f(x) = x² from x = 1 to x = 3?
  **A:** (9 − 1)/(3 − 1) = 4.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Slope quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the slope quiz in the key and have your child finish the stair-lab write-up (measurements, slopes, comparison).

- **Q:** What is the slope of y = 5?
  **A:** 0 (horizontal line).

**Assessment:** Slope quiz and lab write-up.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) (1, 2),(4, 11) → 3. 2) (−2, 5),(2, −3) → −2. 3) (0, 4),(6, 4) → 0. 4) (3, 1),(3, 7) → undefined. 5) A plumber charges $12/ticket — slope means $12 per ticket. 6) Average rate of change of f(x) = x² on [1, 3] → 4. Lab rubric: 4 = measurements with units, correct slopes, compared, explains which is steeper and why it matters (safety/accessibility); 3 = minor errors; 2 = incomplete; 1 = missing.

## Biology – The Living Earth: Evidence for evolution: fossils and the geologic record

**Prepare:** Clear jar, sand, soil, gravel, a small 'fossil' (shell or toy) per layer; 100 pennies and a shoebox for half-life; clay. Reference: carbon-14 half-life is about 5,730 years.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking deeper layers are always younger: by the law of superposition, lower (undisturbed) layers are older.
- Believing radioactive decay depends on temperature or conditions: half-lives are constant.
- Viewing transitional fossils as 'half-and-half' creatures: they show mixtures of ancestral and new traits.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rock layers
*Student:* Build a layered jar of soil and sand to model stratigraphy and relative dating.

*How to teach:* Layer the jar with a 'fossil' in each layer; your child explains relative dating.

- **Q:** In undisturbed layers, which fossils are oldest?
  **A:** Those in the bottom layers (law of superposition).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Radiometric dating
*Student:* Use a coin half-life simulation to estimate the age of a sample.

*How to teach:* Shake pennies, remove 'decayed' (heads), record; each shake = one half-life.

- **Q:** A fossil has 25% of its original carbon-14. How old is it?
  **A:** Two half-lives: 2 × 5,730 = 11,460 years.
- **Q:** After 3 half-lives, what fraction of the parent isotope remains?
  **A:** 1/8.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Transitional fossils
*Student:* Analyze Tiktaalik and whale fossil sequences (Pakicetus to modern whales) and list the traits that changed.

*How to teach:* Analyze Tiktaalik and the whale sequence (Pakicetus → Ambulocetus → Rodhocetus → Basilosaurus → modern whales).

- **Q:** What features make Tiktaalik transitional?
  **A:** Fish features (scales, fins, gills) plus tetrapod-like features (a flat head, a mobile neck, sturdy fin bones like a wrist, lungs).
- **Q:** What traits changed in the whale sequence?
  **A:** Hind limbs shrank, nostrils moved back toward a blowhole, bodies became streamlined for swimming.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Fossil timeline
*Student:* Place major fossil groups on the geologic time scale.

*How to teach:* Place fossil groups on the geologic time scale.

- **Q:** Which came first: fish, dinosaurs, or flowering plants?
  **A:** Fish (Cambrian/Ordovician), then dinosaurs (Triassic), then flowering plants (Cretaceous).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on fossil evidence.

*How to teach:* Fossil-evidence quiz.

- **Q:** What is a transitional fossil?
  **A:** A fossil showing traits intermediate between an ancestral group and its descendants.

**Assessment:** Fossil evidence quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) Superposition. 2) Index fossils help date layers. 3) Half-life definition. 4) 1/16 remaining = 4 half-lives. 5) C-14 dating useful up to ~50,000 years. 6) Tiktaalik features. 7) Whale sequence traits. 8) Order: bacteria → fish → amphibians → reptiles/dinosaurs → mammals radiate → humans.

## World Geography (S1) & Ethnic Studies (S2): Political geography: states, borders, and conflict

**Prepare:** Political world map; a map of Africa's borders after the Berlin Conference; two or three news sources on a current border dispute.

**Watch for:**
- Using 'country,' 'state,' and 'nation' interchangeably: a state is a political unit with sovereignty; a nation is a people with shared identity.
- Thinking straight-line borders are natural: geometric borders often ignore physical and cultural realities.
- Relying on one news source.

### Day 1 (Monday): States and nations
*Student:* Distinguish state, nation, and nation-state with examples.

*How to teach:* Distinguish state, nation, nation-state.

- **Q:** Give an example of a nation without a state.
  **A:** The Kurds (spread across Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria) — also Palestinians are often cited.
- **Q:** Give an example of a nation-state.
  **A:** Japan (largely one nation in one state); also Iceland.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Borders
*Student:* Classify borders (physical, geometric, cultural).

*How to teach:* Classify borders.

- **Q:** Classify the U.S.–Canada border west of the Great Lakes and the Rio Grande border.
  **A:** Geometric (49th parallel); physical (river).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Colonial borders
*Student:* Analyze the 1884 Berlin Conference map and its lasting effects on Africa.

*How to teach:* Analyze the Berlin Conference (1884–85).

- **Q:** What was decided at the Berlin Conference, and who was absent?
  **A:** European powers set rules for claiming African territory; no Africans were represented.
- **Q:** What is a lasting effect of colonial borders?
  **A:** Borders split ethnic groups and combined rivals in one state, contributing to later conflicts.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Conflict
*Student:* Analyze a current border conflict using multiple news sources for bias.

*How to teach:* Analyze a current border conflict using multiple sources.

- **Q:** How can you detect bias in news coverage of a conflict?
  **A:** Compare word choices, which voices are quoted, what's left out, and the outlet's ownership/perspective across several sources.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on political geography.

*How to teach:* Political geography quiz.

- **Q:** Which two African countries were not colonized by 1914?
  **A:** Ethiopia and Liberia.

**Assessment:** Political geography quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: state, nation, nation-state definitions with examples; border types; Berlin Conference date and effect; sovereignty definition; enclave/exclave example (e.g., Lesotho is an enclave in South Africa); bias check method.

## Spanish II: Preterite vs. imperfect

**Prepare:** Print a few short comic strips (or draw 6 simple panels). Make a two-column chart: Imperfecto (background, description, habit, ongoing action, time/age, emotions) vs. Pretérito (completed action, specific time, sequence, interruption). Find a short story in the past (e.g., a graded reader chapter).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a 'long' action must be imperfect: duration does not decide; 'Viví en Texas diez años' is preterite because the period is completed and bounded.
- Using the preterite for the scene-setting (*Fue un día bonito y hizo sol) at the start of a story: background description normally uses the imperfect (Era un día bonito y hacía sol).
- Not recognizing the interruption pattern: the ongoing action is imperfect and the interrupting one preterite — Leía cuando sonó el teléfono.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rules
*Student:* Learn when to use each tense (background vs. completed action).

*How to teach:* Use the chart and a 'movie' analogy: the imperfect is the scenery and ongoing action; the preterite is the events that move the plot. Model a 4-sentence story labeling each verb.

- **Q:** Which tense: 'It was raining when I left' (llover / salir)?
  **A:** Llovía (background, imperfect) cuando salí (completed event, preterite).
- **Q:** Which tense for telling age in the past: 'She was 10'?
  **A:** Imperfect: Tenía diez años.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Practice
*Student:* Choose preterite or imperfect in 25 sentences and label each with the rule (habit, description, completed action, interruption).

*How to teach:* Your child completes 25 sentences, writing the reason code beside each (H = habit, D = description, C = completed, I = interruption). Check the reasons, not just answers.

- **Q:** Choose: Mientras yo (estudiaba/estudié), mi hermano (miraba/miró) la tele.
  **A:** estudiaba; miraba — two simultaneous ongoing actions with mientras.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Story
*Student:* Read a short story and identify why each tense is used.

*How to teach:* Read a short story; your child highlights imperfect verbs in one color and preterite in another and explains three of each.

- **Q:** In 'Había una vez una niña que vivía en el bosque. Un día, salió de su casa', why does 'salió' change tense?
  **A:** Había/vivía set the scene; 'un día' signals a specific completed event, so salió is preterite.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comic
*Student:* Narrate a 6-panel comic strip using both tenses.

*How to teach:* Your child narrates a 6-panel comic, writing at least two sentences per panel using both tenses and connectors (de repente, mientras, entonces, por fin).

- **Q:** Write: 'Suddenly the dog ran into the street.'
  **A:** De repente el perro corrió a la calle.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Preterite vs. imperfect quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the tense quiz and score the comic with the rubric.

- **Q:** Correct the error: 'Cuando era pequeño, fui a la playa todos los veranos.'
  **A:** Cuando era pequeño, iba a la playa todos los veranos (repeated habit = imperfect).

**Assessment:** Tense quiz and comic narration.

**Answer key:** Tense quiz sample key: 1) Cuando era niño, siempre ___ (jugar) — jugaba (habit) 2) Ayer ___ (comer) a las dos — comí (completed) 3) ___ (Ser) las ocho cuando llegó — Eran (time) 4) Yo ___ (dormir) cuando sonó el teléfono — dormía (ongoing/interrupted) 5) De repente ___ (empezar) a llover — empezó (sudden action) 6) Mi abuela ___ (tener) pelo blanco — tenía (description). Comic narration rubric: 4 = 6 panels, both tenses used correctly for background vs. events, connectors (de repente, mientras, entonces); 3 = 1–2 tense errors; 2 = several; 1 = one tense only.

## Visual Art I: Impressionism and landscape painting

**Prepare:** Paints, canvas board or heavy paper; images of Monet's 'Haystacks' (Grainstacks) series (1890–91).

**Watch for:**
- Blending colors smoothly on the canvas. Correct: Impressionist broken color places dabs side by side to mix optically.
- Thinking shadows are gray/black. Correct: Impressionists painted colored shadows (often blues and violets).

### Day 1 (Monday): Impressionism
*Student:* Study Monet's "Haystacks" series and explain how light changes color.

*How to teach:* Study 'Haystacks' and explain how light changes color.

- **Q:** Why did Monet paint the same subject many times?
  **A:** To capture how changing light, weather and seasons alter color and atmosphere.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Thumbnails
*Student:* Paint 3 small studies of the same view at different times of day.

*How to teach:* Three small studies of one view at different times.

- **Q:** How does color change from midday to sunset?
  **A:** Midday light is cooler and brighter with short shadows; sunset is warmer (orange/pink) with long, blue-violet shadows.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Painting
*Student:* Start a landscape painting in broken color.

*How to teach:* Start a landscape in broken color.

- **Q:** What is broken color?
  **A:** Short strokes of unmixed or lightly mixed color placed side by side so the eye blends them.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Finishing
*Student:* Finish the landscape painting.

*How to teach:* Finish the landscape.

- **Q:** What is plein air painting?
  **A:** Painting outdoors directly from the scene.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write an interpretation of a Monet painting citing visual evidence.

*How to teach:* Write an interpretation of a Monet painting citing visual evidence.

- **Q:** Interpret a Monet sunset haystack.
  **A:** Example: warm oranges and long violet shadows suggest the quiet end of a day and the passing of time, shown by the glowing edges where low sun hits the stack.

**Assessment:** Landscape painting and written interpretation.

**Answer key:** Landscape painting: 4 = broken-color brushwork, color reflects a specific time of day, colored shadows, three grounds; interpretation 4 = claim about meaning/mood supported by at least three specific visual details (color, brushwork, light); 3 = two details; 2 = description only; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 9 (Course 1): Pickleball fundamentals

**Prepare:** Pickleball paddles, balls, a public court (or tape a 20 × 44 ft court with a 7-ft non-volley zone).

**Watch for:**
- Volleying while standing in the kitchen. Correct: no volleys while touching the non-volley zone (7 ft from the net).
- Rushing the net after serving. Correct: the two-bounce rule means the serving team must let the return bounce.

### Day 1 (Monday): Basics
*Student:* Learn grip, forehand, backhand, and underhand serve at a public court.

*How to teach:* Grip, forehand, backhand; underhand serve below the waist, diagonal, landing beyond the kitchen.

- **Q:** Describe a legal pickleball serve.
  **A:** Underhand, contact below the waist, paddle moving upward, one foot behind the baseline, diagonal to the opposite service court beyond the kitchen line.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rules
*Student:* Learn the two-bounce rule and the non-volley zone; play practice points.

*How to teach:* Two-bounce rule and non-volley zone.

- **Q:** What is the two-bounce rule?
  **A:** The serve must bounce, and the return must bounce, before either team may volley.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dinking
*Student:* Practice dinks and volleys at the kitchen line.

*How to teach:* Dinks and volleys at the kitchen line.

- **Q:** What is a dink?
  **A:** A soft shot that arcs over the net and lands in the opponent's kitchen.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Strategy
*Student:* Learn the third-shot drop and play doubles.

*How to teach:* Third-shot drop; doubles.

- **Q:** Why use a third-shot drop?
  **A:** A soft shot into the kitchen lets the serving team move up to the net safely.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Skills test and quiz on rules.

*How to teach:* Skills test and rules quiz.

- **Q:** Can the receiving team score a point?
  **A:** No — in traditional scoring, only the serving team scores.

**Assessment:** Pickleball skills test and rules quiz.

**Answer key:** Rules quiz key: 1) games to 11, win by 2 2) only serving team scores 3) serve underhand, below waist, diagonal 4) two-bounce rule 5) kitchen = 7 ft non-volley zone 6) no volley while in kitchen (including follow-through) 7) doubles score call: server score, receiver score, server number (e.g., '4-2-1') 8) ball on the line is in (except kitchen line on serve). Skills test: 7/10 legal serves, 10-shot dink rally.

## Health & Wellness: Mental health conditions and stigma

**Prepare:** Computer; NAMI/NIMH fact sheets on anxiety, depression, ADHD and PTSD. Prepare 12 myth/fact statements. Pick a TV show or movie clip that portrays mental illness.

**Watch for:**
- Believing people with mental illness are violent. Correct: they are more likely to be victims of violence than perpetrators.
- Thinking therapy is only for 'serious' problems. Correct: therapy helps with many everyday challenges.

### Day 1 (Monday): Common conditions
*Student:* Research anxiety, depression, ADHD and PTSD and complete a chart of symptoms and treatments.

*How to teach:* Complete the symptom/treatment chart.

- **Q:** What is PTSD?
  **A:** Post-traumatic stress disorder: after a traumatic event, symptoms like flashbacks, nightmares, avoidance, being easily startled last more than a month; treated with trauma-focused therapy and sometimes medication.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Myths and facts
*Student:* Sort 12 mental health statements into myths and facts and correct each myth.

*How to teach:* Sort myths/facts and correct each myth.

- **Q:** Myth or fact: Mental illness is rare.
  **A:** Myth — about 1 in 5 U.S. adults and many teens experience a mental illness each year.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Treatment options
*Student:* Learn about counseling, therapy types (CBT), medication and support groups.

*How to teach:* Explain counseling, CBT (changing thought patterns and behaviors), medication, support groups.

- **Q:** What does CBT focus on?
  **A:** Identifying and changing unhelpful thoughts and behaviors to improve feelings.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Stigma
*Student:* Analyze how mental illness is portrayed in a TV show or movie and how that could affect help-seeking.

*How to teach:* Analyze the portrayal and how it could affect help-seeking.

- **Q:** How can inaccurate portrayals affect people?
  **A:** They reinforce stereotypes and shame, making people less likely to seek help.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Design an anti-stigma poster with accurate facts and a help resource.

*How to teach:* The child designs the poster.

- **Q:** Give one help resource for a poster.
  **A:** 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call/text 988), school counselor, NAMI HelpLine.

**Assessment:** Anti-stigma poster scored on accuracy and message.

**Answer key:** Poster rubric: 4 = at least two accurate facts, one myth corrected, person-first language, a help resource, clear anti-stigma message; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = one fact, no resource; 1 = inaccurate.
