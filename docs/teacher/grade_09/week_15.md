# Teacher's Guide — 9th Grade, Week 15

## English 9: Poetry analysis, original poem, and recitation

**Prepare:** Bookmark the Poetry Out Loud anthology (poetryoutloud.org). Recording device. Print the Poetry Out Loud evaluation criteria (physical presence, voice and articulation, dramatic appropriateness, evidence of understanding, accuracy). Prepare the Unit 4 test.

**Watch for:**
- Over-acting a recitation: the poem's meaning should lead; let the words carry the drama.
- Analysis essays that list devices: connect each device to how it builds the theme.
- Ending lines randomly in free verse: line breaks should create emphasis or pause.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choose a recitation poem
*Student:* Browse the Poetry Out Loud anthology and choose a 14–25 line poem; begin memorizing and mark pauses and emphasis.

*How to teach:* Your child browses and chooses a 14–25 line poem, marks pauses and emphasis, and begins memorizing (stanza by stanza).

- **Q:** What is a good strategy for memorizing a poem?
  **A:** Learn a few lines at a time, say them aloud repeatedly, add lines cumulatively, and practice from memory daily.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Analysis essay
*Student:* Write a 600-word analysis of your recitation poem explaining how two devices build its theme.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a 600-word analysis explaining how two devices build the theme.

- **Q:** What should each body paragraph in the analysis include?
  **A:** A claim about one device, quotations from the poem, and an explanation of how that device builds the theme.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Original poem
*Student:* Write an original sonnet or free-verse poem using an extended metaphor; revise line breaks for effect.

*How to teach:* Your child writes an original sonnet or free-verse poem built on an extended metaphor; revise line breaks.

- **Q:** What is an extended metaphor?
  **A:** A comparison developed over several lines or a whole poem (like Dickinson's hope as a bird).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rehearse
*Student:* Practice the recitation with attention to volume, pacing, and eye contact; record and self-critique.

*How to teach:* Rehearse with attention to volume, pacing, and eye contact; record and self-critique.

- **Q:** Name two Poetry Out Loud evaluation criteria.
  **A:** Any two: physical presence, voice and articulation, dramatic appropriateness, evidence of understanding, accuracy.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Recite the poem for family (or record it) and submit the analysis and original poem; Unit 4 test.

*How to teach:* Recitation for family or recorded; submit analysis and poem; give the Unit 4 test.

- **Q:** What is a dramatic monologue?
  **A:** A poem spoken by one character to a silent listener, revealing the speaker's personality.

**Assessment:** Recitation scored on Poetry Out Loud criteria; analysis essay; original poem; Unit 4 test.

**Answer key:** Recitation scoring (1–6 per Poetry Out Loud criterion; proficient ≈ 4+): physical presence, voice/articulation, dramatic appropriateness, evidence of understanding, accuracy (fewer than 2 prompts). Analysis essay: Essay rubric (4 levels): 4 = precise, arguable thesis; logically ordered body paragraphs each with a topic sentence, embedded quotations cited correctly, and commentary that explains significance; effective transitions; conclusion extends the idea; few errors. 3 = clear thesis, adequate evidence and explanation, some summary, minor errors. 2 = vague thesis, evidence thin or poorly explained, organization uneven. 1 = no clear thesis, little evidence, errors interfere. Original poem: 4 = sustained extended metaphor, purposeful line breaks, vivid imagery. Unit 4 test sample answers: Dickinson's bird = hope; 'The Road Not Taken' roads worn 'about the same'; Eden allusion in Frost; Sonnet 18 volta line 9; Sonnet 130 parodies conventions; 'Harlem' similes; 'Mother to Son' staircase metaphor; Dunbar's refrain.

## Algebra I: Solving systems by graphing and substitution

**Prepare:** Graph paper, colored pencils, Desmos. Prepare 8 systems for graphing and 12 for substitution.

**Watch for:**
- Stopping after finding x: a solution to a system is an ordered pair — find y too and check in both equations.
- Thinking parallel lines have infinitely many solutions: parallel (same slope, different intercept) means no solution; the same line means infinitely many.
- Substituting into the same equation you solved: substitute into the OTHER equation.

### Day 1 (Monday): Graphing systems
*Student:* Solve 8 systems by graphing; classify each as one, none, or infinitely many solutions.

*How to teach:* Graph y = x + 1 and y = −x + 5; read the intersection (2, 3) and check it in both. Show parallel and identical lines. Your child solves the 8 and classifies each.

- **Q:** Solve by graphing: y = x + 1 and y = −x + 5.
  **A:** (2, 3).
- **Q:** How many solutions do y = 3x + 1 and y = 3x − 2 have?
  **A:** None — parallel lines.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Intersections as solutions
*Student:* Explain why the intersection of f(x) and g(x) solves f(x)=g(x); approximate solutions with a table.

*How to teach:* Explain: at the intersection, f(x) and g(x) have the same output for the same input, so f(x) = g(x) there. Make a table of both functions to approximate where they're equal.

- **Q:** Why does the x-coordinate of the intersection of y = f(x) and y = g(x) solve f(x) = g(x)?
  **A:** At that x both functions give the same y-value, so f(x) = g(x) is true there.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Substitution
*Student:* Solve 12 systems by substitution and check solutions in both equations.

*How to teach:* Model substitution: y = 2x − 1 and 3x + y = 9 → 3x + 2x − 1 = 9 → x = 2, y = 3. Your child solves 12 and checks in both equations.

- **Q:** Solve: y = 2x − 1 and 3x + y = 9.
  **A:** (2, 3).
- **Q:** Solve: x = 2y and 3x − y = 10.
  **A:** 6y − y = 10 → y = 2, x = 4: (4, 2).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Systems in context
*Student:* Write and solve systems for ticket sales and two-plan comparisons.

*How to teach:* Model writing a system: define two variables, write a 'count' equation and a 'value' equation. Your child solves the context problems.

- **Q:** 200 tickets sold: adults $10, children $6, total $1,600. How many of each?
  **A:** a + c = 200; 10a + 6c = 1600 → a = 100, c = 100.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on graphing and substitution.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the key.

- **Q:** Solve y = 3x and x + y = 8.
  **A:** (2, 6).

**Assessment:** Systems quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) y = 3x, x + y = 8 → (2, 6). 2) y = 2x + 3, y = 2x − 1 → no solution. 3) x = 2y, 3x − y = 10 → (4, 2). 4) y = −x + 4, y = 2x − 5 → (3, 1). 5) 2x + 4y = 8, x + 2y = 4 → infinitely many. 6) Two phone plans $20 + $5/GB and $35 + $2/GB → equal at 5 GB ($45).

## Biology – The Living Earth: Mechanisms of evolution beyond selection

**Prepare:** A bag of mixed-color beads or M&Ms (at least 100), several cups. Reading on Ellis–van Creveld syndrome in the Old Order Amish of Lancaster County, Pennsylvania.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking evolution = natural selection only: drift, gene flow, mutation, and nonrandom mating also change allele frequencies.
- Believing genetic drift favors 'better' alleles: drift is random and strongest in small populations.
- Confusing founder effect and bottleneck: founder = a small group starts a new population; bottleneck = a population is drastically reduced.

### Day 1 (Monday): Genetic drift
*Student:* Simulate bottlenecks with beads in a cup and compare small vs. large populations.

*How to teach:* Draw 10 beads from a large population cup vs. 4 from a small one repeatedly; compare color frequencies.

- **Q:** Why does genetic drift affect small populations more?
  **A:** Chance events have a larger effect on proportions when there are few individuals; alleles can be lost or fixed quickly.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Founder effect
*Student:* Read about the Amish population and Ellis–van Creveld syndrome.

*How to teach:* Read about Ellis–van Creveld syndrome in the Amish.

- **Q:** Why is Ellis–van Creveld syndrome more common among the Lancaster County Amish?
  **A:** The community descends from a small group of founders, at least one of whom carried the allele; marrying within the group kept its frequency high (founder effect).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Gene flow
*Student:* Model gene flow by moving beads between two cup populations each generation and record how allele frequencies converge.

*How to teach:* Move beads between two cup populations each generation; track convergence.

- **Q:** What does gene flow do to two populations' allele frequencies over time?
  **A:** Makes them more similar (reduces differences between populations).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing mechanisms
*Student:* Create a chart comparing mechanisms with examples.

*How to teach:* Make a comparison chart: mutation, selection, drift (founder, bottleneck), gene flow.

- **Q:** Example of a bottleneck?
  **A:** Northern elephant seals were hunted down to perhaps 20–100 individuals by the 1890s; today's large population has very low genetic diversity.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on mechanisms of evolution.

*How to teach:* Mechanisms quiz.

- **Q:** Which mechanism is the original source of new alleles?
  **A:** Mutation.

**Assessment:** Mechanisms quiz.

**Answer key:** Mechanisms quiz answers: 1) Natural selection — nonrandom, adaptive. 2) Genetic drift — random, strongest in small populations. 3) Founder effect — Amish/Ellis–van Creveld. 4) Bottleneck — northern elephant seals or cheetahs. 5) Gene flow — migration between populations, reduces differences. 6) Mutation — source of new alleles.

## World Geography (S1) & Ethnic Studies (S2): Human impact on environments: California water case studies

**Prepare:** Calisphere photos of Owens Lake and the Los Angeles Aqueduct (search 'Owens Lake' and 'Los Angeles Aqueduct'); USGS subsidence images (the famous 1977 photo near Mendota showing ~9 m of sinking since 1925); SGMA summary. Prepare the Unit 5 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the aqueduct dispute was simple theft or simple progress: it involved quiet land purchases, legal water rights, and fierce local resistance — weigh perspectives.
- Believing groundwater is unlimited: overpumping causes land to sink permanently and aquifers to lose capacity.
- Ignoring who bears costs of policy.

### Day 1 (Monday): Owens Valley primary sources
*Student:* Examine Calisphere photos of Owens Lake before and after the 1913 Los Angeles Aqueduct and list observable changes.

*How to teach:* Examine before-and-after photos.

- **Q:** What happened to Owens Lake after the aqueduct opened in 1913?
  **A:** Water diversions dried it up by the mid-1920s, leaving a dry lakebed that became a major source of dust pollution.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Aqueduct story
*Student:* Read about William Mulholland and Owens Valley farmers' resistance; build a cause-and-effect chart.

*How to teach:* Read about Mulholland and resistance; build a cause-and-effect chart.

- **Q:** How did Owens Valley residents resist?
  **A:** Farmers and ranchers seized the aqueduct's gates (1924) and dynamited sections of it in the 1920s.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Groundwater and subsidence
*Student:* Analyze USGS images of Central Valley land subsidence and explain the link to pumping.

*How to teach:* Analyze subsidence images.

- **Q:** Why does the Central Valley sink?
  **A:** Pumping groundwater faster than it recharges compacts clay layers in the aquifer, so the land surface drops — in places by more than 8 meters since the 1920s.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Policy evaluation
*Student:* Evaluate the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (2014) by listing who benefits and who bears costs.

*How to teach:* Evaluate SGMA (2014).

- **Q:** What does SGMA require?
  **A:** Local groundwater sustainability agencies must create plans to bring overdrafted basins into balance over about 20 years (by 2040–2042).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 5 test on economic geography and resources.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 5 test.

- **Q:** Who led the building of the Los Angeles Aqueduct?
  **A:** William Mulholland (chief engineer of LA's water department).

**Assessment:** Unit 5 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 5 test sample answers: economic sectors; HDI; GDP per capita; landlocked disadvantage; lithium/cobalt locations; supply chains; Owens Valley and the 1913 aqueduct; subsidence; SGMA; resource curse. Scoring: 80%+ proficient.

## Spanish II: La cocina: tú commands and recipes

**Prepare:** Buy ingredients for guacamole (avocados, onion, tomato, cilantro, lime, salt, optional chile) or tortilla española (potatoes, eggs, onion, olive oil, salt). Find a Spanish recipe. Chart tú commands: affirmative regular = él form (corta, mezcla, añade); irregulars di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven; negative = no + subjunctive (no cortes, no pongas). Supervise knives and hot oil closely.

**Watch for:**
- Forming negative tú commands from the affirmative (*No corta): negative uses the opposite vowel — No cortes, No comas.
- Placing pronouns wrongly: attach to affirmative (Córtalo — add an accent) but put before negative (No lo cortes).
- Forgetting the irregular yo stem in negatives: poner → no pongas, hacer → no hagas, salir → no salgas.

### Day 1 (Monday): Affirmative tú commands
*Student:* Learn regular and irregular forms (pon, haz, ven).

*How to teach:* Model with real actions: '¡Abre la puerta! ¡Pon la mesa! ¡Ven aquí!' Your child forms affirmative commands for 15 verbs and memorizes the 8 irregulars (Di Haz Ve Pon Sal Sé Ten Ven).

- **Q:** Give affirmative tú commands for hacer, poner, and venir.
  **A:** haz, pon, ven
- **Q:** Give the command for 'mix the eggs' using mezclar.
  **A:** Mezcla los huevos.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Negative commands
*Student:* Learn negative tú commands (no cortes, no pongas) and rewrite 10 kitchen safety rules as negative commands.

*How to teach:* Explain: start with yo, drop -o, add the opposite ending (-ar → -es, -er/-ir → -as). Your child rewrites 10 kitchen safety rules as negative commands.

- **Q:** Write: 'Don't touch the hot pan.'
  **A:** No toques la sartén caliente.
- **Q:** Write: 'Don't put the knife in the sink.'
  **A:** No pongas el cuchillo en el fregadero.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cook
*Student:* Follow a Spanish recipe to make guacamole or tortilla española.

*How to teach:* Cook together using the Spanish recipe; your child reads each step aloud and you follow only what they say. Supervise knife and stove use.

- **Q:** Translate 'Add salt and stir' as tú commands.
  **A:** Añade sal y revuelve (or mezcla).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Video
*Student:* Record a 2-minute cooking-show video in Spanish giving at least 8 tú commands while preparing the recipe.

*How to teach:* Your child records a 2-minute cooking show, giving at least 8 tú commands with sequence words.

- **Q:** Rewrite with a pronoun: 'Corta los tomates.'
  **A:** Córtalos.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Commands quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the commands quiz and score the video.

- **Q:** Make negative: 'Fríe las papas.'
  **A:** No frías las papas. (with pronoun: No las frías.)

**Assessment:** Cooking video and commands quiz.

**Answer key:** Commands quiz sample key: 1) cortar — corta / no cortes 2) poner — pon / no pongas 3) hacer — haz / no hagas 4) venir — ven / no vengas 5) freír — fríe / no frías 6) añadir — añade / no añadas 7) mezclar los ingredientes with pronoun — Mézclalos 8) negative of Córtalo — No lo cortes. Cooking video rubric: 4 = 2 minutes, 8+ correct tú commands, clear sequence words (primero, luego, después), food vocabulary; 3 = 6–7 commands; 2 = under 6 or many errors; 1 = incomplete.

## Visual Art I: Relief printmaking: history and design

**Prepare:** Foam printing plates (or soft-cut linoleum), pencil; images of Hokusai's 'The Great Wave off Kanagawa' (c. 1830–32) and José Guadalupe Posada's calaveras (e.g., 'La Calavera Catrina'); Taller de Gráfica Popular prints (founded 1937).

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting prints reverse. Correct: transfer the design in mirror image, especially lettering.
- Thinking in lines only. Correct: relief prints are about bold black and white shapes.

### Day 1 (Monday): Hokusai
*Student:* Study Hokusai's "The Great Wave" and ukiyo-e woodblock printing.

*How to teach:* Study 'The Great Wave' and ukiyo-e.

- **Q:** What does ukiyo-e mean and how were prints made?
  **A:** 'Pictures of the floating world'; designers, carvers and printers collaborated, carving a separate woodblock for each color.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Posada and the Taller de Gráfica Popular
*Student:* Study José Guadalupe Posada's calaveras and Mexican political prints.

*How to teach:* Study Posada and the TGP.

- **Q:** What are Posada's calaveras?
  **A:** Satirical skeleton images in prints for popular broadsheets mocking society and politics in Mexico; 'La Calavera Catrina' satirized upper-class pretensions.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Design
*Student:* Draw 3 thumbnails for a print about your community, thinking in black and white shapes.

*How to teach:* Three thumbnails thinking in black and white.

- **Q:** In relief printing, what prints — the raised or carved areas?
  **A:** The raised (uncarved) surface takes ink and prints; carved areas stay paper-colored.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Transfer
*Student:* Transfer the design in reverse to a foam plate.

*How to teach:* Transfer in reverse to a foam plate.

- **Q:** How do you reverse a design?
  **A:** Trace it, flip the tracing over and transfer, or draw it in mirror image.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain how printmaking was used as public art.

*How to teach:* Explain printmaking as public art.

- **Q:** Why is printmaking good for public messages?
  **A:** Multiple copies can be made cheaply and distributed widely as posters and broadsheets.

**Assessment:** Print design.

**Answer key:** Print design: 4 = strong black/white shape balance, community theme, reversed text/design, ready to carve; written explanation cites ukiyo-e mass production and Posada/TGP political prints as public art; 3 = minor issues; 2 = too detailed/linear; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 9 (Course 1): Choreography

**Prepare:** Music (clean version), phone for filming; choreography worksheet with 8-count boxes.

**Watch for:**
- Making every move at the same level and speed. Correct: vary levels (high/middle/low), pathways (straight/curved/zigzag) and dynamics (sharp/smooth, fast/slow).
- Forgetting choreography. Correct: write it down in 8-counts and rehearse in sections.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choreography elements
*Student:* Learn levels, pathways, and dynamics.

*How to teach:* Teach levels, pathways, dynamics with examples.

- **Q:** Give an example of each element.
  **A:** Level: a jump (high) or floor roll (low); pathway: zigzag across the room; dynamics: sharp punch vs. slow melt.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Create
*Student:* Create 32 counts of choreography to a song.

*How to teach:* Create 32 counts.

- **Q:** How many 8-counts is 32 counts?
  **A:** Four.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Expand
*Student:* Expand to 64 counts, adding a formation change with a partner.

*How to teach:* Expand to 64 counts with a partner formation change.

- **Q:** Name two formation changes.
  **A:** Switching sides, moving from side-by-side to one behind the other, mirroring, canon.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rehearse
*Student:* Rehearse and film; adjust timing.

*How to teach:* Rehearse, film, adjust timing.

- **Q:** Why film rehearsals?
  **A:** To see timing and spacing errors you can't feel while dancing.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform the choreography for family.

*How to teach:* Perform for family.

- **Q:** What makes choreography interesting to watch?
  **A:** Variety in levels, pathways and dynamics, and movement that matches the music.

**Assessment:** Choreography performance rubric.

**Answer key:** Choreography rubric (4 levels): Composition (64 counts, uses levels, pathways, dynamics, formation change), Musicality (on the beat, matches phrasing), Execution (confident, clean), Effort/presentation. Proficient = 3+ each.

## Health & Wellness: Tobacco, nicotine and vaping

**Prepare:** Computer; vape and tobacco ad examples; Kick It California (1-800-300-8086; kickitca.org). Short quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking vaping is harmless water vapor. Correct: aerosol contains nicotine, ultrafine particles, heavy metals (nickel, lead), flavoring chemicals like diacetyl and volatile organic compounds.
- Believing you can't get addicted from occasional vaping. Correct: nicotine is highly addictive and teens can become dependent quickly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Nicotine effects
*Student:* Research short- and long-term health effects of nicotine and vaping, including lung injury.

*How to teach:* Research effects: addiction, harm to attention/learning, mood, increased heart rate; long-term lung and heart disease; EVALI lung injury (2019, linked mainly to THC vapes with vitamin E acetate).

- **Q:** How does nicotine affect the teen brain?
  **A:** It harms parts that control attention, learning, mood and impulse control and primes the brain for other addictions.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): What's in a vape?
*Student:* Analyze an ingredient list of e-cigarette aerosol and identify harmful chemicals.

*How to teach:* Analyze an aerosol ingredient list.

- **Q:** Name two harmful substances in vape aerosol.
  **A:** Nicotine, heavy metals (nickel, tin, lead), ultrafine particles, volatile organic compounds, diacetyl, formaldehyde.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Marketing to teens
*Student:* Analyze vape and tobacco ads for techniques targeting youth (flavors, design, influencers).

*How to teach:* Analyze ads for youth techniques.

- **Q:** Name two techniques that target teens.
  **A:** Candy/fruit flavors, sleek tech-like designs, influencers, social media, colorful packaging.

### Day 4 (Thursday): California laws
*Student:* Learn California's Tobacco 21 law and flavored tobacco ban; research quitting resources (Kick It California).

*How to teach:* Tobacco 21 (since 2016, must be 21 to buy tobacco/vapes in CA); flavored tobacco ban (Prop 31, 2022). Find quit resources.

- **Q:** What is the minimum age to buy tobacco in California?
  **A:** 21.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Create a counter-marketing social media post on vaping and take a short quiz.

*How to teach:* Child creates a counter-marketing post; quiz.

- **Q:** Name a free quit resource for California teens.
  **A:** Kick It California (1-800-300-8086 or text 'Quit Vaping' to 66819), or This is Quitting by Truth Initiative.

**Assessment:** Counter-marketing post and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: nicotine addictive, harms teen brain; aerosol contains heavy metals/chemicals; EVALI; marketing tactics; Tobacco 21; flavored ban (Prop 31); Kick It California. Post: 4 = accurate fact, exposes a tactic, clear audience, call to action.
