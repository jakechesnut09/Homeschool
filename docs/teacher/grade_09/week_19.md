# Teacher's Guide — 9th Grade, Week 19

## English 9: The Odyssey: homecoming (Books 13–16)

**Prepare:** Odyssey Books 13–16. Prepare a 'Telemachus then and now' chart.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Odysseus reveals himself immediately: he stays disguised to test loyalty and plan.
- Overlooking Eumaeus: a humble swineherd shows the truest xenia and loyalty.
- Treating Telemachus as unchanged: by Book 16 he is acting like a leader.

### Day 1 (Monday): Return to Ithaca
*Student:* Read Book 13; explain why Athena disguises Odysseus and what testing others will show.

*How to teach:* Read Book 13; explain Athena's disguise and its purpose.

- **Q:** Why does Athena disguise Odysseus as an old beggar?
  **A:** So he can enter his home safely, test who is loyal, and plan revenge on the suitors without being recognized.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Eumaeus
*Student:* Read Book 14; analyze the swineherd as an example of loyalty and xenia.

*How to teach:* Read Book 14; analyze Eumaeus.

- **Q:** How does Eumaeus show xenia and loyalty?
  **A:** He welcomes and feeds the ragged stranger before knowing who he is and stays loyal to his absent master, mourning him and resenting the suitors.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Telemachus grows
*Student:* Read Books 15–16; chart how Telemachus has changed since Book 1.

*How to teach:* Read Books 15–16; chart Telemachus's change since Book 1.

- **Q:** How has Telemachus changed since Book 1?
  **A:** He has gone from a passive, uncertain youth to one who traveled to seek news, avoided the suitors' ambush, and is ready to act with his father.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Father and son
*Student:* Write a scene analysis of the reunion in Book 16 using three quotations.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a scene analysis of the reunion with three quotations.

- **Q:** Why doesn't Telemachus believe Odysseus at first?
  **A:** Athena has transformed him so dramatically that Telemachus thinks he must be a god.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Reading quiz on Books 13–16 and a vocabulary-in-context check.

*How to teach:* Reading quiz; vocabulary-in-context check.

- **Q:** Where does the father-son reunion take place?
  **A:** In Eumaeus's hut.

**Assessment:** Reading quiz and scene analysis.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) The Phaeacians carry sleeping Odysseus to Ithaca. 2) Athena disguises him as a beggar. 3) Eumaeus is the loyal swineherd. 4) Telemachus returns from Sparta/Pylos and avoids the suitors' ambush. 5) Reunion in Eumaeus's hut. 6) They plan to kill the suitors. Scene analysis: Paragraph rubric: 4 = arguable claim, two well-chosen quotations embedded and cited, commentary that explains HOW the evidence proves the claim; 3 = claim and evidence with thinner commentary; 2 = summary instead of analysis or only one piece of evidence; 1 = off-topic or no evidence.

## Algebra I: Representing and summarizing one-variable data

**Prepare:** Meter stick or 30-cm ruler for the ruler-drop test; a conversion: reaction time t = √(2d/9.8) with d in meters. Spreadsheet or Desmos for statistics.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the mean is always the best center: with skewed data or outliers, the median is more representative.
- Making histogram bars with gaps or unequal bin widths: use equal bins and touching bars.
- Reading the box in a box plot as containing half the data values 'evenly spread': it contains the middle 50% but values may be bunched.

### Day 1 (Monday): Collect data
*Student:* Measure reaction time 20 times (ruler-drop test) for yourself and family members.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate the ruler drop: partner holds a ruler at 0; the catcher's fingers wait at 0; record the catch distance. Convert to time. Your child gathers 20 trials per person.

- **Q:** The ruler falls 15 cm before you catch it. What is your reaction time?
  **A:** t = √(2·0.15/9.8) ≈ 0.175 s.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plots
*Student:* Make a dot plot, histogram, and box plot of the data.

*How to teach:* Show how to find the five-number summary for a box plot and choose histogram bins. Your child makes the three displays for their data.

- **Q:** Find the five-number summary of 2, 4, 4, 5, 7, 9, 12.
  **A:** Min 2, Q1 4, median 5, Q3 9, max 12.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Center
*Student:* Compute mean and median and explain which is more appropriate for skewed data.

*How to teach:* Compute mean and median for a skewed set and discuss which describes a 'typical' value. Your child computes both for their data and explains.

- **Q:** For 3, 4, 4, 5, 24, find the mean and median. Which is the better center?
  **A:** Mean 8, median 4; the median is better because 24 skews the mean.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Spread
*Student:* Compute range, IQR, and standard deviation with technology; interpret each.

*How to teach:* Compute range, IQR, and standard deviation in a spreadsheet (=STDEV.S) or Desmos (stdev). Discuss: SD is roughly the typical distance from the mean.

- **Q:** Two data sets have the same mean, but set A has SD 2 and set B has SD 10. Interpret.
  **A:** Set B's values are much more spread out from the mean; A's are clustered close to the mean.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on data displays and measures.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the key.

- **Q:** IQR of the data with Q1 = 0.15 s and Q3 = 0.21 s?
  **A:** 0.06 s.

**Assessment:** Data display quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) Data 1, 3, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 → mean 47/8 = 5.875; median 5. 2) Five-number summary: 1, 3, 5, 9, 12 (Q1 = median of 1,3,3,4 = 3; Q3 = median of 6,8,10,12 = 9). 3) IQR = 6; range = 11. 4) Right-skewed data → median is the better center. 5) Which display shows individual values? Dot plot. 6) A larger SD means more spread.

## Biology – The Living Earth: DNA structure and replication

**Prepare:** Strawberries (2–3), zip-top bag, dish soap, salt, water, coffee filter, clear cup, rubbing alcohol chilled in the freezer at least 1 hour (adult handles; flammable). Paper DNA model template. Reading on Rosalind Franklin and Photo 51.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking you can see the double helix in the extracted DNA: you see millions of tangled DNA strands clumped together, not the helix.
- Pairing A with G: A pairs with T, and C pairs with G (in DNA).
- Believing replication makes one new and one totally old molecule: it is semiconservative — each new molecule has one old and one new strand.

### Day 1 (Monday): DNA extraction
*Student:* Extract DNA from strawberries with soap, salt, and cold alcohol; observe the strands.

*How to teach:* Mash strawberries with extraction buffer (soap, salt, water), filter, layer cold alcohol, spool DNA.

- **Q:** What do the soap, salt, and cold alcohol each do?
  **A:** Soap breaks down cell and nuclear membranes (lipids); salt helps DNA clump by neutralizing its charges; cold alcohol makes DNA precipitate (DNA doesn't dissolve in alcohol).
- **Q:** Why are strawberries good for DNA extraction?
  **A:** They are octoploid (8 copies of each chromosome set) and soft, so they yield lots of DNA.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): DNA structure
*Student:* Build a paper DNA model showing base pairing and antiparallel strands.

*How to teach:* Build the paper model with base pairs and antiparallel strands.

- **Q:** Write the complementary strand: 5'-ATGCCA-3'.
  **A:** 3'-TACGGT-5'.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): History
*Student:* Read about Rosalind Franklin, Watson, and Crick and Photo 51.

*How to teach:* Read about Franklin, Watson, Crick, and Photo 51.

- **Q:** What did Photo 51 reveal, and who made it?
  **A:** Rosalind Franklin's X-ray diffraction image (with Raymond Gosling, 1952) showed DNA's helical structure; Watson and Crick used it to build their 1953 double-helix model.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Replication
*Student:* Model semiconservative replication with your paper DNA.

*How to teach:* Model semiconservative replication.

- **Q:** After one round of replication, what does each new DNA molecule contain?
  **A:** One original (parent) strand and one newly made strand.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on DNA structure and replication.

*How to teach:* Quiz on DNA.

- **Q:** What are the three parts of a nucleotide?
  **A:** A phosphate group, a deoxyribose sugar, and a nitrogenous base.

**Assessment:** DNA extraction lab write-up and quiz.

**Answer key:** Lab report rubric: 4 = testable question, hypothesis, identified variables (independent, dependent, controlled), clear data table and labeled graph, conclusion that uses data and explains the science, sources of error; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = missing graph or analysis; 1 = incomplete. Quiz answers: 1) Base pairing A–T, C–G. 2) Nucleotide parts. 3) Sugar-phosphate backbone, bases inside. 4) Semiconservative replication. 5) Enzyme that unzips DNA: helicase; builds new strands: DNA polymerase. 6) Franklin's Photo 51.

## World Geography (S1) & Ethnic Studies (S2): What is Ethnic Studies? Identity and community

**Prepare:** CDE Ethnic Studies Model Curriculum overview; a reading on the 1968–69 Third World Liberation Front strike at San Francisco State; George Ella Lyon's 'Where I'm From' (georgeellalyon.com); Census race-category history (Pew Research has a clear summary). Note: Ethnic Studies discussions can be personal — set norms of respect, listening, and the right to pass.

**Watch for:**
- Equating race and ethnicity: race is a social category often based on perceived physical traits; ethnicity refers to shared culture, ancestry, language, or national origin.
- Thinking census categories are scientific or fixed: they have changed nearly every census, reflecting politics and social ideas.
- Believing Ethnic Studies is only for some students: it examines histories that shape everyone's society.

### Day 1 (Monday): Origins of Ethnic Studies
*Student:* Read about the 1968 Third World Liberation Front student strikes at San Francisco State that created the first Ethnic Studies college.

*How to teach:* Read about the 1968 TWLF strike at SF State.

- **Q:** What did the 1968–69 Third World Liberation Front strike at San Francisco State achieve?
  **A:** After a months-long student strike (the longest in U.S. history), the university created the nation's first College of Ethnic Studies in 1969.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Identity chart
*Student:* Create an identity chart (family, language, place, faith, interests) and write about which parts others see and which they don't.

*How to teach:* Create an identity chart; write about seen and unseen parts.

- **Q:** What is the difference between parts of identity that are visible and those that are not?
  **A:** Some aspects (e.g., perceived race, age) are seen by others; others (beliefs, family history, languages) are not unless shared.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Race vs. ethnicity
*Student:* Read definitions and analyze how U.S. Census race categories changed from 1790 to 2020.

*How to teach:* Read definitions; analyze Census changes 1790–2020.

- **Q:** How did the 2000 Census change race reporting?
  **A:** For the first time, people could select more than one race.
- **Q:** What did the 1790 Census count?
  **A:** Free white males and females, all other free persons, and enslaved people.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Where I'm From
*Student:* Read George Ella Lyon's "Where I'm From" and write your own version.

*How to teach:* Read 'Where I'm From'; write your own.

- **Q:** What kinds of details does Lyon use in 'Where I'm From'?
  **A:** Concrete objects, foods, family sayings, places, and names (e.g., 'I am from clothespins').

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Share your poem and complete a vocabulary check on key terms.

*How to teach:* Share poems; vocabulary check.

- **Q:** Define 'ethnicity'.
  **A:** A shared cultural heritage — ancestry, language, traditions, or national origin.

**Assessment:** "Where I'm From" poem and vocabulary check.

**Answer key:** 'Where I'm From' rubric: 4 = vivid, specific sensory details from home, family, place, and heritage; repeated 'I am from' structure; meaningful ending; 3 = some specifics; 2 = general; 1 = incomplete. Vocabulary check: race, ethnicity, identity, culture, Ethnic Studies, Third World Liberation Front, stereotype, community.

## Spanish II: ¿Adónde vamos? Travel vocabulary and the future with ir a

**Prepare:** Gather travel brochures or Spanish-language airline/hotel websites. Chart the future: infinitive + -é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án; irregular stems tendr-, saldr-, pondr-, vendr-, podr-, sabr-, querr-, har-, dir-, habr-.

**Watch for:**
- Adding endings to the stem instead of the infinitive (*hablé for future): the future attaches to the whole infinitive — hablaré.
- Forgetting accents (*hablare): all forms except nosotros carry an accent — hablaré, hablarás, hablará, hablaremos, hablarán.
- Regularizing irregulars (*teneré, *haceré): tendré, haré.

### Day 1 (Monday): Travel vocabulary
*Student:* Learn airport and hotel vocabulary with picture labels and a matching drill.

*How to teach:* Label pictures: el aeropuerto, el pasaporte, la maleta, el boleto, la tarjeta de embarque, el vuelo, la puerta, el hotel, la habitación, la recepción. Your child completes the matching drill.

- **Q:** Give the Spanish for passport, suitcase, ticket, flight.
  **A:** el pasaporte, la maleta, el boleto (el billete in Spain), el vuelo

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ir a + infinitive
*Student:* Write 10 sentences about plans for next weekend using ir a.

*How to teach:* Review ir a + infinitive (Voy a viajar). Your child writes 10 sentences about next weekend's plans.

- **Q:** Translate: 'We are going to visit my cousins.'
  **A:** Vamos a visitar a mis primos (personal a before people).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Simple future
*Student:* Learn regular and irregular future forms (tendré, saldré, haré) and complete a drill.

*How to teach:* Teach the simple future endings and irregular stems; your child completes the drill and writes 5 predictions.

- **Q:** Translate: 'I will have a lot of time and I will travel to Peru.'
  **A:** Tendré mucho tiempo y viajaré a Perú.
- **Q:** Conjugate salir in the future for nosotros.
  **A:** saldremos

### Day 4 (Thursday): Dream trip
*Student:* Interview a family member about a dream trip using the future tense.

*How to teach:* Your child interviews a family member: ¿Adónde viajarás? ¿Con quién irás? ¿Qué harás allí? ¿Cuánto tiempo estarás? Notes go in third person (Mi papá viajará a...).

- **Q:** Ask: 'What will you do there?'
  **A:** ¿Qué harás allí?

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Future tense quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the future-tense quiz.

- **Q:** Fill in: Mañana ellos ___ (venir) y nosotros ___ (hacer) una fiesta.
  **A:** vendrán; haremos

**Assessment:** Future tense quiz.

**Answer key:** Future quiz sample key: viajar/yo — viajaré; comer/tú — comerás; vivir/ellos — vivirán; tener/yo — tendré; salir/nosotros — saldremos; hacer/ella — hará; decir/ustedes — dirán; poder/tú — podrás; poner/yo — pondré; 'I am going to buy a ticket' — Voy a comprar un boleto.

## Visual Art I: Form and space: paper sculpture

**Prepare:** Cardstock, scissors, glue, craft knife and cutting mat (supervise knife use), lamp. Images: Michelangelo's 'David' (1501–04), Barbara Hepworth's pierced forms, Ruth Asawa's looped-wire sculptures.

**Watch for:**
- Making a sculpture that only works from the front. Correct: sculpture in the round should be interesting from every side.
- Ignoring negative space. Correct: holes and openings are part of the form (as in Hepworth and Asawa).

### Day 1 (Monday): Sculpture survey
*Student:* Compare Michelangelo's "David", Barbara Hepworth's pierced forms, and Ruth Asawa's wire sculptures; note how each uses space.

*How to teach:* Compare the three sculptors' use of space.

- **Q:** How does Hepworth use space differently from Michelangelo?
  **A:** Hepworth pierces holes through her abstract forms so negative space becomes part of the sculpture; Michelangelo carved a solid figure from marble.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Paper techniques
*Student:* Practice scoring, folding, curling, tabbing, and slotting cardstock in a technique sampler.

*How to teach:* Technique sampler: score, fold, curl, tab, slot.

- **Q:** Why score paper before folding?
  **A:** Scoring makes a crisp, straight fold without cracking.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Abstract sculpture
*Student:* Build a freestanding abstract paper sculpture at least 10 inches tall that is interesting from every side.

*How to teach:* Build a freestanding abstract sculpture ≥10 inches.

- **Q:** What makes a paper sculpture stable?
  **A:** A wide base, triangular or folded structures, tabs and slots for joins and balanced weight.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Lighting
*Student:* Photograph the sculpture with a lamp from three angles and note how shadows change the form.

*How to teach:* Photograph from three angles with a lamp.

- **Q:** How does lighting change a sculpture?
  **A:** Shadows reveal or hide form; low side light creates dramatic shadows.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Critique the sculpture for balance, unity, and use of negative space.

*How to teach:* Critique for balance, unity, negative space.

- **Q:** What is sculpture 'in the round'?
  **A:** Freestanding sculpture meant to be viewed from all sides.

**Assessment:** Paper sculpture and photo documentation.

**Answer key:** Paper sculpture: 4 = freestanding, ≥10 in, interesting from all sides, uses at least three paper techniques, intentional negative space, clean craftsmanship, three documented photos; 3 = minor issues; 2 = front-facing or unstable; 1 = incomplete.

- Forcing poses into pain. Correct: move to gentle tension and breathe; use modifications.

*Student:* Learn 10 foundational poses and breathing.

*How to teach:* Teach 10 poses with breathing.

*How to teach:* 30-minute flexibility routine; measure sit-and-reach.

*How to teach:* Child leads a 15-minute flow.

## Health & Wellness: Healthy relationships, consent and boundaries

**Prepare:** Parental preview: this unit (weeks 19–25) is taught under the CA Healthy Youth Act. Read the notice and decide on opt-out (Ed. Code 51938); opted-out students do an alternate project (e.g., research on healthy friendships, communication and community health resources). Print a relationship continuum (loveisrespect.org) and behavior cards; choose a short fictional relationship to analyze.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking jealousy and control show love. Correct: they are red flags for unhealthy or abusive relationships.
- Believing consent is the absence of 'no.' Correct: California's affirmative consent standard (used for colleges, SB 967) means an affirmative, conscious, voluntary agreement that is ongoing and can be revoked; silence or lack of resistance is not consent.

### Day 1 (Monday): Unit introduction
*Student:* Parent reviews the CA Healthy Youth Act notice and opt-out option (Ed. Code 51938); student discusses family values and expectations for the unit.

*How to teach:* Review the notice together and discuss family values and expectations; record any opt-out.

- **Q:** Who can you go to with questions during this unit?
  **A:** Parent/guardian, doctor, school nurse or counselor, other trusted adults.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Healthy vs. unhealthy
*Student:* Sort relationship behaviors on a continuum from healthy to abusive and identify red flags.

*How to teach:* Sort behaviors onto the healthy–unhealthy–abusive continuum and list red flags.

- **Q:** Place 'tells you who you can hang out with.'
  **A:** Unhealthy to abusive — controlling and isolating behavior is a red flag.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Consent
*Student:* Learn what affirmative consent means under California law and analyze scenarios for whether consent was given.

*How to teach:* Define affirmative consent; analyze scenarios.

- **Q:** A person agreed to a kiss earlier but now pulls away. Is there consent?
  **A:** No — consent is ongoing and can be withdrawn at any time; stop.
- **Q:** Can someone who is drunk or asleep consent?
  **A:** No — they are not able to give conscious, voluntary agreement.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Boundaries
*Student:* Write examples of personal boundaries and practice communicating them clearly.

*How to teach:* Write physical, emotional and digital boundaries and practice stating them.

- **Q:** Give an example of a digital boundary and how to say it.
  **A:** 'I don't share my passwords. Please don't ask me to.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a short analysis of a fictional relationship using what you learned; quiz.

*How to teach:* The child analyzes the fictional relationship; give a quiz.

- **Q:** Name three characteristics of a healthy relationship.
  **A:** Respect, trust, honesty, equality, good communication, support for each other's independence.

**Assessment:** Relationship analysis and quiz.

**Answer key:** Relationship analysis: 4 = identifies at least three healthy and three unhealthy behaviors with evidence, places them on the continuum, explains consent issues and recommends a resource; 3 = mostly complete; 2 = list without evidence; 1 = incomplete. Quiz key: continuum (healthy/unhealthy/abusive); red flags (jealousy, control, isolation, threats); affirmative consent = clear, voluntary, ongoing, revocable; incapacitated people cannot consent; boundaries are personal limits you can state.
