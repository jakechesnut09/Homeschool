# Teacher's Guide — 9th Grade, Week 31

## English 9: Verdict and aftermath: Chapters 20–26

**Prepare:** TKAM Chapters 20–26. Seminar rubric. Note: Tom's death (Chapter 24) is upsetting; discuss.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the verdict shows Atticus failed: Miss Maudie notes the jury deliberated for hours — 'a baby step' toward justice.
- Missing hypocrisy in Chapter 26: Miss Gates condemns Hitler's persecution yet expressed racist views after the trial.
- Believing Atticus's rhetoric is only emotional: his closing uses logos (evidence), ethos, and pathos.

### Day 1 (Monday): Atticus's closing
*Student:* Read Chapters 20–21; analyze rhetorical appeals in Atticus's closing argument.

*How to teach:* Read Chapters 20–21; analyze appeals in Atticus's closing.

- **Q:** Give an example of logos and pathos in Atticus's closing.
  **A:** Logos: no medical evidence and evidence the attacker was left-handed. Pathos: 'In the name of God, do your duty' / appeal to the courts as 'the great levelers.'
- **Q:** How do the people in the balcony honor Atticus after the verdict?
  **A:** They stand as he passes — 'Miss Jean Louise, stand up. Your father's passin'.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): After the verdict
*Student:* Read Chapters 22–23; compare Jem's, Miss Maudie's, and Atticus's reactions.

*How to teach:* Read Chapters 22–23; compare reactions.

- **Q:** How does Miss Maudie comfort Jem?
  **A:** She says some men are 'born to do our unpleasant jobs for us' and that the long deliberation was progress.
- **Q:** How does Atticus respond when Bob Ewell spits on him?
  **A:** He wipes his face and walks away, saying he'd rather Ewell take it out on him than on his children.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tom's fate
*Student:* Read Chapters 24–25; analyze Mr. Underwood's editorial and the mockingbird symbol.

*How to teach:* Read Chapters 24–25; analyze Underwood's editorial.

- **Q:** How does Tom die?
  **A:** He is shot (seventeen times) while trying to escape from prison.
- **Q:** What does Mr. Underwood compare Tom's death to?
  **A:** 'The senseless slaughter of songbirds by hunters and children' — connecting Tom to the mockingbird symbol.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Miss Gates
*Student:* Read Chapter 26; identify hypocrisy in the current-events lesson.

*How to teach:* Read Chapter 26; identify Miss Gates's hypocrisy.

- **Q:** What is Miss Gates's hypocrisy?
  **A:** She condemns Hitler's persecution of the Jews, but Scout heard her say after the trial that Black people needed to be 'taught a lesson.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Hold a seminar discussion on justice in Maycomb and complete a quiz.

*How to teach:* Seminar on justice in Maycomb; quiz.

- **Q:** What is the verdict?
  **A:** Guilty.

**Assessment:** Seminar rubric and quiz.

**Answer key:** Seminar rubric: 4 = uses quotations from the trial and aftermath, considers multiple characters' views, builds on others; 3 = some evidence; 2 = opinions only; 1 = minimal. Quiz answers: 1) Verdict: guilty. 2) Reverend Sykes tells Scout to stand. 3) Jem cries. 4) Bob Ewell spits on Atticus. 5) Tom is shot escaping. 6) Underwood compares it to killing songbirds. 7) Miss Gates teaches about Hitler/democracy. 8) Her hypocrisy.

## Algebra I: Solving quadratics by factoring and square roots

**Prepare:** Factoring flowchart; Desmos for checking.

**Watch for:**
- Applying the zero-product property before the equation equals zero: x(x − 5) = 6 does NOT mean x = 6 or x − 5 = 6.
- Taking only the positive square root: (x − 2)² = 16 gives x − 2 = ±4.
- Keeping solutions that make no sense in context (negative lengths).

### Day 1 (Monday): Zero product property
*Student:* Solve 15 equations by factoring after writing in standard form.

*How to teach:* Rewrite in standard form, factor, set each factor to zero.

- **Q:** Solve x² − 5x = −6.
  **A:** x² − 5x + 6 = 0 → (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0 → x = 2 or 3.
- **Q:** Solve 2x² = 8x.
  **A:** 2x(x − 4) = 0 → x = 0 or 4.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Square roots
*Student:* Solve equations like 3(x−2)²=48 by square roots.

*How to teach:* Isolate the square, then take ± square roots.

- **Q:** Solve 3(x − 2)² = 48.
  **A:** (x − 2)² = 16 → x − 2 = ±4 → x = 6 or −2.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Word problems
*Student:* Solve 8 area and number problems by factoring (e.g., a rectangle whose length is 3 more than its width has area 70).

*How to teach:* Define the variable, write an equation, solve by factoring, reject impossible answers.

- **Q:** A rectangle's length is 3 more than its width and its area is 70. Find its dimensions.
  **A:** w(w + 3) = 70 → w² + 3w − 70 = 0 → (w + 10)(w − 7) = 0 → w = 7 (reject −10): 7 by 10.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Graphs and solutions
*Student:* Graph 6 quadratics on Desmos and verify that factored solutions match the x-intercepts.

*How to teach:* Graph each quadratic in Desmos and compare x-intercepts with the factored solutions.

- **Q:** Why do the solutions of x² − x − 6 = 0 match the x-intercepts of y = x² − x − 6?
  **A:** The x-intercepts are where y = 0, which is exactly the equation; both give x = 3 and −2.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on factoring and square-root methods.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz.

- **Q:** Solve x² = 81.
  **A:** x = ±9.

**Assessment:** Solving quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) x² + 3x − 10 = 0 → x = −5, 2. 2) x² = 4x + 12 → x = 6, −2. 3) 4x² − 9 = 0 → x = ±3/2. 4) 2(x + 1)² = 50 → x = 4, −6. 5) (x − 3)² = 7 → x = 3 ± √7. 6) A number times 2 more than itself is 48 → 6 (or −8). 7) Rectangle with width w, length 2w, area 72 → 6 × 12.

## Biology – The Living Earth: Human impacts on ecosystems

**Prepare:** Local news articles on invasive species or pollution; pH strips; clean jars for water samples (tap, rain, pond/creek — wear gloves at natural water). Monarch data (Xerces Society Western Monarch Count).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking invasive = any non-native species: invasive species cause harm and spread aggressively; many non-natives are harmless.
- Assuming pH alone shows water is safe: many pollutants don't change pH.
- Blaming one cause for monarch decline: habitat loss (milkweed and overwintering sites), pesticides, and climate all contribute.

### Day 1 (Monday): HIPPO
*Student:* Learn the HIPPO framework (habitat, invasive species, pollution, population, overharvesting).

*How to teach:* Teach HIPPO with an example of each.

- **Q:** What does HIPPO stand for?
  **A:** Habitat loss, Invasive species, Pollution, Population (human growth), Overharvesting.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Invasive species
*Student:* Research a California invasive species (e.g., quagga mussel) and its impacts.

*How to teach:* Research a California invasive species.

- **Q:** How do quagga mussels harm California waterways?
  **A:** They spread rapidly (since arriving in Lake Mead in 2007), clog water pipes and infrastructure, and filter out plankton that native food webs depend on.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pollution test
*Student:* Test local water samples with pH strips and compare to standards.

*How to teach:* Test water samples with pH strips.

- **Q:** Drinking water pH guidelines are about 6.5–8.5. A creek sample reads 5.0. What might this mean?
  **A:** It is acidic — possibly from acid runoff or pollution; more testing is needed.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Data analysis
*Student:* Analyze data on Monarch butterfly population decline.

*How to teach:* Analyze monarch decline data.

- **Q:** What happened to the western monarch overwintering count?
  **A:** It fell from millions in the 1980s to fewer than 2,000 butterflies counted in 2020, with partial rebounds in some later years.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on human impacts.

*How to teach:* Human-impacts quiz.

- **Q:** Give one action that helps monarchs.
  **A:** Plant native milkweed and nectar flowers, reduce pesticide use, protect overwintering groves.

**Assessment:** Human impacts quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) HIPPO components. 2) Invasive species example and impact. 3) Biomagnification of pollutants up food chains. 4) Overharvesting example (e.g., abalone in California). 5) Monarch decline causes. 6) Habitat fragmentation definition.

## World Geography (S1) & Ethnic Studies (S2): Chinese immigration and exclusion

**Prepare:** Chinese Exclusion Act (DocsTeach); Angel Island poems (Angel Island Immigration Station Foundation, aiisf.org); Wong Kim Ark case summary; reading on Chinese railroad workers (Stanford's Chinese Railroad Workers in North America Project).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Chinese immigrants came only for railroads: they came for the Gold Rush (1849+), farming, fishing, and businesses, and many faced violence and discriminatory taxes.
- Believing exclusion was brief: it lasted from 1882 until repeal in 1943, with strict quotas until 1965.
- Confusing Angel Island with Ellis Island: Angel Island (1910–1940) detained many Asian immigrants, sometimes for months.

### Day 1 (Monday): Gold Rush and railroads
*Student:* Read about Chinese workers on the Transcontinental Railroad.

*How to teach:* Read about Chinese railroad workers.

- **Q:** What role did Chinese workers play in building the Transcontinental Railroad?
  **A:** They made up most of the Central Pacific's workforce (about 12,000–20,000 workers), doing dangerous work blasting through the Sierra Nevada, for lower pay than white workers.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Exclusion Act
*Student:* Read the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act (DocsTeach) and list its provisions and the reasons given for it.

*How to teach:* Read the Chinese Exclusion Act.

- **Q:** What did the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act do?
  **A:** Suspended immigration of Chinese laborers for 10 years and barred Chinese immigrants from becoming naturalized citizens — the first U.S. law to ban immigration by nationality.
- **Q:** What reason did the Act give?
  **A:** It claimed Chinese laborers 'endanger the good order of certain localities.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Angel Island
*Student:* Read Angel Island poems and analyze detainees' experiences.

*How to teach:* Read Angel Island poems.

- **Q:** Why did detainees carve poems into the walls at Angel Island?
  **A:** To express frustration, loneliness, and anger at long detentions and interrogations under exclusion laws.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Wong Kim Ark
*Student:* Analyze the 1898 Wong Kim Ark case on birthright citizenship.

*How to teach:* Analyze Wong Kim Ark (1898).

- **Q:** What did the Supreme Court decide in United States v. Wong Kim Ark (1898)?
  **A:** That a person born in the U.S. to Chinese parents is a citizen by birth under the 14th Amendment — establishing birthright citizenship regardless of parents' status.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a source analysis of one Angel Island poem: author's situation, purpose, and what it reveals about exclusion.

*How to teach:* Source analysis of one Angel Island poem.

- **Q:** When was the Chinese Exclusion Act repealed?
  **A:** 1943 (Magnuson Act), during World War II when China was a U.S. ally.

**Assessment:** Source analysis.

**Answer key:** Source-analysis rubric: 4 = identifies author, date, audience, and purpose (sourcing); accurately summarizes content; explains historical context; evaluates reliability/perspective and connects to the question with quotations; 3 = sourcing and content with limited context or evaluation; 2 = summary only; 1 = inaccurate or missing.

## Spanish II: Frida Kahlo and Diego Rivera: describing art

**Prepare:** Bookmark images of Frida Kahlo's 'Las dos Fridas' (1939, Museo de Arte Moderno, Mexico City) and Diego Rivera's 'Detroit Industry' murals (1932–33, Detroit Institute of Arts) or 'Pan American Unity' (1940, San Francisco). Preview: Kahlo's art shows injury and pain; some images include blood or exposed hearts.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Kahlo painted mostly landscapes: about a third of her works are self-portraits (autorretratos).
- Using 'pintura' only for paint: la pintura = painting (art form) or paint; el cuadro = a painting (artwork).

### Day 1 (Monday): Art vocabulary
*Student:* Learn 20 art terms and describe colors, shapes, and placement in one painting.

*How to teach:* Teach 20 terms; your child describes one painting (A la izquierda hay..., en el fondo se ve..., los colores son...).

- **Q:** Translate: 'In the background there are clouds.'
  **A:** En el fondo hay nubes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Frida Kahlo
*Student:* Read a short Spanish biography of Kahlo and explain how her life appears in "Las dos Fridas."

*How to teach:* Read a short Kahlo biography (bus accident at 18, years of pain and surgeries, marriage to Diego Rivera, Mexican identity). Your child explains how her life appears in 'Las dos Fridas.'

- **Q:** What does 'Las dos Fridas' show?
  **A:** Two Fridas seated hand in hand — one in a European-style white dress, one in a Tehuana (Mexican) dress — with their hearts exposed and connected by an artery; it reflects her dual heritage and her pain after her divorce from Rivera.
- **Q:** Why did Kahlo paint so many self-portraits?
  **A:** She spent long periods alone and bedridden after her accident; she said she painted herself because she was the subject she knew best.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Diego Rivera
*Student:* Analyze Rivera's Detroit Industry murals or the San Francisco "Pan American Unity" mural and describe the workers shown.

*How to teach:* Look at the Detroit Industry murals; your child describes the workers, machines, and colors.

- **Q:** What do the Detroit Industry murals show?
  **A:** Workers at the Ford River Rouge auto plant building cars, with huge machines — celebrating industry and labor.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Opinions
*Student:* Write opinions on two works using me parece que, creo que, and me gusta porque.

*How to teach:* Your child writes opinions on two works using me parece que, creo que, me gusta porque.

- **Q:** Write an opinion sentence about a mural.
  **A:** Example: Me parece que el mural es impresionante porque muestra a muchos trabajadores juntos.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Art vocabulary quiz and a 1-minute oral description of a painting.

*How to teach:* Give the vocabulary quiz and the 1-minute oral description.

- **Q:** Translate: 'self-portrait'
  **A:** el autorretrato

**Assessment:** Vocabulary quiz and oral description.

**Answer key:** Art vocabulary quiz sample key: el cuadro = painting; el mural = mural; el autorretrato = self-portrait; el fondo = background; en primer plano = in the foreground; el pintor/la pintora = painter; los colores vivos = bright colors; me parece que = it seems to me that. Oral description rubric: 4 = 1 minute, describes colors, figures, placement (a la izquierda, en el fondo), and gives an opinion with reason; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = list of words; 1 = minimal.

## Visual Art I: Digital art basics

**Prepare:** Computer or tablet with Krita (free) or a browser-based editor (e.g., Photopea). A scanned or photographed earlier drawing.

**Watch for:**
- Working on one layer. Correct: use separate layers for sketch, line, color and effects so changes are easy.
- Copying online images without permission. Correct: use your own photos or public-domain images.

### Day 1 (Monday): Digital tools
*Student:* Learn layers, brushes, selection, and opacity in a free app.

*How to teach:* Learn layers, brushes, selection, opacity.

- **Q:** What is a layer?
  **A:** A separate transparent sheet in a digital file that can be edited without affecting others.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Digital copy
*Student:* Recreate one of your earlier drawings digitally to learn the tools.

*How to teach:* Recreate an earlier drawing digitally.

- **Q:** What does lowering opacity do?
  **A:** Makes a layer more transparent.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Original
*Student:* Create an original digital illustration combining a photo and drawing.

*How to teach:* Original illustration combining photo and drawing.

- **Q:** How do you blend a photo into a drawing?
  **A:** Use layer masks, opacity and blending modes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Refine
*Student:* Refine color and layer effects.

*How to teach:* Refine color and layer effects.

- **Q:** What is a layer mask?
  **A:** A non-destructive way to hide parts of a layer by painting black/white on a mask.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Compare digital vs. traditional workflows in a short reflection.

*How to teach:* Reflect on digital vs. traditional.

**Assessment:** Digital illustration.

**Answer key:** Digital illustration: 4 = original image combining photo and drawing, uses multiple layers, masks/opacity effectively, cohesive color, reflection compares workflows with specifics; 3 = minor issues; 2 = single layer or copied; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 9 (Course 1): Disc golf rounds and course strategy

**Prepare:** Discs, scorecards, local course map (or 9-hole backyard course).

**Watch for:**
- Throwing out of turn. Correct: on the fairway the player farthest from the basket throws first; on the tee, lowest score on the previous hole goes first.
- Trying for distance on every throw. Correct: choose the safest shot that sets up an easy putt.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rules and etiquette
*Student:* Learn scoring, order of play, and etiquette; play 9 holes.

*How to teach:* Rules and etiquette; play 9.

- **Q:** Who throws first from the tee after the first hole?
  **A:** The player with the lowest score on the previous hole.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Course management
*Student:* Plan shots on each hole and play 9 holes, noting choices.

*How to teach:* Plan shots; play 9 noting choices.

- **Q:** What is course management?
  **A:** Choosing shots based on hazards, distance and your skills to minimize strokes.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Practice
*Student:* Practice the shots that cost you the most strokes.

*How to teach:* Practice costliest shots.

- **Q:** Why practice weaknesses?
  **A:** They cost the most strokes, so improvement there lowers scores fastest.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Full round
*Student:* Play 18 holes (or two 9-hole rounds) and walk the course.

*How to teach:* Play 18 (or two 9s).

- **Q:** Par for the course is 27 for 9 holes; you shot 31. What's your score?
  **A:** +4.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Compare scorecards and write about strategy improvements.

*How to teach:* Compare scorecards; reflect on strategy.

- **Q:** Name one etiquette rule.
  **A:** Don't throw until the group ahead is out of range; stay quiet and still while others throw; pick up trash.

**Assessment:** Scorecards and strategy reflection.

**Answer key:** Scorecards complete and totaled relative to par. Strategy reflection: 4 = compares rounds with numbers, identifies costly shot types, describes specific strategy changes and their results; 3 = general comparison; 2 = scores only; 1 = incomplete.

## Health & Wellness: Communicable diseases and immunity

**Prepare:** Glitter or lotion with glitter for handwashing demo; immune system diagram; California school immunization requirements (shotsforschool.org). Write quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking antibiotics cure viral infections like colds or flu. Correct: antibiotics only work on bacteria.
- Believing vaccines cause the disease they prevent. Correct: vaccines contain weakened, inactivated or partial pathogens or instructions (mRNA) that train immunity without causing disease.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pathogens
*Student:* Compare bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites with examples of diseases.

*How to teach:* Compare pathogens: bacteria (strep throat, TB), viruses (flu, COVID-19, measles), fungi (athlete's foot, ringworm), parasites (malaria, giardia).

- **Q:** Give one disease for each pathogen type.
  **A:** Bacteria – strep throat; virus – influenza; fungus – athlete's foot; parasite – malaria (protozoan) or lice/tapeworms.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Transmission
*Student:* Do a glitter handwashing experiment to show how germs spread.

*How to teach:* Put glitter on hands, shake hands/touch objects, then compare washing methods (20 seconds with soap).

- **Q:** How long should you wash hands?
  **A:** At least 20 seconds with soap and water.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Immune system
*Student:* Diagram the immune response and how vaccines train immunity.

*How to teach:* Diagram: first line (skin, mucus), innate (inflammation, white blood cells), adaptive (B cells → antibodies; T cells; memory cells).

- **Q:** How do vaccines work?
  **A:** They expose the immune system to a harmless form or piece of a pathogen so it makes antibodies and memory cells, ready to respond fast to the real pathogen.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Vaccines
*Student:* Review the California school immunization schedule and teen vaccines (Tdap, HPV, meningococcal, flu).

*How to teach:* Review CA requirements (Tdap booster for 7th grade) and recommended teen vaccines (HPV, meningococcal, flu, COVID-19).

- **Q:** Which vaccine is required for California 7th-grade entry?
  **A:** Tdap booster (and varicella if not already immune).

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Take a quiz on communicable diseases and write a paragraph explaining herd immunity using a classroom-sized example.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz; child writes herd immunity paragraph.

- **Q:** Explain herd immunity with a class of 30.
  **A:** If 27 of 30 students are immune, an infected student has few people to spread to, so the disease can't spread widely, protecting the 3 who can't be vaccinated.

**Assessment:** Quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: four pathogen types with examples; transmission routes (direct contact, droplets/airborne, contaminated food/water, vectors); handwashing 20 s; immune defenses; antibodies and memory cells; vaccines train immunity; antibiotics don't kill viruses; Tdap required for 7th grade. Herd immunity paragraph: 4 = explains immune majority blocks chains of transmission and protects vulnerable people with a numeric example.
