# Teacher's Guide — 9th Grade, Week 7

## English 9: Satire and voice: "Harrison Bergeron" and "The Story of an Hour"

**Prepare:** Obtain "Harrison Bergeron" (Vonnegut, in the anthology Welcome to the Monkey House or online at a library) and print "The Story of an Hour." Read both. Parental note: Harrison Bergeron has a shooting at the end.

**Watch for:**
- Concluding Vonnegut is against equality: the satire targets forced sameness that drags everyone down, not equal rights.
- Missing the irony of 'the joy that kills': the doctors think she died of joy at seeing her husband; readers know it was the shock of losing her new freedom.
- Writing a thesis that is a fact ('Mathilde loses a necklace'): a thesis must be arguable.

### Day 1 (Monday): Dystopian satire
*Student:* Read "Harrison Bergeron"; list each handicap and explain what idea about equality Vonnegut is exaggerating.

*How to teach:* Read 'Harrison Bergeron'. Your child lists each handicap and the idea it exaggerates.

- **Q:** What handicaps do George and the ballerinas wear?
  **A:** George wears a mental-handicap radio that blasts noises to stop his thoughts and a bag of birdshot weights; the ballerinas wear weights and masks to hide beauty.
- **Q:** Who enforces equality, and how does the story end for Harrison?
  **A:** Diana Moon Glampers, the Handicapper General, shoots Harrison and the ballerina with a shotgun on live TV.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Satire discussion
*Student:* Hold a recorded 15-minute discussion with a parent on whether the story mocks equality or forced sameness, citing three lines.

*How to teach:* Record a 15-minute discussion: does the story mock equality or forced sameness? Each side cites three lines.

- **Q:** Why do George and Hazel forget Harrison's death so quickly?
  **A:** Their handicaps (and Hazel's average mind) keep them from holding thoughts — Vonnegut shows how forced sameness destroys feeling and memory.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Chopin's hour
*Student:* Read "The Story of an Hour" twice; annotate imagery of the open window and the final line's irony.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Story of an Hour' twice; annotate window imagery and the final line.

- **Q:** What does Louise see through the open window, and what does it symbolize?
  **A:** Spring life — trees, patches of blue sky, sounds of life — symbolizing the new freedom opening before her.
- **Q:** Explain the irony of the last line.
  **A:** Doctors say she died 'of the joy that kills' (joy at Brently being alive), but she actually died from the shock of losing her freedom.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Essay prompt and thesis
*Student:* Choose a story from Units 1–2 and draft three possible thesis statements about theme; select the strongest and outline body paragraphs.

*How to teach:* Your child picks a story from Units 1–2, drafts three thesis statements, chooses the strongest, and outlines body paragraphs.

- **Q:** Which is a stronger thesis: 'The Necklace is about a necklace' or 'In The Necklace, Maupassant shows that pride turns a small mistake into a lifetime of suffering'?
  **A:** The second — it is specific and arguable.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Submit the outline with evidence chart (6 quotations) for feedback; reading check on both stories.

*How to teach:* Collect the outline and evidence chart; give the reading check.

- **Q:** What does Louise whisper repeatedly in her room?
  **A:** 'Free! Body and soul free!' (also 'free, free, free!').

**Assessment:** Outline/evidence chart reviewed for arguable thesis and relevant evidence; reading check.

**Answer key:** Outline/evidence review: proficient = arguable thesis about theme, three body topics that each support it, six relevant quotations with page numbers and a note on how each supports the claim. Reading check answers: 1) Harrison is 14 (and 7 feet tall). 2) Year: 2081. 3) The Handicapper General is Diana Moon Glampers. 4) George's handicap: mental radio and weights. 5) Louise Mallard learns of Brently's death in a railroad accident. 6) Her sister Josephine and husband's friend Richards tell her. 7) Brently returns alive. 8) She dies of heart disease.

## Algebra I: Absolute value equations and inequalities; unit review

**Prepare:** Prepare number lines. Write examples of real tolerances (a 12 mm bolt ± 0.05 mm, thermostat set to 68°F ± 3°). Prepare the Unit 2 review and test (see key).

**Watch for:**
- Solving |x − 3| = 5 with only x = 8: absolute value equations usually have two solutions (distance 5 in both directions).
- Writing |2x + 1| < 7 as 2x + 1 < 7 or 2x + 1 > −7: 'less than' gives an AND (between) statement: −7 < 2x + 1 < 7.
- Solving |x| = −2: an absolute value can't be negative — no solution.

### Day 1 (Monday): Absolute value as distance
*Student:* Solve |x−3|=5 by reasoning about distance on a number line; then solve 12 equations algebraically.

*How to teach:* On a number line, mark 3 and count 5 units both ways to find 8 and −2. Then model the algebra: x − 3 = 5 or x − 3 = −5. Your child solves 12 equations, checking both answers.

- **Q:** Solve |x − 3| = 5.
  **A:** x = 8 or x = −2.
- **Q:** Solve |2x + 1| = 9.
  **A:** 2x + 1 = 9 or −9 → x = 4 or x = −5.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Absolute value inequalities
*Student:* Solve and graph |2x+1|<7 and |x−4|≥3 type inequalities.

*How to teach:* Teach 'less thAND' (between) and 'greatOR' (outside). Model |2x + 1| < 7 → −7 < 2x + 1 < 7. Your child solves and graphs each type.

- **Q:** Solve |2x + 1| < 7.
  **A:** −7 < 2x + 1 < 7 → −4 < x < 3.
- **Q:** Solve |x − 4| ≥ 3.
  **A:** x − 4 ≤ −3 or x − 4 ≥ 3 → x ≤ 1 or x ≥ 7.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tolerance problems
*Student:* Write absolute value inequalities for manufacturing tolerances and thermostat ranges.

*How to teach:* Model a tolerance: a 12 mm bolt acceptable within 0.05 mm is |L − 12| ≤ 0.05. Your child writes and solves inequalities for each scenario and states the acceptable range.

- **Q:** A thermostat keeps the temperature within 3° of 68°F. Write and solve an inequality.
  **A:** |t − 68| ≤ 3 → 65 ≤ t ≤ 71.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit review
*Student:* Complete a mixed review of equations, formulas, and inequalities.

*How to teach:* Your child works the mixed review independently; you mark it and they correct errors, labeling each as a 'concept' or 'careless' mistake.

- **Q:** Solve 3(x − 4) = 2x + 5.
  **A:** x = 17.
- **Q:** Solve V = lwh for h.
  **A:** h = V/(lw).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 2 test.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test (about 50 minutes). Grade with the key and plan review for weak areas.

- **Q:** Before the test: how many solutions does |x + 1| = 0 have?
  **A:** One: x = −1.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test sample answers: 1) 3(x − 4) = 2x + 5 → x = 17. 2) 7 − 2(x + 1) = 3x → x = 1. 3) Solve V = lwh for h → h = V/(lw). 4) −2x + 3 > 11 → x < −4. 5) −5 ≤ 2x − 1 < 7 → −2 ≤ x < 4. 6) |x + 2| = 6 → x = 4 or −8. 7) |3x| ≤ 12 → −4 ≤ x ≤ 4. 8) |x − 1| > 2 → x < −1 or x > 3. 9) Gym A: $30 + $5/visit; Gym B: $50 + $3/visit → equal at 10 visits; B is cheaper after 10. 10) A part must be 5 cm within 0.02 cm: |L − 5| ≤ 0.02. Scoring: 80%+ proficient.

## Biology – The Living Earth: Photosynthesis

**Prepare:** Fresh spinach, hole punch or straw, baking soda, dish soap, 10 mL plastic syringe (no needle), clear cups, lamp, timer, ruler. Practice infiltrating disks once before teaching (pull plunger to vacuum until disks sink).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking plants get their mass from soil: most of a plant's mass comes from carbon in CO₂ from the air.
- Believing plants don't respire: plants do both photosynthesis and cellular respiration.
- Saying light energy 'becomes' glucose: light energy is converted to chemical energy stored in glucose bonds.

### Day 1 (Monday): Photosynthesis model
*Student:* Draw an input–output model of photosynthesis showing light energy stored in glucose bonds.

*How to teach:* Draw the input–output model.

- **Q:** Write the balanced equation for photosynthesis.
  **A:** 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂.
- **Q:** Where does the oxygen released come from?
  **A:** From splitting water molecules in the light reactions.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Floating leaf disk lab
*Student:* Punch spinach disks, infiltrate with baking-soda solution, and time disks floating under light.

*How to teach:* Punch 10 disks; infiltrate with baking-soda solution (with a drop of soap) using the syringe; place under light and record time to float.

- **Q:** Why do the disks float?
  **A:** Photosynthesis produces O₂ gas, which fills the air spaces in the leaf, making the disks buoyant.
- **Q:** What does the baking soda provide?
  **A:** A source of CO₂ (as bicarbonate) for photosynthesis.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Light intensity variable
*Student:* Repeat the leaf disk lab at two lamp distances and graph results.

*How to teach:* Repeat at two lamp distances; graph time for 50% to float.

- **Q:** Predict: will disks float faster near or far from the lamp?
  **A:** Nearer — more light intensity increases the rate of photosynthesis (up to a limit).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Chloroplast structure
*Student:* Label the chloroplast and trace light reactions and the Calvin cycle in a flowchart.

*How to teach:* Label the chloroplast; flowchart light reactions and Calvin cycle.

- **Q:** Where do the light reactions and Calvin cycle occur?
  **A:** Light reactions in the thylakoid membranes; Calvin cycle in the stroma.
- **Q:** What do the light reactions supply to the Calvin cycle?
  **A:** ATP and NADPH.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a lab report and take a photosynthesis quiz.

*How to teach:* Lab report and quiz.

- **Q:** What gas do plants take in for photosynthesis?
  **A:** Carbon dioxide.

**Assessment:** Leaf disk lab report and quiz.

**Answer key:** Lab report rubric: 4 = testable question, hypothesis, identified variables (independent, dependent, controlled), clear data table and labeled graph, conclusion that uses data and explains the science, sources of error; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = missing graph or analysis; 1 = incomplete. Quiz answers: 1) Equation (above). 2) Chlorophyll absorbs light (mainly red and blue). 3) Thylakoid/stroma locations. 4) O₂ from water. 5) Light intensity increases rate until another factor limits. 6) Most plant mass comes from CO₂.

## World Geography (S1) & Ethnic Studies (S2): Population distribution and growth

**Prepare:** Gapminder Tools (gapminder.org/tools); population pyramids (populationpyramid.net) for Japan, Nigeria, U.S.; world population density map.

**Watch for:**
- Equating dense with overpopulated: density alone doesn't show whether resources are sufficient.
- Thinking population pyramids show one moment only: their shape reveals past birth rates and predicts the future.
- Assuming the demographic transition model fits every country exactly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Distribution
*Student:* Map world population density and explain clusters.

*How to teach:* Map population density and explain clusters.

- **Q:** Name the world's major population clusters.
  **A:** East Asia, South Asia, Europe, and (smaller) the northeastern U.S.; also the Nile Valley and West Africa.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Population pyramids
*Student:* Compare pyramids for Japan, Nigeria, and the U.S.

*How to teach:* Compare pyramids.

- **Q:** Describe Nigeria's and Japan's pyramids and what they mean.
  **A:** Nigeria: wide base — high birth rate, young population, rapid growth. Japan: narrow base and wide top — low birth rate, aging population, shrinking.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Demographic transition
*Student:* Learn the demographic transition model and place countries.

*How to teach:* Teach the demographic transition model and place countries.

- **Q:** What happens in stage 2 of the demographic transition?
  **A:** Death rates fall (better food, medicine, sanitation) while birth rates stay high, so population grows rapidly.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Gapminder
*Student:* Use Gapminder to compare life expectancy and income over time.

*How to teach:* Use Gapminder for life expectancy and income.

- **Q:** What general trend does Gapminder show since 1800?
  **A:** Most countries moved toward longer lives and higher incomes, especially since 1950, though gaps remain.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on population.

*How to teach:* Population quiz.

- **Q:** What is the rate of natural increase?
  **A:** Birth rate minus death rate (usually as a percent).

**Assessment:** Population quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) Population density = people per area. 2) Pyramid shapes (expansive vs. constrictive). 3) DTM stages 1–4/5. 4) Japan stage 5 (declining); U.S. stage 4; Nigeria stage 2–3. 5) Natural increase: birth rate 30‰ − death rate 10‰ = 2%. 6) Dependency ratio = dependents (young + old) to working-age.

## Spanish II: Describing people and places in the past

**Prepare:** Gather drawing paper. Find a short story told in the imperfect (a Spanish-learner podcast like 'Cuentos de mi niñez' or a graded reader). Prepare a list of describing adjectives (alto, bajo, simpático, travieso, tranquilo).

**Watch for:**
- Using 'era' for location (*La casa era en el centro): location uses estar — La casa estaba en el centro.
- Pluralizing 'había' (*habían muchos árboles): había (there was/were) is always singular — Había muchos árboles.
- Adjective agreement errors: mi maestra era simpática (feminine), mis vecinos eran ruidosos (plural).

### Day 1 (Monday): Description
*Student:* Describe your childhood home and neighborhood using era, tenía, había.

*How to teach:* Model: 'Mi casa era grande. Tenía tres dormitorios. Había un parque cerca.' Your child draws the childhood home and neighborhood and writes 8 sentences using era, tenía, había, and estaba.

- **Q:** Translate: 'There were many children in my neighborhood.'
  **A:** Había muchos niños en mi vecindario (barrio).
- **Q:** Translate: 'The school was near my house.'
  **A:** La escuela estaba cerca de mi casa.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): People
*Student:* Write a 6-sentence description of a childhood friend or teacher (appearance, personality, what you used to do together).

*How to teach:* Your child writes a 6-sentence description of a friend or teacher: appearance (era alto, tenía pelo rizado), personality, and what they used to do together.

- **Q:** Translate: 'She had brown eyes and was very funny.'
  **A:** Tenía ojos marrones (or café) y era muy graciosa (or divertida).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Listening
*Student:* Listen to a story told in the imperfect and answer questions.

*How to teach:* Play the story twice; your child answers questions and lists imperfect verbs heard. Discuss how the imperfect sets the scene.

- **Q:** Why does a story often begin 'Había una vez...'?
  **A:** It means 'Once upon a time there was...' — the imperfect sets background, like describing the scene.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Conversation
*Student:* Compare childhoods with a partner in a guided conversation.

*How to teach:* Use prompts: ¿Dónde vivías? ¿Cómo era tu escuela? ¿Quién era tu mejor amigo? You answer about your own childhood; your child compares (Yo vivía en..., pero tú vivías en...).

- **Q:** Ask: 'What was your school like?'
  **A:** ¿Cómo era tu escuela?

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Writing quiz on descriptions in the past.

*How to teach:* Give the writing quiz: describe a childhood place and a person in at least 8 sentences. Score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Correct: 'Habían muchos árboles y la casa era en una calle tranquila.'
  **A:** Había muchos árboles y la casa estaba en una calle tranquila.

**Assessment:** Descriptive writing quiz.

**Answer key:** Writing quiz rubric (descriptive paragraph in the past): 4 = 8+ sentences, correct era/estaba/tenía/había, adjectives agree, varied vocabulary; 3 = 6–7 sentences, few errors; 2 = 4–5 sentences or frequent errors; 1 = fragments. Proficient sample: 'Mi casa era pequeña y azul. Estaba cerca de un parque. Había un árbol grande en el jardín. Mi vecina se llamaba Rosa; era alta y muy simpática.'

## Visual Art I: Two-point perspective

**Prepare:** Ruler, long paper (tape two sheets) for far-apart vanishing points.

**Watch for:**
- VPs too close, causing distortion. Correct: place them far apart, even off the page.
- Forgetting eye level. Correct: you see tops of objects below the horizon and bottoms above.

### Day 1 (Monday): Two-point boxes
*Student:* Draw boxes in two-point perspective at different heights.

*How to teach:* Two-point boxes at different heights.

- **Q:** What stays vertical in two-point perspective?
  **A:** All vertical edges.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Building
*Student:* Draw your house or a local building in two-point.

*How to teach:* Draw your house or a local building.

- **Q:** How many VPs does a building corner facing you use?
  **A:** Two — one for each side receding away.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Worm's/bird's-eye
*Student:* Draw a box from a bird's-eye and worm's-eye view.

*How to teach:* Bird's-eye and worm's-eye boxes (three-point).

- **Q:** What is a bird's-eye view?
  **A:** Looking down from high above; verticals converge to a third vanishing point below.

### Day 4 (Thursday): City block
*Student:* Draw an imaginary city block in two-point perspective.

*How to teach:* Imaginary city block.

- **Q:** How can you add variety to a city block?
  **A:** Vary building heights, rooflines, windows, signs and overlapping.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Self-critique perspective drawings with a checklist.

*How to teach:* Self-critique with checklist.

- **Q:** What's one item on your checklist?
  **A:** All horizontal edges go to the correct VP; verticals are vertical.

**Assessment:** Two-point city drawing.

**Answer key:** Two-point city drawing: 4 = two VPs on horizon, all edges converge correctly, multiple buildings with overlap, varied detail, clean ruler lines; 3 = minor errors; 2 = frequent errors; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 9 (Course 1): Jumping and throwing events

**Prepare:** Tape measure, soft ball or frisbee, sand or soft grass, cones for distance marks, protractor sketch for angles.

**Watch for:**
- Landing stiff-legged. Correct: land with knees bent, arms forward to avoid falling back.
- Thinking 45° is always best. Correct: with release above the ground, the optimal angle is a bit below 45°.

### Day 1 (Monday): Standing long jump
*Student:* Learn arm swing and two-foot takeoff; measure 5 jumps.

*How to teach:* Teach arm swing and two-foot takeoff; measure 5 jumps.

- **Q:** Where do you measure a jump?
  **A:** From the takeoff line to the back of the closest landing mark (usually the heel).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Running long jump
*Student:* Practice a short approach and landing on grass or sand.

*How to teach:* Short approach (5–7 steps), one-foot takeoff, two-foot landing on grass/sand.

- **Q:** Why does an approach increase distance?
  **A:** It adds horizontal speed (momentum) at takeoff.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Throwing
*Student:* Practice overhand throwing with step and follow-through; measure distances.

*How to teach:* Overhand throw: side to target, step with opposite foot, hip-shoulder rotation, follow-through.

- **Q:** What body sequence produces a powerful throw?
  **A:** Legs → hips → trunk → shoulder → elbow → wrist (kinetic chain).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Biomechanics
*Student:* Experiment with release angles and record which gives the longest throw.

*How to teach:* Test release angles (about 30°, 40°, 50°) and record.

- **Q:** Which angle likely produced the longest throw?
  **A:** Around 35–42° for a release above ground level; very high angles lose horizontal speed, very low ones hit the ground early.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Mini-meet: jump and throw for best marks and record them.

*How to teach:* Mini-meet; record best marks.

- **Q:** What is a personal best?
  **A:** The best mark you've achieved in an event.

**Assessment:** Mini-meet results and release-angle data.

**Answer key:** Mini-meet results recorded with units; release-angle data table with 3+ angles and distances, and a conclusion identifying the best angle with explanation (balance of height/time in air and horizontal speed; release height lowers the optimum below 45°).

## Health & Wellness: Food marketing, supplements and food safety

**Prepare:** Gather cereal boxes or online food ads, a food thermometer, and a list of safe temperatures. Write a 6-question food safety quiz.

**Watch for:**
- 'Health halo' — thinking 'natural,' 'organic' or 'multigrain' means healthy. Correct: check the label.
- Thinking food is safe if it smells fine. Correct: many pathogens don't change smell; follow time and temperature rules.

### Day 1 (Monday): Marketing techniques
*Student:* Analyze three food ads or cereal boxes for techniques (celebrity, health halo, cartoon characters) and identify the target audience.

*How to teach:* Analyze three ads or boxes for techniques and target audience.

- **Q:** What is a 'health halo'?
  **A:** When one healthy-sounding claim ('gluten-free,' 'made with real fruit') makes people think the whole product is healthy.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Supplements and energy drinks
*Student:* Research caffeine content and FDA regulation of supplements and energy drinks and write a consumer warning paragraph.

*How to teach:* Research caffeine content and FDA regulation of supplements (not approved before sale); write a consumer warning.

- **Q:** Why can't you assume a supplement is safe?
  **A:** Supplements don't need FDA approval before being sold; the FDA acts after problems are reported, and products may contain hidden ingredients.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Clean, separate, cook, chill
*Student:* Learn the four food safety steps and use a food thermometer to check safe internal temperatures while helping cook dinner.

*How to teach:* Teach Clean, Separate, Cook, Chill. Check temperatures while cooking: poultry 165°F, ground meats 160°F, whole cuts of beef/pork 145°F with 3-minute rest, leftovers 165°F.

- **Q:** What's the safe internal temperature for ground beef?
  **A:** 160°F.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Kitchen audit
*Student:* Audit your home kitchen for food safety (fridge temperature, cutting boards, leftovers dates) and record fixes.

*How to teach:* Audit fridge (40°F or below), freezer (0°F), cutting boards and leftovers (eat within 3–4 days).

- **Q:** How long can leftovers stay in the fridge?
  **A:** 3–4 days.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Create a counter-ad that markets a healthy food using the same persuasive techniques; take a short food-safety quiz.

*How to teach:* The child creates a counter-ad; give the quiz.

- **Q:** What is the 'danger zone'?
  **A:** 40°F–140°F, where bacteria multiply quickly; don't leave food out more than 2 hours (1 hour above 90°F).

**Assessment:** Counter-ad and food safety quiz.

**Answer key:** Food-safety quiz key: 1) Clean, Separate, Cook, Chill 2) poultry 165°F 3) ground meat 160°F 4) fridge ≤40°F 5) danger zone 40–140°F, 2-hour rule 6) leftovers 3–4 days. Counter-ad: 4 = uses two persuasive techniques accurately for a healthy food, clear audience and message.
