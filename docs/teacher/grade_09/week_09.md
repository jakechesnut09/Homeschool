# Teacher's Guide — 9th Grade, Week 9

## English 9: Rhetorical appeals in famous speeches

**Prepare:** Find texts and videos: Patrick Henry's 'Speech to the Virginia Convention' (1775) and JFK's 1961 Inaugural Address (JFK Library website has video). Collect 10 ads from magazines or online.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking logos means 'logical-sounding': logos is an appeal using facts, statistics, and reasoning.
- Confusing antithesis with any contrast: antithesis places opposing ideas in parallel structure ('Ask not... ask...').
- Naming devices without explaining their effect on the audience.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rhetorical triangle
*Student:* Learn speaker–audience–purpose and the three appeals; identify appeals in 10 modern advertisements.

*How to teach:* Teach speaker–audience–purpose and ethos, pathos, logos. Your child labels appeals in 10 ads.

- **Q:** An ad says '9 out of 10 dentists recommend our toothpaste.' Which appeal?
  **A:** Logos (statistic) — with some ethos (dentists' authority).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Patrick Henry
*Student:* Read "Give me liberty or give me death"; annotate rhetorical questions and emotional appeals and explain the speech's purpose.

*How to teach:* Read Henry's speech; annotate rhetorical questions and emotional appeals.

- **Q:** What is the purpose of Henry's speech?
  **A:** To persuade the Virginia Convention to prepare for armed resistance against Britain.
- **Q:** Explain the effect of 'Is life so dear, or peace so sweet, as to be purchased at the price of chains and slavery?'
  **A:** The rhetorical question frames peace with Britain as slavery, stirring emotion (pathos) and making resistance seem the only honorable choice.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Kennedy's Inaugural
*Student:* Watch and read JFK's 1961 Inaugural; find examples of antithesis and parallelism ("Ask not...").

*How to teach:* Watch and read JFK's Inaugural; find antithesis and parallelism.

- **Q:** Identify the device in 'Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country.'
  **A:** Antithesis (and chiasmus) — reversed, contrasting ideas in parallel form.

### Day 4 (Thursday): SOAPSTone analysis
*Student:* Complete a SOAPSTone chart for one speech and write a rhetorical analysis paragraph.

*How to teach:* Complete a SOAPSTone chart (Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject, Tone) for one speech; write a rhetorical analysis paragraph.

- **Q:** SOAPSTone for JFK's Inaugural: occasion and audience?
  **A:** Occasion: his inauguration, January 20, 1961, during the Cold War. Audience: Americans and the world, including allies and adversaries.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Deliver a 2-minute oral analysis of one rhetorical device; vocabulary quiz on rhetorical terms.

*How to teach:* Your child gives a 2-minute oral analysis of a device; give the term quiz.

- **Q:** Define parallelism.
  **A:** Repeating the same grammatical structure for related ideas (e.g., 'pay any price, bear any burden, meet any hardship').

**Assessment:** Rhetorical analysis paragraph and oral explanation; term quiz.

**Answer key:** Rhetorical paragraph: Paragraph rubric: 4 = arguable claim, two well-chosen quotations embedded and cited, commentary that explains HOW the evidence proves the claim; 3 = claim and evidence with thinner commentary; 2 = summary instead of analysis or only one piece of evidence; 1 = off-topic or no evidence. Oral explanation: 4 = names device, quotes it, explains effect on audience clearly in 2 minutes with eye contact. Term quiz answers: ethos = credibility; pathos = emotion; logos = logic/evidence; rhetorical question = question asked for effect, not answer; antithesis = contrasting ideas in parallel structure; parallelism = repeated grammatical structure; anaphora = repetition at the start of clauses; tone = speaker's attitude.

## Algebra I: Interpreting graphs and key features

**Prepare:** Make 8 graph cards and 8 story cards (filling a bathtub, a walk to the store and back, a ball thrown up, a cooling drink...). Find a real daily-temperature graph online for Friday.

**Watch for:**
- Treating a graph as a picture of the path: a distance-time graph of a hill walk is not the shape of the hill.
- Confusing increasing with 'positive': a function can be increasing while its values are negative.
- Ignoring units when comparing rates in a table vs. a graph: compute each rate as change in output over change in input.

### Day 1 (Monday): Graph stories
*Student:* Match 8 graphs to stories (e.g., filling a bathtub) and sketch graphs for 4 new stories.

*How to teach:* Narrate one story while sketching its graph (bathtub: rises steadily while filling, flat while bathing, falls while draining). Your child matches the 8 pairs and sketches 4 new ones with labeled axes.

- **Q:** Sketch distance-from-home vs. time for: walk to a friend's house, stay 20 minutes, run home.
  **A:** Line rises steadily, flat segment for 20 min, then a steeper line falling back to 0.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Key features
*Student:* Identify intercepts, intervals of increase/decrease, and maxima/minima on 10 graphs.

*How to teach:* Model identifying key features on f(x) = x² − 4: intercepts, where it increases/decreases, and the minimum. Your child labels features on 10 graphs.

- **Q:** For f(x) = x² − 4: find intercepts, the minimum, and where it increases.
  **A:** x-intercepts ±2, y-intercept −4; minimum −4 at x = 0; decreasing for x < 0, increasing for x > 0.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Context domain
*Student:* Determine reasonable domains for functions modeling a phone battery, a ball's height, and a savings plan.

*How to teach:* Discuss what inputs make sense: a phone battery percent goes from 0 to 100; a ball's height function only makes sense from launch until it lands. Your child writes reasonable domains for the three models with reasons.

- **Q:** A ball is thrown and lands after 3 seconds. What is a reasonable domain for its height function h(t)?
  **A:** 0 ≤ t ≤ 3 seconds.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing functions
*Student:* Compare two functions given as a table and a graph (e.g., two runners) and decide which grows faster.

*How to teach:* Compute each runner's rate: change in distance ÷ change in time. Your child compares a table to a graph and decides which grows faster.

- **Q:** Runner A: (0 s, 0 m), (1, 6), (2, 12), (3, 18). Runner B's graph is a line through (0, 0) and (4, 28). Who is faster?
  **A:** A: 6 m/s; B: 28/4 = 7 m/s. Runner B is faster.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph interpreting a real graph (e.g., daily temperatures) and take a short quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a paragraph interpreting the real temperature graph (when highest/lowest, when rising/falling, overall change), then takes the short quiz.

- **Q:** On a temperature graph, what does the highest point represent?
  **A:** The maximum temperature and the time of day it occurred.

**Assessment:** Graph interpretation paragraph and quiz.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric: 4 = names max/min with values and times, intervals of increase/decrease, rate comparison, and uses units; 3 = most features, minor errors; 2 = vague description; 1 = inaccurate. Quiz answers (for f(x) = −(x − 2)² + 9): 1) vertex/maximum (2, 9). 2) x-intercepts −1 and 5. 3) y-intercept 5. 4) increasing for x < 2, decreasing for x > 2. 5) A tub draining at 3 gal/min from 45 gal: domain 0 ≤ t ≤ 15 min.

## Biology – The Living Earth: Building biomolecules from sugar

**Prepare:** Iodine tincture (poison — adult use only, keep away from mouth), brown paper bag, droppers, foods: bread, potato, apple, butter, nuts, cooked egg white, cracker. Paper clips.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all carbohydrates are sugar: starch and cellulose are complex polysaccharides made of glucose.
- Believing proteins are only for muscles: proteins also act as enzymes, hormones, antibodies, and transporters.
- Confusing dehydration synthesis and hydrolysis: synthesis removes water to join monomers; hydrolysis adds water to split them.

### Day 1 (Monday): Macromolecules
*Student:* Build a chart of the four macromolecules, their monomers, and functions.

*How to teach:* Build the four-macromolecule chart.

- **Q:** Give the monomer of each macromolecule.
  **A:** Carbohydrates — monosaccharides (glucose); proteins — amino acids; nucleic acids — nucleotides; lipids — not true polymers (often glycerol + fatty acids).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Food testing
*Student:* Test foods for starch with iodine and lipids with the brown-paper test; record results.

*How to teach:* Test foods with iodine (starch turns blue-black) and brown paper (translucent spot = lipid).

- **Q:** A potato turns blue-black with iodine. What does it contain?
  **A:** Starch.
- **Q:** Butter leaves a translucent spot on paper. What does it contain?
  **A:** Lipid (fat).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): From sugar to protein
*Student:* Explain how carbon skeletons from glucose combine with nitrogen to form amino acids.

*How to teach:* Explain that plants use glucose's carbon skeletons plus nitrogen (from soil nitrates) to build amino acids.

- **Q:** Which element must be added to glucose-derived carbon skeletons to make amino acids?
  **A:** Nitrogen (from soil nitrates or ammonium, ultimately from nitrogen fixation).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Modeling polymers
*Student:* Build polymer chains with paper clips to model dehydration synthesis and hydrolysis.

*How to teach:* Build paper clip chains: remove a 'water' card for each link (dehydration synthesis); add one to split (hydrolysis).

- **Q:** How many water molecules are released when 5 monomers join in a chain?
  **A:** 4 (one per bond).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on biomolecules.

*How to teach:* Biomolecules quiz.

- **Q:** Which macromolecule stores genetic information?
  **A:** Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA).

**Assessment:** Food test data table and quiz.

**Answer key:** Food test data table: proficient = each food tested for starch and lipid with result and observation; correct interpretations (bread, potato, cracker: starch; butter, nuts: lipid; apple: little starch; egg white: protein, neither test positive). Quiz answers: 1) Monomers (above). 2) Enzymes are proteins. 3) Starch test = iodine blue-black. 4) Lipids: energy storage, membranes. 5) Dehydration synthesis joins monomers releasing water. 6) Hydrolysis breaks polymers with water.

## World Geography (S1) & Ethnic Studies (S2): Settlement patterns

**Prepare:** Topographic maps of San Francisco and Sacramento (USGS or Google Terrain), sketch paper. Prepare the Unit 3 test.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing site and situation: site = physical characteristics of the place itself; situation = location relative to other places.
- Expecting real cities to match models exactly: models are simplifications.
- Ignoring transportation in urban growth.

### Day 1 (Monday): Site and situation
*Student:* Analyze why cities like San Francisco and Sacramento grew where they did.

*How to teach:* Analyze site and situation for SF and Sacramento.

- **Q:** Describe San Francisco's site and situation.
  **A:** Site: hilly peninsula beside a large, deep natural harbor. Situation: gateway between the Pacific Ocean and the interior; port for the Gold Rush and Pacific trade.
- **Q:** Why did Sacramento grow where it did?
  **A:** At the confluence of the Sacramento and American Rivers, near the gold fields — a river port and later the western terminus of the Central Pacific Railroad.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Settlement patterns
*Student:* Compare clustered and dispersed rural settlement.

*How to teach:* Compare clustered and dispersed rural settlement.

- **Q:** Where would you expect dispersed settlement?
  **A:** In areas of large farms or ranches (e.g., the U.S. Great Plains), where farmhouses are spread out.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Urban models
*Student:* Sketch the concentric zone and sector models, then test each against a map of Sacramento or Los Angeles and decide which fits better.

*How to teach:* Sketch concentric zone and sector models; test against a city map.

- **Q:** Why doesn't Los Angeles fit the concentric zone model well?
  **A:** LA has many centers (downtown, Hollywood, Long Beach, etc.) and grew along rail and freeway corridors — closer to multiple-nuclei and sector patterns.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Local study
*Student:* Use Google Maps satellite view to map your town's downtown, residential areas, and commercial strips, and explain the pattern in a paragraph.

*How to teach:* Map your town's land uses.

- **Q:** Where do commercial strips usually develop?
  **A:** Along major roads and highways with high traffic.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 3 test.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 test.

- **Q:** What model shows the city as rings around the CBD?
  **A:** Burgess's concentric zone model.

**Assessment:** Unit 3 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 3 test sample answers: population clusters; pyramid interpretation; DTM stages; push/pull classification; refugee definition; Great Migration/Dust Bowl; site vs. situation; settlement patterns; urban models (concentric zone, sector, multiple nuclei). Scoring: 80%+ proficient.

## Spanish II: Las celebraciones: preterite of regular verbs

**Prepare:** Make a preterite chart: -ar: -é, -aste, -ó, -amos, -asteis, -aron; -er/-ir: -í, -iste, -ió, -imos, -isteis, -ieron. Have a calendar to talk about 'ayer, anoche, la semana pasada'.

**Watch for:**
- Missing accents that change meaning: hablo (I speak) vs. habló (he spoke).
- Forgetting spelling changes in yo forms: buscar → busqué, llegar → llegué, empezar → empecé.
- Confusing nosotros forms: -ar/-ir nosotros are the same in present and preterite (hablamos, vivimos); context and time words tell which.

### Day 1 (Monday): Preterite forms
*Student:* Learn regular preterite endings and conjugate 20 verbs.

*How to teach:* Explain the preterite tells completed actions at a specific time. Model 'Ayer comí pizza y miré una película.' Your child conjugates 20 verbs; check accents on yo and él forms.

- **Q:** Conjugate cantar in the preterite for yo, él, and ellos.
  **A:** canté, cantó, cantaron
- **Q:** Conjugate salir for tú.
  **A:** saliste

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Time expressions
*Student:* Write 10 sentences about what you did yesterday or last weekend.

*How to teach:* Review time expressions (ayer, anoche, anteayer, el fin de semana pasado, el año pasado). Your child writes 10 sentences about yesterday or last weekend.

- **Q:** Translate: 'Last night I studied and then I slept.'
  **A:** Anoche estudié y luego dormí.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Spelling changes
*Student:* Practice -car/-gar/-zar yo forms (busqué, llegué, empecé).

*How to teach:* Show why: to keep the sound, c→qu, g→gu, z→c before é. Practice busqué, saqué, toqué, llegué, pagué, jugué, empecé, almorcé, comencé.

- **Q:** Give the yo preterite of tocar, pagar, and almorzar.
  **A:** toqué, pagué, almorcé
- **Q:** Why is 'él buscó' spelled with c but 'yo busqué' with qu?
  **A:** Before o the c already sounds like k; before é it must be qu to keep the k sound.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Birthday story
*Student:* Write a 120-word paragraph about your last birthday celebration: who came, what you ate, and what you did.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a 120-word paragraph about the last birthday: who came, what you ate, what you did. Check that all completed actions are in the preterite.

- **Q:** Translate: 'My cousins came and we ate cake.'
  **A:** Mis primos vinieron y comimos pastel (or torta).
- **Q:** Translate: 'I opened the gifts.'
  **A:** Abrí los regalos.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Preterite quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the preterite quiz and review any spelling-change errors.

- **Q:** Fill in: Ayer yo ___ (llegar) tarde y ___ (empezar) la tarea a las nueve.
  **A:** llegué; empecé

**Assessment:** Preterite quiz.

**Answer key:** Preterite quiz sample key: hablar/yo — hablé; comer/tú — comiste; vivir/ella — vivió; bailar/nosotros — bailamos; abrir/ellos — abrieron; buscar/yo — busqué; llegar/yo — llegué; empezar/yo — empecé; jugar/yo — jugué; celebrar/ustedes — celebraron.

## Visual Art I: Color theory

**Prepare:** Tempera or acrylic primaries plus white and black, palette, brushes, water, paper. Images of Picasso Blue Period works ('The Old Guitarist,' 1903–04). Write a 10-question color quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Muddy mixtures from too many colors. Correct: mix two at a time; clean brushes.
- Confusing triadic and complementary. Correct: triadic = three evenly spaced; complementary = two opposites.

### Day 1 (Monday): Color wheel
*Student:* Mix a 12-step color wheel from primaries.

*How to teach:* Mix a 12-step color wheel.

- **Q:** How do you mix a tertiary color?
  **A:** Mix a primary with a neighboring secondary, e.g., blue + green = blue-green.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tints and shades
*Student:* Create tint and shade scales for one color.

*How to teach:* Tint and shade scales.

- **Q:** Tint vs. shade?
  **A:** Tint = color + white; shade = color + black.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Color schemes
*Student:* Paint four small squares in complementary, analogous, triadic, and monochromatic schemes.

*How to teach:* Four squares in complementary, analogous, triadic, monochromatic.

- **Q:** Give a triadic scheme.
  **A:** Red, yellow, blue (or orange, green, violet).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Color and mood
*Student:* Analyze how Picasso's Blue Period paintings use color for mood.

*How to teach:* Analyze Blue Period color and mood.

- **Q:** Why did Picasso use blue in this period?
  **A:** Blues express melancholy and poverty; the period followed the suicide of his friend Carlos Casagemas.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Color theory quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz.

- **Q:** Name a complementary pair.
  **A:** Red–green, blue–orange, yellow–violet.

**Assessment:** Color wheel and quiz.

**Answer key:** Color quiz key: 1) primaries red, yellow, blue 2) secondaries orange, green, violet 3) tertiary definition 4) complementary = opposite 5) analogous = adjacent 6) triadic = 3 evenly spaced 7) monochromatic = one hue + tints/shades 8) tint 9) shade 10) warm vs cool colors. Color wheel: 12 accurate, evenly mixed segments.

## PE 9 (Course 1): Badminton fundamentals

**Prepare:** Badminton rackets, shuttlecocks, net or string at about 5 ft (official 5 ft 1 in at posts). Targets (hoops/tape) for serves.

**Watch for:**
- Gripping the racket like a frying pan (panhandle). Correct: 'handshake' forehand grip; rotate thumb onto the flat bevel for backhand.
- Serving above the waist. Correct: the shuttle must be hit below 1.15 m (about waist height), with an underhand motion.

### Day 1 (Monday): Grips and footwork
*Student:* Learn forehand and backhand grips and ready position; rally 100 hits with a partner.

*How to teach:* Forehand and backhand grips, ready position; rally 100 hits.

- **Q:** Describe the forehand grip.
  **A:** Shake hands with the handle, forming a V between thumb and index finger along the side; hold loosely.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Serves
*Student:* Practice short and long serves into targets.

*How to teach:* Short and long serves into targets.

- **Q:** When is a short serve used?
  **A:** In doubles especially, to land just past the short service line and prevent an attack.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Clears
*Student:* Practice overhead forehand clears, focusing on follow-through.

*How to teach:* Overhead forehand clears with follow-through.

- **Q:** What is a clear?
  **A:** A high, deep shot to the opponent's back line to push them back and gain time.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Drop shots
*Student:* Practice drop shots and net play.

*How to teach:* Drop shots and net play.

- **Q:** Why use a drop shot?
  **A:** It lands just over the net, forcing a deep opponent to rush forward.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Skills test: serve accuracy and rally count.

*How to teach:* Skills test: serve accuracy and rally count.

- **Q:** What's a legal badminton serve?
  **A:** Underhand, below about waist height (1.15 m), diagonal into the opponent's service court, feet stationary.

**Assessment:** Badminton skills test.

**Answer key:** Skills test: serve accuracy (e.g., 10 serves to target: 7+ proficient), longest cooperative rally (20+ proficient), correct grips and ready position observed.

## Health & Wellness: Mental wellness, self-esteem and resilience

**Prepare:** Health journal; find a list of mental wellness signs (e.g., NAMI or Mental Health America) and an emotion wheel. Choose a short biography of someone who overcame adversity (e.g., Malala Yousafzai, Simone Biles, Temple Grandin).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking mental wellness means always being happy. Correct: it means coping with normal stresses, functioning and having a range of emotions.
- Confusing self-esteem with arrogance. Correct: healthy self-esteem is realistic respect for yourself.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mental wellness
*Student:* Brainstorm signs of good mental health and compare to a list from a mental health organization.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm signs, then compare to the organization's list.

- **Q:** Name three signs of good mental health.
  **A:** Coping with stress, healthy relationships, expressing emotions appropriately, adapting to change, feeling purpose, asking for help when needed.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Emotions
*Student:* Use an emotion wheel to name emotions felt this week and identify body cues for each.

*How to teach:* Use the emotion wheel to name feelings and body cues.

- **Q:** What body cues might come with anxiety?
  **A:** Racing heart, tight chest, sweaty hands, stomachache, shallow breathing, muscle tension.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Self-esteem
*Student:* Write a list of 10 personal strengths and ask a family member to add three more.

*How to teach:* The child lists 10 strengths; a family member adds three.

- **Q:** Why does naming strengths help?
  **A:** It builds realistic self-esteem and confidence, which supports resilience.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Resilience stories
*Student:* Read a short biography of someone who overcame adversity and identify the protective factors that helped them.

*How to teach:* Read the biography; identify protective factors.

- **Q:** What protective factors helped the person?
  **A:** Answers vary by biography; e.g., Malala — supportive father, education, purpose and global support network.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a reflection describing one setback you faced and the resilience skills you used or could use.

*How to teach:* Child writes the reflection.

- **Q:** Name two resilience skills.
  **A:** Problem solving, positive self-talk, seeking support, flexible thinking, self-care.

**Assessment:** Resilience reflection scored with a rubric.

**Answer key:** Resilience reflection rubric: 4 = specific setback, at least two resilience skills used or planned, connection to protective factors, insight about growth; 3 = setback and one skill; 2 = general description; 1 = incomplete.
