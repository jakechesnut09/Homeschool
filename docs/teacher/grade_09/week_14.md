# Teacher's Guide — 9th Grade, Week 14

## English 9: Sound and form: sonnets and the Harlem Renaissance

**Prepare:** Print Sonnets 18 and 130, Hughes's 'Harlem' and 'Mother to Son,' and Dunbar's 'We Wear the Mask.' Review iambic pentameter (da-DUM ×5) and the Shakespearean form (3 quatrains + couplet, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Sonnet 130 insults the speaker's love: it mocks exaggerated poetic comparisons and ends by declaring his love 'as rare' as any.
- Marking stress by syllable count only: say the line aloud to hear natural stresses.
- Reading 'We Wear the Mask' as about individuals only: Dunbar writes about African Americans hiding pain from a society that doesn't want to see it.

### Day 1 (Monday): Shakespearean sonnet
*Student:* Scan Sonnet 18 for iambic pentameter and mark the rhyme scheme and volta; paraphrase each quatrain.

*How to teach:* Scan line 1 of Sonnet 18 together; mark rhyme scheme and volta; your child paraphrases each quatrain.

- **Q:** What is the rhyme scheme of Sonnet 18 and where is the volta?
  **A:** ABAB CDCD EFEF GG; the volta comes at line 9, 'But thy eternal summer shall not fade.'
- **Q:** What does the couplet claim?
  **A:** As long as people live and read, this poem will live and give the beloved life — the poem makes her beauty immortal.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Parody of praise
*Student:* Read Sonnet 130 and compare its attitude toward love with Sonnet 18 in a Venn diagram.

*How to teach:* Read Sonnet 130; compare with 18 in a Venn diagram.

- **Q:** What does 'My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun' do to traditional love poetry?
  **A:** It parodies the exaggerated comparisons poets use; the couplet says his love is as rare as any woman 'belied with false compare.'

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hughes's images
*Student:* Read "Harlem" and "Mother to Son"; explain how similes and the staircase metaphor express deferred dreams and perseverance.

*How to teach:* Read both Hughes poems; explain similes and the staircase metaphor.

- **Q:** What similes describe a deferred dream in 'Harlem'?
  **A:** It might 'dry up like a raisin in the sun,' 'fester like a sore,' 'stink like rotten meat,' 'crust and sugar over like a syrupy sweet,' or 'sag like a heavy load' — or explode.
- **Q:** What does 'Life for me ain't been no crystal stair' mean?
  **A:** Her life has been hard — full of 'tacks,' 'splinters,' and 'boards torn up' — but she keeps 'climbin'' and urges her son not to quit.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Dunbar's mask
*Student:* Read "We Wear the Mask"; analyze its rondeau form and refrain and connect it to Hughes.

*How to teach:* Read 'We Wear the Mask'; analyze the rondeau form and refrain; connect to Hughes.

- **Q:** What does the mask hide, according to Dunbar?
  **A:** Pain and suffering ('torn and bleeding hearts'); the mask 'grins and lies' so the world sees only smiles.
- **Q:** What is the effect of the refrain 'We wear the mask'?
  **A:** Repetition emphasizes the constant, unavoidable nature of hiding pain.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on poetic terms and scansion; write a comparative paragraph on two poems' use of form.

*How to teach:* Poetic-terms/scansion quiz; comparative paragraph on form.

- **Q:** How many syllables in a line of iambic pentameter?
  **A:** Ten (five iambs).

**Assessment:** Poetic terms/scansion quiz and comparative paragraph.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) iamb = unstressed-stressed. 2) pentameter = five feet. 3) Shakespearean sonnet = 14 lines, 3 quatrains + couplet. 4) volta = turn. 5) refrain = repeated line. 6) Scan 'Shall I com-PARE thee TO a SUM-mer's DAY' (shall I / com-PARE / thee TO / a SUM / mer's DAY). 7) Rhyme scheme of Sonnet 130 = ABAB CDCD EFEF GG. 8) Simile vs. metaphor definitions. Paragraph rubric: 4 = arguable claim, two well-chosen quotations embedded and cited, commentary that explains HOW the evidence proves the claim; 3 = claim and evidence with thinner commentary; 2 = summary instead of analysis or only one piece of evidence; 1 = off-topic or no evidence.

## Algebra I: Linear modeling project

**Prepare:** Safety: an adult supervises candle burning (stable holder, away from curtains, fire extinguisher or water nearby) — or use the water-bottle option. Ruler, timer, data table, Desmos. Plan the Unit 4 test.

**Watch for:**
- Forcing the line through the first and last points: a line of fit should balance points above and below.
- Predicting far beyond the data without caveats: extrapolation is less reliable (a candle's wick stub, a bottle's pressure change).
- Interpreting slope without units: −0.1 cm/min, not just −0.1.

### Day 1 (Monday): Collect data
*Student:* Burn a candle (or drain a bottle of water through a hole) and record height every 5 minutes.

*How to teach:* Set up the candle or bottle with a ruler behind it; record height every 5 minutes for at least 45 minutes. Your child keeps the data table and notes conditions (drafts, etc.).

- **Q:** Why record at equal time intervals?
  **A:** Equal intervals make it easy to see whether the change is constant (linear) and to compute rates.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Model the data
*Student:* Plot data, draw a line of fit, and write its equation.

*How to teach:* Plot data on paper and in Desmos; draw a line of fit and find its equation from two points on the line (or Desmos regression). Your child writes the equation with units.

- **Q:** Your line of fit goes through (0, 15) and (30, 12) (minutes, cm). Find its equation.
  **A:** m = −3/30 = −0.1 cm/min; h = 15 − 0.1t.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Interpret
*Student:* Interpret slope and intercept and predict when the candle will burn out.

*How to teach:* Interpret slope (burn rate) and intercept (starting height), then predict burnout by setting h = 0. Your child writes interpretations and a prediction.

- **Q:** Using h = 15 − 0.1t, when does the candle burn out?
  **A:** 0 = 15 − 0.1t → t = 150 minutes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Report
*Student:* Write a one-page report with graph, equation, prediction, and limitations.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a one-page report: question, method, data table, graph, equation, interpretation, prediction, limitations. Give feedback on clarity.

- **Q:** Name one limitation of a linear model for this experiment.
  **A:** Burn rate may change (wick length, drafts, wax shape), and the model can't be trusted beyond the data range.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 4 test on linear functions.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 4 test (about 50 minutes).

- **Q:** Slope of a line through (0, 4) and (2, 0)?
  **A:** −2.

**Assessment:** Project report and Unit 4 test.

**Answer key:** Report rubric: 4 = complete data table, accurate graph with labeled axes and units, correct line of fit, slope/intercept interpreted with units, reasonable prediction, two thoughtful limitations; 3 = minor errors; 2 = missing parts; 1 = little evidence. Unit 4 test sample answers: 1) Slope through (−2, 1), (2, 9) → 2. 2) y = 2x + 5 for that line. 3) Line through (3, −1) with slope −2 → y = −2x + 5. 4) Perpendicular to y = ⅓x + 2 through origin → y = −3x. 5) 4x + 2y = 12 intercepts (3, 0), (0, 6). 6) Inverse of f(x) = 5x + 2 → (x − 2)/5. 7) Average rate of change interpretation with units.

## Biology – The Living Earth: Natural selection and probability

**Prepare:** Stream HHMI BioInteractive 'The Making of the Fittest: Natural Selection and Adaptation' (rock pocket mice, about 10 minutes). Spreadsheet software.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the mice turned dark because they lived on dark rocks: the dark-fur mutation arose randomly; predators selected it.
- Ignoring probability: a small advantage can change frequencies a lot over many generations.
- Confusing frequency (proportion) with number of individuals.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rock pocket mice
*Student:* Watch the HHMI rock pocket mouse film and record evidence of selection.

*How to teach:* Watch the film; record evidence of selection.

- **Q:** Why are dark mice common on lava flows but light mice common on sandy desert?
  **A:** Predators (owls, hawks) more easily see mice that contrast with the background, so matching fur survives and reproduces more.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Frequency calculations
*Student:* Calculate changes in trait proportions over generations from data.

*How to teach:* Calculate trait proportions over generations from data.

- **Q:** A population of 200 mice has 50 dark mice. What is the dark-fur frequency? If it becomes 120 of 200, what is the change?
  **A:** 0.25 → 0.60; an increase of 0.35 (35 percentage points).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Spreadsheet model
*Student:* Build a spreadsheet model where a trait gives a 10% survival advantage and graph frequency over 20 generations.

*How to teach:* Spreadsheet: new p = 1.1p / (1.1p + (1 − p)); graph 20 generations.

- **Q:** Starting at 10% dark mice with a 10% survival advantage, about what frequency after 20 generations?
  **A:** About 43% (0.43).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Explain adaptation
*Student:* Write a CER explaining how dark fur became common on lava flows.

*How to teach:* Write a CER explaining dark fur on lava flows.

- **Q:** What gene was linked to dark fur in some rock pocket mice?
  **A:** Mutations in the Mc1r gene (melanocortin-1 receptor) in Arizona populations.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on probability and selection.

*How to teach:* Quiz on probability and selection.

- **Q:** If a trait has no survival advantage, what happens to its frequency (ignoring chance)?
  **A:** It stays about the same.

**Assessment:** CER and quiz.

**Answer key:** CER rubric: 4 = claim answers the question; evidence uses specific data (numbers, units, observations); reasoning connects the evidence to a scientific principle; 3 = claim and evidence with partial reasoning; 2 = claim with vague evidence; 1 = claim only or incorrect. Proficient CER: claim — dark fur became common on lava because of natural selection; evidence — predation rates/survival data and frequency changes from the film; reasoning — random mutation produced dark fur, which was camouflaged on dark rock, so those mice survived and reproduced more, passing on the allele. Quiz answers: frequency calculations as above; 10% advantage model reaches ~0.87 after 20 generations starting at 0.5.

## World Geography (S1) & Ethnic Studies (S2): Resources, trade, and supply chains

**Prepare:** Clothing and electronics labels around the house; world map; data on Port of Los Angeles/Long Beach (portoflosangeles.org lists top trading partners). Lithium and cobalt readings.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 'Made in' means all parts came from one place: global supply chains span many countries.
- Believing resources are fixed in value: a resource's importance changes with technology (lithium for batteries).
- Assuming countries with resources are always rich (the 'resource curse').

### Day 1 (Monday): Resource map
*Student:* Map world reserves of oil, lithium, fresh water, and arable land and identify which countries hold leverage.

*How to teach:* Map reserves of oil, lithium, fresh water, arable land.

- **Q:** Which region holds the largest lithium reserves?
  **A:** The 'Lithium Triangle' of Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia (Australia is the largest producer).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Label hunt
*Student:* Record the country of origin of 15 items in your home and plot them on a world map.

*How to teach:* Record origins of 15 items and plot them.

- **Q:** What pattern usually appears in a label hunt?
  **A:** Many manufactured goods come from Asia (China, Vietnam, Bangladesh, etc.).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Smartphone supply chain
*Student:* Research where a phone's cobalt, chips, and assembly come from and draw the chain with distances.

*How to teach:* Research a smartphone's supply chain.

- **Q:** Where does most of the world's cobalt come from?
  **A:** The Democratic Republic of the Congo (around 70%).
- **Q:** Where are most advanced chips made?
  **A:** Taiwan (TSMC), with others in South Korea and elsewhere; final assembly is largely in China (also India, Vietnam).

### Day 4 (Thursday): California trade
*Student:* Use port of Los Angeles/Long Beach data to identify California's top trading partners and goods.

*How to teach:* Identify California's top trading partners via port data.

- **Q:** Name the top trading partner through the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach.
  **A:** China (followed by partners such as Japan, Vietnam, South Korea, and Taiwan).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining how one resource's meaning has changed over time (e.g., lithium).

*How to teach:* Paragraph on how a resource's meaning changed.

- **Q:** How has lithium's importance changed?
  **A:** Once a niche mineral (used in ceramics and medicine), it became 'white gold' for rechargeable batteries in phones and electric vehicles.

**Assessment:** Supply chain map and resource paragraph.

**Answer key:** Supply chain map: proficient = at least 5 locations labeled with role (mining, refining, chip making, assembly), arrows and approximate distances. Resource paragraph: Paragraph rubric: 4 = clear claim, two specific pieces of evidence (with source names/dates), explanation of how evidence supports the claim, accurate history; 3 = claim and evidence with thin explanation; 2 = general statements without evidence; 1 = inaccurate.

## Spanish II: En el restaurante: object pronouns

**Prepare:** Find a real menu from a Mexican or Spanish restaurant (online). Chart direct object pronouns (me, te, lo, la, nos, os, los, las) and indirect (me, te, le, nos, os, les). Set up a table with a napkin and 'la cuenta' slip for the role-play.

**Watch for:**
- Using 'le' for direct objects of things (*Le compré — the book): things take lo/la/los/las — Lo compré.
- Putting the pronoun after a conjugated verb (*Compro lo): it goes before a conjugated verb (Lo compro) or attached to an infinitive (Voy a comprarlo).
- Thinking tipping works the same everywhere: in Spain service is included in the price and small tips (rounding up) are customary but not required; in the U.S. 15–20% is expected.

### Day 1 (Monday): Direct object pronouns
*Student:* Replace nouns with lo/la/los/las in 20 sentences.

*How to teach:* Model: '¿Tienes la tarea? — Sí, la tengo.' Your child replaces nouns with lo/la/los/las in 20 sentences, matching gender and number.

- **Q:** Replace the noun: 'Compro las manzanas.'
  **A:** Las compro.
- **Q:** Replace the noun: 'Voy a preparar el arroz.'
  **A:** Voy a prepararlo. / Lo voy a preparar.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Indirect object pronouns
*Student:* Practice me, te, le, nos, les with verbs like traer and servir.

*How to teach:* Indirect objects answer 'to/for whom.' Model 'El mesero me trae el agua. Le sirvo café a mi papá.' Your child writes sentences with traer and servir.

- **Q:** Translate: 'The waiter brings us the menu.'
  **A:** El mesero nos trae el menú.
- **Q:** Translate: 'I serve them soup.'
  **A:** Les sirvo sopa.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Restaurant role-play
*Student:* Role-play ordering a full meal with a family member as server.

*How to teach:* You play the mesero: '¿Qué desean tomar?' Your child orders a full meal using usted, quiero/quisiera, and asks '¿Me trae la cuenta, por favor?'

- **Q:** How do you politely ask for the check?
  **A:** La cuenta, por favor. / ¿Me trae la cuenta, por favor?
- **Q:** How do you politely order: 'I would like the chicken'?
  **A:** Quisiera el pollo, por favor.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cultural norms
*Student:* Compare restaurant customs (tipping, la sobremesa) in Spain and the U.S.

*How to teach:* Discuss la sobremesa (staying at the table to talk after a meal) and tipping differences. Your child writes 4 comparison sentences.

- **Q:** What is 'la sobremesa'?
  **A:** The time spent relaxing and talking at the table after a meal; in Spain, servers don't rush you or bring the check until you ask.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Pronoun quiz.

*How to teach:* Give the pronoun quiz and score the role-play.

- **Q:** Answer using a pronoun: '¿Pediste el postre?'
  **A:** Sí, lo pedí. / No, no lo pedí.

**Assessment:** Pronoun quiz and role-play rubric.

**Answer key:** Pronoun quiz sample key: 1) Pido la sopa — La pido 2) Traigo los platos — Los traigo 3) Comemos las tortillas — Las comemos 4) El mesero trae el menú a nosotros — Nos trae el menú 5) Sirvo agua a mis padres — Les sirvo agua 6) Voy a pedir el flan — Voy a pedirlo / Lo voy a pedir. Role-play rubric: 4 = greets, orders drink/main/dessert, asks for la cuenta, uses usted and 2+ pronouns correctly; 3 = minor errors; 2 = incomplete; 1 = needs heavy prompting.

## Visual Art I: Principles of design

**Prepare:** Magazines, scissors, glue, colored paper. List of principles: balance, emphasis, contrast, movement, rhythm, pattern, proportion/scale, unity, variety.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing elements and principles. Correct: elements (line, shape, form, color, value, texture, space) are the tools; principles are how they're arranged.
- Thinking asymmetrical means unbalanced. Correct: asymmetrical balance uses different elements of equal visual weight.

### Day 1 (Monday): Principles hunt
*Student:* Find magazine examples of each principle of design and make a reference sheet.

*How to teach:* Find magazine examples of each principle.

- **Q:** Define emphasis.
  **A:** Creating a focal point that draws the viewer's eye first.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Balance
*Student:* Create symmetrical, asymmetrical, and radial balance compositions with cut paper.

*How to teach:* Cut-paper symmetrical, asymmetrical, radial balance.

- **Q:** Give an example of radial balance.
  **A:** A flower, a mandala or a rose window — designs radiate from a center.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Emphasis
*Student:* Make a composition that uses contrast to create a focal point.

*How to teach:* Contrast composition with a focal point.

- **Q:** Name two ways to create emphasis.
  **A:** Contrast in color or value, isolation, placement, size, converging lines.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rhythm
*Student:* Create a rhythm/pattern design.

*How to teach:* Rhythm/pattern design.

- **Q:** Difference between pattern and rhythm?
  **A:** Pattern is a repeated element; rhythm is the visual movement created by repetition (regular, alternating, progressive).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Principles quiz.

*How to teach:* Principles quiz.

- **Q:** What is unity?
  **A:** The sense that all parts of an artwork belong together.

**Assessment:** Principles reference sheet and quiz.

**Answer key:** Principles quiz key: balance (symmetrical/asymmetrical/radial), emphasis (focal point), contrast, movement (path of the eye), rhythm (repetition creating movement), pattern (repeated motif), proportion/scale, unity (wholeness), variety (difference for interest); identify elements vs principles. Reference sheet: one labeled example per principle.

## PE 9 (Course 1): Folk and cultural dances

**Prepare:** Music and tutorial videos for zapateado (ballet folklórico), the hora (Israeli circle dance, e.g., 'Hava Nagila'), Tinikling (Philippines; two long poles or tape lines) and square-dance calls.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Tinikling is Korean. Correct: Tinikling is the national folk dance of the Philippines, imitating the tikling bird stepping between bamboo traps.
- Getting feet caught in Tinikling poles. Correct: practice with tape lines first; pole clappers keep a steady 3-count (tap, tap, close).

### Day 1 (Monday): Mexican folk dance
*Student:* Learn basic zapateado steps from ballet folklórico.

*How to teach:* Teach basic zapateado (heel-toe stamps, rhythmic patterns).

- **Q:** Where does ballet folklórico come from?
  **A:** Mexico — traditional regional dances, popularized as a stage form in the 1950s by Amalia Hernández.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Hora
*Student:* Learn the Israeli hora in a circle with family.

*How to teach:* Teach the hora in a circle: step left, cross right behind, step left, hop/kick, etc.

- **Q:** What occasion is the hora often danced at?
  **A:** Celebrations such as Jewish weddings and bar/bat mitzvahs.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Filipino folk dance
*Student:* Learn basic steps of Tinikling (with sticks or tape lines).

*How to teach:* Teach Tinikling basic: two hops between lines/poles, one hop outside, in 3/4 rhythm.

- **Q:** What does Tinikling imitate?
  **A:** The tikling bird stepping between grass stems or dodging bamboo traps set by farmers.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Square dance
*Student:* Learn basic square dance calls.

*How to teach:* Teach square-dance calls: honor, do-si-do, swing, promenade, allemande left.

- **Q:** What does 'allemande left' mean?
  **A:** Turn to your corner, join left hands (or forearms) and walk around each other back to place.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform one cultural dance and explain its origin.

*How to teach:* Perform one dance and explain its origin.

- **Q:** What should an origin explanation include?
  **A:** Country/culture, when or why it's danced, and what movements represent.

**Assessment:** Cultural dance performance and explanation.

**Answer key:** Performance and explanation: 4 = performs dance accurately on the beat and explains origin (country, cultural meaning, traditional occasion or costume) accurately; 3 = minor errors; 2 = performance only or inaccurate origin; 1 = incomplete.

## Health & Wellness: The brain and addiction

**Prepare:** Brain diagram (reward pathway: ventral tegmental area → nucleus accumbens → prefrontal cortex) and NIDA 'Drugs, Brains, and Behavior' materials. Short quiz.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking addiction only happens with illegal drugs. Correct: alcohol, nicotine and prescription drugs are addictive.
- Believing the teen brain is fully mature. Correct: the prefrontal cortex develops into the mid-20s, so teens are more vulnerable to addiction.

### Day 1 (Monday): Reward pathway
*Student:* Label a diagram of the brain's reward pathway and explain how dopamine reinforces behavior.

*How to teach:* Label the reward pathway; explain drugs cause large dopamine surges that reinforce use.

- **Q:** What role does dopamine play?
  **A:** It signals reward and teaches the brain to repeat a behavior; drugs flood the system with dopamine, strongly reinforcing use.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Teen brain
*Student:* Read NIDA materials on adolescent brain development and summarize why the prefrontal cortex matters.

*How to teach:* Read NIDA on adolescent brains and summarize the prefrontal cortex's role.

- **Q:** What does the prefrontal cortex do?
  **A:** Planning, judgment, impulse control and decision making.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tolerance and dependence
*Student:* Create a flowchart showing the progression from use to tolerance, dependence and addiction.

*How to teach:* Flowchart: use → misuse → tolerance → dependence (withdrawal) → addiction.

- **Q:** What is withdrawal?
  **A:** Uncomfortable physical/mental symptoms when a drug is stopped after dependence develops.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Addiction as disease
*Student:* Discuss how genetics, environment and early use affect addiction risk.

*How to teach:* Discuss genetics (about 40–60% of risk), environment and early use.

- **Q:** Why does early use increase addiction risk?
  **A:** The developing brain adapts more readily, so earlier use is linked to higher rates of later addiction.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write an explanation of addiction for a younger student and take a short quiz.

*How to teach:* Child writes the explanation; give the quiz.

- **Q:** Is addiction curable or treatable?
  **A:** Treatable and manageable like other chronic diseases; relapse is possible.

**Assessment:** Quiz and explanatory paragraph.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: dopamine; reward pathway; prefrontal cortex – judgment/impulse control, matures mid-20s; tolerance; dependence/withdrawal; addiction = chronic relapsing brain disease; risk factors: genetics, environment, early use. Paragraph: 4 = accurate, simple, mentions brain changes and treatment.
