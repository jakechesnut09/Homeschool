# Teacher's Guide — 9th Grade, Week 6

## English 9: Structure and time: "The Monkey's Paw" and "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge"

**Prepare:** Print "The Monkey's Paw" and "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge." Read both first. Parental note: Owl Creek opens with a Civil War hanging; The Monkey's Paw includes a grisly off-page death. Prepare colon examples.

**Watch for:**
- Reading Part III of 'Owl Creek' as a real escape: the ending reveals the escape was imagined in the moments before Farquhar's death.
- Assuming the paw's wishes are 'coincidences' or 'magic' without considering ambiguity: Jacobs keeps both explanations possible.
- Colon misuse after a verb ('My favorites are: ...'): use a colon after a complete independent clause.

### Day 1 (Monday): Foreshadowing
*Student:* Read "The Monkey's Paw" and track every warning sign in a foreshadowing log; predict before each wish.

*How to teach:* Read 'The Monkey's Paw'. Your child keeps a foreshadowing log and predicts before each wish.

- **Q:** What warnings does Sergeant-Major Morris give about the paw?
  **A:** He says a fakir put a spell on it to show that fate rules people's lives and those who interfere suffer; he throws it in the fire and says the first owner's third wish was for death.
- **Q:** What happens after the first wish for £200?
  **A:** Herbert is killed in the machinery at work, and the company pays exactly £200 in compensation.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Three-part structure
*Student:* Diagram how Jacobs divides the story into three scenes and explain why the final knock is never answered on the page.

*How to teach:* Diagram the three scenes. Discuss why the final knock is never answered on the page.

- **Q:** Why doesn't Jacobs show what is behind the door?
  **A:** Leaving it unseen lets the reader imagine something horrible and keeps the story ambiguous — the horror is stronger unseen, and Mr. White's third wish (that it go away) leaves only an empty road.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Bierce's timeline
*Student:* Read "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge"; draw a timeline separating real time from imagined time in Part III.

*How to teach:* Read 'Owl Creek'. Your child makes a timeline separating real time (seconds) from imagined time in Part III.

- **Q:** What is the real event of the story?
  **A:** Peyton Farquhar, a Southern planter, is hanged from Owl Creek Bridge; his long escape in Part III happens in his mind in the instant before his neck breaks.
- **Q:** What does Part II reveal?
  **A:** A flashback: a Federal scout disguised as a Confederate soldier tricked Farquhar into trying to burn the bridge.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Compare structure
*Student:* Write a comparison paragraph: How do Jacobs and Bierce each use structure to create surprise? Practice colons introducing lists and explanations.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a comparison paragraph on structure and surprise; teach colons introducing lists and explanations.

- **Q:** How does Bierce's structure create surprise?
  **A:** He moves from the hanging (I) to a flashback (II) and then a detailed escape (III) told as real, so the final sentence revealing Farquhar is dead shocks the reader.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a structure quiz and peer-review the comparison paragraph with a rubric.

*How to teach:* Structure quiz; peer review with the rubric.

- **Q:** Where is 'The Monkey's Paw' first wish made, in which part?
  **A:** Part I ends with the wish; the consequence arrives in Part II.

**Assessment:** Comparison paragraph and structure quiz.

**Answer key:** Comparison paragraph: Paragraph rubric: 4 = arguable claim, two well-chosen quotations embedded and cited, commentary that explains HOW the evidence proves the claim; 3 = claim and evidence with thinner commentary; 2 = summary instead of analysis or only one piece of evidence; 1 = off-topic or no evidence. Proficient content: Jacobs uses three scenes that each build to a wish and its consequence, withholding the final horror; Bierce uses nonlinear order and an imagined escape revealed only in the last line. Structure quiz answers: 1) Monkey's Paw has 3 parts. 2) Owl Creek's Part II is a flashback. 3) Farquhar imagines escape. 4) The last line reveals he was hanged. 5) Foreshadowing example: Morris's warnings. 6) A colon used correctly before a list.

## Algebra I: Linear inequalities in one variable

**Prepare:** Draw or print blank number lines. Make a 'flip rule' card showing −2 < 3 becoming 2 > −3 after multiplying by −1.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting to reverse the inequality when multiplying or dividing by a negative: test a value to see the reversed version is the true one.
- Open vs. closed circle confusion: < and > use open circles; ≤ and ≥ use closed circles.
- Graphing 'or' as overlap: 'and' is the overlap (between), 'or' is everything in either piece.

### Day 1 (Monday): Inequality basics
*Student:* Graph 12 inequalities on number lines and test values to verify solution sets.

*How to teach:* Model graphing x > −2 (open circle at −2, shade right) and test values (0 works, −5 doesn't). Your child graphs 12 inequalities and tests one value on each side of every boundary.

- **Q:** Graph x ≤ 3 and test x = 3 and x = 4.
  **A:** Closed circle at 3, shade left. 3 ≤ 3 is true; 4 ≤ 3 is false.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Solving inequalities
*Student:* Solve multi-step inequalities, explaining why the sign reverses when multiplying by a negative.

*How to teach:* Show why the sign flips: −2 < 3, but multiplying by −1 gives 2 and −3, and 2 > −3. Your child solves multi-step inequalities, circling any step where the sign reverses.

- **Q:** Solve −3x + 4 ≤ 19.
  **A:** −3x ≤ 15 → x ≥ −5 (divide by −3, reverse sign).
- **Q:** Why does the sign reverse when you divide by a negative?
  **A:** Multiplying by a negative reverses order on the number line (e.g., −2 < 3 but 2 > −3).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compound inequalities
*Student:* Solve and graph "and" and "or" inequalities such as −3 < 2x+1 ≤ 9.

*How to teach:* Model −3 < 2x + 1 ≤ 9 by doing each operation to all three parts → −2 < x ≤ 4. Then an 'or' example. Your child solves and graphs the set.

- **Q:** Solve and graph −3 < 2x + 1 ≤ 9.
  **A:** −4 < 2x ≤ 8 → −2 < x ≤ 4: open circle at −2, closed at 4, shaded between.
- **Q:** Solve x + 2 < −1 or x − 1 > 4.
  **A:** x < −3 or x > 5 (two separate rays).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Inequality word problems
*Student:* Write and solve inequalities for budgets, grades needed, and minimum sales.

*How to teach:* Translate key phrases: 'at most' ≤, 'at least' ≥, 'no more than' ≤, 'more than' >. Your child writes and solves inequalities for budget, grade, and sales problems and answers in context (whole items).

- **Q:** You have $40, spent $12, and snacks cost $3.50 each. How many snacks can you buy?
  **A:** 12 + 3.5n ≤ 40 → n ≤ 8: at most 8 snacks.
- **Q:** Scores so far: 82, 75, 90. What 4th score gives an average of at least 85?
  **A:** (247 + x)/4 ≥ 85 → x ≥ 93.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on solving and graphing inequalities.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz in the key. Check graphs carefully for circle type and direction.

- **Q:** Solve 5 − x > 2.
  **A:** −x > −3 → x < 3.

**Assessment:** Inequality quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz answers: 1) 2x − 5 > 7 → x > 6 (open circle at 6, shade right). 2) −4x ≤ 12 → x ≥ −3 (closed circle). 3) 1 ≤ 3x − 2 < 10 → 1 ≤ x < 4. 4) x + 2 < −1 or x − 1 > 4 → x < −3 or x > 5. 5) A plan costs $25 plus $0.05 per text; budget at most $40 → 25 + 0.05t ≤ 40 → t ≤ 300 texts. 6) −(x + 1) ≥ 2 → x ≤ −3. Graph points: correct circle and direction required for full credit.

## Biology – The Living Earth: Early Earth and the oxygen revolution

**Prepare:** Toilet paper roll (at least 46 squares; 1 square = 100 million years) or 460 squares (1 square = 10 million years); markers. Steel wool (soap-free), white vinegar, jars. Images of Shark Bay stromatolites.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking early Earth had oxygen like today: early atmosphere had almost no free O₂; it accumulated after cyanobacteria (Great Oxidation Event ~2.4 billion years ago).
- Imagining humans appeared early: on a 4.6-billion-year timeline, humans occupy the last tiny fraction of the final square.
- Treating Earth and life as separate: each changed the other (coevolution).

### Day 1 (Monday): Earth's timeline
*Student:* Build a scaled toilet-paper timeline of Earth's 4.6 billion years marking the origin of life and oxygen.

*How to teach:* Build the timeline: Earth forms 4.6 bya; first life ~3.5–3.8 bya; Great Oxidation ~2.4 bya; multicellular life later; dinosaurs; humans at the very end.

- **Q:** On a 460-square timeline (1 square = 10 million years), where does the Great Oxidation Event (2.4 bya) fall?
  **A:** 240 squares from the 'today' end (about square 220 from the start).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rusting model
*Student:* Soak steel wool in vinegar and observe rusting to model how oxygen created banded iron formations.

*How to teach:* Soak steel wool in vinegar (removes coating), place in a jar, observe rust.

- **Q:** How does rusting steel wool model banded iron formations?
  **A:** Iron reacts with oxygen to form iron oxide (rust); in ancient oceans, oxygen from cyanobacteria reacted with dissolved iron, which settled as iron-rich layers.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Stromatolites
*Student:* Examine images of stromatolites at Shark Bay and explain their role in the oxygen revolution.

*How to teach:* Examine stromatolite images.

- **Q:** What are stromatolites and why do they matter?
  **A:** Layered structures built by cyanobacteria mats trapping sediment; ancient ones are among the oldest evidence of life, and their photosynthesis released the oxygen that changed the atmosphere.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Coevolution argument
*Student:* Write an argument: How did life and Earth's atmosphere change each other?

*How to teach:* Your child writes an argument on how life and atmosphere changed each other.

- **Q:** Give one way life changed Earth and one way Earth changed life.
  **A:** Life → cyanobacteria added O₂ to the atmosphere (and an ozone layer formed). Earth → rising oxygen allowed aerobic respiration and complex life, and killed off many anaerobic organisms.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Quiz on Earth's early atmosphere.

*How to teach:* Quiz on early atmosphere.

- **Q:** About when did oxygen begin to accumulate in the atmosphere?
  **A:** About 2.4 billion years ago (the Great Oxidation Event).

**Assessment:** Argument paragraph and quiz.

**Answer key:** Argument rubric: 4 = claim that life and atmosphere coevolved, evidence from stromatolites, banded iron formations, and timing of the Great Oxidation Event, reasoning about photosynthesis and aerobic life; 3 = two pieces of evidence; 2 = one; 1 = none. Quiz answers: 1) Earth age 4.6 billion years. 2) Early atmosphere: CO₂, N₂, water vapor, little O₂. 3) Cyanobacteria produced oxygen. 4) Banded iron formations = iron + oxygen layers. 5) Ozone layer formed from O₂, shielding land life from UV.

## World Geography (S1) & Ethnic Studies (S2): Biomes and human-environment interaction

**Prepare:** Biome maps (National Geographic Education). News articles on drought or wildfire in California. Prepare the Unit 2 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking wildfire is purely 'natural' or purely 'human': ignition, fuel buildup, climate, and building in the wildland-urban interface all matter.
- Treating biomes as having sharp borders (they transition gradually).
- Assuming people in harsh climates are 'less developed': they show sophisticated adaptations.

### Day 1 (Monday): Biomes
*Student:* Create a biome chart with climate, vegetation, and location.

*How to teach:* Create a biome chart.

- **Q:** Which biome has the greatest biodiversity?
  **A:** Tropical rainforest.
- **Q:** Describe chaparral.
  **A:** Hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters; dense shrubs adapted to fire; found in California and other Mediterranean climates.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Adaptations
*Student:* Compare how people adapt to living in a desert vs. Arctic.

*How to teach:* Compare desert and Arctic adaptations.

- **Q:** Give one traditional adaptation for desert living and one for the Arctic.
  **A:** Desert: thick-walled adobe homes, loose light clothing, oasis settlement. Arctic: insulated fur/skin clothing, snow houses, diet of marine mammals.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Natural hazards
*Student:* Analyze drought and wildfire effects on California communities.

*How to teach:* Analyze drought and wildfire in California.

- **Q:** How does drought increase wildfire risk?
  **A:** Drought dries vegetation into fuel and weakens trees; combined with heat and wind, fires spread faster.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Current events
*Student:* Relate a current news event to physical geography.

*How to teach:* Relate a current news event to physical geography.

- **Q:** What makes a good geography connection to news?
  **A:** It explains how a physical feature or process (climate, landform, hazard) shaped the event and people's response.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 2 test.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test.

- **Q:** What is the wildland-urban interface?
  **A:** Areas where homes are built next to or within wildland vegetation.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test sample answers: plate boundaries; San Andreas transform; landforms by agent; climate controls; climograph reading; rain shadow; Köppen Mediterranean; biome characteristics; human adaptations; drought–wildfire link. Scoring: 80%+ proficient.

## Spanish II: Los juguetes y los juegos: childhood games around the Spanish-speaking world

**Prepare:** Find short Spanish readings or videos on rayuela (hopscotch), trompo (spinning top), lotería, and canicas (marbles). Print or buy a lotería set (printable sets are free online) and prepare a Venn diagram.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking lotería is a number game like bingo: it uses pictures with names (El gallo, La sirena, La luna), often called with a riddle or rhyme.
- Assuming games are identical across cultures: many are shared (hide-and-seek = escondidas/escondite) but names and rules vary by country.

### Day 1 (Monday): Traditional games
*Student:* Learn about rayuela, trompo, lotería, and canicas through short Spanish readings.

*How to teach:* Read the short texts together, pausing to match each game with a picture. Your child writes one sentence per game in Spanish (La rayuela es un juego con tiza y una piedra).

- **Q:** What is la rayuela?
  **A:** Hopscotch — a chalk grid where you toss a stone and hop through the squares.
- **Q:** What do you need to play el trompo?
  **A:** A wooden top and a string (cuerda) to wind and throw it.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Lotería
*Student:* Play lotería with family, calling cards in Spanish.

*How to teach:* Play lotería: the caller (your child first) reads each card in Spanish; players mark with beans. Switch callers so everyone practices pronunciation.

- **Q:** What do players say when they win?
  **A:** ¡Lotería! (some families also say ¡Buenas!)

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Interview
*Student:* Ask a family member what games they played as a child and take notes in Spanish.

*How to teach:* Your child asks a family member: ¿A qué jugabas de niño? ¿Con quién jugabas? ¿Dónde jugaban? and takes notes in Spanish using the imperfect.

- **Q:** Write the question 'What did you used to play when you were a child?'
  **A:** ¿A qué jugabas cuando eras niño/a?
- **Q:** Report: 'My dad used to play marbles with his brothers.'
  **A:** Mi papá jugaba a las canicas con sus hermanos.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparison
*Student:* Compare games across cultures in a Venn diagram.

*How to teach:* Your child compares two games (e.g., U.S. hopscotch vs. rayuela, or bingo vs. lotería) in a Venn diagram and writes three comparison sentences in Spanish.

- **Q:** Write: 'Both games use cards.'
  **A:** Los dos juegos usan cartas (or tarjetas).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Short reading check on traditional games.

*How to teach:* Give the short reading check and review the interview notes with the rubric.

- **Q:** How do you say 'to hide' and 'hide-and-seek'?
  **A:** esconderse; las escondidas (Latin America) or el escondite (Spain)

**Assessment:** Reading check and interview notes.

**Answer key:** Reading check sample key: 1) La rayuela = hopscotch, drawn with chalk and played by tossing a stone; 2) El trompo = a wooden spinning top wound with string; 3) La lotería = a Mexican picture game played with tablas and frijoles as markers; 4) Las canicas = marbles; 5) 'jugar a las escondidas' = to play hide-and-seek. Interview notes rubric: 4 = 8+ notes in Spanish using the imperfect (Mi papá jugaba...), with details on where/with whom; 3 = 5–7 notes; 2 = few notes or mostly English; 1 = incomplete.

## Visual Art I: One-point perspective

**Prepare:** Ruler, pencil, paper; printout of Leonardo's 'The Last Supper' (c. 1495–98).

**Watch for:**
- Placing the vanishing point off the horizon. Correct: the VP always sits on the horizon line (eye level).
- Drawing vertical lines toward the VP in one-point. Correct: verticals stay vertical; only orthogonals converge.

### Day 1 (Monday): Perspective in art
*Student:* Analyze Leonardo's "The Last Supper" and trace its orthogonals on a printout to find the vanishing point.

*How to teach:* Trace orthogonals on 'The Last Supper' to find the VP.

- **Q:** Where is the vanishing point in 'The Last Supper'?
  **A:** At Christ's head (near his right temple), at the center, drawing all eyes to him.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Boxes in space
*Student:* Draw floating boxes above, below, and on the horizon line in one-point perspective.

*How to teach:* Floating boxes in one-point.

- **Q:** What are orthogonals?
  **A:** Lines that recede toward the vanishing point.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hallway
*Student:* Draw a hallway or room interior in one-point perspective.

*How to teach:* Draw a hallway or room interior.

- **Q:** Where do the floor and ceiling lines meet?
  **A:** They converge at the vanishing point.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Street scene
*Student:* Draw a one-point street scene with buildings, windows, and sidewalk.

*How to teach:* Draw a one-point street scene.

- **Q:** How do windows change as buildings recede?
  **A:** They get smaller and closer together, following orthogonals.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Add value and details to the street scene.

*How to teach:* Add value and details.

- **Q:** How does value add depth?
  **A:** Contrast and darker values in front, lighter in distance, plus shadows, make space believable.

**Assessment:** One-point drawing assessed for correct convergence.

**Answer key:** One-point drawing: 4 = single VP on horizon line, all orthogonals converge accurately, verticals/horizontals true, detail and value add depth; 3 = 1–2 errors; 2 = many errors; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 9 (Course 1): Sprinting and starts

**Prepare:** Cones at 0, 10, 20, 40 m; stopwatch; phone camera.

**Watch for:**
- Standing upright immediately out of the start. Correct: maintain forward lean during the drive phase (first ~10–20 m).
- Tense, side-to-side arms. Correct: powerful front-to-back arm action, relaxed hands.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sprint drills
*Student:* Do wall drills, high knees, and acceleration runs; learn the drive phase.

*How to teach:* Wall drills, high knees, acceleration runs; teach drive phase.

- **Q:** What is the drive phase?
  **A:** The first part of a sprint where the body leans forward and pushes back powerfully into the ground to accelerate.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Starts
*Student:* Practice three-point and standing starts over 20 m.

*How to teach:* Three-point and standing starts over 20 m.

- **Q:** Why lean forward at the start?
  **A:** It directs force horizontally (backward into the ground), producing forward acceleration.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Timed sprints
*Student:* Time 6 × 40 m sprints with full recovery.

*How to teach:* Time 6×40 m with full recovery (2–3 min).

- **Q:** Why take full recovery between sprints?
  **A:** To restore phosphocreatine energy so each sprint is at maximum speed and quality.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Video feedback
*Student:* Film sprints and use the video to correct arm action.

*How to teach:* Film sprints; correct arm action.

- **Q:** How should the arms move?
  **A:** Bent ~90°, swinging from the shoulders front to back, hands from hip to cheek height, not crossing the midline.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Sprint test (40 m) and a written explanation of how force and lean help acceleration.

*How to teach:* 40 m sprint test; written explanation of force and lean.

- **Q:** How does Newton's third law apply to sprinting?
  **A:** The runner pushes back and down on the ground; the ground pushes forward and up with an equal and opposite force, propelling the runner.

**Assessment:** 40 m sprint test and written biomechanics explanation.

**Answer key:** Written explanation proficient: explains pushing backward against the ground creates an equal, opposite ground reaction force (Newton's 3rd law); forward lean directs more of that force horizontally to accelerate; as speed builds the body rises to upright for maximum velocity; arm action balances leg drive. Sprint test recorded.

## Health & Wellness: Energy balance, body image and eating disorders

**Prepare:** Parental preview: eating disorders and body image. Find a media-literacy lesson with edited vs. unedited images (e.g., Dove Self-Esteem Project) and the KidsHealth article 'Eating Disorders.' Avoid discussing specific weights or calorie numbers. Watch for warning signs in your own teen.

**Watch for:**
- Believing eating disorders are about vanity or only affect thin girls. Correct: they are serious mental illnesses with among the highest death rates of any mental illness, affecting all genders and sizes.
- Thinking crash diets work. Correct: severe restriction slows metabolism, causes muscle loss and usually leads to regain and possible disordered eating.

### Day 1 (Monday): Energy balance
*Student:* Learn how calories in and calories out relate to growth during adolescence and calculate the energy cost of three activities.

*How to teach:* Explain energy in vs. out and growth needs. The child estimates the energy cost of three activities using a chart (e.g., about 8–10 calories/min running vs. about 1–2 at rest, varying by body size).

- **Q:** Why do teens need more energy than many adults?
  **A:** Rapid growth in height, muscle and bones plus activity increase energy needs.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Body image and media
*Student:* Compare edited and unedited images from a media-literacy lesson and write about how filtered images affect body image.

*How to teach:* Compare images and write how filters/edits shape body expectations.

- **Q:** How can filtered images affect body image?
  **A:** They set unrealistic standards, leading to comparison, dissatisfaction and sometimes unhealthy dieting.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Eating disorders
*Student:* Read a KidsHealth article on eating disorders and make a chart of types, warning signs and health effects.

*How to teach:* Read the article; chart anorexia, bulimia, binge eating disorder (and ARFID): signs and health effects.

- **Q:** Name two health effects of anorexia.
  **A:** Slow heart rate, low blood pressure, bone loss (osteoporosis), loss of periods, fatigue, organ damage; it can be fatal.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Diet culture
*Student:* Evaluate three fad diets (keto, detox teas, intermittent fasting for teens) for safety and evidence and explain why crash diets backfire.

*How to teach:* Evaluate three fad diets for teens; discuss why crash diets backfire.

- **Q:** Why are detox teas not recommended?
  **A:** Many are laxatives or diuretics causing water loss and dehydration, not fat loss; the liver and kidneys already detox the body.

### Day 5 (Friday): Check
*Student:* Write a letter to a friend showing warning signs, explaining your concern and naming two places to get help; take a short quiz.

*How to teach:* Child writes the letter; give a short quiz.

- **Q:** Name two places to get help for an eating disorder.
  **A:** A parent or trusted adult, doctor, school counselor, NEDA/ANAD helpline resources.

**Assessment:** Supportive letter scored for accurate information and resources; quiz.

**Answer key:** Letter rubric: 4 = names specific warning signs observed, expresses concern with I-statements, avoids blame/appearance comments, gives two valid help sources and encourages telling an adult; 3 = accurate but one element missing; 2 = vague; 1 = inaccurate. Quiz key: energy balance; edited images unrealistic; anorexia/bulimia/BED definitions; eating disorders are mental illnesses; crash diets slow metabolism.
