# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 17

## ELA 5: Poets play with language: Hughes and Carroll; poetry anthology

**Prepare:** Print 'Dreams' and 'April Rain Song' by Langston Hughes and 'Jabberwocky' by Lewis Carroll. Gather art supplies for the anthology. Choose an unfamiliar poem for the cold read.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking nonsense words have no meaning. Grammar position shows part of speech.
- Free verse = no rules. It has no regular rhyme/meter but still uses line breaks and imagery deliberately.
- Thinking informal language is wrong. Choose register to fit audience and purpose.

### Day 1 (Monday): Langston Hughes
*Student:* Read 'Dreams' and 'April Rain Song', identify the metaphor and personification, and write a free-verse weather poem.

*How to teach:* Read the Hughes poems; identify metaphor (life is a broken-winged bird) and personification (let the rain kiss you). Your child writes a free-verse weather poem.

- **Q:** What metaphors does Hughes use in 'Dreams'?
  **A:** If dreams die, life is a broken-winged bird that cannot fly; if dreams go, life is a barren field frozen with snow.
- **Q:** Example of personification in 'April Rain Song'?
  **A:** 'Let the rain kiss you' or 'the rain sing you a lullaby.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): 'Jabberwocky'
*Student:* Read 'Jabberwocky' and use grammar clues to decide the part of speech and meaning of 8 nonsense words.

*How to teach:* Read 'Jabberwocky'; use grammar to decide the part of speech of 8 nonsense words. Model the first line: a word after 'the' is a noun; a word after 'did' is a verb. Your child completes a chart of 8 words with part of speech and an invented meaning.

- **Q:** In 'the slithy toves did gyre and gimble,' what parts of speech are slithy, toves, gyre?
  **A:** slithy = adjective, toves = noun, gyre = verb.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Varieties of English
*Student:* Compare formal and informal registers in poems and rewrite a stanza in a different register.

*How to teach:* Compare formal and informal registers; your child rewrites a stanza in a different register. Discuss when informal language fits (friends) and when formal fits (speeches). Your child rewrites one stanza and explains the change.

- **Q:** Rewrite formally: Hold on to your dreams, ok?
  **A:** Hold fast to your dreams.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Anthology
*Student:* Compile 5 original poems (narrative stanza, metaphor, free verse, rhyme, nonsense) into an illustrated anthology.

*How to teach:* Compile 5 original poems into an illustrated anthology. Your child revises and copies each poem neatly, adds an illustration and a table of contents.

- **Q:** Name the 5 required poem types.
  **A:** Narrative stanza, metaphor, free verse, rhyming, nonsense.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 assessment
*Student:* Hold a poetry café reading of 2 original poems and take a cold-read poetry assessment on theme and figurative language.

*How to teach:* Hold a poetry café reading of 2 original poems; give the cold-read poetry assessment. Set up snacks and an audience; then give the cold read independently and score it with the rubric.

- **Q:** What is the theme of 'Dreams'?
  **A:** Hold on to your dreams because life without them is empty and limited.

**Assessment:** Poetry anthology rubric; performance; Unit 4 cold-read assessment.

**Answer key:** Anthology rubric: 4 = all 5 types present, each uses its feature well, illustrated, polished; 3 = all present, minor issues; 2 = missing types; 1 = incomplete. Performance: clear, expressive. Cold-read: theme stated as full sentence; two figurative language examples identified and explained.

## Math 5: Line plots with fractional data and unit assessment

**Prepare:** Ruler marked in eighths of an inch, 12 pencils or leaves, measuring cups. Practice reading eighths on the ruler.

**Watch for:**
- Reading the ruler from 1 instead of 0. Line the object up at 0.
- Spacing a line plot unevenly. Mark every 1/8 at equal intervals even if no data falls there.
- Adding line plot values by counting Xs instead of their values. Multiply each value by its number of Xs, then add.

### Day 1 (Monday): Collect data
*Student:* Measure 12 pencils or leaves to the nearest 1/8 inch and record the data in a table.

*How to teach:* Show how to read eighths on the ruler (1/4 = 2/8, 1/2 = 4/8). The child measures 12 items to the nearest 1/8 inch and records them in a table.

- **Q:** A pencil ends 3 marks past the 5. Each mark is 1/8. How long is it?
  **A:** 5 3/8 inches.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Build the line plot
*Student:* Draw a line plot of the measurement data with a scale in eighths and title and label it.

*How to teach:* Draw a number line covering the data's range in eighths, add an X per measurement, a title and units. Check that marks are evenly spaced.

- **Q:** Why include 5 1/8 on the line if no item measured that?
  **A:** The scale must have equal intervals; empty values show there is no data there.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Questions from the plot
*Student:* Use the line plot to find the total length, the difference between longest and shortest, and how much liquid each beaker would hold if redistributed equally.

*How to teach:* Use the plot to find total length and the difference between longest and shortest. Then solve the beaker problem: redistribute liquid equally.

- **Q:** Beakers hold 1/4, 1/2, 1/2 and 3/4 L. If shared equally, how much in each?
  **A:** Total 2 L ÷ 4 = 1/2 L each.
- **Q:** Longest 6 7/8 in, shortest 4 3/8 in. Difference?
  **A:** 2 1/2 inches.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit review
*Student:* Complete a mixed review of unlike fractions, mixed numbers and line plot problems.

*How to teach:* Mixed review of unlike fractions, mixed numbers and a short line plot problem. Work two problems of each type together first, then your child finishes independently while you check every third answer.

- **Q:** 2 5/8 + 1 1/2 = ?
  **A:** 4 1/8

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 4 test on adding and subtracting fractions and line plots.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 4 test independently, including a graded line plot. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** A line plot shows three Xs at 3/8. What is their total?
  **A:** 9/8 = 1 1/8.

**Assessment:** Unit 4 test covering 5.NF.A.1–A.2 and 5.MD.B.2; graded line plot.

**Answer key:** Unit 4 test key (suggested): 1) 2/3 + 1/4 = 11/12 2) 5/6 − 1/4 = 7/12 3) 3 1/2 + 2 3/5 = 6 1/10 4) 6 1/3 − 2 3/4 = 3 7/12 5) Estimate 9/10 + 5/12 ≈ 1 1/2 6) 2/3 + 1/6 = 3/6 is unreasonable; correct 5/6. Line plot data: 1/8, 3/8, 3/8, 1/2, 1/2, 1/2, 5/8, 7/8 — 7) most common 1/2 8) total 31/8 = 3 7/8 9) range 7/8 − 1/8 = 3/4 10) equal share 31/8 ÷ 8 = 31/64. Line plot rubric: title, units, even eighth scale, accurate Xs.

## Science 5: Decomposers and the matter cycle

**Prepare:** Bread slices, zip bags, fruit scraps, clear bottle, soil, leaves, magnifier. Safety: keep mold bags sealed; dispose of them sealed (mold spores can trigger allergies).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking mold comes from nowhere. Mold spores are in the air.
- Thinking decomposers are pests. They return nutrients to soil.
- Thinking dead things just disappear.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mold investigation
*Student:* Set up sealed bags with bread under different conditions (dry, damp, fridge, warm) to observe mold growth (do not open).

*How to teach:* Set up bread in sealed bags: dry, damp, fridge, warm. Label and date them. Your child predicts which molds first.

- **Q:** Which do you predict will mold first?
  **A:** Damp bread in a warm place — mold grows best with moisture and warmth.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Bottle compost
*Student:* Build a mini compost column in a clear bottle with soil, leaves and fruit scraps and draw day 1.

*How to teach:* Build a compost column with layers of soil, leaves and fruit scraps; your child draws day 1. Layer soil, leaves, fruit scraps, then soil again; mist lightly and poke air holes. Your child draws and labels day 1.

- **Q:** Why add soil?
  **A:** Soil contains decomposers (bacteria, fungi, small organisms).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Decomposer research
*Student:* Read about fungi, bacteria and worms and explain how each breaks down dead matter.

*How to teach:* Read about fungi, bacteria and worms; your child explains how each breaks down dead matter. Read short passages together. Your child makes a 3-column chart: decomposer, how it breaks matter down, what it returns to the soil.

- **Q:** How do earthworms help decomposition?
  **A:** They eat dead plant matter and leave castings that enrich soil.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Observations
*Student:* Record and sketch changes in the mold bags and compost column with dates.

*How to teach:* Your child records and sketches changes with dates. Your child sketches each bag and the column with dates, noting color, fuzz and size changes. Compare with the predictions.

- **Q:** What changes do you observe?
  **A:** Mold patches, color changes, softening fruit, possible smell.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Add decomposers to last week's food web with arrows back to soil and air and explain the cycle.

*How to teach:* Your child adds decomposers to the food web with arrows to soil and air and explains the cycle. Use last week's web. Your child adds decomposers with arrows from dead organisms and waste to soil and air, then explains the cycle aloud.

- **Q:** What happens to matter decomposers break down?
  **A:** It returns to the soil as nutrients and to the air as carbon dioxide; plants reuse it.

**Assessment:** Observation log and revised food web with decomposers.

**Answer key:** Observation log: dated sketches and notes for all bags and the column. Revised food web: decomposers connected to dead organisms with arrows to soil/air; explanation of matter cycling.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Cooperation: trade, alliances and exchange

**Prepare:** Image of Benjamin West's 'Penn's Treaty with the Indians' (1771); fur trade map.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking paintings are photos.
- Thinking only colonists gave technology.
- Thinking cooperation lasted.

### Day 1 (Monday): Agriculture and survival
*Student:* Read about how Native peoples taught colonists to grow corn and use local resources.

*How to teach:* Read about Native farming knowledge (corn, fish fertilizer, the Three Sisters). Your child lists resources colonists learned to use.

- **Q:** What did Native peoples teach?
  **A:** Growing corn with fish fertilizer and the Three Sisters.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): The fur trade
*Student:* Map the French and Dutch fur trade networks and explain what each side gained.

*How to teach:* Map French and Dutch fur trade networks. Your child lists what Native traders and Europeans each gained.

- **Q:** What did Native traders get?
  **A:** Metal tools, cloth, guns.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Primary source: painting
*Student:* Analyze Benjamin West's painting of Penn's treaty and discuss how a painting made 90 years later can shape memory.

*How to teach:* Look at West's 1771 painting of the 1682 treaty. Discuss how a painting made about 90 years later can idealize events.

- **Q:** Why can a later painting shape memory?
  **A:** It shows an idealized version.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cultural exchange
*Student:* List words, foods and technologies exchanged between Native nations and colonists.

*How to teach:* Your child lists words (moccasin, raccoon, canoe), foods (corn, squash, turkey) and technologies (canoes, snowshoes) exchanged. Before finishing, have your child explain their work aloud in 2–3 sentences; correct any errors then.

- **Q:** Name 2 Native words in English.
  **A:** Moccasin, raccoon.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a summary of three forms of cooperation with examples.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a summary of three forms of cooperation with examples. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** Three forms of cooperation?
  **A:** Farming knowledge, trade, alliances.

**Assessment:** Painting analysis and cooperation summary.

**Answer key:** Painting analysis and summary rubric: accurate details, explanation.

## Art & Music 5: Native American art: pattern and symbolism

**Prepare:** Open images from the Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian: Pueblo pottery (e.g., Maria Martinez's black-on-black ware), Navajo (Diné) weavings, Haudenosaunee raised beadwork. Read the museum labels. Gather graph paper, colored pencils, and two colors of construction paper cut into strips for weaving.

**Watch for:**
- Copying sacred symbols. Some designs have spiritual meanings and belong to specific nations; create your own symbols instead.
- Thinking Native American art is only historical. Native artists continue these traditions and create new art today.

### Day 1 (Monday): Looking at art
*Student:* Examine images of Pueblo pottery, Navajo weavings and Haudenosaunee beadwork and describe their patterns.

*How to teach:* Your child describes patterns in each art form (geometric, symmetry, repetition, color). Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** What kind of symmetry is common in Navajo weavings?
  **A:** Bilateral (mirror) symmetry with geometric shapes like diamonds and zigzags.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Symbols and meaning
*Student:* Read museum labels explaining what certain designs mean and why some symbols are sacred and should not be copied.

*How to teach:* Read museum labels; discuss why some symbols are sacred. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** Why shouldn't you copy sacred symbols?
  **A:** They have spiritual or cultural meaning to a specific people; copying them can be disrespectful.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pattern design
*Student:* Design your own symmetrical geometric pattern on graph paper using personal symbols.

*How to teach:* Design a symmetrical geometric pattern on graph paper using personal symbols. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What is a motif?
  **A:** A repeated design element or symbol in a pattern.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Paper weaving
*Student:* Weave a paper mat in two colors to understand over-under structure.

*How to teach:* Weave a paper mat with over-under pattern. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** What is the over-under structure?
  **A:** Strips alternate going over and under, creating a strong woven pattern.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write how art preserves culture and explain the meaning of your own symbols.

*How to teach:* Write how art preserves culture and explain personal symbols. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** How does art preserve culture?
  **A:** It passes on stories, beliefs, skills and identity from generation to generation.

**Assessment:** Pattern design and written reflection.

**Answer key:** Pattern design: symmetrical, repeated motifs, personal (not copied sacred) symbols, neat craftsmanship. Reflection rubric: 4 = explains how art preserves culture with an example and meaning of own symbols; 3 = one missing; 2 = vague; 1 = missing.

## PE 5: Basketball dribbling

**Prepare:** Gather a basketball (size 6 or 5) or playground ball and cones. A smooth driveway or court works best. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Slapping the ball with the palm. Dribble with the finger pads, pushing the ball down.
- Watching the ball. Keep eyes up and feel the ball with your fingertips.

### Day 1 (Monday): Stationary dribbling
*Student:* Do ball-handling drills (pound, low, crossover, around the leg) for 1 minute each.

*How to teach:* Do 1-minute drills: pound dribble, low dribble, crossover, around the leg. Cue: 'fingertips, waist high, eyes up.'

- **Q:** Which part of the hand controls the dribble?
  **A:** The finger pads (fingertips), not the palm.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Moving dribble
*Student:* Dribble through cones with the right hand, left hand and crossover at each cone.

*How to teach:* Dribble through cones with the right hand, then the left, then crossing over at each cone.

- **Q:** Which hand should you dribble with when a defender is on your right?
  **A:** Your left hand, keeping your body between the defender and the ball.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Speed and control
*Student:* Alternate speed dribbles and control dribbles on a whistle.

*How to teach:* On a whistle, switch between speed dribble (ball pushed out in front, higher bounce) and control dribble (low, close, body protecting).

- **Q:** When would you use a control dribble?
  **A:** When a defender is close or you're in traffic.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Protecting the ball
*Student:* Practice dribbling while a partner tries to tap the ball away, keeping the body between.

*How to teach:* Your partner tries to tap the ball away; your child keeps the body and off-arm between the defender and the ball.

- **Q:** How do you protect the ball while dribbling?
  **A:** Keep the ball low on the side away from the defender, with the body and off-arm (not pushing) as a shield.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a timed dribble course with both hands and record time.

*How to teach:* Time your child through the cone course using both hands. Record time and cue checklist. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** Why practice with your weaker hand?
  **A:** So you can dribble away from defenders on either side.

**Assessment:** Dribble course time and cue checklist.

**Answer key:** Dribble course time recorded. Cue checklist: fingertips, ball at or below waist, eyes up, uses both hands, crossover at cones, keeps control. Proficient = 5 of 6.

## Health 5: Introduction to puberty (parent notification and opt-out honored)

**Prepare:** Gather the parent letter/unit outline for weeks 17–23, a box with a slot for anonymous questions, slips of paper, and the AMAZE.org video 'Puberty: Some Changes to Expect' (or similar). Find your child's past heights (doctor records or growth chart). PARENT PREVIEW: watch the videos and read materials before your child does; decide how you'll answer personal or values questions, and keep the anonymous question box open.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking puberty happens at the same age for everyone. It can begin anywhere from about age 8 to 14 and still be completely normal.
- Thinking puberty only happens to girls (or only to boys). Everyone goes through puberty; some changes are shared and some differ.

### Day 1 (Monday): Family kickoff
*Student:* Parent reviews the unit outline and materials together with the student; set up an anonymous question box.

*How to teach:* Sit together and look over the unit outline and materials so nothing is a surprise. Explain the question box: any question, no names, answered honestly. Say clearly that questions are welcome and nothing is 'weird' to ask.

- **Q:** Who can you ask about puberty questions?
  **A:** A parent or guardian, doctor, school nurse or another trusted adult — or the anonymous question box.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): What is puberty?
*Student:* Watch an AMAZE.org video on puberty and list changes that happen to everyone.

*How to teach:* Watch the AMAZE video together, pausing to discuss. Your child lists changes that happen to everyone (growth spurt, body hair, more sweat and body odor, oily skin/acne, emotional changes).

- **Q:** Name three changes that happen to everyone during puberty.
  **A:** Any three: growth spurt, new body hair (underarms, pubic area), more sweat/body odor, oilier skin and acne, stronger emotions/mood changes.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Different timelines
*Student:* Read about how puberty can start between ages 8 and 14 and write why comparing yourself to friends isn't useful.

*How to teach:* Read that puberty usually starts between about 8 and 14. Your child writes 2–3 sentences on why comparing with friends isn't useful.

- **Q:** Your friend has already grown 4 inches and you haven't. Is something wrong?
  **A:** No — each body follows its own timeline; starting earlier or later is normal.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Growth spurts
*Student:* Graph your height over the past few years and learn about growth spurts and growing pains.

*How to teach:* Your child graphs their height by year and looks for a steep part. Explain growth spurts (rapid height gain, bigger feet first) and that growing pains are aches in the legs, often at night.

- **Q:** What is a growth spurt?
  **A:** A period of rapid growth in height (and weight), common during puberty.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Answer questions from the question box with a parent and complete a 5-question check.

*How to teach:* Pull questions from the box and answer them together calmly; it's fine to say 'Let me find out.' Then give the 5-question check orally.

- **Q:** What starts puberty?
  **A:** Hormones — chemical messengers released when the brain (pituitary gland) signals the body to begin changing.

**Assessment:** Quick check and discussion.

**Answer key:** Quick check key: 1) Puberty = the time when the body develops from a child's into an adult's 2) Hormones start it 3) Usually begins about ages 8–14 4) Shared changes: growth spurt, body hair, sweat/odor, acne, emotions 5) Ask a trusted adult or valid source. Discussion: proficient if the child names at least one trusted adult and explains that different timelines are normal.
