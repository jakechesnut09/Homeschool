# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 13

## ELA 5: Research: note-taking and drafting an informative report

**Prepare:** Have note cards, the week 12 sources and an outline template ready. Model one paraphrased card yourself.

**Watch for:**
- Paraphrasing by swapping a few words. Read, cover the text, write from memory, then check.
- Forgetting to label sources on note cards. Code each card with question # and source letter.
- Introduction that just says 'My report is about…'. Use a hook and preview the subtopics.

### Day 1 (Monday): Note-taking
*Student:* Take 15 paraphrased note cards from 3 sources, coding each card to a research question and its source.

*How to teach:* Model one card: source code, question number, paraphrase in your own words. Your child makes 15 cards from 3 sources.

- **Q:** What goes on every note card?
  **A:** The source, the research question it answers, and a fact in your own words (quotation marks if copied exactly).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Paraphrase vs. plagiarism
*Student:* Practice paraphrasing 5 passages and compare to the originals; finish note cards.

*How to teach:* Your child paraphrases 5 passages and compares to the originals, then finishes cards. Show a copied sentence with a few words swapped and explain why it is still plagiarism. Then use 'read, cover, write, check.'

- **Q:** What is plagiarism?
  **A:** Using someone else's words or ideas as your own without credit.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Outlining
*Student:* Sort note cards into 3–4 subtopics and build an outline with headings.

*How to teach:* Sort cards into 3–4 subtopics and build an outline with headings. Lay the cards on the table and let your child physically group them; name each group as a heading.

- **Q:** How do you choose headings?
  **A:** Group cards that answer the same question; name each group with a short topic heading.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Drafting
*Student:* Draft the introduction and two body sections of the report using transition words like 'in addition' and 'however'.

*How to teach:* Draft introduction and two body sections with transitions. Your child writes from the outline, one section per heading, starting each section with a topic sentence.

- **Q:** Use 'however' correctly in a sentence.
  **A:** Colonial children worked hard; however, they also played games like marbles.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Finish the draft with a conclusion and a list of sources; self-check against the informative rubric.

*How to teach:* Finish the draft with a conclusion and source list; self-check against the informative rubric. Have your child read the rubric aloud and mark where the draft meets each line.

- **Q:** What does a conclusion do in an informative report?
  **A:** Restates the main ideas and leaves the reader with a final thought.

**Assessment:** Note cards and outline; complete informative draft.

**Answer key:** Note cards: 15 cards, each coded, paraphrased, accurate. Outline: 3–4 headed subtopics with supporting facts. Informative draft rubric: 4 = clear intro with hook and focus, organized sections with facts, transitions, conclusion, source list; 3 = all parts, some thin sections; 2 = disorganized or copied text; 1 = incomplete.

## Math 5: Equivalent fractions and common denominators

**Prepare:** Fraction strips (or paper strips folded into halves, fourths, eighths, thirds, sixths), and a set of fraction cards for matching (e.g., 6/8 and 3/4).

**Watch for:**
- Adding the same number to top and bottom (1/2 = 2/3). Equivalent fractions multiply or divide both by the same number.
- Not simplifying fully (12/18 → 6/9). Use the greatest common factor or keep dividing until no common factor remains.
- Thinking bigger denominators mean bigger fractions. Compare 1/3 and 1/8 with strips.

### Day 1 (Monday): Equivalent fractions review
*Student:* Fold paper strips to show 1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8 and generate 3 equivalent fractions for each of 10 fractions using multiplication.

*How to teach:* Fold a strip in half, then fourths, then eighths to show 1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8. The child generates 3 equivalent fractions for each of 10 fractions by multiplying top and bottom by the same number.

- **Q:** Give 3 fractions equal to 2/3.
  **A:** 4/6, 6/9, 8/12 (any n×2 / n×3).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Simplifying
*Student:* Simplify 15 fractions by dividing by common factors and match simplified and unsimplified fraction cards.

*How to teach:* Find common factors of numerator and denominator and divide. The child simplifies 15 fractions, then matches cards.

- **Q:** Simplify 12/18.
  **A:** 2/3 (divide by 6).
- **Q:** Simplify 15/20.
  **A:** 3/4

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Common denominators
*Student:* List multiples to find common denominators for 12 pairs like 2/3 and 3/4 and rename both fractions.

*How to teach:* List multiples of each denominator to find the least common multiple; rename both fractions. The child does 12 pairs.

- **Q:** Rename 2/3 and 3/4 with a common denominator.
  **A:** 8/12 and 9/12.
- **Q:** Rename 5/6 and 1/4.
  **A:** 10/12 and 3/12.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing unlike fractions
*Student:* Compare 12 pairs of unlike fractions by renaming with common denominators and by benchmark reasoning.

*How to teach:* Compare 3/5 and 4/7 by renaming (21/35 vs 20/35). Also compare by benchmarks: 5/8 > 1/2 > 3/7. The child compares 12 pairs.

- **Q:** Compare 3/5 and 4/7.
  **A:** 3/5 > 4/7 (21/35 > 20/35).
- **Q:** Compare 5/8 and 3/7 using 1/2.
  **A:** 5/8 is more than 1/2 and 3/7 is less than 1/2, so 5/8 > 3/7.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on equivalent fractions, simplifying and common denominators.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz independently; correct with strips. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** Is 9/12 equivalent to 3/4?
  **A:** Yes, divide both by 3.

**Assessment:** Quiz on equivalent fractions and common denominators.

**Answer key:** Suggested key: 1) 3/4 = 9/12 2) 2/5 = 8/20 3) simplify 8/12 = 2/3 4) simplify 10/25 = 2/5 5) simplify 18/24 = 3/4 6) common denominator for 1/6 and 3/8 = 24: 4/24 and 9/24 7) 2/3 and 1/5 → 10/15 and 3/15 8) 3/4 > 5/8 9) 2/3 < 7/9 10) 4/9 < 1/2 < 5/9. Full credit: correct equivalent forms shown.

## Science 5: Plants need air and water

**Prepare:** Spinach leaves, baking soda, water, needle-free syringe, a single-hole punch or straw, clear cups, lamp, clear nail polish, clear tape, magnifier or phone camera zoom. Leaf disk directions: dissolve 1/8 tsp baking soda in 300 mL water with a drop of dish soap; pull air out of disks with the syringe so they sink.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking plants take in oxygen for photosynthesis. They take in carbon dioxide and release oxygen.
- Thinking stomata are pores for water in only. Stomata let gases in and out and water vapor out.
- Thinking plants don't need light to make food.

### Day 1 (Monday): Floating leaf disks
*Student:* Do the floating leaf disk investigation with spinach in baking soda water under a lamp and count disks rising over time.

*How to teach:* Punch 10 spinach disks, remove air with the syringe so they sink in baking soda water, and place under a lamp. Count floating disks every minute.

- **Q:** Why do the disks float after time under light?
  **A:** Photosynthesis produces oxygen gas inside the leaf, making the disks buoyant.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Light vs. dark
*Student:* Repeat the leaf disk test in darkness and compare results to show plants need light.

*How to teach:* Repeat the test in darkness and compare. Wrap a second cup in foil or place it in a closet, timing it the same as yesterday. Your child records both data sets in one table.

- **Q:** What happens in the dark and what does it show?
  **A:** The disks stay down; without light, plants can't photosynthesize.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Stomata peel
*Student:* Paint clear nail polish on a leaf, peel it with tape, and look for stomata with a magnifier or phone microscope.

*How to teach:* Paint nail polish on the underside of a leaf, let dry, peel with tape, and look for stomata. Paint a small patch on the underside, let dry 10 minutes, then lift with tape and stick it on a dark paper or slide. Zoom with a magnifier or phone; stomata look like tiny lips or coffee beans.

- **Q:** What are stomata?
  **A:** Tiny openings, mostly on the underside of leaves, that let carbon dioxide in and oxygen and water vapor out.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Plant growth check
*Student:* Measure plant heights in the soil cups and paper towel bags and record growth data.

*How to teach:* Measure plant heights in both setups and record growth. Measure from soil (or seed) to the top of the tallest leaf in centimeters. Record the date and compare with earlier measurements.

- **Q:** Are the paper towel plants growing? Why might they stop?
  **A:** They grow at first from stored seed food and water; without soil nutrients they may slow over time.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draw a model showing air and water entering a plant and sugar being made, then take a quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child draws a model: water entering roots, carbon dioxide entering leaves, light energy, sugar made, oxygen released. Take a quiz.

- **Q:** What do plants make from air and water using light?
  **A:** Sugar (food), releasing oxygen.

**Assessment:** Leaf disk data and plant model diagram; quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) photosynthesis needs light, water, carbon dioxide 2) it produces sugar and oxygen 3) water enters through roots 4) carbon dioxide enters through stomata 5) leaf disks float because of oxygen 6) in the dark, disks don't float. Model diagram: arrows for water, CO2, light, sugar, O2 correctly placed.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: The First Great Awakening and colonial daily life

**Prepare:** Excerpt from Benjamin Franklin's Autobiography on Whitefield; materials for a craft (cream and jar for butter). South Central flashcards. Write the Unit 3 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Great Awakening was a single event.
- Thinking apprentices were paid workers.
- Mixing up South Central capitals.

### Day 1 (Monday): Great Awakening
*Student:* Read about Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield and Franklin's account of hearing Whitefield preach; explain how revivals encouraged questioning authority.

*How to teach:* Read about Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield, then Franklin's account. Discuss how emotional preaching encouraged people to think for themselves about religion and authority.

- **Q:** What did Franklin do when he heard Whitefield?
  **A:** He emptied his pockets into the collection plate despite planning not to.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Colonial work
*Student:* Read about apprentices and artisans (blacksmith, cooper, printer) and choose a trade to research.

*How to teach:* Read about apprentices (young people who learned a trade from a master for several years) and artisans. Your child chooses one trade and researches its tools and products.

- **Q:** What did a cooper make?
  **A:** Barrels.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Hands-on colonial life
*Student:* Try a colonial craft or chore (dipping a candle with adult help, making butter in a jar, writing with a quill).

*How to teach:* Choose one activity with adult help (shaking cream in a jar for butter is easiest). Afterward, your child writes how long it took and what it shows about colonial work.

- **Q:** How does butter form?
  **A:** Shaking cream makes fat clump.

### Day 4 (Thursday): States strand: South
*Student:* Review Southeast states and learn the South Central states and capitals (TX, OK, AR, LA).

*How to teach:* Review Southeast states with flashcards, then add Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas and Louisiana. Your child labels all on a blank map.

- **Q:** Capital of Louisiana?
  **A:** Baton Rouge.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 assessment
*Student:* Take the Unit 3 test on founding the colonies and colonial life.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 test independently; review errors with the 13 colonies map. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** Who founded Pennsylvania?
  **A:** William Penn.

**Assessment:** Unit 3 test; trade research card.

**Answer key:** Unit 3 test key: Jamestown 1607; tobacco; Plymouth 1620; Mayflower Compact; Massachusetts Bay; Roger Williams; Penn; Oglethorpe; Great Awakening. South Central capitals: Texas–Austin, Oklahoma–Oklahoma City, Arkansas–Little Rock, Louisiana–Baton Rouge.

## Art & Music 5: Native American and early colonial music

**Prepare:** Find Smithsonian Folkways recordings (free excerpts at folkways.si.edu): a Native American song (e.g., a Lakota, Navajo or Ojibwe song) and a 'lining out' psalm (often found by searching 'lined-out hymn'). Gather materials for a drum or rattle: an oatmeal canister or coffee can with a balloon or packing tape top; a bottle or container with dried beans.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all Native American music sounds the same. There are hundreds of nations with distinct languages, songs and instruments.
- Thinking vocables are 'nonsense.' Vocables are syllables without dictionary meaning but are an important, intentional part of many songs.

### Day 1 (Monday): Native American music
*Student:* Listen to a Smithsonian Folkways recording of a Native American song and describe the instruments, vocables and steady drum.

*How to teach:* Listen to the recording; your child describes the instruments (drum, rattle, flute), the steady drumbeat, and vocables (syllables like 'hey-ya').

- **Q:** What are vocables?
  **A:** Sung syllables that don't have a word meaning but are a meaningful part of the song.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Purposes of music
*Student:* Discuss and chart purposes of songs (ceremony, storytelling, celebration, work) with examples.

*How to teach:* Chart purposes of music (ceremony, storytelling, celebration, work, lullaby) with examples.

- **Q:** Name three purposes of music in a culture.
  **A:** Ceremony/worship, storytelling, celebration, work, healing, lullabies.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Colonial psalms
*Student:* Listen to a 'lining out' psalm from colonial New England and try call-and-response singing.

*How to teach:* Listen to a lined-out psalm: a leader sings a line and the congregation repeats. Try call-and-response with your child.

- **Q:** Why did colonial congregations use 'lining out'?
  **A:** Many people didn't have hymn books or couldn't read music, so a leader sang each line and everyone repeated it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Making an instrument
*Student:* Make a simple drum or rattle from household items and play a steady-beat pattern.

*How to teach:* Make a drum or rattle and play a steady-beat pattern. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** How does a drum make sound?
  **A:** The surface vibrates when struck, making the air vibrate.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write how music connected to daily life in one culture, citing a recording.

*How to teach:* Your child writes how music connected to daily life in one culture, citing a recording. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** Give an example of a connection between music and daily life.
  **A:** Example: colonial psalm singing was part of weekly worship; a Native American song might accompany a ceremony or dance.

**Assessment:** Listening log and written response.

**Answer key:** Listening log: for each recording, the culture, instruments, purpose and musical features (steady beat, vocables, call and response). Written response rubric: 4 = explains a connection to daily life and cites a specific recording and detail; 3 = connection without specific evidence; 2 = vague; 1 = missing.

## PE 5: Overhand throwing for accuracy and distance

**Prepare:** Gather tennis balls, a hula hoop or chalk targets on a wall, and a tape measure. Mark throw lines at 5, 10 and 15 m. Use a slow-motion phone camera for Thursday. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Stepping with the same foot as the throwing arm. Step toward the target with the opposite foot (opposition).
- Throwing with just the arm. Power comes from rotating the hips and trunk, then the arm whips through.

### Day 1 (Monday): Throwing cues
*Student:* Practice the overhand throw cues (side to target, step with opposite foot, rotate, follow through) at a wall target.

*How to teach:* Teach the cues: side to target, arm back, step with opposite foot, rotate hips, follow through across the body. Throw 20 at a wall target.

- **Q:** A right-handed thrower steps with which foot?
  **A:** The left foot (opposite foot).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Distance throws
*Student:* Throw for distance at 3 different release angles and measure to find the best angle.

*How to teach:* Throw for distance at a low (flat), medium (about 45°), and high (straight up-ish) release; measure each. Your child decides which is best.

- **Q:** Which release angle usually gives the most distance?
  **A:** A medium angle, around 35–45 degrees; too flat hits the ground early, too high goes up instead of out.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Accuracy targets
*Student:* Throw at targets from 5, 10 and 15 m and record hits.

*How to teach:* Throw 5 at the target from 5, 10 and 15 m; record hits at each distance. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** What should you change when the target is farther away?
  **A:** Use more force (bigger step and hip rotation) and a slightly higher release angle.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Video analysis
*Student:* Record your throw in slow motion and compare to the cues.

*How to teach:* Record a slow-motion throw; compare each cue on video. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** On video, how can you tell hip rotation happened?
  **A:** The belly button turns from facing the side to facing the target before the arm comes through.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain in writing how force and angle affected your distance results.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a paragraph using their data about how force and angle affected distance.

- **Q:** If you throw with more force at the same angle, what happens?
  **A:** The ball travels farther.

**Assessment:** Throwing data and written explanation.

**Answer key:** Throwing data: distances for three angles and hits at three distances recorded. Written explanation (proficient): more force → farther; a middle angle (≈35–45°) went farthest; accuracy drops as distance increases; cites own numbers. 4 = all with data; 3 = most; 2 = vague; 1 = missing.

## Health 5: Media, ads and body image

**Prepare:** Gather magazines or catalogs, and preview online: a short photo-retouching video (e.g., Dove 'Evolution') and two kids' food commercials. Make a list of the 6 advertising techniques with definitions. Gather art supplies for the counter-ad.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking photos in ads and social media show real bodies. Most are posed, lit, filtered and digitally edited.
- Thinking ads are made to inform. Ads are made to sell; they highlight what's appealing and leave out what isn't.
- Thinking advertising doesn't affect you. Repeated messages shape what we want and how we see ourselves, even when we know they're ads.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ad techniques
*Student:* Learn 6 advertising techniques (bandwagon, celebrity, fun, health claims, giveaways, beautiful people) and find examples in magazines.

*How to teach:* Explain the 6 techniques: bandwagon (everyone has it), celebrity endorsement, fun/excitement, health claims, giveaways/prizes, beautiful people. Your child finds and labels one example of each in magazines.

- **Q:** An ad shows a famous athlete drinking a sports drink. Which technique is that?
  **A:** Celebrity endorsement (testimonial).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Edited images
*Student:* Watch a short before-and-after photo-retouching video and discuss how it affects body image.

*How to teach:* Watch the retouching video; discuss what changed (skin smoothed, neck lengthened, eyes enlarged) and how it might make viewers feel.

- **Q:** How can edited images affect people's body image?
  **A:** They create unrealistic standards that no real body matches, which can make people feel bad about their own bodies.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Food ads
*Student:* Analyze two kids' food commercials: target audience, techniques and what they leave out.

*How to teach:* Watch two kids' food commercials; your child fills in: target audience, techniques used, and what the ad leaves out (sugar, nutrition, price).

- **Q:** Who is the target audience of a cereal ad with cartoon characters and a toy prize, and what's left out?
  **A:** Young children; it leaves out nutrition facts like high added sugar and low fiber.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Gender messages
*Student:* Compare toy or clothing ads aimed at boys vs. girls and note stereotypes about how bodies should look.

*How to teach:* Compare ads aimed at boys vs. girls (toys or clothing); list stereotypes about bodies and behaviors.

- **Q:** Give an example of a gender stereotype in ads and why it's unfair.
  **A:** Example: boys shown only as muscular and tough, girls only as thin and pretty — it limits how kids think they should look or act.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Create a counter-ad that promotes healthy body image or a healthy food honestly.

*How to teach:* Your child creates a counter-ad promoting healthy body image or an honest healthy food, and labels the technique it uses or answers.

- **Q:** What makes a counter-ad honest?
  **A:** It uses real, unedited images and true, accurate information, and doesn't make exaggerated claims.

**Assessment:** Counter-ad with technique explanation.

**Answer key:** Counter-ad rubric: 4 = clear health-enhancing message, honest images and claims, names the advertising technique it uses or counters and explains it in 2–3 sentences; 3 = message plus technique named; 2 = message unclear or technique missing; 1 = incomplete.
