# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 14

## ELA 5: Publishing and presenting the research report

**Prepare:** Computer, poster board or slideshow program, presentation checklist. Pick two short informational articles on the same topic for the cold read.

**Watch for:**
- Adding text features that don't connect to the text. Each feature needs a caption explaining it.
- Reading slides word for word. Slides show key points; the speaker explains.
- Comparing two texts by summarizing each separately. Integrate: say how they agree, differ and add to each other.

### Day 1 (Monday): Revising
*Student:* Revise the report for precise domain vocabulary and add one quotation and one text feature (map, diagram, timeline).

*How to teach:* Your child revises for precise vocabulary, adds one quotation and one text feature (map, diagram or timeline). Help your child choose a feature that adds information the words cannot show easily, and write a caption for it.

- **Q:** Replace with a domain word: The colonists used the big pot to make soap.
  **A:** The colonists used the kettle to make lye soap (any precise term).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Typing and editing
*Student:* Type the final report in one sitting and edit for commas, titles and spelling.

*How to teach:* Type the final report and edit for commas, title punctuation and spelling. Typing in one sitting builds stamina; afterward your child edits a printed copy with a pen.

- **Q:** Punctuate the title: I read the article colonial kitchens.
  **A:** I read the article “Colonial Kitchens.”

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Visual display
*Student:* Create a poster or 4-slide display highlighting the report's main ideas with captioned images.

*How to teach:* Create a poster or 4 slides with captioned images highlighting the main ideas. Each slide or poster section gets a heading, a few key words and a captioned image — not paragraphs.

- **Q:** What should a caption do?
  **A:** Explain what the image shows and how it connects to the report.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Presenting
*Student:* Present the report in 3–4 minutes to family, speaking clearly and answering two audience questions.

*How to teach:* Your child presents for 3–4 minutes and answers two questions. Rehearse once first. During the talk, note eye contact, volume and pace on the checklist.

- **Q:** Give two speaking tips.
  **A:** Make eye contact, speak loudly and clearly at a steady pace.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 assessment
*Student:* Take a cold-read assessment comparing two informational texts on the same topic (structure, point of view, integration).

*How to teach:* Give the cold-read comparison of two informational texts (structure, point of view, integration). Choose two short articles on one topic. Do not help during the assessment; score it afterward with the rubric.

- **Q:** How do you integrate two texts?
  **A:** State what both say, what each adds that the other doesn't, and how together they explain the topic better.

**Assessment:** Research report rubric; presentation checklist; Unit 3 assessment.

**Answer key:** Report rubric (4 levels): 4 = focused, well-organized, accurate facts with precise vocabulary, quotation, text feature, sources listed, few errors; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = missing parts or many errors; 1 = incomplete. Presentation checklist: 3–4 minutes, clear voice, eye contact, visual used, answered 2 questions. Unit 3 cold-read: proficient answers name each text's structure, describe each author's point of view, and combine information from both with evidence.

## Math 5: Adding and subtracting fractions with unlike denominators

**Prepare:** Fraction strips; optional free Math Learning Center Fractions app. Review finding common denominators.

**Watch for:**
- Adding numerators and denominators (1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5). Show with strips that 2/5 is less than 1/2 — impossible for a sum.
- Renaming only one fraction. Both fractions must have the common denominator.
- Leaving improper answers or unsimplified answers. Convert to a mixed number and simplify.

### Day 1 (Monday): Adding with models
*Student:* Use fraction strips to add 1/2 + 1/3 and 8 other pairs, recording the renamed equation for each.

*How to teach:* Lay a 1/2 and a 1/3 strip end to end and find which single strip size fits exactly: sixths, 5/6. Record 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6. The child models 8 more pairs.

- **Q:** 1/2 + 1/3 = ?
  **A:** 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6
- **Q:** 1/4 + 1/8 = ?
  **A:** 2/8 + 1/8 = 3/8

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Adding with equations
*Student:* Add 15 pairs of unlike fractions by renaming, writing sums greater than 1 as mixed numbers.

*How to teach:* Rename both fractions with a common denominator, add numerators, then convert sums > 1 to mixed numbers. The child solves 15.

- **Q:** 3/4 + 5/6 = ?
  **A:** 9/12 + 10/12 = 19/12 = 1 7/12
- **Q:** 2/5 + 1/2 = ?
  **A:** 4/10 + 5/10 = 9/10

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Subtracting with models
*Student:* Model 3/4 - 1/6 on a number line and solve 8 more subtraction problems with models.

*How to teach:* Draw a number line in twelfths; mark 3/4 (9/12) and jump back 1/6 (2/12) to land on 7/12. The child models 8 more.

- **Q:** 3/4 − 1/6 = ?
  **A:** 7/12

### Day 4 (Thursday): Subtracting with equations
*Student:* Subtract 15 unlike-fraction problems and simplify answers.

*How to teach:* Rename, subtract numerators, simplify. The child solves 15 problems.

- **Q:** 5/6 − 3/8 = ?
  **A:** 20/24 − 9/24 = 11/24
- **Q:** 7/10 − 1/2 = ?
  **A:** 7/10 − 5/10 = 2/10 = 1/5

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a 12-problem quiz on adding and subtracting unlike fractions.

*How to teach:* Give the 12-problem quiz independently and check answers with benchmark estimates. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** 2/3 + 1/4 = ?
  **A:** 8/12 + 3/12 = 11/12

**Assessment:** Quiz on adding/subtracting unlike fractions.

**Answer key:** Suggested key: 1) 1/3 + 1/6 = 1/2 2) 2/3 + 1/5 = 13/15 3) 3/8 + 1/4 = 5/8 4) 5/6 + 3/4 = 1 7/12 5) 1/2 + 4/5 = 1 3/10 6) 2/7 + 1/2 = 11/14 7) 3/4 − 1/3 = 5/12 8) 5/8 − 1/4 = 3/8 9) 9/10 − 2/5 = 1/2 10) 1 − 3/7 = 4/7 11) 2/3 − 1/4 = 5/12 12) 7/8 − 1/6 = 17/24.

## Science 5: Energy in food comes from the sun

**Prepare:** Food labels from 5 items, a thermometer, a jump rope, drawing supplies. Check if your child has any exercise restrictions.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking food energy comes from the stomach. It originally comes from the sun captured by plants.
- Thinking animals make their own energy from nothing.
- Thinking calories are ingredients. Calories measure energy stored in food.

### Day 1 (Monday): Tracing breakfast
*Student:* Trace every food in your breakfast back to a plant and the sun with arrows.

*How to teach:* List every breakfast food and trace each back to a plant and the sun with arrows (e.g., egg ← chicken ← corn ← sun). Model one chain. Your child does every breakfast food; check that every chain ends at a plant and then the sun.

- **Q:** Trace milk back to the sun.
  **A:** Milk ← cow ← grass ← sun.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): How bodies use energy
*Student:* Measure your pulse and body temperature at rest and after exercise and discuss how food energy powers motion and warmth.

*How to teach:* Measure pulse and temperature at rest and after 2 minutes of jumping rope. Discuss how food energy powers motion and warmth.

- **Q:** Why does your pulse rise after exercise?
  **A:** Muscles need more energy and oxygen, so the heart pumps faster; energy from food is used for movement and released as heat.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Food labels
*Student:* Compare calories on 5 food labels and explain that calories measure stored energy originally from sunlight.

*How to teach:* Compare calories on 5 labels; explain calories measure stored energy originally from sunlight. Have your child put labels in order of calories per serving. Ask: 'Where did the plant get the energy stored in this food?'

- **Q:** Where did the energy in a granola bar's calories originally come from?
  **A:** The sun — plants captured sunlight to make the oats, sugars and nuts.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Energy model
*Student:* Create an energy flow diagram from sun to grass to cow to human, labeling uses of energy at each step.

*How to teach:* Your child creates an energy flow diagram: sun → grass → cow → human, labeling uses (growth, movement, body heat). Your child labels what each organism uses energy for. Point out that only some energy passes to the next level because much is used or lost as heat.

- **Q:** Why do arrows point from the grass to the cow?
  **A:** Arrows show the direction energy and matter flow — from food to eater.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write an explanation of how the energy used to run a race was once energy from the sun.

*How to teach:* Your child writes how the energy used to run a race was once energy from the sun. Encourage a step-by-step chain: sunlight → plant (photosynthesis) → food → my body → my muscles. Score with the rubric.

- **Q:** Model explanation.
  **A:** Sunlight was captured by plants and stored as sugar; I ate plants (or animals that ate plants) and my body used that stored energy to move my muscles in the race.

**Assessment:** Energy flow diagram and written explanation.

**Answer key:** Diagram and explanation rubric: 4 = complete chain from sun through producer and consumers, arrows in correct direction, uses of energy labeled, explanation names photosynthesis and stored energy; 3 = mostly complete; 2 = missing the sun or reversed arrows; 1 = incomplete.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Colonial economy and the British system

**Prepare:** Comparison chart; trade goods list.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking colonists could trade freely.
- Thinking Spanish missions were only churches.
- Thinking France had many settlers.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mercantilism
*Student:* Play a simple trade simulation showing raw materials going to England and finished goods returning, then discuss who benefits.

*How to teach:* Play a simple trade: your child (colonist) sells raw cotton cheaply to you (England), then must buy back a shirt at a high price. Discuss who benefits and why colonists might resent it.

- **Q:** Who benefits under mercantilism?
  **A:** England.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Free enterprise roots
*Student:* Read how colonists ran farms, shops and ships for profit and explain the start of a free-market economy.

*How to teach:* Read how colonists ran farms, shops and ships for profit. Your child explains in their own words what free enterprise means.

- **Q:** What is free enterprise?
  **A:** People own businesses and make choices for profit.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Spanish and French colonies
*Student:* Read about Spanish missions and presidios (including California) and French fur-trading posts.

*How to teach:* Read about Spanish missions and presidios (including California) and French fur-trading posts. Your child notes the purpose of each.

- **Q:** Purpose of California missions?
  **A:** Spread Christianity and control land.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing systems
*Student:* Complete a chart comparing British, Spanish and French colonies in government, economy, religion and relations with Native peoples.

*How to teach:* Your child fills a chart comparing British, Spanish and French colonies on government, economy, religion and relations with Native peoples. Before finishing, have your child explain their work aloud in 2–3 sentences; correct any errors then.

- **Q:** How did French relate to Natives?
  **A:** Mostly trade partnerships.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining one major difference between British and Spanish colonies.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a paragraph explaining one major difference between British and Spanish colonies with two details. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** One difference?
  **A:** British colonies had more self-government.

**Assessment:** Colonial systems chart and paragraph.

**Answer key:** Chart and Paragraph rubric: 4 = clear topic sentence, three or more accurate, specific details or quotations from sources, explanation of how they support the point, conclusion; 3 = accurate with two details; 2 = general or partly inaccurate; 1 = off topic or incomplete.

## Art & Music 5: Songs of the Revolution and the nation

**Prepare:** Print the lyrics for 'Yankee Doodle,' 'The Star-Spangled Banner,' 'America the Beautiful,' and 'My Country, 'Tis of Thee.' Find a recording of each. Recorder music for 'Yankee Doodle' (G G A B G B A, in G).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 'Yankee Doodle' was always a patriotic song. British soldiers sang it to mock colonists; Patriots adopted it with pride.
- Thinking 'The Star-Spangled Banner' was written during the Revolution. It was written during the War of 1812 (1814).

### Day 1 (Monday): Yankee Doodle
*Student:* Learn the history of 'Yankee Doodle' as a British insult turned Patriot anthem and play it on recorder.

*How to teach:* Read the history; play 'Yankee Doodle' on recorder. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** How did 'Yankee Doodle' change meaning?
  **A:** British soldiers sang it to make fun of colonists, but Patriots made it their own proud song.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Star-Spangled Banner
*Student:* Read about Francis Scott Key and Fort McHenry and sing the first verse with attention to the wide range.

*How to teach:* Read about Francis Scott Key watching the bombardment of Fort McHenry (Baltimore, 1814); sing the first verse, noting the wide range.

- **Q:** What event inspired 'The Star-Spangled Banner'?
  **A:** The British bombardment of Fort McHenry in 1814 during the War of 1812; Key saw the flag still flying at dawn.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): America the Beautiful
*Student:* Learn 'America the Beautiful' and list the geographic images Katharine Lee Bates used.

*How to teach:* Learn 'America the Beautiful' (Katharine Lee Bates, inspired by the view from Pikes Peak); list geographic images.

- **Q:** List three geographic images in 'America the Beautiful.'
  **A:** Spacious skies, amber waves of grain, purple mountain majesties, the fruited plain, from sea to shining sea.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Expressive singing
*Student:* Choose dynamics and tempo to express the meaning of one song and record a performance.

*How to teach:* Choose dynamics and tempo to express one song's meaning; record it. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** Why might you sing the end of 'America the Beautiful' louder?
  **A:** To build to a proud, powerful ending expressing the song's ideal.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform two songs and explain the ideal each expresses.

*How to teach:* Perform two songs and explain each song's ideal. Set up a simple 'stage' and audience, have your child introduce the work, and hold applause until the end; afterward ask them to name one strength and one thing to improve before you add one specific comment.

- **Q:** What ideal does 'My Country, 'Tis of Thee' express?
  **A:** Liberty/freedom ('sweet land of liberty').

**Assessment:** Patriotic song performance and explanation.

**Answer key:** Performance rubric: 4 = accurate melody and lyrics, chosen dynamics/tempo match meaning, confident; 3 = minor errors; 2 = many errors; 1 = incomplete. Explanation: Star-Spangled Banner — courage, survival of the nation; America the Beautiful — beauty and unity; My Country 'Tis of Thee — liberty; Yankee Doodle — Patriot pride.

## PE 5: Catching on the move

**Prepare:** Gather a foam football or tennis ball and cones to mark routes. Review 'target hands': thumbs together for balls above the waist, pinkies together for balls below the waist. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Catching with stiff arms. Reach out and 'give' (pull the ball in toward the body) to absorb it.
- Watching the thrower instead of the ball. Track the ball all the way into the hands.

### Day 1 (Monday): Target hands
*Student:* Practice catching above and below the waist with proper hand position.

*How to teach:* Toss balls high and low. Your child shows target hands: thumbs together above the waist, pinkies together below.

- **Q:** How should your hands be for a ball below the waist?
  **A:** Pinkies together, fingers pointing down.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Moving catches
*Student:* Run routes (slant, out, curl) and catch passes from a partner.

*How to teach:* Teach three routes with cones: slant (diagonal inward), out (straight then cut toward sideline), curl (run then turn back). Throw to your child on each route.

- **Q:** Describe an 'out' route.
  **A:** Run straight downfield, then cut sharply toward the sideline.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Catch and throw
*Student:* Catch on the move and immediately throw to a second partner or target.

*How to teach:* Your child catches on the run and immediately throws to a second target or person without extra steps.

- **Q:** What helps you transition from catch to throw quickly?
  **A:** Catch with hands, bring the ball up to the throwing position right away, and step toward the new target.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Team passing game
*Student:* Play 'Ultimate ball' (no running with the ball) with teammates of all skill levels.

*How to teach:* Play Ultimate ball: no running with the ball, pass to advance, score by catching in the end zone. Encourage passing to everyone.

- **Q:** Why is a 'no running with the ball' rule good for including everyone?
  **A:** The team must pass to advance, so every player gets involved.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete 10 moving catches and record success rate.

*How to teach:* Throw 10 passes to your child on the move and record catches. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** If you catch 7 of 10, what is your success rate?
  **A:** 70%.

**Assessment:** Moving-catch success rate.

**Answer key:** Moving-catch success rate out of 10 recorded with percent. Cue check: target hands correct for high/low balls, eyes on the ball, gives with the catch, transitions to a throw. Proficient = 6/10 or better with 3 of 4 cues.

## Health 5: Skin, hair and body hygiene

**Prepare:** Gather samples or photos of deodorant and antiperspirant labels, gentle face wash, shampoo and nail clippers. Preview KidsHealth articles on body odor and acne.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking acne comes from being dirty or eating chocolate. Acne comes mainly from hormones making oil glands more active; pores clog with oil and dead skin. Scrubbing hard makes it worse.
- Thinking deodorant and antiperspirant are the same. Deodorant fights odor; antiperspirant reduces sweat.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sweat and oil
*Student:* Learn how sweat and oil glands become more active in puberty and why body odor and acne happen.

*How to teach:* Explain that in puberty hormones make sweat and oil glands more active; bacteria breaking down sweat cause body odor, and oil clogging pores causes acne.

- **Q:** What actually causes body odor?
  **A:** Bacteria on the skin breaking down sweat (especially from glands in the armpits and groin).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Skin care
*Student:* Read about gentle face washing and acne myths; write a simple morning and evening skin routine.

*How to teach:* Read about gentle washing and acne myths; your child writes a morning and evening routine.

- **Q:** Should you scrub your face hard to stop pimples? Why?
  **A:** No — scrubbing irritates skin and can make acne worse; wash gently twice a day with mild cleanser.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Deodorant vs. antiperspirant
*Student:* Compare product labels for deodorant and antiperspirant and explain the difference.

*How to teach:* Compare labels: antiperspirants list aluminum compounds as the active ingredient. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What's the difference between deodorant and antiperspirant?
  **A:** Deodorant reduces odor (covers scent, fights bacteria); antiperspirant reduces sweating (usually with aluminum compounds). Many products are both.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Hair and nails
*Student:* Learn how often to wash hair as it gets oilier and practice nail care.

*How to teach:* Discuss washing hair more often as it gets oily; practice trimming nails straight across. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** Why trim toenails straight across?
  **A:** To help prevent ingrown toenails.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Build a personal hygiene checklist and complete a hygiene myth-or-fact quiz.

*How to teach:* Build a personal hygiene checklist and take the myth-or-fact quiz. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** Myth or fact: you only need to shower when you look dirty.
  **A:** Myth — during puberty, daily showers (especially after exercise) prevent odor even if you don't look dirty.

**Assessment:** Hygiene checklist and quiz.

**Answer key:** Myth/fact key: Acne is caused by dirt — myth; Puberty makes sweat glands more active — fact; Deodorant stops sweating — myth (antiperspirant reduces sweat); Popping pimples helps them heal — myth; Washing face gently twice daily helps — fact; Shower after exercise — fact. Checklist: daily shower, deodorant, face wash AM/PM, clean clothes, hair washing, nail care, brushing teeth. Proficient = 80% on quiz and complete checklist.
