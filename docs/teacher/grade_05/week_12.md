# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 12

## ELA 5: Biography: 'And Then What Happened, Paul Revere?' by Jean Fritz; research launch

**Prepare:** Borrow 'And Then What Happened, Paul Revere?' by Jean Fritz; preview it. Gather note cards and visit the library catalog.

**Watch for:**
- Treating a biography as a story with made-up parts. Biographies are factual, though authors choose which details to emphasize.
- Writing research questions answerable with yes/no. Use how/why/what questions.
- Underlining article titles. Underline or italicize book titles; put article and chapter titles in quotation marks.

### Day 1 (Monday): Reading the biography
*Student:* Read the Jean Fritz biography and make a timeline of Paul Revere's life events.

*How to teach:* Read the biography; your child builds a timeline of Revere's life events (born in Boston, silversmith, Sons of Liberty, Boston Tea Party, midnight ride in April 1775). Read aloud together; Fritz's humor makes it quick. Your child adds a date or age to each timeline event where the book gives one.

- **Q:** What was Paul Revere's trade?
  **A:** Silversmith (he also made engravings and other metalwork).
- **Q:** Why is the book called 'And Then What Happened'?
  **A:** Revere was always busy with one activity after another, and Fritz keeps the story moving through his many adventures and mishaps.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Relationships
*Student:* Explain how three events or people (Sons of Liberty, the British, Revere's trade) influenced Revere's actions in a cause/effect chart.

*How to teach:* Your child completes a cause/effect chart on how the Sons of Liberty, the British, and his trade influenced Revere. Model one cause/effect row, then have your child explain each row aloud.

- **Q:** How did the British influence Revere's actions?
  **A:** British taxes and soldiers in Boston made him join the Patriots, take part in the Boston Tea Party and ride to warn of troop movements.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Choosing a topic
*Student:* Choose a research topic about colonial life or an explorer and write 4 research questions about different aspects of it.

*How to teach:* Your child chooses a colonial life or explorer topic and writes 4 research questions on different aspects. Check that each question asks how or why and that the four questions cover different aspects (food, work, school, homes).

- **Q:** Rewrite as an open question: Did colonists make candles?
  **A:** How did colonists make candles, and why were they important?

### Day 4 (Thursday): Finding sources
*Student:* Locate 3 sources (library book, encyclopedia, reliable website) and record them in a source list with titles italicized or underlined.

*How to teach:* Find 3 sources and record a source list with titles correctly formatted. Show how to judge a website (who wrote it, .gov/.edu/.org, date). Your child records author, title and publisher for each.

- **Q:** Format: book Colonial Times; article Daily Life.
  **A:** Colonial Times (italicized or underlined); “Daily Life” in quotation marks.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on the biography and on title punctuation, and conference with a parent on research questions.

*How to teach:* Give a quiz on the biography and title punctuation; conference on the research questions. During the conference, help your child cut or sharpen any question that is too broad.

- **Q:** What happened to Revere at the end of the midnight ride?
  **A:** British soldiers stopped and captured him; they took his horse and he did not reach Concord.

**Assessment:** Cause/effect chart; research questions and source list; title punctuation quiz.

**Answer key:** Cause/effect chart: three accurate causes with effects. Research questions: 4 open-ended questions on different aspects. Title punctuation quiz key: books, magazines, films italicized/underlined; stories, poems, articles, songs in quotation marks. Biography quiz (suggested): silversmith; Boston; Sons of Liberty; Boston Tea Party; ride to warn that the British were coming; captured before Concord.

## Math 5: Decimal word problems and unit assessment

**Prepare:** A recipe card with decimal amounts (e.g., 1.5 cups flour, 0.75 cup sugar, 2.25 tsp baking powder) and grocery ads or prices with sizes. Write the Unit 3 test.

**Watch for:**
- Choosing operations by key words alone ('each' always means multiply). Draw a bar model to see whether you are combining, comparing, grouping or sharing.
- Comparing total prices instead of unit prices. Divide price by size to compare cost per ounce.
- Rounding money in the middle of a problem. Keep all digits until the final answer.

### Day 1 (Monday): Which operation?
*Student:* Sort 12 decimal word problems by operation and solve 6 of them with a bar model.

*How to teach:* Read each problem and sketch a bar model before choosing an operation. The child sorts 12 problems into +, −, ×, ÷ and solves 6.

- **Q:** A 2.4 m ribbon is cut into 6 equal pieces. How long is each? Which operation?
  **A:** Division: 2.4 ÷ 6 = 0.4 m.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Unit price
*Student:* Compare unit prices for 4 pairs of grocery items (e.g., 12 oz for $3.48 vs 16 oz for $4.32) by dividing.

*How to teach:* Divide price by ounces for each item. The child compares 4 pairs and states the better buy.

- **Q:** 12 oz for $3.48 or 16 oz for $4.32 — which is the better buy?
  **A:** $3.48 ÷ 12 = $0.29/oz; $4.32 ÷ 16 = $0.27/oz. The 16 oz is cheaper per ounce.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Recipe scaling
*Student:* Multiply a recipe's decimal measurements by 2.5 and divide by 2 to make a double-and-a-half batch and a half batch.

*How to teach:* Multiply each recipe amount by 2.5 and divide each by 2, recording two new recipes. Ask your child to estimate first (2.5 times is between 2 and 3 times as much). Have them write the scaled recipes in a table: original, × 2.5, ÷ 2.

- **Q:** 1.5 cups of flour × 2.5 = ? And ÷ 2 = ?
  **A:** 3.75 cups; 0.75 cup.
- **Q:** 0.75 cup sugar × 2.5 = ?
  **A:** 1.875 cups

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit review
*Student:* Complete a mixed decimal operations review and a 'my mistakes' log of errors to watch.

*How to teach:* Do a mixed review of all four decimal operations, then the child starts a 'my mistakes' log listing errors and the fix for each. Pull problems your child missed on earlier quizzes. For each log entry they write the wrong step, the correct step and a tip to remember.

- **Q:** 4.2 × 0.3 + 1.74 ÷ 0.6 = ?
  **A:** 1.26 + 2.9 = 4.16

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 3 test on all four decimal operations and word problems.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 3 test independently and review errors against the mistakes log. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** Pencils cost $0.35 each. How many can you buy with $5?
  **A:** 5 ÷ 0.35 ≈ 14.28, so 14 pencils.

**Assessment:** Unit 3 test covering 5.NBT.B.7.

**Answer key:** Unit 3 test key (suggested): 1) 6.75 + 3.8 = 10.55 2) 15 − 6.42 = 8.58 3) 3.4 × 0.6 = 2.04 4) 12 × 1.25 = 15 5) 7.2 ÷ 9 = 0.8 6) 4.8 ÷ 0.16 = 30 7) 5.1 ÷ 2 = 2.55 8) Unit price: 24 oz for $5.04 → $0.21/oz 9) 3 notebooks at $2.89 = $8.67 10) $20 − $8.67 = $11.33 change 11) 2.5 kg split into 4 bags = 0.625 kg each 12) A 0.75 L bottle refilled 6 times = 4.5 L. Scoring: 11–12 mastery, 9–10 proficient.

## Science 5: Where does a plant's mass come from?

**Prepare:** Bean or radish seeds, potting soil (dry it in a sunny spot first), cups with drainage, kitchen scale, water, zip bags and paper towels. Read about Jan van Helmont's willow experiment (1600s).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking plants 'eat' soil. Most plant mass comes from carbon dioxide in the air and water.
- Thinking food for plants comes from fertilizer. Fertilizer supplies small amounts of nutrients, not most of the mass.
- Weighing wet soil at the start and dry at the end. Use dry soil both times.

### Day 1 (Monday): Initial ideas
*Student:* Write and draw initial ideas about where a giant tree's material comes from.

*How to teach:* Your child writes and draws initial ideas about where a giant tree's material comes from. Accept all ideas; this is a starting model.

- **Q:** Where do you think a tree's mass comes from?
  **A:** Accept any initial idea; the scientific answer (to be learned) is mostly from carbon dioxide in the air plus water.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Van Helmont's experiment
*Student:* Read about Jan van Helmont's 5-year willow experiment and analyze his data on tree mass and soil mass.

*How to teach:* Read about van Helmont's experiment: he planted a 5-pound willow in 200 pounds of dry soil and watered it for 5 years. The tree gained about 164 pounds; the soil lost only about 2 ounces.

- **Q:** What did van Helmont's data show?
  **A:** The tree gained about 164 pounds but the soil lost only about 2 ounces, so the mass did not come from soil.
- **Q:** What did he conclude, and was he fully right?
  **A:** He concluded it came from water; partly right — we now know most comes from carbon dioxide in the air, plus water.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Set up investigation
*Student:* Weigh dry soil and plant bean seeds in cups, recording starting soil mass for a multi-week investigation.

*How to teach:* Weigh dry soil for each cup, plant seeds and record starting mass. Tare the scale with an empty cup, add dry soil and record the mass on the cup label. Plant 2–3 seeds per cup and water lightly.

- **Q:** Why record the soil's starting mass?
  **A:** To compare at the end and see if soil mass decreased as the plant grew.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Hydroponic test
*Student:* Start seeds on wet paper towels in a bag with no soil to see if plants can grow without soil.

*How to teach:* Start seeds on wet paper towels in a bag with no soil. Fold a damp paper towel in a zip bag, place seeds inside and tape it to a window. Your child writes a prediction in the notebook.

- **Q:** Predict: Can seeds sprout without soil?
  **A:** Yes — they sprout using stored food in the seed and water; with light, air and water they can keep growing for a while.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a preliminary claim about where plant mass comes from, citing van Helmont's evidence.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a preliminary claim about where plant mass comes from, citing van Helmont. Your child cites van Helmont's numbers (tree gained about 164 lb, soil lost about 2 oz). Accept 'water' or 'air' as hypotheses; the key is that it's not mainly soil.

- **Q:** Write a preliminary claim.
  **A:** Plant mass does not come mainly from soil; it comes from something else, like water or air.

**Assessment:** Initial model and preliminary claim; investigation setup check.

**Answer key:** Initial model: any idea with a drawing. Preliminary claim rubric: 4 = claim that mass is not mainly from soil, citing van Helmont's numbers; 3 = claim with general evidence; 2 = claim only; 1 = none. Setup check: soil massed, seeds planted, data recorded.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Middle and Southern colonies

**Prepare:** Excerpt of William Penn's letter to the Lenape (1681); map of the 13 colonies.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Middle Colonies were all Quaker.
- Thinking Georgia was founded early.
- Mixing up regions.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pennsylvania
*Student:* Read about William Penn and the Quakers and analyze an excerpt of Penn's letter to the Lenape about fair dealings.

*How to teach:* Read about William Penn, the Quakers and his 'holy experiment.' Read his letter to the Lenape; your child lists what he promised.

- **Q:** What did Penn promise the Lenape?
  **A:** To live in peace and deal fairly.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): New York, New Jersey, Delaware
*Student:* Learn how New Netherland became New York and why the Middle Colonies were diverse 'breadbasket' colonies.

*How to teach:* Explain that the Dutch colony of New Netherland was taken by England in 1664 and renamed New York. Ask why fertile land and a mild climate made the Middle Colonies 'breadbasket' colonies.

- **Q:** When?
  **A:** 1664.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Maryland and the South
*Student:* Read about Lord Baltimore's Catholic haven in Maryland and the founding of the Carolinas and Georgia.

*How to teach:* Read about Lord Baltimore (Maryland, 1634), the Carolinas and Georgia (1732). Your child notes the reason each was founded.

- **Q:** Who founded Georgia and why?
  **A:** James Oglethorpe in 1732, for debtors and as a buffer against Spanish Florida.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Regions chart
*Student:* Complete a 3-region chart comparing geography, economy, religion and founders of the colonies.

*How to teach:* Your child fills a chart comparing New England, Middle and Southern colonies on geography, economy, religion and founders. Before finishing, have your child explain their work aloud in 2–3 sentences; correct any errors then.

- **Q:** Why 'breadbasket'?
  **A:** Grew lots of wheat.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Label all 13 colonies and take a quiz on Middle and Southern colony founders.

*How to teach:* Your child labels all 13 colonies on a blank map, then takes the quiz. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** Name the Southern Colonies.
  **A:** Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia.

**Assessment:** 13 colonies map and regions chart.

**Answer key:** 13 colonies map: all labeled. Regions chart: geography, economy, religion, founders accurate.

## Art & Music 5: Finishing and critiquing the landscape

**Prepare:** Have the landscape painting, fine brushes, black construction paper slightly larger than the painting, a ruler, and a glue stick. Print the four critique steps: Describe (what do you see?), Analyze (how are elements and principles used?), Interpret (what mood or meaning?), Judge (is it successful and why?).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking critique means criticism. A critique is a careful look at what works and what could be stronger, supported by evidence.
- Jumping straight to 'I like it / I don't.' Describe and analyze first so the judgment is backed by evidence.

### Day 1 (Monday): Refining
*Student:* Add final details and adjust values to strengthen depth in the landscape.

*How to teach:* Your child adds final details with a fine brush and darkens foreground values or lightens distant ones to strengthen depth.

- **Q:** Name two ways to make the landscape look deeper.
  **A:** Darken and add detail to the foreground; keep the background lighter, cooler and less detailed; overlap shapes.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Family critique
*Student:* Lead a family critique describing, analyzing, interpreting and judging your painting.

*How to teach:* Your child leads family through the four critique steps, asking each question aloud and listening to answers.

- **Q:** What happens in the 'interpret' step?
  **A:** You explain the mood or meaning of the artwork, supported by what you saw (e.g., 'The cool blues and soft light feel peaceful').

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Revision
*Student:* Make one revision based on critique feedback.

*How to teach:* Your child chooses one suggestion from the critique and revises (e.g., adds shadows, strengthens a focal point).

- **Q:** Why is revision part of the art process?
  **A:** Artists improve work by responding to feedback and fresh eyes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Matting
*Student:* Mount the painting on a black paper mat with clean edges.

*How to teach:* Center the painting on the black paper with even borders (measure all four sides) and glue neatly.

- **Q:** Why do you measure the borders of a mat?
  **A:** Even borders look neat and professional and keep the focus on the artwork.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write an artist statement and add the painting to your portfolio.

*How to teach:* Your child writes an artist statement (subject, techniques, mood, revision) and files the painting.

- **Q:** Which watercolor techniques did you use, and where?
  **A:** Answer varies; e.g., 'wet-on-wet in the sky, dry brush for grass texture, salt on the rocks.'

**Assessment:** Landscape painting rubric and artist statement.

**Answer key:** Landscape rubric (1–4 each): depth through foreground/middle/background; atmospheric perspective; at least two watercolor techniques used purposefully; craftsmanship (clean edges, neat mat). Artist statement proficient if it names techniques, describes mood with evidence, and explains the revision made after critique.

## PE 5: Small-sided soccer games and strategy

**Prepare:** Gather a ball, cones for a small field (about 20 × 15 m) and goals, and different-colored shirts. Invite siblings, neighbors or co-op friends for 2-v-2 or 3-v-3 if possible. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Everyone chasing the ball ('beehive soccer'). On offense, spread out to open space; on defense, mark a player.
- Defending by rushing in. Stay goal-side (between the attacker and your goal) and wait for a chance to take the ball.

### Day 1 (Monday): Keep-away
*Student:* Play 2-v-1 keep-away and discuss how to move to open space.

*How to teach:* Play 2-v-1 keep-away (you as the defender). Pause and ask where the open space is. Walk through the rules and boundaries first, then play; pause once to ask a quick strategy question ('Where is the open space?') and praise fair play and effort as much as skill.

- **Q:** Where should the player without the ball move?
  **A:** To open space where there is a clear passing line, away from the defender.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Defense
*Student:* Practice marking a player and staying goal-side in 1-v-1 drills.

*How to teach:* Your child defends you 1-v-1, staying goal-side, knees bent, side-on, and not diving in. Set clear boundaries and a safety reminder before starting, keep rounds short so everyone stays active, and finish by asking what strategy worked best.

- **Q:** What does 'goal-side' mean?
  **A:** Positioned between the attacker and the goal you're defending.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Small-sided game
*Student:* Play 2-v-2 or 3-v-3 soccer (family or park) applying offensive and defensive strategies.

*How to teach:* Play a small-sided game. Pause once each half to ask where teammates should be. Walk through the rules and boundaries first, then play; pause once to ask a quick strategy question ('Where is the open space?') and praise fair play and effort as much as skill.

- **Q:** Name one offensive and one defensive strategy you used.
  **A:** Offense: spread out/move to open space/support the ball carrier. Defense: mark a player/stay goal-side/close passing lanes.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Sportsmanship
*Student:* Play a game where players self-officiate and resolve disputes with rock-paper-scissors.

*How to teach:* Play with players calling their own fouls and out-of-bounds; any disagreement is settled quickly by rock-paper-scissors.

- **Q:** Why is self-officiating a test of sportsmanship?
  **A:** Players must be honest and fair even when it costs their team, and solve disputes calmly.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 3 assessment
*Student:* Play a game while a parent uses a skills and strategy checklist, then reflect on sportsmanship.

*How to teach:* Play a game while you mark the skill and strategy checklist; afterwards your child writes 3–4 sentences on their sportsmanship.

- **Q:** Give an example of good sportsmanship in soccer.
  **A:** Helping up a fallen opponent, admitting a ball went out off you, shaking hands, or encouraging teammates.

**Assessment:** Game-play checklist and sportsmanship reflection.

**Answer key:** Game-play checklist (✓ observed): dribbles with control, passes to an open teammate, moves to space without the ball, stays goal-side on defense, shoots when open, follows rules/calls honestly. Proficient = 4 of 6. Reflection rubric: 4 = specific examples of fair play and a goal for improvement; 3 = specific example; 2 = general statement; 1 = missing.

## Health 5: Healthy body image

**Prepare:** Have the health journal ready. Read the KidsHealth article on dieting for kids ahead of time. Model positive talk about bodies at home this week. Watch for signs of serious body worries (skipping meals, intense exercise, negative self-talk) and talk with your pediatrician if you see them.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking there is one 'right' body type. Healthy bodies come in many shapes and sizes, largely shaped by genetics.
- Thinking dieting is healthy for kids. Growing bodies need regular balanced meals; restrictive dieting can harm growth and energy.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is body image?
*Student:* Discuss what body image means and write three things your body lets you do that you appreciate.

*How to teach:* Discuss body image; your child writes three things their body lets them do. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What is body image?
  **A:** How you see, think and feel about your body.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Many shapes and sizes
*Student:* Look at family photos to see how genetics shapes body type and list factors that affect size.

*How to teach:* Look at family photos to notice inherited traits; list factors affecting size (genetics, growth stage, activity, nutrition).

- **Q:** Name two factors that affect body size.
  **A:** Genetics and growth stage (also nutrition, activity, sleep).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dieting dangers
*Student:* Read a KidsHealth article on dieting and list why skipping meals or fad diets harm growing kids.

*How to teach:* Read about dieting; list harms of skipping meals and fad diets. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** Why are fad diets harmful for kids?
  **A:** They cut out nutrients needed for growth, lower energy and focus, and can lead to unhealthy eating patterns.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Kind words
*Student:* Practice replacing body-shaming comments with respectful statements in 5 scenario cards.

*How to teach:* Replace body-shaming comments in 5 scenarios with respectful ones. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** Replace: 'You're too skinny to play.'
  **A:** 'Come play with us!' — or say nothing about bodies and focus on the person.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph on healthy body image and answer a 5-question check.

*How to teach:* Write a paragraph on healthy body image and answer the check. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** How can you build a positive body image?
  **A:** Focus on what your body can do, avoid comparing, use kind self-talk, and limit media that makes you feel bad.

**Assessment:** Paragraph and quick check.

**Answer key:** Quick check: 1) body image = how you see/feel about your body 2) genetics shapes body type 3) dieting harms growing kids 4) respectful alternative 5) one strategy for positive body image. Paragraph rubric: 4 = defines body image, explains genetics/diversity, gives two strategies; 3 = one strategy; 2 = vague; 1 = missing.
