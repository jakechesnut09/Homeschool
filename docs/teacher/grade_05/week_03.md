# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 3

## ELA 5: 'Thank You, Ma'am' by Langston Hughes; drafting narratives

**Prepare:** Print 'Thank You, Ma'am' (CommonLit) and read it first — about 4 pages. Make a dialogue punctuation anchor chart: “Hello,” she said. / She said, “Hello.” / “Why,” he asked, “are you here?”

**Watch for:**
- Putting the comma outside the closing quotation mark. In American English commas and periods go inside.
- Starting a new paragraph only for each new sentence. Start a new paragraph each time the speaker changes.
- Writing a theme as one word ('kindness'). A theme is a full statement: 'Kindness can change a person more than punishment.'

### Day 1 (Monday): Theme from actions
*Student:* Read 'Thank You, Ma'am' and track what Mrs. Jones does and says at three points; write a theme statement supported by those actions.

*How to teach:* Read the story aloud. Your child records what Mrs. Jones does at three points (when Roger grabs her purse; at her home; at the end) and writes a theme statement supported by those actions.

- **Q:** What does Mrs. Jones do instead of calling the police?
  **A:** She drags Roger to her home, has him wash his face, feeds him, and gives him ten dollars for the blue suede shoes he wanted.
- **Q:** Write a theme statement.
  **A:** Example: Treating someone with trust and kindness can teach more than punishment can.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Dialogue craft
*Student:* Study how Hughes uses dialogue to show character; punctuate 10 lines of dialogue and learn interjections (Oh!, Well,).

*How to teach:* Read aloud a stretch of dialogue and ask what it shows about each character. Your child punctuates 10 dialogue lines and adds interjections (Oh! Well,).

- **Q:** Punctuate: you could have asked me she said
  **A:** “You could have asked me,” she said.
- **Q:** Add an interjection: That hurts!
  **A:** “Ouch! That hurts!” (or Oh, that hurts!)

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Drafting the lead
*Student:* Draft the opening of the personal narrative three ways (action, dialogue, sensory description) and choose the strongest.

*How to teach:* Your child drafts three different openings for the personal narrative: action, dialogue, sensory detail. Read them aloud and choose the strongest.

- **Q:** Which lead type is this: “Don't let go!” Dad yelled.
  **A:** Dialogue lead.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Drafting the middle
*Student:* Draft the rest of the narrative, including at least 4 lines of dialogue and commas in a series of sensory details.

*How to teach:* Your child drafts the rest of the narrative including at least 4 lines of correctly punctuated dialogue and a series of sensory details with commas. Read the draft back together and put a check beside each dialogue line that shows a new speaker on a new line.

- **Q:** Punctuate the series: The kitchen smelled of cinnamon apples and burnt toast.
  **A:** The kitchen smelled of cinnamon, apples, and burnt toast. (Serial comma optional but consistent.)

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Discuss theme with a partner using 'I think... because in the text...' stems, then take a quiz on dialogue punctuation and commas in a series.

*How to teach:* Discuss theme with stems ('I think… because in the text…'), then give a 10-item dialogue and series-comma quiz. Listen for a 'because' tied to a specific action of Mrs. Jones, then grade the quiz with the anchor chart beside you.

- **Q:** Why does Roger say only 'Thank you, ma'am' at the end?
  **A:** He is so moved by her kindness that he can't find words for what he wants to say; the text says he wanted to say more but couldn't.

**Assessment:** Theme statement with evidence; complete narrative draft; punctuation quiz.

**Answer key:** Theme statement: proficient = complete-sentence theme (e.g., kindness and trust can change a person) supported by two of Mrs. Jones's actions. Narrative draft: complete beginning/middle/end, 4+ dialogue lines. Punctuation quiz key: commas/periods inside quotation marks; capital letter starting each quote; new paragraph per speaker; commas between 3+ items in a series; interjection followed by ! or ,.

## Math 5: Reading, writing and comparing decimals to thousandths

**Prepare:** Thousandths grid paper (10×10 squares each split into 10 strips, or print 1,000-square grids), index cards, place value chart. Write a list of Olympic-style race times (e.g., 9.58, 9.63, 9.69, 9.79 seconds).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 0.35 < 0.305 because 305 > 35. Line up digits by place value or annex zeros: 0.350 vs 0.305.
- Using 'and' for whole numbers (three hundred and five). 'And' only marks the decimal point.
- Thinking 0.4 and 0.400 are different amounts. Shade the grids to see the same area.

### Day 1 (Monday): Three forms
*Student:* Convert 12 decimals among standard form, word form (three and fifty-two thousandths) and expanded form including fraction form like 3 x 1 + 5 x (1/100).

*How to teach:* Model 3.052 in all three forms: standard, word ('three and fifty-two thousandths') and expanded (3 × 1 + 5 × (1/100) + 2 × (1/1,000)). The child converts 12 decimals, checking that 'and' appears only at the decimal point.

- **Q:** Write 3.052 in word form and expanded form.
  **A:** Three and fifty-two thousandths; 3 × 1 + 0 × (1/10) + 5 × (1/100) + 2 × (1/1,000).
- **Q:** Write 'forty and seven hundredths' in standard form.
  **A:** 40.07

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Modeling thousandths
*Student:* Shade 1,000-square grids to show 0.4, 0.40 and 0.400 and explain in writing why these are equivalent.

*How to teach:* Shade 4 columns (0.4), then 40 small squares (0.40), then 400 tiny pieces (0.400) on three grids. The child writes why they are equal: the same area is shaded; only the size of the pieces changes.

- **Q:** Are 0.4, 0.40 and 0.400 equal? Why?
  **A:** Yes. 4 tenths = 40 hundredths = 400 thousandths; the shaded area is identical.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Comparing decimals
*Student:* Compare 20 pairs of decimals (like 0.305 vs 0.35) by lining up place values and record with symbols.

*How to teach:* Line up 0.305 and 0.35 by place value, annexing a zero (0.350), and compare from the left. The child compares 20 pairs with <, >, =.

- **Q:** Compare 0.305 __ 0.35.
  **A:** 0.305 < 0.35 (3 tenths each, but 0 hundredths < 5 hundredths).
- **Q:** Compare 2.09 __ 2.1.
  **A:** 2.09 < 2.1

### Day 4 (Thursday): Ordering game
*Student:* Play 'Decimal Line-Up': draw 5 index cards with decimals and order them least to greatest on a number line, then order Olympic race times.

*How to teach:* Play 'Decimal Line-Up': draw 5 cards and place them on a number line, explaining each placement. Then order the race times from fastest (least) to slowest.

- **Q:** Order least to greatest: 0.5, 0.45, 0.405, 0.054.
  **A:** 0.054, 0.405, 0.45, 0.5
- **Q:** Race times 9.69, 9.58, 9.63: who won?
  **A:** 9.58 — the least time is the fastest.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz writing decimals in three forms and comparing/ordering 10 sets, then explain one error a student might make.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz below, then have the child describe one error a student might make (e.g., thinking longer means larger) and how to avoid it. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** Write 0.608 in expanded form.
  **A:** 6 × (1/10) + 8 × (1/1,000), or 0.6 + 0.008.

**Assessment:** Quiz on decimal forms and comparisons; observation of ordering game reasoning.

**Answer key:** Suggested key: 1) 'five and twenty-three thousandths' = 5.023 2) 0.716 in words = seven hundred sixteen thousandths 3) 8.409 expanded = 8 × 1 + 4 × (1/10) + 9 × (1/1,000) 4) 2 × 10 + 3 × (1/100) = 20.03 5) 0.7 > 0.69 6) 1.250 = 1.25 7) 3.08 < 3.8 8) 0.099 < 0.1 9) Order 4.5, 4.05, 4.505, 4.055 → 4.05, 4.055, 4.5, 4.505 10) Order 0.3, 0.033, 0.303 → 0.033, 0.3, 0.303. Error explanation (proficient): names a real error such as 'more digits means bigger' and explains lining up place values. Ordering game observation: child compares from the greatest place first.

## Science 5: Properties of materials

**Prepare:** Collect samples: aluminum foil, steel paper clip, plastic spoon, wooden stick, rubber band, glass marble, salt, baking soda; a magnet, flashlight, simple circuit kit (battery, bulb, wires), metal/plastic/wooden spoons and hot water (adult handles). Pre-test the circuit.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all metals are magnetic. Only some (iron, steel, nickel, cobalt) are; aluminum foil is not.
- Thinking 'heavy' is a property used to identify a material regardless of size. Use properties like hardness, conductivity and magnetism.
- Thinking metal spoons feel colder because they are colder. They conduct heat away from your hand faster.

### Day 1 (Monday): Describing properties
*Student:* Observe 8 material samples and record color, texture, hardness (scratch test) and flexibility in a table.

*How to teach:* Your child observes each sample and records color, texture, hardness (does it scratch with a nail?) and flexibility in a table.

- **Q:** Which is harder: the glass marble or the wooden stick? How can you test it?
  **A:** The glass marble; a scratch test with a nail scratches wood but not glass.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Magnetism and reflectivity
*Student:* Test each sample with a magnet and a flashlight beam and add results to the table.

*How to teach:* Test each sample with a magnet and with a flashlight beam (shiny/reflective or not). Add results to the table.

- **Q:** Which samples did the magnet attract?
  **A:** Only the steel paper clip; aluminum foil, plastic, wood, rubber, glass, salt and baking soda are not magnetic.
- **Q:** Which reflect light best?
  **A:** Aluminum foil (shiny metal) and the glass marble.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Electrical conductivity
*Student:* Build a simple circuit with a battery and bulb and test which samples complete the circuit.

*How to teach:* Build a circuit with a gap; place each sample in the gap. If the bulb lights, it is a conductor. Record results.

- **Q:** Which samples completed the circuit?
  **A:** Aluminum foil and the paper clip (metals); plastic, wood, rubber, glass and dry salt are insulators.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Thermal conductivity
*Student:* Place metal, plastic and wooden spoons in hot water and compare which handle warms fastest after 3 minutes.

*How to teach:* Stand metal, plastic and wooden spoons in hot water (adult pours). After 3 minutes your child carefully touches each handle and ranks warmth.

- **Q:** Which handle warms fastest and why?
  **A:** The metal spoon, because metal is a good thermal conductor; plastic and wood are insulators.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Use the property table to identify 3 'mystery' materials and explain which properties gave them away.

*How to teach:* Give your child 3 'mystery' samples. They use the property table to identify each and explain which properties gave it away.

- **Q:** A shiny, bendable material conducts electricity but isn't attracted by a magnet. What could it be?
  **A:** Aluminum foil.

**Assessment:** Property data table and mystery-material identification with explanations.

**Answer key:** Property table complete for 8 samples across 7 properties. Mystery identification: correct material plus 2 supporting properties each (e.g., paper clip = magnetic + conducts; plastic spoon = insulator + not magnetic + flexible; glass marble = hard, transparent, insulator).

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Peoples of the Great Plains and the Eastern Woodlands

**Prepare:** Find an excerpt of the Great Law of Peace and an image of the Hiawatha Belt (NMAI Native Knowledge 360). Choose a traditional Haudenosaunee or Lakota story from a reliable source.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Native nations had no organized government. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy had a written-in-wampum constitution and councils.
- Thinking Plains peoples always lived on horseback. Horses arrived with the Spanish; they transformed Plains life after the 1600s.
- Mixing up a longhouse and a tipi.

### Day 1 (Monday): Plains nations
*Student:* Read about the Lakota and other Plains nations and diagram the many uses of the bison.

*How to teach:* Read about the Lakota and the bison. Your child draws a bison and labels uses for each part (meat, hide, bones, sinew, horns).

- **Q:** Name 4 uses of the bison.
  **A:** Meat for food, hides for tipis and clothing, bones for tools, sinew for thread and bowstrings, horns for cups and spoons.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Woodlands life
*Student:* Read about Eastern Woodlands peoples (Haudenosaunee, Cherokee) and compare a longhouse to a tipi.

*How to teach:* Read about Haudenosaunee and Cherokee life. Your child makes a Venn diagram comparing a longhouse and a tipi.

- **Q:** How is a longhouse different from a tipi?
  **A:** A longhouse is a long, permanent bark-covered home for several related families in a farming village; a tipi is a portable cone of poles and hides for moving with the bison.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Primary source: Great Law of Peace
*Student:* Analyze an excerpt of the Great Law of Peace and the Hiawatha wampum belt and explain how the five (later six) nations governed together.

*How to teach:* Read the excerpt and look at the Hiawatha Belt. Explain that the five nations (Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca) united under the Great Law, later joined by the Tuscarora.

- **Q:** What does the Hiawatha Belt show?
  **A:** Five symbols linked by a line — the five nations joined in peace, with the Onondaga (Great Tree of Peace) at the center.
- **Q:** How did the Confederacy govern?
  **A:** A Grand Council of chiefs from each nation, chosen by clan mothers, made decisions by discussion and agreement.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Folklore
*Student:* Read a Haudenosaunee or Lakota traditional story and identify what it teaches about values or nature.

*How to teach:* Read a traditional story aloud. Your child identifies what it teaches about values or nature.

- **Q:** What do many traditional stories teach?
  **A:** Respect for nature, living in balance, and values like generosity and courage.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on Plains and Woodlands peoples and explain one feature of Haudenosaunee government.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Your child explains one feature of Haudenosaunee government in writing.

- **Q:** What role did clan mothers play?
  **A:** They chose and could remove the chiefs who sat on the council.

**Assessment:** Quiz and primary-source analysis sheet.

**Answer key:** Quiz key (suggested): 1) Plains peoples relied on the bison 2) tipis were portable 3) longhouses housed several families 4) Haudenosaunee = Five (later Six) Nations 5) Great Law of Peace united them 6) clan mothers chose chiefs 7) Cherokee were Eastern Woodlands farmers. Primary-source sheet: describes belt symbols and explains unity.

## Art & Music 5: Perspective and space

**Prepare:** Gather a ruler, pencil, eraser and sketchbook. Practice one-point perspective yourself once first: a horizontal horizon line, one dot (vanishing point) on it, and lines from box corners to that dot.

**Watch for:**
- Placing the vanishing point off the horizon line. In one-point perspective, the vanishing point always sits on the horizon line.
- Drawing all receding lines to different points. All lines going 'into' the picture meet at the single vanishing point; vertical lines stay vertical and horizontal lines stay horizontal.

### Day 1 (Monday): Showing depth
*Student:* Draw a landscape that uses overlapping, size change and placement to show foreground, middle ground and background.

*How to teach:* Your child draws a landscape using overlapping, size change (far = smaller) and placement (far = higher on the page) to show foreground, middle ground and background.

- **Q:** Name three ways to show depth without perspective lines.
  **A:** Overlapping, size (farther objects smaller), and placement (farther objects higher on the page); also less detail and lighter color in the distance.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): One-point perspective
*Student:* Draw 3D boxes in one-point perspective with a horizon line and vanishing point.

*How to teach:* Draw a horizon line and vanishing point. Your child draws squares above, below and on the line, connects corners to the vanishing point, and closes the boxes.

- **Q:** If you draw a box below the horizon line, which side can you see?
  **A:** The top of the box.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Perspective road
*Student:* Draw a road or hallway disappearing to a vanishing point with trees or doors along it.

*How to teach:* Draw a road or hallway narrowing to the vanishing point; add trees or doors that get smaller as they go back.

- **Q:** Why do trees along the road get smaller toward the vanishing point?
  **A:** Objects appear smaller the farther they are from the viewer.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Place of significance
*Student:* Begin a perspective drawing of your street, room or a favorite place.

*How to teach:* Your child begins a perspective drawing of a meaningful place (street, room, favorite spot), placing the horizon at eye level.

- **Q:** Where should the horizon line be in your drawing?
  **A:** At your eye level.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Finish the place drawing and label its horizon line and vanishing point.

*How to teach:* Finish the drawing; label the horizon line and vanishing point. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What is a vanishing point?
  **A:** The point on the horizon line where parallel lines going away from the viewer appear to meet.

**Assessment:** One-point perspective drawing of a personally significant place.

**Answer key:** Perspective drawing rubric: 4 = horizon line and vanishing point labeled, all receding lines converge correctly, objects shrink with distance, foreground/middle/background clear, place meaningful; 3 = minor line errors; 2 = inconsistent perspective; 1 = no perspective.

## PE 5: Muscular strength, endurance and flexibility

**Prepare:** Cut a cardboard curl-up strip 4.5 inches wide (FITNESSGRAM size for ages 10+). Gather a mat or towel, a ruler/yardstick, and a box with a ruler on top for sit-and-reach. Print or write a form checklist for each test. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Pulling on the neck during curl-ups. Arms stay straight at the sides with palms sliding along the mat; the head comes up because the stomach muscles curl the shoulders.
- Arching the back too high in the trunk lift. Lift slowly to no more than 12 inches; higher is not better and can strain the back.
- Sagging hips in push-ups. Keep a straight line from head to heels like a plank.

### Day 1 (Monday): Curl-up form
*Student:* Learn FITNESSGRAM curl-up form with a cadence and practice 3 sets.

*How to teach:* Model the curl-up: knees bent, feet flat, arms straight with fingertips at the strip's near edge, curl until fingertips reach the far edge, then back so the head touches the mat. Use the cadence of one curl-up every 3 seconds; do 3 sets of 10.

- **Q:** How far do your fingertips slide on each curl-up?
  **A:** Across the 4.5-inch strip, from the near edge to the far edge.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Push-up form
*Student:* Learn the 90-degree push-up and practice with modified versions (wall, knee) to build up.

*How to teach:* Model the 90-degree push-up: hands under shoulders, body straight, lower until elbows bend to 90 degrees, push up. Practice wall and knee push-ups if needed, then try full ones at a 3-second cadence.

- **Q:** How low do you go in the FITNESSGRAM push-up?
  **A:** Until the elbows bend to a 90-degree angle (upper arms parallel to the floor), keeping the body straight.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Trunk lift and flexibility
*Student:* Practice the trunk lift and back-saver sit-and-reach and shoulder stretch with correct technique.

*How to teach:* Trunk lift: face down, hands under thighs, slowly lift the upper body up to 12 inches and hold while you measure chin-to-floor. Sit-and-reach: one leg straight, the other knee bent, reach forward four times and hold the fourth. Measure each side.

- **Q:** What is the maximum score counted for the trunk lift, and why?
  **A:** 12 inches — lifting higher than that is not needed and could strain the lower back.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Strength circuit
*Student:* Complete a 6-station circuit (squats, planks, curl-ups, push-ups, wall sit, lunges) 30 seconds each.

*How to teach:* Run a 6-station circuit (squats, plank, curl-ups, push-ups, wall sit, lunges), 30 seconds each with 15 seconds rest. Watch form, not speed.

- **Q:** Which two stations mostly build core endurance?
  **A:** Plank and curl-ups.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Watch a partner perform each test and give feedback on form using a checklist.

*How to teach:* Your child watches you perform each test and marks the form checklist, then tells you one thing done well and one fix.

- **Q:** During a push-up, my hips sag toward the floor. What feedback do you give?
  **A:** Tighten your stomach and keep a straight line from head to heels.

**Assessment:** Form checklist for each fitness test item.

**Answer key:** Form checklist (one point per cue): Curl-up — knees bent ~140°, feet flat, arms straight, fingers slide across strip, head returns to mat, 3-second cadence. Push-up — hands under shoulders, straight body, elbows to 90°, full extension, cadence. Trunk lift — slow lift, eyes on a spot on the floor, ≤12 in, no bouncing. Sit-and-reach — one knee bent, straight leg flat, hands stacked, four reaches, hold the last. Proficient = performs and identifies at least 80% of cues.

## Health 5: Growing and changing; reliable health information

**Prepare:** Collect baby/childhood photos and any recorded heights (doctor records, growth chart, doorframe). Pick one health topic (e.g., sunscreen) and find a .gov page, a product ad and a social-media post about it. Write a 4-question reliability checklist: Who wrote it? Are they an expert? Are they selling something? Is it current and supported by evidence?

**Watch for:**
- Believing information is true because it's online or has many likes. Check who wrote it, their expertise, purpose and date.
- Thinking ads are neutral information. Ads are written to sell a product.

### Day 1 (Monday): Growth timeline
*Student:* Make a timeline of your own growth using family photos and recorded heights, and note skills gained at each stage.

*How to teach:* Your child builds a timeline with photos, heights and skills gained (walking, reading, riding a bike).

- **Q:** Name a skill you gained in early childhood and one in middle childhood.
  **A:** Example: walking/talking (early); reading chapter books, riding a bike (middle).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Life cycle
*Student:* Compare stages of the human life cycle (infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood, older adulthood) in a 5-column chart.

*How to teach:* Make a 5-column chart: infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood, older adulthood with ages and main changes.

- **Q:** What stage begins with puberty?
  **A:** Adolescence (roughly ages 10–19).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Is it reliable?
*Student:* Compare a .gov health page, a product ad and a social-media post on the same topic using a 4-question reliability checklist.

*How to teach:* Apply the checklist to the three sources and rank them. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** Why is a .gov page usually more reliable than a product ad?
  **A:** Government health sites are written by experts, reviewed, and not trying to sell a product; ads aim to persuade you to buy.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Trusted adults
*Student:* List at least four trusted adults and two valid sources you could use for health questions and discuss with a parent.

*How to teach:* Your child lists four trusted adults (parent, doctor, school nurse, relative) and two valid sources (e.g., KidsHealth.org, CDC.gov). Discuss together.

- **Q:** What makes an adult 'trusted'?
  **A:** They are safe, care about you, listen, give accurate help, and you can go to them with problems.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a source-sorting activity (reliable/unreliable) and write a paragraph on how you will check health information.

*How to teach:* Sort 8 source cards as reliable or unreliable, then write a paragraph on how to check health information.

- **Q:** A website selling a 'miracle vitamin' says it cures colds. Reliable? Why?
  **A:** No — it is trying to sell, makes an unsupported claim, and isn't from a medical expert.

**Assessment:** Source-sorting activity and paragraph.

**Answer key:** Source sort: reliable — government (.gov) health agencies, major children's hospitals and medical organizations, KidsHealth, a doctor/school nurse; unreliable — product ads, anonymous social-media posts, sites selling 'cures,' undated blogs. Paragraph rubric: 4 = names at least three checks (author/expertise, purpose/selling, evidence, date) and a trusted adult; 3 = two checks; 2 = one; 1 = none.
