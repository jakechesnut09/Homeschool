# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 9

## ELA 5: Hatchet chapters 18–19 and epilogue; literary essay

**Prepare:** Preview chapters 18–19 and the epilogue of Hatchet. Prepare an essay organizer (thesis, 3 reasons, quotations). Write the Unit 2 test.

**Watch for:**
- A thesis that is a fact ('Brian is in Hatchet'). A thesis is an arguable claim.
- Body paragraphs with quotes but no explanation. Use quote sandwich: introduce, quote, explain.
- Forgetting transitions between paragraphs.

### Day 1 (Monday): Finishing the novel
*Student:* Read chapters 18–19 and the epilogue; discuss how the epilogue changes our view of Brian's experience.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 18–19 and the epilogue. Discuss how the epilogue (facts about his rescue and life afterward) changes our view of his experience.

- **Q:** How is Brian rescued?
  **A:** He retrieves the survival pack from the plane; it contains an emergency transmitter he switches on, and a pilot lands on the lake to rescue him.
- **Q:** How long was Brian alone?
  **A:** 54 days.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Essay planning
*Student:* Choose a prompt (How does Brian change?) and fill an organizer with a thesis, three reasons and quotations.

*How to teach:* Your child chooses a prompt and fills the organizer: thesis, three reasons, quotations for each. Check that the thesis is arguable and that each reason has at least one quotation with a page number before drafting.

- **Q:** Model thesis for 'How does Brian change?'
  **A:** Brian changes from a frightened, self-pitying city boy into a patient, observant survivor who solves problems himself.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Drafting
*Student:* Draft a five-paragraph literary essay with an introduction, three body paragraphs and a conclusion.

*How to teach:* Draft the five-paragraph essay; introduction with thesis, three body paragraphs, conclusion. Write one body paragraph together as a model if needed; your child drafts the rest. Expect this to take the full lesson.

- **Q:** What goes in a conclusion?
  **A:** A restated thesis in new words and a final thought about why the change matters.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revising and editing
*Student:* Revise for transition words and evidence explanation; edit for perfect-tense verbs and spelling.

*How to teach:* Revise for transitions and explanation; edit for perfect tenses and spelling. Read the essay aloud together; your child adds a transition at the start of each body paragraph and one more sentence of explanation after each quotation.

- **Q:** Name 3 transitions for body paragraphs.
  **A:** First, In addition, Finally (also: For example, However, As a result).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 assessment
*Student:* Take the Unit 2 assessment on Hatchet (character, theme, figurative language, roots) and submit the final essay.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test and collect the final essay. Give the test without help, then score the essay separately with the rubric and return both with one specific compliment and one goal.

- **Q:** Does Brian tell his father about the Secret?
  **A:** The epilogue says he never told his father.

**Assessment:** Literary essay rubric; Unit 2 test.

**Answer key:** Essay rubric (4 levels): 4 = arguable thesis, three well-developed body paragraphs each with a quotation and explanation, transitions, conclusion, few errors; 3 = all parts present with some thin explanation; 2 = weak thesis or missing evidence; 1 = incomplete. Unit 2 test key (suggested): 1) Brian is going to visit his father 2) the hatchet was from his mother 3) fire from sparks on rock 4) the low point follows the plane passing overhead 5) the moose and tornado destroy his shelter 6) the tornado lifts the plane's tail into view 7) a transmitter brings rescue after 54 days 8) theme: resilience/learning from mistakes 9) simile vs metaphor labeled 10) spect/port/struct/rupt meanings.

## Math 5: Adding and subtracting decimals

**Prepare:** Hundredths grids, play money, a store flyer or printed online prices. Set a $50 budget for the shopping scenarios.

**Watch for:**
- Lining up the right-most digits (4.7 + 12.35 as 4.7 under 2.35). Line up decimal points and write 4.70.
- Subtracting 8 − 2.36 as 2.36 − 8 or getting 6.36. Write 8.00 and regroup.
- Forgetting to carry when tenths sum past 10. Use grids: 10 tenths make a whole.

### Day 1 (Monday): Modeling decimal addition
*Student:* Shade hundredths grids to add 10 pairs like 0.46 + 0.38 and record with a place value equation.

*How to teach:* Shade 0.46 in one color and 0.38 in another on a hundredths grid, count the total, then write 0.46 + 0.38 = 0.84 with a place value equation. The child models 10 pairs.

- **Q:** 0.46 + 0.38 = ?
  **A:** 0.84 (4 tenths + 3 tenths = 7 tenths; 6 + 8 hundredths = 14 hundredths = 1 tenth 4 hundredths).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Adding with regrouping
*Student:* Add 15 decimals including different numbers of places (4.7 + 12.35) by lining up place values and using zero placeholders.

*How to teach:* Line up decimal points, annex zeros so places match, and add with regrouping. The child solves 15 problems.

- **Q:** 4.7 + 12.35 = ?
  **A:** 4.70 + 12.35 = 17.05
- **Q:** 9.68 + 3.5 = ?
  **A:** 13.18

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Subtracting decimals
*Student:* Subtract 15 problems like 8 - 2.36 using models and then the written method, checking by adding.

*How to teach:* Model 8 − 2.36 by writing 8.00 and regrouping, then check by adding 5.64 + 2.36 = 8. The child solves 15 problems with an addition check.

- **Q:** 8 − 2.36 = ?
  **A:** 5.64
- **Q:** 12.4 − 7.85 = ?
  **A:** 4.55

### Day 4 (Thursday): Shopping problems
*Student:* Use a store flyer to plan purchases under $50, adding prices and subtracting from the budget for 5 scenarios.

*How to teach:* Give the child the flyer and $50. For each of 5 scenarios, list items, add prices and subtract from $50 to find the change.

- **Q:** You buy items for $12.49, $8.75 and $19.99. How much is left from $50?
  **A:** Total $41.23; change $8.77.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a 12-problem quiz on decimal addition and subtraction including 2 word problems.

*How to teach:* Give the 12-problem quiz independently, including 2 word problems; check work by estimating. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** A runner ran 3.75 km then 2.6 km. How far in all?
  **A:** 6.35 km

**Assessment:** Quiz on decimal addition and subtraction; budget scenarios checked for accuracy.

**Answer key:** Suggested key: 1) 0.27 + 0.65 = 0.92 2) 3.4 + 5.89 = 9.29 3) 12.06 + 7.9 = 19.96 4) 0.8 + 0.25 = 1.05 5) 15 + 2.75 = 17.75 6) 6.3 − 2.8 = 3.5 7) 10 − 4.27 = 5.73 8) 7.05 − 3.6 = 3.45 9) 20.1 − 9.99 = 10.11 10) 5.5 − 0.55 = 4.95 11) Word: $20 − $13.47 = $6.53 12) Word: 1.25 L + 0.8 L = 2.05 L. Budget scenarios: totals correct and stay at or under $50.

## Science 5: Conservation of weight in reactions

**Prepare:** Plastic bottle, balloon, baking soda, vinegar, kitchen scale, cup, graph paper. Check the rust jars from last week. Write the Unit 2 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking mass is destroyed in a reaction. Particles rearrange; the gas escaped from the open cup.
- Thinking the balloon gas weighs nothing. It's matter with mass.
- Expecting the closed system to weigh exactly the same on every scale. A puffed balloon can push up slightly on the air, so expect tiny differences.

### Day 1 (Monday): Open reaction
*Student:* Weigh vinegar and baking soda in an open cup before and after reacting and record the weight change.

*How to teach:* Weigh vinegar and baking soda separately in an open cup, combine and weigh after bubbling stops. Record the change.

- **Q:** Before: 150 g. After the open-cup reaction: 146 g. Where did 4 g go?
  **A:** Carbon dioxide gas escaped into the air.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Closed reaction
*Student:* Repeat the reaction in a bottle sealed with a balloon and compare the before and after weights.

*How to teach:* Repeat in a bottle sealed with a balloon holding the baking soda. Weigh before and after tipping it in.

- **Q:** What do you expect the closed system to weigh after reacting?
  **A:** About the same as before — the gas is trapped in the balloon.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Explaining the difference
*Student:* Graph both trials and explain why weight seemed to decrease in the open cup using particles of gas escaping.

*How to teach:* Your child graphs both trials and explains the difference using gas particles escaping. Draw paired bars for before/after in each trial. Ask: 'Did the matter disappear or go somewhere?' to guide the gas explanation.

- **Q:** Why does the open cup seem to lose weight?
  **A:** The gas produced (made of particles with mass) leaves the cup, so the scale no longer measures it.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rust results
*Student:* Examine the rust jars, describe evidence of a new substance, and connect the result to particles rearranging.

*How to teach:* Examine the rust jars. Your child describes the evidence of a new substance and connects it to particles rearranging.

- **Q:** What is the evidence of rust?
  **A:** Reddish-brown flaky material forms — a new substance made when iron combines with oxygen and water.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 2 assessment
*Student:* Take the Unit 2 assessment on conservation and new substances and write a final CER.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 2 test and the final CER. Give the test independently, then the CER on conservation in reactions using the open vs. closed data.

- **Q:** Is mass conserved in a chemical reaction?
  **A:** Yes — the total mass of the substances before equals the total after, when nothing escapes.

**Assessment:** Unit 2 test; CER on conservation in chemical reactions.

**Answer key:** Unit 2 test key (suggested): 1) mass is conserved when melting/freezing 2) 3 signs of a chemical reaction: gas, color change, new solid/temperature change 3) salt water is a solution 4) recovering salt by evaporation 5) open cup loses mass because gas escapes 6) closed system keeps mass constant 7) rust is a new substance 8) dissolving is not a chemical reaction. CER rubric: 4 = clear, accurate claim; specific evidence with numbers from the data; reasoning that links evidence to the science idea (particles, conservation, etc.); 3 = accurate claim and evidence, reasoning partial; 2 = claim with vague evidence; 1 = claim only or inaccurate.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Jamestown and the Virginia Colony

**Prepare:** John Smith's 1612 map of Virginia (Library of Congress); photos of Jamestown.

**Watch for:**
- Believing the Pocahontas 'rescue' story is proven. It comes only from John Smith's later account.
- Thinking Jamestown succeeded quickly.
- Thinking Pocahontas was an adult in 1607. She was about 11.

### Day 1 (Monday): Founding Jamestown
*Student:* Read about the Virginia Company's goals and the 1607 founding and list the problems settlers faced.

*How to teach:* Read about the Virginia Company (an investment company hoping for gold and profit) and the 1607 landing. Your child lists at least 5 problems the settlers faced.

- **Q:** What problems did settlers face?
  **A:** Swampy land, disease, bad water, hunger (the 'Starving Time' 1609–10), conflict with the Powhatan.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Primary source: John Smith's map
*Student:* Analyze John Smith's 1612 map of Virginia, noting Powhatan towns and what the map reveals about the land.

*How to teach:* Look at Smith's 1612 map together; note that it is oriented with west at the top. Your child circles Powhatan towns and writes what the map shows about who already lived there.

- **Q:** What does the map show?
  **A:** Many Powhatan towns along rivers — the land was already populated.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Powhatan and Pocahontas
*Student:* Read about the Powhatan Confederacy and the real Pocahontas and separate fact from legend.

*How to teach:* Read about the Powhatan Confederacy and Pocahontas. Your child sorts statements into 'documented fact' and 'legend' (e.g., her marriage to John Rolfe is fact; the rescue story comes only from Smith's later writing).

- **Q:** What is known about Pocahontas?
  **A:** She was Powhatan's daughter, was captured by the English, married John Rolfe in 1614 and died in England in 1617.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Tobacco and Anglicanism
*Student:* Explain how tobacco saved the colony and describe the role of the Anglican Church in Virginia.

*How to teach:* Explain how John Rolfe's sweet tobacco (about 1612) became a cash crop and how the Anglican Church was the official church. Ask: what new problems did tobacco create?

- **Q:** How did tobacco help?
  **A:** Rolfe's tobacco became a profitable cash crop.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a cause-and-effect chart of Jamestown's struggles and successes.

*How to teach:* Your child makes a cause-and-effect chart with 4+ rows from Jamestown's struggles and successes. Check each effect truly follows its cause.

- **Q:** Effect of tobacco?
  **A:** Profit, need for more land and labor, conflict with the Powhatan.

**Assessment:** Map analysis sheet and cause-effect chart.

**Answer key:** Map analysis: notes towns, rivers, purpose. Cause-effect chart: 4+ accurate pairs.

## Art & Music 5: Color theory

**Prepare:** Gather tempera or watercolors in red, yellow, blue, white and black; brushes, water cups, a palette (or paper plate) and paper. Draw a 12-section circle for the color wheel.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking black and white are on the color wheel. They change value (tints and shades) but aren't hues on the wheel.
- Muddy colors from dirty brushes. Rinse and blot between colors.

### Day 1 (Monday): Color wheel
*Student:* Paint a 12-part color wheel mixing secondary and tertiary colors from primaries.

*How to teach:* Your child paints primaries (red, yellow, blue), mixes secondaries (orange, green, violet) between them, then tertiaries (red-orange, yellow-orange, yellow-green, blue-green, blue-violet, red-violet).

- **Q:** How do you make a tertiary color?
  **A:** Mix a primary with the secondary next to it (e.g., blue + green = blue-green).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tints and shades
*Student:* Paint a tint and shade strip for one color, adding white and black in steps.

*How to teach:* Make a tint strip (add white in steps) and a shade strip (add black in small amounts) for one color.

- **Q:** What's the difference between a tint and a shade?
  **A:** A tint adds white (lighter); a shade adds black (darker).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Color schemes
*Student:* Paint the same simple design three times with complementary, analogous and monochromatic schemes.

*How to teach:* Paint the same design with complementary (opposites), analogous (neighbors) and monochromatic (one hue with tints/shades) schemes.

- **Q:** Name the complement of blue.
  **A:** Orange.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Brush care
*Student:* Practice proper brush cleaning and palette care, then compare how each color scheme changes mood.

*How to teach:* Teach brush care (rinse, blot, reshape, store bristles up) and palette cleanup; compare moods of the three schemes.

- **Q:** Which scheme usually feels calm and harmonious?
  **A:** Analogous (and monochromatic); complementary feels high-contrast and energetic.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a color-theory quiz and write which scheme you prefer and why.

*How to teach:* Take the quiz; your child writes which scheme they prefer and why. Have your child work independently; afterward, go over any errors together and let them explain the correct answer back to you.

- **Q:** What are the three complementary pairs of primary/secondary colors?
  **A:** Red–green, blue–orange, yellow–violet (purple).

**Assessment:** Color wheel, scheme studies and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: primaries = red, yellow, blue; secondaries = orange, green, violet; tertiary = primary + adjacent secondary; complementary = opposite on wheel (red-green, blue-orange, yellow-violet); analogous = 3 neighbors; tint = + white; shade = + black; monochromatic = one hue. Color wheel rubric: 4 = all 12 colors mixed accurately in order; 3 = 1–2 off; 2 = several errors; 1 = incomplete.

## PE 5: Dribbling and ball control with feet

**Prepare:** Gather a size-4 soccer ball and 6–10 cones. Set a cone line with cones about 1 m apart, a figure-8 with two cones 5 m apart, and a 20 m straight. Your child should wear sneakers or cleats. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Kicking the ball far ahead while dribbling in tight spaces. Use small, soft touches so the ball stays within one step.
- Looking only at the ball. Glance up between touches to see space and defenders.

### Day 1 (Monday): Close control
*Student:* Dribble through a cone line using small touches with both feet.

*How to teach:* Model small touches with the inside and outside of both feet through the cone line. Your child goes through 6 times, alternating feet.

- **Q:** Why use small touches when dribbling in traffic?
  **A:** Keeping the ball close gives you control and makes it harder for a defender to take.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Changing direction
*Student:* Practice inside and outside cuts around cones and a figure-8 pattern.

*How to teach:* Teach an inside cut (pull the ball across your body with the inside of the foot) and outside cut (push away with the outside). Practice around cones and in a figure 8.

- **Q:** Which part of the foot do you use to cut the ball away from your body?
  **A:** The outside of the foot.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Speed dribbling
*Student:* Dribble 20 m at speed with longer touches and stop the ball with the sole.

*How to teach:* Dribble 20 m at speed with longer pushes, then stop the ball with the sole. Repeat 6 times.

- **Q:** How is a speed dribble different from close control?
  **A:** Longer touches pushed into open space ahead so you can run faster; fewer touches.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Dribble tag
*Student:* Play dribble tag where players must keep control while avoiding a tagger.

*How to teach:* Play dribble tag: everyone dribbles in a small square; the tagger tries to tag players while keeping their own ball. Tagged players do 5 toe taps and rejoin.

- **Q:** How do you keep control while escaping a tagger?
  **A:** Keep the ball close, use cuts to change direction, and keep your head up to find open space.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a timed cone dribble course twice and compare times and control.

*How to teach:* Time your child through a cone course twice and note control (ball touches each cone? lost ball?).

- **Q:** Which matters more in the course: speed or control? Why?
  **A:** Control first — losing the ball costs more time than dribbling a little slower.

**Assessment:** Cone course time and control checklist.

**Answer key:** Cone course: record two times. Control checklist: uses both feet, small touches near cones, inside and outside cuts, head up at least sometimes, stops with sole at the end. Proficient = 4 of 5 cues and second time equal or faster with no lost balls.

## Health 5: Components of physical fitness

**Prepare:** Gather a stopwatch, a yardstick taped to a box for sit-and-reach, a mat, and a fitness log. Measure a walk/run route so distance can be estimated (laps of a known loop).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking being thin means being fit. Fitness is about the five components, not appearance.
- Thinking flexibility doesn't matter. It helps prevent injury and allows full movement.

### Day 1 (Monday): Five components
*Student:* Learn the five health-related components of fitness and match an activity to each.

*How to teach:* Teach the five components and have your child match an activity to each (jogging, push-ups, plank, stretching).

- **Q:** Which component is improved by swimming laps for 20 minutes?
  **A:** Cardiorespiratory endurance.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Endurance test
*Student:* Time a 10-minute walk/run and record distance and heart rate.

*How to teach:* Time a 10-minute walk/run; record distance and heart rate at the end. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** What does a faster heart rate recovery after exercise show?
  **A:** Better cardiorespiratory fitness.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Strength tests
*Student:* Count curl-ups and push-ups in 30 seconds each and record them.

*How to teach:* Count curl-ups and push-ups in 30 seconds each, using good form. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** Is doing many curl-ups strength or endurance?
  **A:** Muscular endurance — doing a movement many times.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Flexibility test
*Student:* Do a sit-and-reach test with a yardstick on a box and record the best of three.

*How to teach:* Sit-and-reach with the yardstick; record best of three reaching slowly without bouncing. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** Why reach slowly instead of bouncing?
  **A:** Bouncing can strain or tear muscles; slow reaching is safer and gives a truer measure.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Chart your baselines and write one fitness goal for each component.

*How to teach:* Chart baselines and write one goal per component. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** Write a goal for flexibility.
  **A:** Example: 'Improve my sit-and-reach by 2 inches in 6 weeks by stretching 5 minutes after activity 4 days a week.'

**Assessment:** Fitness baseline chart and goals.

**Answer key:** Baseline chart: all five components recorded (walk/run distance and heart rate, curl-ups, push-ups, sit-and-reach; body composition discussed only as a concept — do not weigh or measure body fat). Goals rubric: 4 = a SMART goal for each measured component; 3 = most components; 2 = vague goals; 1 = missing.
