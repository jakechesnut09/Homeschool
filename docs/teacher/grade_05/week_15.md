# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 15

## ELA 5: Narrative poetry: 'Paul Revere's Ride' by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow

**Prepare:** Print 'Paul Revere's Ride' by Longfellow (Poetry Foundation). Keep the Jean Fritz biography handy for comparison.

**Watch for:**
- Believing the poem is accurate history. Longfellow (1860) changed facts to create a national hero.
- Confusing stanza and line. A stanza is a group of lines.
- Labeling rhyme scheme by counting lines instead of end sounds.

### Day 1 (Monday): First read
*Student:* Read 'Paul Revere's Ride' aloud and summarize what happens in each stanza in one line.

*How to teach:* Read aloud with drama. Your child writes a one-line summary for each stanza.

- **Q:** What does 'One if by land, and two if by sea' mean?
  **A:** One lantern in the Old North Church tower if the British come by land, two if they cross the river by boat.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Structure and rhythm
*Student:* Mark the rhyme scheme and galloping rhythm of the first three stanzas and explain how the sound matches the action.

*How to teach:* Mark the rhyme scheme and galloping rhythm in the first three stanzas; explain how sound matches action. Clap the beat of a few lines together. Label end sounds with letters (A, B) to find the pattern.

- **Q:** Rhyme scheme of stanza 1 (hear / Revere / Seventy-five / alive / year)?
  **A:** AABBA.
- **Q:** How does the rhythm match the action?
  **A:** The quick da-da-DUM beat sounds like galloping hooves.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Poem vs. history
*Student:* Compare the poem's events with the Jean Fritz biography and list what Longfellow changed and why.

*How to teach:* Compare the poem with Fritz's biography; list changes Longfellow made. Make a two-column chart: 'In the poem' vs. 'What really happened.' Ask why a poet might change facts.

- **Q:** What did Longfellow change?
  **A:** Revere actually arranged the lantern signal for others; other riders (William Dawes, Samuel Prescott) also rode; Revere was captured and never reached Concord.
- **Q:** Why might he change it?
  **A:** To create a simple, inspiring hero story for Americans before the Civil War.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Imagery
*Student:* Illustrate the three most vivid images from the poem and label the words that created each picture.

*How to teach:* Your child illustrates the three most vivid images and labels the words that created each. Your child copies the exact words under each drawing and circles the sensory words.

- **Q:** Name a vivid image from the poem.
  **A:** The lantern shining in the belfry of the Old North Church; the horse's hoof sparks in the dark.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Recite two memorized stanzas with expression and take a quiz on stanza, rhyme scheme and imagery.

*How to teach:* Recite two memorized stanzas; give the poetry terms quiz. Have your child practice the stanzas daily this week; give the quiz after the recitation.

- **Q:** Define imagery.
  **A:** Words that create pictures in the reader's mind using the senses.

**Assessment:** Recitation; poetry terms quiz; poem vs. history chart.

**Answer key:** Recitation: memorized accurately, expressive. Poetry terms quiz key: stanza = group of lines; rhyme scheme = pattern of end rhymes (AABBA in stanza 1); rhythm = beat pattern; imagery = sensory pictures; narrative poem = tells a story. Poem vs. history chart: at least 3 accurate differences.

## Math 5: Adding and subtracting mixed numbers

**Prepare:** Fraction circles, measuring cups (1/2, 1/3, 2/3, 1 cup) and a clear container that holds at least 3 cups of water.

**Watch for:**
- Subtracting the smaller fraction from the larger regardless of order (5 1/4 − 2 2/3 = 3 5/12). Rename first: 3/12 − 8/12 needs regrouping.
- Regrouping a whole as 10 (like base ten). One whole = 12/12, 4/4, etc., matching the denominator.
- Leaving 3 17/12 as a final answer. Rename 17/12 as 1 5/12 and add the whole: 4 5/12.

### Day 1 (Monday): Mixed numbers and improper fractions
*Student:* Convert 15 numbers between mixed and improper form using fraction circles and then multiplication.

*How to teach:* Show 2 3/4 with fraction circles and count the fourths: 11/4. Then the shortcut: 2 × 4 + 3 = 11. Reverse: 17/5 → 3 wholes and 2/5. The child converts 15 numbers.

- **Q:** Write 2 3/4 as an improper fraction.
  **A:** 11/4
- **Q:** Write 17/5 as a mixed number.
  **A:** 3 2/5
- **Q:** Write 4 3/8 as an improper fraction.
  **A:** 35/8

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Adding mixed numbers
*Student:* Add 12 mixed numbers like 2 3/4 + 1 2/3, renaming and regrouping fraction sums greater than 1.

*How to teach:* Add wholes and fractions separately after renaming: 2 9/12 + 1 8/12 = 3 17/12 = 4 5/12. The child solves 12.

- **Q:** 2 3/4 + 1 2/3 = ?
  **A:** 4 5/12
- **Q:** 1 1/2 + 2 1/4 = ?
  **A:** 3 3/4

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Subtracting mixed numbers
*Student:* Subtract 12 mixed numbers like 5 1/4 - 2 2/3, regrouping a whole when needed.

*How to teach:* Rename, check if the fraction part can be subtracted, and if not, regroup one whole: 5 3/12 → 4 15/12. The child solves 12.

- **Q:** 5 1/4 − 2 2/3 = ?
  **A:** 4 15/12 − 2 8/12 = 2 7/12
- **Q:** 6 − 1 3/5 = ?
  **A:** 4 2/5

### Day 4 (Thursday): Kitchen fractions
*Student:* Use measuring cups to combine 1 1/2 cups and 2/3 cup of water, then compute the total and how much more is needed to make 3 cups.

*How to teach:* Pour 1 1/2 cups and 2/3 cup of water into the container. Compute the total, then how much more makes 3 cups; check by pouring.

- **Q:** 1 1/2 + 2/3 cups = ? How much more to make 3 cups?
  **A:** 2 1/6 cups; 5/6 cup more.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on adding and subtracting mixed numbers.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz independently; check with estimation (each answer should be near the whole-number estimate). Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** 3 5/6 + 2 1/3 = ?
  **A:** 6 1/6

**Assessment:** Quiz on mixed number addition and subtraction.

**Answer key:** Suggested key: 1) 1 2/3 = 5/3 2) 23/6 = 3 5/6 3) 3 1/2 + 1 1/3 = 4 5/6 4) 2 5/8 + 1 3/4 = 4 3/8 5) 4 2/5 + 3 7/10 = 8 1/10 6) 6 3/4 − 2 1/2 = 4 1/4 7) 5 1/3 − 1 5/6 = 3 1/2 8) 8 − 3 2/7 = 4 5/7 9) 7 1/8 − 4 1/2 = 2 5/8 10) 3 1/6 − 1 3/4 = 1 5/12.

## Science 5: Plant investigation results and argument

**Prepare:** Plant cups from week 12, kitchen scale, graph paper. Spread soil to dry for a day before weighing. Write the Unit 4 test.

**Watch for:**
- Expecting soil mass to drop by as much as plant mass increases.
- Weighing plants wet with soil stuck on.
- Ignoring air as a source of mass.

### Day 1 (Monday): Harvest
*Student:* Carefully remove plants, dry the soil and weigh it; compare to starting soil mass.

*How to teach:* Carefully remove plants, shake soil back into its cup, dry the soil, and weigh. Compare to the starting mass.

- **Q:** What did you find about soil mass?
  **A:** It stayed about the same (only a tiny change).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Plant mass
*Student:* Weigh the plants and compare the gain in plant mass to the change in soil mass in a table.

*How to teach:* Weigh the plants (rinse soil off, pat dry). Record plant gain vs. soil change in a table.

- **Q:** Plant gained 8 g; soil lost 0.2 g. What does this mean?
  **A:** Most of the plant's mass did not come from soil.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Graphing
*Student:* Graph soil mass change vs. plant mass gain for each cup.

*How to teach:* Your child graphs soil mass change vs. plant mass gain for each cup.

- **Q:** What pattern do you expect?
  **A:** Plant mass gains are much larger than soil losses.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Argument writing
*Student:* Write a scientific argument with claim, evidence from your data and van Helmont, and reasoning about air and water.

*How to teach:* Your child writes an argument: claim, evidence from data and van Helmont, reasoning about air and water. Model the claim-evidence-reasoning format on a whiteboard. Evidence must include numbers from your cups.

- **Q:** What is the reasoning?
  **A:** Plants use carbon dioxide from the air and water to make sugar through photosynthesis; that sugar builds the plant's body.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 assessment
*Student:* Take the Unit 4 assessment and share the argument aloud with family.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 4 test; your child shares the argument aloud. Give the test independently. During the oral sharing, ask one 'why' question about the reasoning.

- **Q:** Where does most of a plant's mass come from?
  **A:** Carbon dioxide from the air (with water).

**Assessment:** Written scientific argument; Unit 4 test.

**Answer key:** Argument rubric: 4 = claim (mass from air and water), evidence from own data with numbers and van Helmont, reasoning using photosynthesis; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = claim with weak evidence; 1 = incorrect claim. Unit 4 test key (suggested): 1) plant mass mainly from air and water 2) van Helmont's soil lost ~2 oz 3) photosynthesis needs light 4) stomata exchange gases 5) energy in food comes from the sun 6) calories measure stored energy.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Slavery in the colonies

**Prepare:** Phillis Wheatley poem 'On Being Brought from Africa to America' (1773), 1688 Germantown Petition excerpt, Equiano excerpt. Preview for sensitive content.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking slavery existed only in the South.
- Thinking enslaved people did not resist.
- Thinking slavery was legal from 1619.

### Day 1 (Monday): 1619 and beyond
*Student:* Read about the arrival of enslaved Africans in Virginia in 1619 and how slavery became law over the following decades.

*How to teach:* Read about the 1619 arrival of enslaved Africans at Point Comfort, Virginia. Explain how laws over the next decades made slavery lifelong and inherited.

- **Q:** What did Virginia's 1662 law say?
  **A:** Children of enslaved mothers were enslaved.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Lives of enslaved families
*Student:* Read about enslaved families' work, culture, music and faith and how they kept traditions alive.

*How to teach:* Read about enslaved families' work, culture, music and faith. Ask how they kept traditions alive despite cruelty.

- **Q:** How did they keep traditions?
  **A:** Music, stories, faith, family.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Resistance
*Student:* Learn about forms of resistance including slowdowns, escape and the Stono Rebellion, and record examples.

*How to teach:* Read about forms of resistance (slowdowns, breaking tools, escape, the 1739 Stono Rebellion). Your child records examples in a chart.

- **Q:** What was the Stono Rebellion?
  **A:** A 1739 uprising in South Carolina.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Opposing slavery
*Student:* Analyze the 1688 Germantown Quaker Petition and Phillis Wheatley's poem as early voices in the debate.

*How to teach:* Read the 1688 Germantown Petition and Wheatley's poem. Your child notes each author's argument or message.

- **Q:** What did the Germantown Petition argue?
  **A:** Slavery is wrong; treat others as you would want to be treated.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining how slavery became institutionalized in the South, using two sources.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a paragraph using two sources explaining how slavery became institutionalized in the South. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** How did slavery become institutionalized?
  **A:** Through laws making it lifelong and hereditary.

**Assessment:** Source-based paragraph.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric: 4 = clear topic sentence, three or more accurate, specific details or quotations from sources, explanation of how they support the point, conclusion; 3 = accurate with two details; 2 = general or partly inaccurate; 1 = off topic or incomplete.

## Art & Music 5: Spirituals and work songs

**Prepare:** Find recordings of 'Swing Low, Sweet Chariot' (e.g., the Fisk Jubilee Singers' historic version), 'Wade in the Water,' and 'Follow the Drinking Gourd,' plus two different performances of one spiritual. Print lyrics. Read a short background on spirituals (Library of Congress). Note: historians debate how often songs were used as literal codes, so present code meanings as 'possible' or 'traditionally told.'

**Watch for:**
- Thinking spirituals were just sad songs. They expressed sorrow but also hope, faith, freedom and resistance.
- Treating 'code song' stories as proven fact. Some meanings are traditional interpretations that historians still debate.

### Day 1 (Monday): Spirituals
*Student:* Listen to 'Swing Low, Sweet Chariot' and learn about the origins of spirituals among enslaved African Americans.

*How to teach:* Listen to 'Swing Low'; explain spirituals were created by enslaved African Americans, blending African musical traditions (call-and-response, syncopation) with Christian hymns.

- **Q:** Who created spirituals, and what did they express?
  **A:** Enslaved African Americans; they expressed faith, suffering, hope and longing for freedom.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Code songs
*Student:* Read the lyrics of 'Follow the Drinking Gourd' and 'Wade in the Water' and discuss their possible hidden meanings.

*How to teach:* Read lyrics of 'Follow the Drinking Gourd' and 'Wade in the Water'; discuss possible hidden meanings.

- **Q:** What is the 'drinking gourd' traditionally said to mean?
  **A:** The Big Dipper, which points to the North Star — a guide north toward freedom.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Work songs
*Student:* Learn a call-and-response work song and explain how rhythm helped people work together.

*How to teach:* Learn a call-and-response work song (e.g., 'Pick a Bale of Cotton' or a sea shanty); discuss rhythm.

- **Q:** How did rhythm help people work together?
  **A:** A steady beat let workers move in time, coordinate tasks and make hard work feel lighter.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Expressive performance
*Student:* Compare two performances of the same spiritual and note differences in tempo, dynamics and timbre.

*How to teach:* Compare two performances of the same spiritual for tempo, dynamics and timbre. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What is timbre?
  **A:** The unique tone color of a voice or instrument.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Sing a spiritual with chosen expressive qualities and write about the performers' intent.

*How to teach:* Your child sings a spiritual with chosen expressive qualities and writes about performers' intent.

- **Q:** Why might a performer sing a spiritual slowly and softly?
  **A:** To express sorrow, reverence or longing.

**Assessment:** Listening comparison and performance.

**Answer key:** Listening comparison: compares tempo, dynamics, timbre and accompaniment of two performances with specific examples (4 = all four, 3 = three, 2 = two, 1 = one). Performance: expressive choices that match the song's meaning, accurate pitch and lyrics.

## PE 5: Flying disc skills

**Prepare:** Gather a flying disc (a 175 g Ultimate disc or a lighter kids' disc), hula hoops or cones for targets, and household objects to serve as 6 disc-golf 'holes.' Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Flinging with a big arm swing. The backhand is a short pull across the body ending in a wrist snap, which gives the disc spin.
- Releasing the disc tilted. A tilted disc curves; keep it flat for a straight throw.

### Day 1 (Monday): Backhand throw
*Student:* Practice the backhand grip, stance and wrist snap with short throws.

*How to teach:* Teach the backhand: thumb on top, fingers curled under the rim, side toward target, reach back across the body, step, and snap the wrist releasing the disc flat. Short 5 m throws.

- **Q:** What makes a disc fly steadily?
  **A:** Spin from the wrist snap keeps it stable.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Catching
*Student:* Practice pancake and rim catches at different heights.

*How to teach:* Practice the pancake catch (one hand above, one below, clap together) and rim catches (thumb down for high, thumb up for low).

- **Q:** Which catch is safest for beginners at chest height?
  **A:** The pancake (clap) catch.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Accuracy
*Student:* Throw through a hula hoop target from increasing distances.

*How to teach:* Throw through a hula hoop (held or hung) from 3, 5, 8 and 10 m. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** Your throws keep curving left. What should you check?
  **A:** Whether the disc is tilted at release; keep it level and follow through toward the target.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Disc golf
*Student:* Set up a 6-hole backyard or park disc golf course and play a round, counting throws.

*How to teach:* Set up 6 'holes' (trees, a bucket, a chair) and play a round, counting throws per hole. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** In disc golf, is a lower or higher score better?
  **A:** Lower — fewer throws to reach each target.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain how angle of the disc affects its flight and demonstrate a straight throw.

*How to teach:* Your child explains how the disc's angle affects flight and demonstrates a straight throw.

- **Q:** What happens if the disc is tilted when released?
  **A:** It curves toward the side that is tipped down; a flat disc flies straight.

**Assessment:** Disc golf scorecard and demonstration.

**Answer key:** Disc golf scorecard: throws for each of 6 holes totaled correctly. Demonstration: flat release, step toward target, wrist snap, straight flight about 10 m (4 = consistent; 3 = mostly; 2 = sometimes; 1 = rarely). Explanation: a tilted disc curves toward the lower side.

## Health 5: Dental health and check-ups

**Prepare:** Get a new toothbrush, floss, a 2-minute timer, and disclosing tablets (pharmacy) or dark grape juice. Find your child's last dental and medical check-up records.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking brushing once a day is enough. Brush twice a day for two minutes and floss once daily.
- Thinking sugar itself eats teeth. Plaque bacteria eat sugar and make acid that dissolves enamel.

### Day 1 (Monday): Plaque check
*Student:* Use a disclosing tablet or grape juice to see plaque, then brush for two minutes and recheck.

*How to teach:* Chew a disclosing tablet to show plaque; brush 2 minutes with small circles at the gumline, then recheck.

- **Q:** What is plaque?
  **A:** A sticky film of bacteria that forms on teeth.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Flossing
*Student:* Practice flossing technique and explain how sugar and plaque cause cavities.

*How to teach:* Practice flossing: 18 inches, wrap around middle fingers, C-shape around each tooth, gently below the gumline.

- **Q:** How do sugar and plaque cause cavities?
  **A:** Plaque bacteria feed on sugar and produce acid that dissolves tooth enamel, making holes (cavities).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Check-ups
*Student:* Interview a parent about your last dental and medical check-up and list what was checked.

*How to teach:* Your child interviews you about the last check-ups and lists what was checked (teeth cleaning, X-rays, height, weight, vision, vaccines).

- **Q:** Why have check-ups when you feel fine?
  **A:** To catch problems early, track growth, and keep vaccines up to date.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Orthodontics and sports
*Student:* Read about braces and mouth guards and when they are needed.

*How to teach:* Read about braces (straighten teeth/jaw alignment by an orthodontist) and mouth guards (contact sports).

- **Q:** When should you wear a mouth guard?
  **A:** In contact sports or activities where your mouth could be hit (football, basketball, martial arts, skateboarding).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Create a dental care poster and answer 5 questions on oral health.

*How to teach:* Make a dental poster and answer 5 questions. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** How does fluoride help teeth?
  **A:** It strengthens enamel, making teeth more resistant to acid and cavities.

**Assessment:** Dental poster and questions.

**Answer key:** Oral health answers: 1) brush twice a day for 2 minutes 2) floss once a day 3) plaque = sticky bacteria film 4) bacteria + sugar → acid → cavity 5) fluoride strengthens enamel. Poster rubric: 4 = accurate brushing, flossing, diet and check-up tips with visuals; 3 = 3 of 4; 2 = 2; 1 = 1 or fewer.
