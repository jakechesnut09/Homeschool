# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 25

## ELA 5: Drafting and revising the opinion essay

**Prepare:** Have the opinion organizer and revision checklist ready.

**Watch for:**
- Ignoring the other side. Addressing a counterclaim makes an argument stronger.
- Ending abruptly. Conclusions restate and call to action.
- Omitting commas in direct address ('Thanks Mom').

### Day 1 (Monday): Introduction
*Student:* Draft an introduction with a hook, background and opinion statement.

*How to teach:* Share 2–3 hook styles (question, surprising fact, short scene). Your child drafts an introduction with a hook, 1–2 sentences of background and the opinion statement.

- **Q:** Write a hook for recess.
  **A:** Example: Imagine sitting still for six hours straight.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Body paragraphs
*Student:* Draft three body paragraphs linking reasons and evidence with words like 'consequently' and 'specifically'.

*How to teach:* Model one body paragraph: reason as topic sentence, evidence, then explanation using 'specifically' or 'consequently.' Your child drafts the other two.

- **Q:** Use 'consequently.'
  **A:** Students sit for hours; consequently, they lose focus.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Counterclaim and conclusion
*Student:* Add a sentence addressing the other side and draft a conclusion that restates the opinion and calls readers to act.

*How to teach:* Ask, 'What would someone who disagrees say?' Your child writes that counterclaim and answers it, then drafts a conclusion that restates the opinion and calls readers to act.

- **Q:** Write a counterclaim sentence.
  **A:** Some people argue that longer recess takes away learning time; however, research shows breaks improve attention.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Commas for yes/no, tags and address
*Student:* Learn commas for yes/no, tag questions and direct address and edit dialogue examples, then revise the essay with a checklist.

*How to teach:* Teach with examples: 'Yes, I agree.' 'It's late, isn't it?' 'Sam, come here.' Your child fixes dialogue examples, then revises the essay with the checklist.

- **Q:** Punctuate: You agree don't you?
  **A:** You agree, don't you?
- **Q:** Punctuate: Mom can I go?
  **A:** Mom, can I go?

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Read the draft aloud to a family member who gives two 'glows' and one 'grow', and revise accordingly.

*How to teach:* The listener gives two specific 'glows' (what works) and one 'grow' (what to fix). Your child revises the draft that day while the feedback is fresh.

- **Q:** What is a 'grow'?
  **A:** A specific suggestion for improvement.

**Assessment:** Complete revised draft; comma quiz embedded in editing.

**Answer key:** Draft rubric: introduction with hook and opinion; 3 body paragraphs each with reason + evidence; counterclaim addressed; conclusion with call to action. Comma items correct: Yes, …; …, isn't it?; Mom, ….

## Math 5: Volume formulas

**Prepare:** Rulers, 4 household boxes (cereal, shoe, tissue, small gift box).

**Watch for:**
- Adding the dimensions instead of multiplying.
- Using square units for volume. Volume is cubic units (in³, cm³).
- Thinking the base must be the bottom as placed. Any face can be the base B if height is measured perpendicular to it.

### Day 1 (Monday): V = l x w x h
*Student:* Connect layers of cubes to the formula and find volumes of 12 prisms with given dimensions.

*How to teach:* Connect 'layer × layers' to length × width × height. The child finds volumes of 12 prisms.

- **Q:** V of a 6 × 4 × 3 prism?
  **A:** 72 cubic units.
- **Q:** V of a 10 cm × 5 cm × 2 cm box?
  **A:** 100 cubic cm.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): V = B x h
*Student:* Find the base area and multiply by height for 10 prisms; show the associative property gives the same volume.

*How to teach:* Find base area B = l × w, then V = B × h. Show (6 × 4) × 3 = 6 × (4 × 3): same volume. The child does 10.

- **Q:** B = 20 sq in, h = 7 in. Volume?
  **A:** 140 cubic inches.
- **Q:** Show 5 × 3 × 4 two ways.
  **A:** (5 × 3) × 4 = 60 and 5 × (3 × 4) = 60.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Measuring real boxes
*Student:* Measure 4 household boxes to the nearest inch and calculate their volumes in cubic inches.

*How to teach:* Measure 4 boxes to the nearest inch, record l, w, h, and compute V in cubic inches. Show how to line up the ruler at zero and round to the nearest inch. Your child records l, w, h for each box in a table and multiplies.

- **Q:** A shoebox is 13 in × 8 in × 5 in. Volume?
  **A:** 520 cubic inches.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Missing dimensions
*Student:* Solve 8 problems where volume and two dimensions are known and find the third.

*How to teach:* Use V ÷ (two known dimensions) to find the missing one. The child solves 8.

- **Q:** V = 120, l = 5, w = 4. Find h.
  **A:** 120 ÷ 20 = 6.
- **Q:** V = 96 cubic ft, B = 16 sq ft. h?
  **A:** 6 ft

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on volume formulas and missing dimensions.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz on formulas and missing dimensions. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** V of a cube with 5 cm edges?
  **A:** 125 cubic cm.

**Assessment:** Quiz on V = lwh and V = Bh; box measurement table.

**Answer key:** Suggested key: 1) 8 × 3 × 2 = 48 2) 12 × 5 × 4 = 240 3) B = 36, h = 5 → 180 4) cube edge 4 = 64 5) V = 200, l = 10, w = 5 → h = 4 6) V = 84, B = 12 → h = 7 7) units: cubic inches/cm. Box table: correct measurements and products for each box.

## Science 5: California's water: sources and the water cycle

**Prepare:** Map of California water systems (e.g., DWR map of State Water Project, Central Valley Project, LA Aqueduct), zip bag, marker, tape, clear cup, gravel, sand, clay. Write the Unit 7 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking groundwater is an underground lake. It fills spaces between rock and sand.
- Thinking all CA water comes from local rain.
- Ignoring snowpack as storage.

### Day 1 (Monday): Water cycle in a bag
*Student:* Draw the water cycle on a zip bag with a little water, tape it to a sunny window, and observe evaporation and condensation.

*How to teach:* Draw the water cycle on a zip bag with a little water; tape it to a sunny window. Draw sun, clouds, mountains and ocean on the bag with marker; add 1/4 cup water and tape it to a sunny window. Check after a few hours.

- **Q:** What happens in the bag?
  **A:** Water evaporates, condenses on the top and drips down, like rain.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): California snowpack
*Student:* Read about the Sierra Nevada snowpack and trace on a map how it reaches cities through rivers and aqueducts.

*How to teach:* Read about Sierra Nevada snowpack; trace how it reaches cities via rivers and aqueducts. Read about the snowpack and look at a map of aqueducts. Your child traces the path from mountains to your city.

- **Q:** Why is the snowpack important?
  **A:** It stores water in winter and melts slowly in spring and summer, supplying roughly a third of California's water.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Groundwater
*Student:* Build an aquifer model in a clear cup with gravel, sand and clay and pour 'rain' to show how groundwater forms.

*How to teach:* Build an aquifer model and pour 'rain.' Layer gravel, sand and clay in a clear cup. Pour water slowly and watch where it collects.

- **Q:** Where does the water go?
  **A:** Through sand and gravel; clay slows it, forming a groundwater layer.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Home water audit
*Student:* Measure how much water your family uses in one day by estimating faucet, shower and toilet use.

*How to teach:* Estimate household water use in one day. Use a stopwatch and measuring cup to estimate flow rates. Your child totals daily household use.

- **Q:** A 10-minute shower at 2 gallons per minute uses?
  **A:** 20 gallons.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 assessment
*Student:* Take the Unit 7 assessment on water distribution and graph reading.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test. Give the test without help, then review graph-reading questions.

- **Q:** Name a CA water source.
  **A:** Sierra Nevada snowpack/rivers, groundwater, Colorado River.

**Assessment:** Home water audit; Unit 7 test.

**Answer key:** Water audit: estimates for faucets, shower, toilet, total. Unit 7 test key (suggested): 97% salt water; most fresh water frozen; groundwater; snowpack; aqueducts; graph reading items correct.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Allies, heroes and Native nations in the war

**Prepare:** Biography cards; map of Spanish Gulf Coast campaigns.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Americans fought alone.
- Thinking all Native nations sided with Britain.
- Thinking Black Americans fought only for Patriots.

### Day 1 (Monday): France joins
*Student:* Read about Franklin's negotiations in Paris and the French alliance of 1778.

*How to teach:* Read about Franklin's negotiations and the 1778 alliance. Pause after the reading to ask your child to retell the key facts in their own words before writing anything.

- **Q:** What did France provide?
  **A:** Troops, navy, money.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Foreign heroes
*Student:* Create biography cards for Lafayette, von Steuben, Kosciuszko and Bernardo de Gálvez.

*How to teach:* Your child creates 4 biography cards. Model the first item yourself, then let your child finish while you check accuracy against the source.

- **Q:** What did Gálvez do?
  **A:** Captured British forts on the Gulf Coast.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Native alliances
*Student:* Read about the Oneida who supported the Patriots and Joseph Brant (Mohawk) who supported the British, and explain each nation's reasons.

*How to teach:* Read about the Oneida and Joseph Brant; your child explains each side's reasons. Ask 'How do you know?' and have your child point to the source or map for each answer.

- **Q:** Why did Brant side with Britain?
  **A:** To protect Mohawk lands.

### Day 4 (Thursday): African American soldiers
*Student:* Read about the 1st Rhode Island Regiment and Lord Dunmore's Proclamation and explain why Black Americans fought on both sides.

*How to teach:* Read about the 1st Rhode Island Regiment and Dunmore's Proclamation. Before finishing, have your child explain their work aloud in 2–3 sentences; correct any errors then.

- **Q:** What did Dunmore offer?
  **A:** Freedom to enslaved people held by Patriots (rebels) who escaped and fought for the British.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on allies and individuals who shaped the war.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** Who trained troops at Valley Forge?
  **A:** Baron von Steuben.

**Assessment:** Biography cards and quiz.

**Answer key:** Cards: accurate roles. Quiz key: Lafayette, von Steuben, Kosciuszko, Gálvez, Oneida, Brant, 1st Rhode Island.

## Art & Music 5: Form and space: paper sculpture

**Prepare:** Gather cardstock, scissors, glue, tape. Find images of Alexander Calder's mobiles and stabiles and Ruth Asawa's looped wire sculptures (de Young Museum, San Francisco).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking sculpture is only about the material. Empty space (negative space) is part of the design.
- Building flat. A freestanding sculpture must look interesting from all sides.

### Day 1 (Monday): Paper techniques
*Student:* Practice curling, folding, scoring, fringing and tab joints to make a paper techniques sampler.

*How to teach:* Practice curling, folding, scoring, fringing and tab joints. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** Why score paper before folding?
  **A:** A light crease line makes a crisp, straight fold.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sculptor study
*Student:* Look at Alexander Calder and Ruth Asawa sculptures and discuss positive and negative space.

*How to teach:* Look at Calder and Asawa; discuss positive and negative space. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What is negative space in a sculpture?
  **A:** The empty space around and through the sculpture.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Abstract sculpture
*Student:* Build a freestanding abstract paper sculpture at least 8 inches tall.

*How to teach:* Build a freestanding abstract sculpture at least 8 inches tall. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** How can you make a tall paper sculpture stable?
  **A:** A wide base, folded or rolled columns, and tab joints.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Viewing from all sides
*Student:* Photograph the sculpture from 4 angles and adjust any weak side.

*How to teach:* Photograph from 4 angles; fix weak sides. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** Why view a sculpture from all sides?
  **A:** It's three-dimensional and seen from every angle.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write about how your sculpture uses positive and negative space.

*How to teach:* Write about positive and negative space. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** Give an example of positive space in your sculpture.
  **A:** The paper forms themselves.

**Assessment:** Paper sculpture and written reflection.

**Answer key:** Sculpture rubric: 4 = freestanding, 8+ inches, uses 3+ paper techniques, interesting from all sides, intentional negative space; 3 = minor issues; 2 = unstable or flat; 1 = incomplete. Reflection explains positive/negative space with examples.

## PE 5: Volleyball: set, serve and rally

**Prepare:** Gather a beach ball or soft volleyball, rope net, and tape or cones to mark serve zones. Review 'triangle hands' (thumbs and index fingers form a triangle above the forehead). Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Slapping the set with palms. Contact with the finger pads, then extend arms and legs.
- Tossing too high on the underhand serve. Hold the ball low and still, swing the other arm like a pendulum, hit the bottom of the ball.

### Day 1 (Monday): Overhead set
*Student:* Practice the set with 'triangle hands' using self-sets and partner tosses.

*How to teach:* Teach the set: triangle hands above the forehead, contact with finger pads, extend arms and legs. Self-sets, then partner tosses.

- **Q:** Where should your hands be when you set?
  **A:** Above the forehead in a triangle shape, using finger pads.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Underhand serve
*Student:* Practice the underhand serve over a rope net from short, then longer distances.

*How to teach:* Teach the underhand serve: opposite foot forward, hold ball at waist, swing the hitting arm back and through, contact the bottom of the ball with the heel of the hand. Start 3 m from the net, then move back.

- **Q:** Which foot is forward for a right-handed underhand serve?
  **A:** The left foot.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Cooperative rally
*Student:* Rally with a partner over the net using bumps and sets and count your best rally.

*How to teach:* Rally over the net using bumps and sets; count your best cooperative rally. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** When do you set rather than bump?
  **A:** When the ball is high (above the forehead); bump low balls.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Serve targets
*Student:* Serve to marked zones and explain how force and contact point changed placement.

*How to teach:* Serve to marked zones; your child explains how force and contact point changed placement. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** If your serve goes into the net, what should you change?
  **A:** Use more force and/or contact slightly lower on the ball so it travels upward.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Record serve success rate and best cooperative rally.

*How to teach:* Record serve success rate (out of 10) and best rally. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** If you made 7 of 10 serves, what percent is that?
  **A:** 70%.

**Assessment:** Serve success rate and rally count.

**Answer key:** Serve success rate /10 with correct percent; best rally count. Form: opposite foot forward, still ball, pendulum swing, heel of hand contact. Proficient = 5+/10 serves and a rally of 6+.

## Health 5: E-cigarettes and vaping

**Prepare:** Download the FDA or CDC 'E-cigarettes/vaping' fact sheet for youth. Write 10 vaping statements on cards (myths and facts). Find recent youth vaping survey data (National Youth Tobacco Survey: in recent years roughly 9 in 10 middle and high schoolers do NOT vape) and images of vape packaging from a news or health site.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking vape aerosol is 'just water vapor.' It contains nicotine, ultrafine particles, flavoring chemicals and metals like nickel and lead.
- Thinking 'everyone vapes.' Survey data show most youth do not vape.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is a vape?
*Student:* Read the FDA fact sheet on e-cigarettes and list what is in vape aerosol.

*How to teach:* Read the fact sheet; your child lists what's in vape aerosol. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** Name three things found in vape aerosol.
  **A:** Nicotine, ultrafine particles, flavoring chemicals (like diacetyl), volatile organic compounds, heavy metals (nickel, tin, lead).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Myth vs. fact
*Student:* Sort 10 vaping statements (e.g., 'it's just water vapor') into myths and facts with evidence.

*How to teach:* Sort 10 statements into myths and facts with evidence from the sheet. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** Myth or fact: Vapes are safe because they don't burn tobacco.
  **A:** Myth — vape aerosol still contains nicotine and harmful chemicals that damage lungs and the developing brain.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Flavors and packaging
*Student:* Analyze images of vape packaging and explain how flavors and colors target young people.

*How to teach:* Look at packaging images; discuss bright colors, candy and fruit flavors, cartoon designs.

- **Q:** How do flavors and packaging target young people?
  **A:** Sweet candy/fruit flavors and bright, fun packaging appeal to kids and make vaping seem harmless.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Real norms
*Student:* Read survey data showing most youth do not vape and make a bar graph.

*How to teach:* Make a bar graph from survey data showing most youth don't vape. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** Why does knowing the real norm help?
  **A:** It shows that not vaping is normal, which reduces pressure to try it.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a short public service announcement correcting one vaping myth.

*How to teach:* Write a 30–60 second PSA script correcting one myth. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** What should a strong PSA include?
  **A:** A clear message, an accurate fact from a valid source, and a call to action.

**Assessment:** PSA script.

**Answer key:** PSA rubric: 4 = names a specific myth, corrects it with an accurate fact from a valid source (FDA/CDC), engaging delivery and a clear call to action; 3 = accurate correction but no source or call to action; 2 = vague or partly inaccurate; 1 = missing.
