# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 5

## ELA 5: Hatchet chapters 1–4: the crash

**Prepare:** Borrow Hatchet by Gary Paulsen and preview chapters 1–4. Parental note: chapter 1–2 describe the pilot's fatal heart attack; 'the Secret' is that Brian saw his mother kissing a man who was not his father — be ready to discuss divorce. Find a map of northern Canada.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up internal and external conflict. External = against nature or others (crash, hunger); internal = inside the mind (fear, the Secret).
- Thinking a flashback is a dream. A flashback interrupts the present to show an earlier event.
- Using 'had' for every past action. Past perfect (had flown) shows an action finished before another past action.

### Day 1 (Monday): Chapters 1–2
*Student:* Read chapters 1–2 and list sentences Paulsen uses to create suspense (short fragments, repetition); explain the effect of one.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 1–2. Your child copies 3 sentences or fragments that build suspense (short, repeated phrases) and explains how one makes the reader feel.

- **Q:** What happens to the pilot, and why is Brian in danger?
  **A:** The pilot has a heart attack and dies; Brian, who has never flown a plane, is alone in a small bush plane over the Canadian wilderness.
- **Q:** Why does Paulsen use short fragments during the emergency?
  **A:** They imitate Brian's panicked thoughts and speed up the pace, building suspense.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Flashbacks
*Student:* Read chapter 3 and identify the flashback to 'the Secret'; explain how this chapter fits the overall structure of the opening.

*How to teach:* Read chapter 3; identify the flashback to 'the Secret' and discuss how memories interrupt the present in the opening chapters. Ask your child to find the exact words where the present story shifts into memory and back.

- **Q:** What is 'the Secret'?
  **A:** Brian saw his mother in a car kissing a man who wasn't his father; his parents are divorced and he hasn't told his father.
- **Q:** Why include the flashback?
  **A:** It reveals Brian's internal conflict and the emotional pain he carries into the survival story.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Perfect tenses
*Student:* Learn present, past and future perfect tenses with examples from Hatchet and write 9 sentences about the crash using each tense.

*How to teach:* Teach perfect tenses: present perfect (has crashed), past perfect (had crashed), future perfect (will have crashed). Your child writes 9 sentences about the crash, 3 per tense.

- **Q:** Write in past perfect: Brian (land) the plane before he realized he was hurt.
  **A:** Brian had landed the plane before he realized he was hurt.
- **Q:** Name the tense: By tomorrow, Brian will have spent three days alone.
  **A:** Future perfect.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Chapter 4 and conflict
*Student:* Read chapter 4 and create a two-column chart of Brian's internal and external conflicts with page numbers.

*How to teach:* Read chapter 4 and fill a two-column chart of internal and external conflicts with page numbers. Model one row of the chart first, then let your child complete at least 3 of each type.

- **Q:** Give one internal and one external conflict.
  **A:** Internal: fear, self-pity, memories of the Secret. External: hunger, thirst, mosquitoes, being lost in the wilderness.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Discuss 'What would you do first?' using text evidence, then take a quiz on chapters 1–4 and perfect tenses.

*How to teach:* Discuss 'What would you do first?' using evidence, then give a chapters 1–4 quiz with perfect-tense items. During discussion, require each idea to be tied to something Brian actually had or knew in the book.

- **Q:** What gift did Brian's mother give him?
  **A:** A hatchet, which he wears on his belt.

**Assessment:** Reading journal entries; chapters 1–4 quiz including perfect tense items.

**Answer key:** Quiz key (suggested): 1) Brian is 13 and flying to visit his father in the Canadian oil fields 2) the pilot has a heart attack 3) Brian crash-lands the plane into a lake 4) his mother gave him the hatchet 5) the Secret = he saw his mother with another man 6) internal conflict example: fear/memories 7) external example: hunger/insects 8) 'had landed' = past perfect 9) 'has survived' = present perfect 10) 'will have waited' = future perfect. Journal entries: complete, dated, with at least one quotation.

## Math 5: Multiplying multi-digit whole numbers

**Prepare:** Grid paper (1 digit per square keeps columns aligned), multiplication fact cards, a timer for the 3-minute fact check.

**Watch for:**
- Forgetting the placeholder zero in the second partial product (multiplying by the tens digit). Say 'I'm multiplying by 30, not 3' and write the zero first.
- Adding carried digits before multiplying. Multiply first, then add the regrouped digit.
- Misaligned columns. Use grid paper and check against an estimate.

### Day 1 (Monday): Estimating products
*Student:* Round factors to estimate 15 products (like 389 x 42 ≈ 400 x 40) and do a 3-minute multiplication fact fluency check.

*How to teach:* Model rounding each factor to one non-zero digit: 389 × 42 ≈ 400 × 40 = 16,000 (4 × 4 = 16, then add three zeros). The child estimates 15 products, then does a 3-minute fact check.

- **Q:** Estimate 389 × 42.
  **A:** About 400 × 40 = 16,000 (actual 16,338).
- **Q:** Estimate 612 × 28.
  **A:** About 600 × 30 = 18,000.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Area model
*Student:* Solve 8 problems such as 236 x 34 with an area model and add the partial products.

*How to teach:* Draw a rectangle split into 200 + 30 + 6 by 30 + 4 and fill the 6 boxes. Add the partial products. The child solves 8 problems.

- **Q:** Use an area model for 236 × 34.
  **A:** 6,000 + 900 + 180 + 800 + 120 + 24 = 8,024.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Standard algorithm 3x2
*Student:* Connect partial products to the standard algorithm and solve 10 three-digit by two-digit problems on grid paper to keep columns aligned.

*How to teach:* Solve 236 × 34 with the algorithm next to the area model: 236 × 4 = 944 and 236 × 30 = 7,080 are the two rows. The child does 10 three-by-two problems on grid paper.

- **Q:** 472 × 23 = ?
  **A:** 472 × 3 = 1,416; 472 × 20 = 9,440; total 10,856.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Standard algorithm 4x2 and 3x3
*Student:* Solve 10 larger problems like 2,407 x 56, checking each against an estimate.

*How to teach:* Model 2,407 × 56 with an estimate first (2,400 × 60 = 144,000). The child solves 10 larger problems (4-by-2 and 3-by-3) and compares each answer to its estimate.

- **Q:** 2,407 × 56 = ?
  **A:** 120,350 + 14,442 = 134,792.
- **Q:** 315 × 124 = ?
  **A:** 1,260 + 6,300 + 31,500 = 39,060.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a 10-problem multiplication quiz and an error analysis: find and fix the mistake in 3 worked examples.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-problem quiz. Then show three worked examples with errors (a missing placeholder zero, a regrouping slip, misaligned columns); the child finds and fixes each.

- **Q:** A student wrote 53 × 27 = 477. Find the error.
  **A:** They multiplied 53 × 2 = 106 without the placeholder zero and added 371 + 106 = 477. Correct: 371 + 1,060 = 1,431.

**Assessment:** Quiz on standard-algorithm multiplication with estimation checks.

**Answer key:** Suggested key: 1) 34 × 26 = 884 2) 58 × 47 = 2,726 3) 213 × 32 = 6,816 4) 407 × 15 = 6,105 5) 625 × 48 = 30,000 6) 1,234 × 21 = 25,914 7) 3,050 × 64 = 195,200 8) 248 × 135 = 33,480 9) 512 × 206 = 105,472 10) 999 × 12 = 11,988. Each answer should be close to its estimate (e.g., #8 ≈ 200 × 100 = 20,000 or 250 × 140 = 35,000). Error analysis: student names and corrects each error.

## Science 5: Particle model and states of matter

**Prepare:** Ice cubes, zip bags, a pot with a lid (adult handles the stove), food coloring, cups of hot and cold tap water, a timer, poster paper. Write the Unit 1 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking particles themselves melt or expand. The particles stay the same; their motion and spacing change.
- Thinking the water on a cold lid leaked through. It is water vapor condensing.
- Thinking steam you see is a gas. Visible 'steam' is tiny liquid droplets; water vapor is invisible.

### Day 1 (Monday): Melting race
*Student:* Time ice cubes melting in different places (hand, counter, sunny window) and graph the times.

*How to teach:* Place identical ice cubes in bags in different spots (hand, counter, sunny window). Time each until fully melted and graph the times.

- **Q:** Why does ice melt fastest in your hand?
  **A:** Your hand transfers heat energy to the ice, making its particles move faster and break out of their fixed positions.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Food coloring diffusion
*Student:* Drop food coloring into hot and cold water and compare how quickly it spreads, linking speed to particle motion.

*How to teach:* Drop food coloring into hot and cold water at the same time. Your child observes and links speed to particle motion.

- **Q:** Why does color spread faster in hot water?
  **A:** Hot water particles move faster and bump the color particles around more quickly.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Evaporation and condensation
*Student:* Observe water boiling and condensing on a cold lid (adult handles the stove) and draw particle diagrams for each step.

*How to teach:* Adult boils water and holds a cold lid above it. Your child draws particle diagrams for evaporation (liquid to gas) and condensation (gas to liquid).

- **Q:** Where do the drops on the lid come from?
  **A:** Water vapor from the boiling water cools on the lid and condenses into liquid.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Model poster
*Student:* Create a poster showing particle arrangements in solid, liquid and gas with arrows for melting, freezing, evaporating and condensing.

*How to teach:* Your child creates a poster showing particles in solid, liquid and gas with labeled arrows for melting, freezing, evaporating and condensing. Sketch one state together, then your child completes the rest. Check spacing: solid packed in rows, liquid close but jumbled, gas far apart.

- **Q:** Name the change from gas to liquid and from liquid to solid.
  **A:** Condensation; freezing.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 1 assessment
*Student:* Take a Unit 1 assessment on particles and properties and present the model poster.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 1 test and have your child present the poster, explaining each arrow. Give the test without help. During the poster talk, ask 'What happens to the particles during condensation?' to check reasoning.

- **Q:** Does a substance's mass change when it melts?
  **A:** No — the same particles are there, just arranged differently.

**Assessment:** Unit 1 test and particle model poster rubric.

**Answer key:** Unit 1 test key (suggested): 1) matter is made of particles too small to see 2) dissolved sugar is still present 3) air has weight and takes up space 4) gas particles are far apart and move quickly 5) metals conduct heat and electricity 6) baking soda fizzes in vinegar 7) melting = solid to liquid 8) evaporation = liquid to gas 9) condensation = gas to liquid 10) hot water spreads dye faster because particles move faster. Poster rubric: correct particle spacing for each state, four labeled changes, neat key.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Why explore? Motives and technology

**Prepare:** Images of an astrolabe and caravel; cardboard, string, washer, protractor and straw for an astrolabe.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking explorers went only for adventure. Main motives were trade, wealth, religion and national competition.
- Thinking people then believed the Earth was flat. Educated Europeans knew it was round; the question was its size.
- Thinking an astrolabe finds longitude. It measures latitude by angle of the sun or a star.

### Day 1 (Monday): Gold, God and glory
*Student:* Read about the reasons for European exploration (trade, wealth, religion, Reconquista, competition) and rank them with evidence.

*How to teach:* Read about motives: trade routes to Asia's spices, gold, spreading Christianity, the Reconquista's end in 1492, and rivalry among nations. Your child ranks them with evidence.

- **Q:** Why did Europeans want a sea route to Asia?
  **A:** Overland trade routes were long and controlled by others; a sea route would bring spices and silk more cheaply.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Navigation tools
*Student:* Build a simple astrolabe from cardboard and a protractor and measure the angle of a tall object.

*How to teach:* Build an astrolabe: tape a straw to the protractor's straight edge, hang a weighted string from the center. Sight the top of a tall object and read the angle.

- **Q:** What did sailors measure with an astrolabe?
  **A:** The angle of the sun or North Star above the horizon, to find latitude.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Ships
*Student:* Compare a caravel to earlier ships and explain how its design made long voyages possible.

*How to teach:* Compare pictures of a caravel and older ships. Your child explains features that helped long voyages.

- **Q:** What made the caravel useful?
  **A:** Triangular (lateen) sails let it sail into the wind; it was small, fast and could explore shallow coasts.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Entrepreneurs
*Student:* Analyze how explorers had to convince sponsors to fund voyages; write a short 'pitch' as an explorer.

*How to teach:* Explain that voyages cost a fortune and sponsors wanted a return. Your child writes and delivers a 1-minute pitch naming the goal, route, and what the sponsor gains.

- **Q:** Who sponsored Columbus?
  **A:** King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella of Spain.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on motives and navigation technology.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz without notes, then have your child correct missed items using the week's readings. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** What does 'God, gold and glory' mean?
  **A:** Spreading Christianity, gaining wealth and winning fame for the nation.

**Assessment:** Quiz and explorer pitch.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) motives: trade, wealth, religion, competition 2) astrolabe measures latitude 3) compass shows direction 4) caravel lateen sails 5) sponsors were monarchs/merchants. Pitch rubric: states goal, benefits to sponsor, plan, persuasive tone.

## Art & Music 5: Beat, meter and note values

**Prepare:** Bookmark musictheory.net (Lessons: Note Duration, Measures and Time Signature) and gather 8 rhythm cards (quarter notes, eighth pairs, half notes, whole notes, quarter rests). Choose three recordings: a march (e.g., Sousa 'Stars and Stripes Forever'), a waltz (e.g., Strauss 'Blue Danube'), and a pop song in 4.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing the beat with the rhythm. The beat is the steady pulse; the rhythm is the pattern of long and short sounds over it.
- Thinking a rest means stop counting. Rests take up beats of silence; keep counting through them.

### Day 1 (Monday): Feel the beat
*Student:* Pat the steady beat to 3 recordings (a march, a waltz, a pop song) and decide whether each feels in 2, 3 or 4.

*How to teach:* Play each recording; your child pats the beat and finds the strong beat (beat 1) to decide if it's in 2, 3 or 4.

- **Q:** A waltz feels 'ONE-two-three, ONE-two-three.' What meter is it in?
  **A:** Triple meter (3/4).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Note values
*Student:* Complete the musictheory.net lessons on note durations and clap 8 rhythm cards with counting (1-and-2-and).

*How to teach:* Do the musictheory.net note-duration lessons; clap and count 8 rhythm cards ('1-and-2-and').

- **Q:** How many beats does a half note get in 4/4? A whole note?
  **A:** Half note = 2 beats; whole note = 4 beats.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Time signatures
*Student:* Draw bar lines into 6 rhythm lines in 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4.

*How to teach:* Your child draws bar lines in 6 rhythm lines (2/4, 3/4, 4/4), counting beats per measure. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What does the top number of a time signature tell you?
  **A:** How many beats are in each measure (the bottom number tells which note gets one beat; 4 = quarter note).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Rhythm dictation
*Student:* Listen to 6 clapped rhythms and write them in notation.

*How to teach:* Clap 6 short rhythms; your child writes each in notation. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** Write the rhythm for 'ta ta ti-ti ta.'
  **A:** Quarter, quarter, two eighth notes, quarter (4 beats).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Read and clap a 4-measure rhythm at sight and take a note-value quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child sight-claps a 4-measure rhythm and takes the note-value quiz. Have your child work independently; afterward, go over any errors together and let them explain the correct answer back to you.

- **Q:** How many eighth notes equal one quarter note?
  **A:** Two.

**Assessment:** Note-value quiz and sight-clapping check.

**Answer key:** Note-value quiz: whole = 4 beats; half = 2; quarter = 1; eighth = ½; quarter rest = 1; half rest = 2; whole rest = 4 (or a full measure); two eighths = 1 beat; 3/4 = 3 beats per measure. Sight-clapping: 4 = steady beat, all rhythms correct; 3 = 1–2 errors; 2 = several errors or unsteady beat; 1 = cannot complete.

## PE 5: Individual jump rope skills

**Prepare:** Size each rope: standing on the middle, handles should reach the armpits. Choose music with a clear, steady beat (about 120 beats per minute). Jump on a smooth, non-slip surface. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Jumping too high. Jump only an inch or two off the ground, landing softly on the balls of the feet.
- Turning the rope with whole arms. Keep elbows close to the sides and turn with the wrists.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rope basics
*Student:* Size the rope and practice the basic bounce and alternate step for 3 x 1-minute sets.

*How to teach:* Check rope size. Model the basic bounce (both feet, small jumps) and alternate step (like jogging in place). Practice 3 × 1-minute sets with rests.

- **Q:** How do you know a rope is the right length?
  **A:** Standing on the middle with both feet, the handles reach about the armpits.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Footwork tricks
*Student:* Learn skier, bell and side-straddle tricks and practice each 20 times.

*How to teach:* Teach skier (jump side to side), bell (jump forward and back) and side-straddle (feet apart, together). Each 20 times; say the name aloud as you jump.

- **Q:** Describe the bell trick.
  **A:** Feet together, jumping forward and backward like a bell swinging.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Jumping to music
*Student:* Jump to a song with a steady beat, changing tricks every 8 counts.

*How to teach:* Play the song and jump, switching trick every 8 counts. Count aloud '1–8' with your child at first.

- **Q:** Why count in 8s when jumping to music?
  **A:** Most popular music is grouped in phrases of 8 beats, so changing every 8 counts keeps you on the beat.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Heart rate check
*Student:* Measure heart rate after 2 minutes of continuous jumping and compare to jogging.

*How to teach:* Take resting pulse, jump for 2 minutes continuously, take pulse. Compare to the jogging number from week 2.

- **Q:** Was jump rope more or less intense than jogging for you? How do you know?
  **A:** Answer depends on the data — the activity with the higher heart rate afterward was more intense (jump rope is often as high or higher).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform a 30-second trick sequence and count successful jumps in 1 minute.

*How to teach:* Your child performs a 30-second trick sequence, then you count successful jumps in 1 minute of basic bounce.

- **Q:** What two cues help you jump longer without missing?
  **A:** Small low jumps on the balls of the feet, and turning with the wrists with elbows in.

**Assessment:** Jump rope trick checklist and 1-minute count.

**Answer key:** Trick checklist (✓ = 5 in a row without a miss): basic bounce, alternate step, skier, bell, side-straddle. 1-minute count recorded (typical 5th-grade range is roughly 60–120; compare with your child's later scores). Proficient = all five tricks checked and a steady rhythm.

## Health 5: Added sugars, sodium and saturated fat

**Prepare:** Collect labels from soda, juice, sports drink, chocolate milk and water; sugar or sand and a teaspoon. Gather labels for canned soup, chips, crackers and a fresh/frozen vegetable. Key fact: 4 grams of sugar ≈ 1 teaspoon.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 100% juice has no sugar. It has natural sugar and is less filling than whole fruit; whole fruit is a better choice.
- Reading % Daily Value as a percentage of the food. %DV shows how much of a day's recommended amount one serving provides.

### Day 1 (Monday): Sugar in drinks
*Student:* Measure the teaspoons of sugar in 5 drinks (soda, juice, sports drink, chocolate milk, water) from their labels and display them with sugar or sand.

*How to teach:* Your child finds grams of sugar on each label, divides by 4 to get teaspoons, and measures that many teaspoons into cups.

- **Q:** A 12-oz soda has 39 g of sugar. How many teaspoons is that?
  **A:** About 10 teaspoons (39 ÷ 4 ≈ 9.75).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sodium hunt
*Student:* Compare sodium on labels of canned soup, chips, crackers and fresh vegetables and rank them.

*How to teach:* Rank the four foods by sodium per serving. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** Which usually has the least sodium: canned soup, chips, crackers or fresh vegetables?
  **A:** Fresh vegetables.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fats
*Student:* Sort food cards into mostly unsaturated vs. saturated fat sources and explain why plant oils and nuts are better choices.

*How to teach:* Sort food cards: unsaturated (olive oil, nuts, avocado, fish) vs. saturated (butter, fatty meat, cheese).

- **Q:** Why are plant oils and nuts better choices than butter?
  **A:** They're mostly unsaturated fats, which are better for heart health than saturated fat.

### Day 4 (Thursday): 5/20 rule
*Student:* Learn the 5% low / 20% high Daily Value rule and apply it to 4 pantry labels.

*How to teach:* Teach 5/20: 5% DV or less is low; 20% DV or more is high. Apply to 4 labels. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** A snack has 22% DV sodium. Is that high or low?
  **A:** High (20% or more).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a label-reading worksheet and write three swaps that cut added sugar or sodium.

*How to teach:* Complete the worksheet and write three swaps. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** Give a swap that cuts added sugar.
  **A:** Water or plain milk instead of soda; whole fruit instead of fruit snacks.

**Assessment:** Label-reading worksheet.

**Answer key:** Label worksheet: teaspoons calculated as grams ÷ 4; correctly labels nutrients low (≤5% DV) or high (≥20% DV); identifies saturated vs. unsaturated fats. Swaps: three realistic substitutions (e.g., water for soda, fresh veggies for chips, unsalted nuts for crackers).
