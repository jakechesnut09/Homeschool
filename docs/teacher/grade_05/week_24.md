# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 24

## ELA 5: Planning an opinion essay

**Prepare:** Help your child gather 2+ sources on a debatable topic. Opinion organizer ready.

**Watch for:**
- Choosing a question with no real sides.
- Forgetting the comma after introductory elements.
- Recording evidence without source names.

### Day 1 (Monday): Choosing a position
*Student:* Choose a debatable question (e.g., Should schools have longer recess?) and write a clear opinion statement.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm 3–4 debatable questions together and pick one with real evidence on both sides. Your child writes an opinion statement that states a position and a preview of reasons.

- **Q:** Is 'Recess is fun' a good opinion statement?
  **A:** No — make it debatable: 'Schools should have longer recess because it improves focus.'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Gathering evidence
*Student:* Gather 6 facts or quotes from at least 2 sources and record them with source names.

*How to teach:* Help locate 2 reliable sources. Your child records 6 facts or quotes on cards, each with the source name, using quotation marks for exact words.

- **Q:** Why record source names?
  **A:** To credit sources and show the evidence is reliable.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Organizing reasons
*Student:* Group evidence under 3 reasons and order them from least to most convincing.

*How to teach:* Your child sorts the evidence cards into 3 piles and names each pile as a reason. Then order them, saving the most convincing for last.

- **Q:** Why save the strongest reason for last?
  **A:** It leaves the reader with the most powerful point.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Introductory elements
*Student:* Learn commas after introductory words, phrases and clauses and write 10 sentences for the essay using them.

*How to teach:* Model three types: word (However,), phrase (After lunch,), clause (Because…,). Your child writes 10 sentences for the essay, each starting with an introductory element and comma.

- **Q:** Punctuate: After lunch students need to move.
  **A:** After lunch, students need to move.
- **Q:** Punctuate: Yes recess matters.
  **A:** Yes, recess matters.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Conference on the organizer and take a quiz on commas with introductory elements.

*How to teach:* Conference: ask your child to explain the opinion and the strongest reason aloud. Then give the comma quiz.

- **Q:** Punctuate: Because exercise helps the brain recess should be longer.
  **A:** Because exercise helps the brain, recess should be longer.

**Assessment:** Completed opinion organizer; comma quiz.

**Answer key:** Organizer: debatable opinion, 3 reasons, 6 evidence items with sources. Comma quiz key: comma after introductory words (Yes, Well, However), phrases (After lunch,), and clauses (Because…, When…, If…).

## Math 5: Understanding volume with unit cubes

**Prepare:** Sugar cubes or centimeter cubes (about 100), 3 small boxes, printed box nets (e.g., 3×2×2 and 4×2×1 cm), scissors, tape.

**Watch for:**
- Counting only visible cubes in a picture. Count one layer, then multiply by the number of layers to include hidden cubes.
- Confusing volume with area. Area covers a surface (square units); volume fills space (cubic units).
- Leaving gaps or overlaps. Unit cubes must pack with no gaps.

### Day 1 (Monday): What is volume?
*Student:* Fill 3 small boxes with sugar cubes and compare which holds the most; define a unit cube and cubic unit.

*How to teach:* Fill 3 boxes with cubes and count. Define a unit cube (1 unit on each edge) and cubic unit. Discuss which box holds most.

- **Q:** What does it mean that a box has a volume of 24 cubic centimeters?
  **A:** 24 one-centimeter cubes fill it with no gaps or overlaps.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Building prisms
*Student:* Build 6 rectangular prisms with given dimensions from cubes and record the number of cubes in each.

*How to teach:* Build prisms of given dimensions (e.g., 3 × 2 × 4) and record the cube count. Give dimensions such as 3 × 2 × 4, 5 × 1 × 2 and 2 × 2 × 3. Your child records length, width, height and the number of cubes in a table, then looks for a shortcut.

- **Q:** How many cubes in a 3 × 2 × 4 prism?
  **A:** 24

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Layers
*Student:* Count cubes in one layer and multiply by number of layers for 8 prism pictures.

*How to teach:* Count cubes in the bottom layer (length × width), then multiply by the number of layers. The child does 8 pictures.

- **Q:** A prism has 5 × 3 cubes in each layer and 4 layers. Volume?
  **A:** 15 × 4 = 60 cubic units.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Nets and boxes
*Student:* Cut out and fold box nets, predict how many centimeter cubes fill each, then test.

*How to teach:* Cut and fold nets; predict the number of centimeter cubes that fill each, then test. Your child writes the prediction (layer × layers) before filling, then counts the cubes to test it.

- **Q:** A box net folds to 4 cm × 2 cm × 3 cm. How many cm cubes fill it?
  **A:** 24

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz finding volume of prisms by counting cubes and layers.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz on finding volume by counting cubes and layers. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** A prism is 2 cubes long, 2 wide, 2 high. Volume?
  **A:** 8 cubic units.

**Assessment:** Quiz on volume by counting unit cubes; observation during building.

**Answer key:** Suggested key: 1) 4 × 3 × 2 = 24 cubic units 2) layer of 6, 5 layers = 30 3) 2 × 2 × 6 = 24 4) 5 × 5 × 1 = 25 5) layer 3 × 4, 3 layers = 36 6) Which is larger: 3×3×3 (27) or 2×4×3 (24)? 3×3×3. Observation: child builds without gaps and counts by layers.

## Science 5: Where is Earth's water?

**Prepare:** 1 L water bottle, measuring cups/spoons, eyedropper, graph paper, USGS water distribution data (Water Science School).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking most water is fresh. About 97% is salt water.
- Thinking most fresh water is in lakes and rivers. Most is ice and groundwater.
- Thinking water is unlimited because the water cycle recycles it.

### Day 1 (Monday): Model all the water
*Student:* Use 1 liter of water to model Earth's water, measuring out 97% ocean, then dividing the remaining 3% into ice, groundwater and surface water.

*How to teach:* Pour 970 mL into a container for ocean (97%). Divide the remaining 30 mL: about 20 mL ice, about 9 mL groundwater, and about 1 mL (a few drops) surface water.

- **Q:** Of Earth's water, about what percent is salt water?
  **A:** About 97%.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): USGS data
*Student:* Read USGS Water Science School data on water distribution and record percentages in a table.

*How to teach:* Read USGS data; record percentages. Read the USGS page together. Your child records percentages in a table.

- **Q:** Where is most of Earth's fresh water?
  **A:** In ice caps and glaciers (about 69%), then groundwater (about 30%).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Graphing
*Student:* Make a circle graph and a bar graph of salt water vs. fresh water and fresh-water reservoirs.

*How to teach:* Make circle and bar graphs. Help divide a circle into 97% and 3%. Your child makes a second graph of fresh water reservoirs.

- **Q:** What portion of fresh water is in lakes and rivers?
  **A:** Around 1% or less of fresh water.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Thinking about the data
*Student:* Answer questions like 'What fraction of fresh water is easily available?' using the graphs.

*How to teach:* Answer data questions. Your child answers questions using the graphs, citing numbers.

- **Q:** What fraction of fresh water is easily available?
  **A:** Only a tiny fraction (about 1%) is in lakes, rivers and soil.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write an explanation, using the graphs as evidence, for why fresh water is a limited resource.

*How to teach:* Your child writes why fresh water is limited, using graphs. Your child writes a paragraph with at least two percentages as evidence.

- **Q:** Why is fresh water limited?
  **A:** Only about 3% is fresh, and most of that is frozen or underground.

**Assessment:** Graphs and written explanation.

**Answer key:** Graphs: accurate proportions, labels. Explanation rubric: 4 = uses percentages from graphs and explains that easily available fresh water is tiny; 3 = mostly; 2 = general; 1 = incorrect.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Early battles and turning points

**Prepare:** Revolutionary War battle map; Valley Forge letter excerpt.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking Americans won every battle.
- Thinking Valley Forge was a battle.
- Thinking France joined immediately.

### Day 1 (Monday): Strengths and weaknesses
*Student:* Compare the strengths and weaknesses of the British and Continental armies in a chart.

*How to teach:* Your child charts strengths and weaknesses of both armies. Pause after the reading to ask your child to retell the key facts in their own words before writing anything.

- **Q:** American advantage?
  **A:** Fighting on home ground.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Bunker Hill to Trenton
*Student:* Map Bunker Hill, the loss of New York and Washington's crossing to Trenton.

*How to teach:* Map Bunker Hill, New York and Trenton. Model the first item yourself, then let your child finish while you check accuracy against the source.

- **Q:** Why was Trenton important?
  **A:** A surprise victory boosted morale.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Saratoga
*Student:* Read about Saratoga and explain why it convinced France to join the war.

*How to teach:* Read about Saratoga; your child explains why it brought France in. Ask 'How do you know?' and have your child point to the source or map for each answer.

- **Q:** Why significant?
  **A:** Convinced France to ally.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Valley Forge
*Student:* Read an excerpt of Washington's letter describing Valley Forge hardships and list soldiers' needs.

*How to teach:* Read Washington's letter; your child lists soldiers' needs. Before finishing, have your child explain their work aloud in 2–3 sentences; correct any errors then.

- **Q:** Needs?
  **A:** Food, clothing, shoes.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Create an annotated battle map with 5 battles and their significance.

*How to teach:* Your child creates an annotated battle map. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** Five battles?
  **A:** Lexington/Concord, Bunker Hill, Trenton, Saratoga, Yorktown.

**Assessment:** Annotated battle map.

**Answer key:** Annotated map: 5 battles with significance.

## Art & Music 5: Musical form: AB, ABA, rondo and theme and variations

**Prepare:** Find recordings: Mozart's 'Twelve Variations on Ah vous dirai-je, Maman,' K. 265; Beethoven's 'Für Elise'; Mouret's 'Rondeau.' Prepare colored shapes (A = red circle, B = blue square, C = green triangle).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a section must be identical to repeat. A returning section may change slightly but is recognizable.
- Confusing rondo and ABA. Rondo has A returning between several different sections (ABACA).

### Day 1 (Monday): AB and ABA
*Student:* Listen to two short pieces and label sections A and B using colored shapes.

*How to teach:* Listen to two short pieces; label A and B with shapes. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** What is ternary (ABA) form?
  **A:** A section, contrasting B section, then A returns.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Rondo
*Student:* Listen to Mouret's 'Rondeau' and map the ABACA form with a listening map.

*How to teach:* Map Mouret's 'Rondeau' (ABACA). Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** In a rondo, which section keeps returning?
  **A:** The A section.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Theme and variations
*Student:* Listen to Mozart's 'Twelve Variations on Ah vous dirai-je, Maman' and describe how 3 variations change the theme.

*How to teach:* Listen to Mozart's variations; describe how 3 change the theme. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** How can a composer vary a theme?
  **A:** Change rhythm, add notes, change from major to minor, change tempo or dynamics.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Movement map
*Student:* Create a movement routine that changes with each section of 'Für Elise' to show its form.

*How to teach:* Create a movement routine changing with each section of 'Für Elise' (A–B–A–C–A). Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What form is 'Für Elise'?
  **A:** Rondo (A B A C A).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 6 assessment
*Student:* Take a listening assessment identifying form, major/minor and melodic contour.

*How to teach:* Listening assessment: form, major/minor, contour. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** What is theme and variations?
  **A:** A melody presented and then changed in several ways.

**Assessment:** Listening map and Unit 6 assessment.

**Answer key:** Listening map: correctly shows sections for 'Rondeau' (ABACA). Unit 6 assessment: identifies form (AB, ABA, rondo, theme & variations), major vs minor, and contour of 3 melodies. Proficient = 80%.

## PE 5: Volleyball: forearm pass

**Prepare:** Gather a beach ball or volleyball trainer (soft), and set a rope 'net' between two chairs about 1.5–2 m high. Review cues: ready position, flat platform, legs lift, no swing. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Swinging the arms. Hold a still platform and lift with the legs.
- Hitting with the hands or fists. Contact the ball on the forearms above the wrists.

### Day 1 (Monday): Ready position
*Student:* Practice ready position and shuffling to a tossed ball.

*How to teach:* Ready position: feet shoulder-width, knees bent, weight forward, arms out. Toss balls to the side; your child shuffles (no crossing feet) to get behind the ball.

- **Q:** Why shuffle instead of run when moving to the ball?
  **A:** To stay square to the target and balanced in ready position.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Making a platform
*Student:* Practice the forearm platform with self-bumps of a beach ball.

*How to teach:* Make the platform: one hand in the other, thumbs together and pointing down, elbows locked. Self-bump a beach ball.

- **Q:** Where on your arms should the ball hit?
  **A:** On the flat part of the forearms, between the wrists and elbows.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Partner bumps
*Student:* Bump tosses from a partner back to them, counting consecutive good passes.

*How to teach:* Toss to your child; they bump back to you. Count consecutive good passes. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** Your bump goes straight up behind you. What happened?
  **A:** The arms swung too high or the platform was angled up; keep the platform still and aimed at the target, lift with the legs.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Bump to target
*Student:* Bump to a hula hoop target from tosses at different heights.

*How to teach:* Toss at different heights; your child bumps to a hula hoop target. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** How do you aim a bump?
  **A:** Angle the platform toward the target and face it (square shoulders).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Self-assess bump form with cues (flat platform, legs lift, no swing).

*How to teach:* Your child rates bump form on the three cues. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** Name the three bump cues.
  **A:** Flat platform, legs lift, no swing.

**Assessment:** Bump consecutive-count and cue self-assessment.

**Answer key:** Bump consecutive count recorded. Cue self-assessment (flat platform, legs lift, no swing, ready position) marked accurately. Proficient = 5+ consecutive bumps and 3 of 4 cues.

## Health 5: Tobacco and nicotine effects

**Prepare:** Gather straws (thin coffee stirrers work best), a jar with cotton balls (to show tar), and a body diagram. Read the CDC or NIDA for Teens fact sheet on tobacco.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking smoking only harms lungs. It harms the heart, blood vessels, mouth, teeth, skin and nearly every organ.
- Thinking you can't get addicted from 'just a few.' Teen brains get addicted to nicotine faster than adult brains.

### Day 1 (Monday): What's in a cigarette
*Student:* Read about nicotine, tar and carbon monoxide and label a body diagram with their effects.

*How to teach:* Read about nicotine (addictive drug), tar (sticky residue that coats lungs) and carbon monoxide (poison gas that reduces oxygen in blood). Label the body diagram with effects.

- **Q:** What does carbon monoxide do in the body?
  **A:** It replaces oxygen in the blood, so less oxygen reaches the body's organs and muscles.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Straw breathing
*Student:* Jog in place 1 minute, then breathe through a thin straw for 30 seconds to simulate damaged lungs; write a reflection.

*How to teach:* Jog in place 1 minute, then breathe only through a thin straw for 30 seconds (stop at once if dizzy). Write a reflection.

- **Q:** What did the straw activity model?
  **A:** How damaged lungs or narrowed airways from smoking make it hard to breathe.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Addiction and the brain
*Student:* Learn how nicotine changes the brain's reward system and why teens get addicted faster.

*How to teach:* Explain nicotine triggers dopamine in the reward system; the brain starts craving it. Teen brains are still developing, so addiction happens faster.

- **Q:** Why do teens get addicted to nicotine faster than adults?
  **A:** The teen brain is still developing and is more sensitive to nicotine's effects on the reward system.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Secondhand smoke
*Student:* Research secondhand smoke effects and CA smoke-free laws.

*How to teach:* Research secondhand smoke (causes asthma attacks, ear infections, lung problems in non-smokers) and CA laws (tobacco sales age 21, smoke-free workplaces, restaurants and parks).

- **Q:** What is secondhand smoke?
  **A:** Smoke breathed in by people near someone smoking.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a tobacco-effects quiz.

*How to teach:* Tobacco-effects quiz. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** Name three harmful substances in cigarette smoke.
  **A:** Nicotine, tar, carbon monoxide (also many other chemicals).

**Assessment:** Tobacco quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) nicotine = addictive drug 2) tar coats lungs 3) CO reduces oxygen in blood 4) teens get addicted faster 5) secondhand smoke harms others 6) CA legal age for tobacco = 21 7) effects: heart disease, lung cancer, stained teeth, bad breath. Proficient = 80%.
