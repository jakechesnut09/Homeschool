# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 28

## ELA 5: The Secret Garden chapters 8–15: the garden and Dickon

**Prepare:** Preview chapters 8–15 of The Secret Garden; prepare sentence-combining cards from the novel.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking sentence combining just means adding 'and.' Use clauses, appositives and phrases.
- Seeing Colin as just a villain. He is frightened and lonely like Mary was.
- Confusing the garden being 'dead' with 'wick' (alive).

### Day 1 (Monday): Chapters 8–10
*Student:* Read chapters 8–10 and compare the garden's condition to Mary's condition at the start.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 8–10. Ask your child to make a two-column chart: the garden's condition and Mary's condition, finding parallels.

- **Q:** How are Mary and the garden alike?
  **A:** Both seem neglected and dead but are actually alive inside and begin to grow with care.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Chapters 11–12
*Student:* Read chapters 11–12 and write a character sketch of Dickon with 3 quotations.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 11–12. Your child writes a character sketch of Dickon with 3 traits, each backed by a quotation.

- **Q:** Describe Dickon.
  **A:** Martha's brother, kind and cheerful, who can charm animals and knows how to make things grow.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Sentence combining
*Student:* Combine, expand and reduce 12 sentences from the novel to improve style.

*How to teach:* Model combining two short sentences with a clause or phrase, then expanding and reducing. Your child completes 12 cards and reads the improved versions aloud.

- **Q:** Combine: Dickon played a pipe. Animals came to him.
  **A:** When Dickon played his pipe, animals came to him.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Chapters 13–15
*Student:* Read chapters 13–15 and compare Mary and Colin as two 'contrary' children in a chart.

*How to teach:* Read chapters 13–15. Your child charts Mary and Colin: how each acts, why, and what they need.

- **Q:** Who is Colin?
  **A:** Mr. Craven's sickly son, Mary's cousin, who believes he will die young.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a descriptive paragraph of the garden in spring using combined sentences and sensory details.

*How to teach:* Brainstorm sights, sounds and smells of the spring garden from the book. Your child writes a paragraph using at least 3 combined sentences.

- **Q:** What makes descriptive writing strong?
  **A:** Sensory details and precise words.

**Assessment:** Character sketch; descriptive paragraph; comparison chart.

**Answer key:** Character sketch: 3 traits each supported by a quotation. Descriptive paragraph: sensory details, combined sentences, varied beginnings. Comparison chart: similarities (spoiled, lonely, contrary) and differences (Colin bedridden, Mary active).

## Math 5: Metric conversions and multi-step measurement problems

**Prepare:** Meter stick, liter bottle, measuring cup with mL, kitchen scale with grams. Write Unit 7 test.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up prefixes. kilo = 1,000; centi = 1/100; milli = 1/1,000.
- Moving the decimal the wrong way. Bigger unit → smaller unit gives a bigger number.
- Forgetting to convert all quantities to the same unit before computing.

### Day 1 (Monday): Metric length
*Student:* Measure objects in mm, cm and m and convert 15 measurements by multiplying or dividing by 10, 100 and 1,000.

*How to teach:* Measure objects in mm, cm, and m and convert using powers of 10. The child converts 15 measurements.

- **Q:** 2.5 m = ? cm = ? mm
  **A:** 250 cm; 2,500 mm.
- **Q:** 450 mm = ? cm
  **A:** 45 cm

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Metric mass and capacity
*Student:* Measure liquids in mL and L and masses in g and kg; solve 12 conversion problems.

*How to teach:* Measure water in mL/L and masses in g/kg; convert 12 problems. Pour water into a measuring cup in mL, then into a liter bottle. Your child converts 12 problems, using × 1,000 for L → mL and kg → g.

- **Q:** 3.2 kg = ? g
  **A:** 3,200 g
- **Q:** 750 mL = ? L
  **A:** 0.75 L

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Multi-step problems
*Student:* Solve 6 multi-step problems such as how many 250 mL cups can be filled from three 2 L bottles.

*How to teach:* Solve multi-step problems by converting first, then computing. Model the 250 mL cup problem step by step. Your child converts first in every problem, then computes, labeling units.

- **Q:** How many 250 mL cups can be filled from three 2 L bottles?
  **A:** 6 L = 6,000 mL; 6,000 ÷ 250 = 24 cups.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Unit review
*Student:* Complete a mixed customary and metric review and make a conversion reference card.

*How to teach:* Mixed review of customary and metric conversions; the child makes a reference card. Your child solves a mix of customary and metric problems and makes a card listing key equivalences (12 in = 1 ft, 1,000 mL = 1 L, etc.).

- **Q:** Which is longer: 1 yard or 1 meter?
  **A:** 1 meter (about 39 inches).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 test
*Student:* Take the Unit 7 test on converting measurement units.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 7 test. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** A 1.5 km walk is how many meters?
  **A:** 1,500 m

**Assessment:** Unit 7 test covering 5.MD.A.1.

**Answer key:** Unit 7 test key (suggested): 1) 4 ft = 48 in 2) 5 yd = 15 ft 3) 2 1/2 lb = 40 oz 4) 3 gal = 12 qt 5) 6 c = 48 fl oz 6) 3.6 m = 360 cm 7) 85 mm = 8.5 cm 8) 2.4 L = 2,400 mL 9) 500 g = 0.5 kg 10) 4 boxes of 750 g = 3 kg 11) 5 ft 3 in + 2 ft 11 in = 8 ft 2 in 12) A 2 m board cut into 25 cm pieces = 8 pieces.

## Science 5: Engineering a solution: reduce, reuse or protect

**Prepare:** Recycled materials, tape, scissors, water, vegetable oil, cotton, feathers, sponge. Write the Unit 8 test.

**Watch for:**
- Changing several variables.
- Thinking a prototype must work first try.
- Ignoring trade-offs.

### Day 1 (Monday): Define the problem
*Student:* Choose a problem (oil spill cleanup or water-saving planter) and define criteria and constraints.

*How to teach:* Choose a problem; define criteria and constraints. Discuss both problems; your child chooses one. They write two criteria and two constraints.

- **Q:** Criteria for oil spill cleanup?
  **A:** Removes the most oil, quickly.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Design options
*Student:* Sketch two possible solutions and compare their trade-offs.

*How to teach:* Sketch two solutions; compare trade-offs. Your child sketches two designs and lists one pro and one con of each.

- **Q:** What is a trade-off?
  **A:** Giving up one benefit to gain another.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Build and test
*Student:* Build a prototype from recycled materials and test it (e.g., cleaning a vegetable-oil spill from water with cotton, feathers, sponge).

*How to teach:* Build and test a prototype. Your child builds and tests, recording data (e.g., how much oil remains).

- **Q:** Which material absorbs oil best?
  **A:** Usually cotton or sponge.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Improve
*Student:* Identify a failure point, change one variable, and retest.

*How to teach:* Identify a failure point; change one variable; retest. Your child identifies the main failure and changes one thing.

- **Q:** Why retest?
  **A:** To see if the change improved results.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 8 assessment
*Student:* Present the solution and take the Unit 8 assessment on protecting Earth's resources.

*How to teach:* Present the solution; take the Unit 8 test. Your child presents; give the test.

- **Q:** How is a real oil spill cleaned?
  **A:** Booms contain oil; skimmers and absorbents remove it.

**Assessment:** Engineering notebook and presentation; Unit 8 test.

**Answer key:** Engineering notebook rubric: problem, sketches, data, improvement. Unit 8 test key (suggested): renewable vs. nonrenewable; human impacts; conservation strategies; engineering design steps.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: The Articles of Confederation

**Prepare:** Articles of Confederation excerpt; simulation cards.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the Articles had a president.
- Thinking Congress could tax.
- Thinking Shays' Rebellion was large.

### Day 1 (Monday): A weak union
*Student:* Read about the Articles of Confederation and list 5 powers Congress did and did not have.

*How to teach:* Read about the Articles; list 5 powers Congress had and did not have. Pause after the reading to ask your child to retell the key facts in their own words before writing anything.

- **Q:** What couldn't Congress do?
  **A:** Tax, regulate trade, enforce laws.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Simulation
*Student:* Play a simulation where each 'state' prints its own money and taxes trade, then discuss problems.

*How to teach:* Play the money-and-trade simulation; discuss problems. Model the first item yourself, then let your child finish while you check accuracy against the source.

- **Q:** Problems?
  **A:** Confusion, trade barriers.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Shays' Rebellion
*Student:* Read about Shays' Rebellion and explain why it alarmed leaders like Washington.

*How to teach:* Read about Shays' Rebellion; explain why it alarmed leaders. Ask 'How do you know?' and have your child point to the source or map for each answer.

- **Q:** Why alarming?
  **A:** Showed government too weak.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Critics' views
*Student:* Read quotes from critics of the Articles and match each to a weakness.

*How to teach:* Match critics' quotes to weaknesses. Before finishing, have your child explain their work aloud in 2–3 sentences; correct any errors then.

- **Q:** Weakness?
  **A:** No national courts.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining why leaders called the Constitutional Convention.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a paragraph on why the Convention was called. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** Why the Convention?
  **A:** To fix weaknesses.

**Assessment:** Weaknesses chart and paragraph.

**Answer key:** Chart: 5 powers. Paragraph rubric: 4 = clear topic sentence, three or more accurate, specific details or quotations from sources, explanation of how they support the point, conclusion; 3 = accurate with two details; 2 = general or partly inaccurate; 1 = off topic or incomplete.

## Art & Music 5: Found-object and environmental sculpture

**Prepare:** Find images of Andy Goldsworthy's nature art (leaves, stones, ice) and Louise Nevelson's wooden assemblages. Gather recycled objects (caps, cardboard tubes, buttons), a shoebox lid painted one color, glue, and a camera. Choose a safe outdoor spot for environmental art.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking art has to last forever. Ephemeral art is meant to change or disappear; the photograph records it.
- Gluing random objects. Assemblage works when objects are arranged by color, shape and repetition into a unified design.

### Day 1 (Monday): Artist study
*Student:* Look at Andy Goldsworthy's nature art and Louise Nevelson's assemblages and discuss materials and meaning.

*How to teach:* Compare Goldsworthy and Nevelson: materials and meaning. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** Why did Louise Nevelson paint assemblages one color?
  **A:** To unify many different found objects so viewers focus on shape, pattern and shadow.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Collecting
*Student:* Collect natural or recycled materials and sort them by color, shape and texture.

*How to teach:* Collect materials and sort by color, shape and texture. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** Why sort materials before building?
  **A:** It helps you see patterns and plan unity.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Environmental sculpture
*Student:* Build an ephemeral outdoor sculpture from natural materials and photograph it.

*How to teach:* Build an outdoor ephemeral sculpture; photograph it. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** What does ephemeral mean?
  **A:** Lasting only a short time.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Assemblage
*Student:* Assemble a small found-object sculpture in a shoebox lid painted one color.

*How to teach:* Assemble a found-object sculpture in a painted shoebox lid. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What is an assemblage?
  **A:** A 3D artwork made by combining found objects.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 7 assessment
*Student:* Write a comparison of your two sculptures and present photos in your portfolio.

*How to teach:* Write a comparison of the two sculptures; add photos to the portfolio. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** How are your two sculptures different?
  **A:** One is temporary and natural; the other is permanent and made of found objects.

**Assessment:** Unit 7 sculpture portfolio entry.

**Answer key:** Unit 7 portfolio entry: photos of both sculptures with a written comparison (materials, permanence, design principles, meaning). Rubric: 4 = clear comparison with art terms (unity, repetition, positive/negative space); 3 = some terms; 2 = vague; 1 = missing.

## PE 5: Batting fundamentals

**Prepare:** Gather a plastic or foam bat, wiffle balls, and a tee (or a traffic cone). Mark a safe zone: no one stands within two bat lengths of the batter. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Choking the bat with knuckles misaligned. Line up the 'door-knocking' knuckles for a loose, quick swing.
- Throwing the bat after hitting. Drop the bat before running.

### Day 1 (Monday): Grip and stance
*Student:* Learn the batting grip (knuckles aligned) and athletic stance, and practice dry swings.

*How to teach:* Teach the grip (dominant hand on top, knuckles aligned), athletic stance (feet shoulder-width, knees bent, side to the pitcher). Dry swings.

- **Q:** For a right-handed batter, which hand is on top?
  **A:** The right hand.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tee hitting
*Student:* Hit 30 balls off a tee, aiming for line drives.

*How to teach:* Hit 30 balls off a tee, aiming for line drives. Cue: swing level, eyes on the ball. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** What is a line drive?
  **A:** A hit that travels fairly straight and low through the air, not high up or on the ground.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Soft toss
*Student:* Hit soft tosses from a partner, tracking the ball to the bat.

*How to teach:* Soft toss from the side (from a safe angle); your child tracks the ball to the bat. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** Why watch the ball all the way to the bat?
  **A:** To time the swing and make solid contact.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Bat safety
*Student:* Review bat safety (safe zone, dropping not throwing the bat) and play a hitting-target game.

*How to teach:* Review bat safety (safe zone, drop the bat, only swing in the batter's box) and play a target hitting game.

- **Q:** After you hit the ball, what do you do with the bat?
  **A:** Drop it (not throw it) and run.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Record swing on video and check against batting cues.

*How to teach:* Video a swing and check cues. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** Name three batting cues.
  **A:** Knuckles aligned grip, side to the pitcher, step toward the pitcher, level swing, eyes on ball, follow through.

**Assessment:** Batting cue checklist.

**Answer key:** Batting cue checklist: grip knuckles aligned, balanced stance, small step toward pitcher, hips rotate, level swing, follow-through, drops bat safely. Proficient = 5 of 7.

## Health 5: Cannabis and inhalants

**Prepare:** Read NIDA for Teens articles on marijuana and inhalants. Gather household product labels (spray paint, glue, aerosol cleaner) to read warnings — do NOT open or sniff them. Save the Poison Control number (1-800-222-1222).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking cannabis is harmless because it's legal for adults in California. It's illegal under 21 and can harm memory, learning and reaction time in young people.
- Thinking household products are safe because they're sold in stores. Sniffing them can cause sudden death even the first time.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cannabis facts
*Student:* Read a NIDA for kids article on marijuana and list effects on memory, learning and reaction time.

*How to teach:* Read the NIDA article; your child lists effects on memory, learning and reaction time. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What chemical in cannabis causes its effects?
  **A:** THC.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Edibles danger
*Student:* Learn why cannabis edibles can look like candy and what to do if you find one.

*How to teach:* Discuss edibles that look like candy or cookies; if found, don't eat, tell an adult, call Poison Control if eaten.

- **Q:** What should you do if you find a candy that might be an edible?
  **A:** Don't eat it; leave it and tell a trusted adult right away.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Inhalants
*Student:* Read warning labels on household products and explain why sniffing them can cause sudden death.

*How to teach:* Read warning labels; explain that inhalant fumes can cause 'sudden sniffing death' (heart failure) and brain damage.

- **Q:** Why are inhalants so dangerous?
  **A:** They can cause the heart to fail suddenly, even the first time, and damage the brain.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Laws and norms
*Student:* Learn that cannabis is illegal under 21 in CA and review data showing most youth don't use it.

*How to teach:* Learn CA law (cannabis illegal under 21) and data showing most youth don't use it. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** How old must you be to legally use cannabis in California?
  **A:** 21.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a matching quiz on cannabis and inhalant effects.

*How to teach:* Matching quiz. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** Match: THC, inhalant, edible.
  **A:** THC = active chemical in cannabis; inhalant = fumes sniffed from household products; edible = cannabis in food.

**Assessment:** Matching quiz.

**Answer key:** Matching key: THC → chemical in cannabis that affects the brain; edible → food containing cannabis, can look like candy; inhalant → household product fumes; sudden sniffing death → heart failure from inhalants; Poison Control → 1-800-222-1222; legal age → 21. Proficient = 5 of 6.
