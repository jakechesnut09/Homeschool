# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 32

## ELA 5: Myths across cultures: comparing themes

**Prepare:** Find Prometheus (D'Aulaires), a Native American fire tale (Grandmother Spider / Coyote) and an Anansi tale. Comparison matrix.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all trickster tales have the same lesson.
- Judging other cultures' myths as less true. Treat all with respect.
- Comparing only plots, not themes.

### Day 1 (Monday): Prometheus
*Student:* Read the myth of Prometheus bringing fire and note what it explains and the hero's traits.

*How to teach:* Read the Prometheus myth. Your child notes what it explains (how humans got fire) and Prometheus's traits (brave, clever, defiant).

- **Q:** What did Prometheus do?
  **A:** Stole fire from the gods and gave it to humans; Zeus punished him by chaining him to a rock where an eagle ate his liver.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Another culture's fire story
*Student:* Read a Native American fire-bringing tale (e.g., Grandmother Spider or Coyote) and note what it explains.

*How to teach:* Read a Native American fire tale such as Grandmother Spider. Your child notes what it explains and how the hero succeeds.

- **Q:** In the Cherokee tale, who brings fire?
  **A:** Grandmother Spider, carrying it in a clay pot after Possum and Buzzard failed.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Trickster tales
*Student:* Read an Anansi tale and compare the trickster to Coyote and Prometheus in a matrix.

*How to teach:* Read an Anansi tale. Your child fills a matrix: hero, trick or deed, what is explained, lesson, for Prometheus, Coyote/Grandmother Spider and Anansi.

- **Q:** What is a trickster?
  **A:** A clever character who uses tricks to get what he wants.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Writing the comparison
*Student:* Write two paragraphs comparing how two myths approach the theme of sharing knowledge or fire.

*How to teach:* Your child writes two paragraphs: one on similarities and one on differences, with details from both myths.

- **Q:** Common theme?
  **A:** Fire/knowledge is precious and comes at a cost or through courage/cleverness.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a cold-read quiz comparing two new folktales on theme.

*How to teach:* Give two new short folktales and the cold-read quiz without help; score with the rubric.

- **Q:** How to compare themes?
  **A:** State each theme, then explain how they are alike and different with evidence.

**Assessment:** Comparison paragraphs; cold-read quiz.

**Answer key:** Comparison paragraphs: 4 = shared theme identified, details from both myths, differences explained; 3 = mostly; 2 = summaries; 1 = incomplete. Cold-read rubric (choose an unfamiliar grade-5 story from CommonLit): 4 = states a precise theme/central idea, explains character with 2+ exact quotations correctly punctuated and explained; 3 = accurate answer with at least one relevant quotation and explanation; 2 = accurate but general answer, evidence paraphrased or loosely connected; 1 = inaccurate or no evidence.

## Math 5: Attributes of two-dimensional figures

**Prepare:** Protractor, ruler, about 20 shape cut-outs (triangles, quadrilaterals, pentagons, hexagons), 12 triangle drawings.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking a triangle's orientation changes its type. Measure, don't judge by looks.
- Believing a triangle can have two right angles. The angles sum to 180°.
- Mixing up classification by sides (equilateral, isosceles, scalene) and by angles (acute, right, obtuse).

### Day 1 (Monday): Polygon attributes
*Student:* Sort 20 shape cut-outs by number of sides, parallel sides and right angles, recording attributes in a table.

*How to teach:* Sort shapes by number of sides, pairs of parallel sides, and right angles; record in a table. Your child sorts the cut-outs three ways and records attributes in a table. Ask which shapes ended up in more than one group.

- **Q:** A shape has 4 sides, 2 pairs of parallel sides and 4 right angles. Name it.
  **A:** Rectangle (possibly a square).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Classifying triangles
*Student:* Measure sides and angles of 12 triangles and classify each by sides and by angles.

*How to teach:* Measure sides and angles of 12 triangles and classify each two ways. Model measuring an angle with the protractor (line up the center and baseline). Your child records side lengths and angles and classifies each triangle by sides and by angles.

- **Q:** Sides 5, 5, 8 cm; angles 34°, 34°, 112°. Classify.
  **A:** Isosceles obtuse triangle.
- **Q:** Angles 60°, 60°, 60°?
  **A:** Equilateral, acute.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Can it exist?
*Student:* Try to draw 8 triangles (e.g., right obtuse, equilateral right) and explain which are impossible.

*How to teach:* Try to draw 8 triangles; explain which are impossible using the 180° angle sum. Your child tries to draw each triangle and explains why some are impossible (e.g., two right angles would already total 180°).

- **Q:** Can a triangle be right and obtuse?
  **A:** No — 90° + more than 90° already exceeds 180°.
- **Q:** Can a right triangle be isosceles?
  **A:** Yes, angles 90°, 45°, 45°.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Attribute riddles
*Student:* Write 5 'Who am I?' shape riddles using precise geometric attributes and solve a partner's riddles.

*How to teach:* Write 5 'Who am I?' riddles using precise attributes; solve the partner's. Model one riddle with 3 clues. Your child writes 5 riddles and solves yours; check that clues use precise words like parallel, right angle, congruent.

- **Q:** I have 3 sides, all different lengths, and one angle of 90°. Who am I?
  **A:** A scalene right triangle.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on polygon attributes and triangle classification.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz on polygon attributes and triangle classification. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** Can an equilateral triangle be right?
  **A:** No — all its angles are 60°.

**Assessment:** Quiz on attributes and classifying triangles.

**Answer key:** Suggested key: 1) pentagon = 5 sides 2) hexagon = 6 sides 3) a trapezoid has at least one pair of parallel sides 4) angles 90°, 30°, 60° = right scalene 5) sides 4, 4, 4 = equilateral acute 6) angles 100°, 40°, 40° = obtuse isosceles 7) impossible: two obtuse angles 8) a parallelogram has 2 pairs of parallel sides.

## Science 5: Shadows during the day

**Prepare:** Stick or pencil in clay, chalk, measuring tape, compass, flashlight, globe, toothpick. Pick a sunny day.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the sun moves around Earth.
- Thinking shadows are always the same length.
- Thinking noon shadows point south. In California they point north.

### Day 1 (Monday): Shadow stick
*Student:* Set up a shadow stick and mark and measure the shadow every hour from 9 a.m. to 3 p.m.

*How to teach:* Set up a shadow stick; mark and measure the shadow hourly from 9 a.m. to 3 p.m.

- **Q:** When is the shadow shortest?
  **A:** Near midday (solar noon), when the sun is highest.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Graphing shadows
*Student:* Graph shadow length vs. time of day and describe the pattern.

*How to teach:* Graph shadow length vs. time.

- **Q:** What pattern?
  **A:** Long in the morning, shortest at midday, long again in the afternoon.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Shadow direction
*Student:* Use a compass to record the shadow direction for each hour and explain why it moves.

*How to teach:* Record shadow direction with a compass. Use a compass to label N, S, E, W. Your child records direction each hour.

- **Q:** Why does the shadow move?
  **A:** Earth rotates, so the sun's position in the sky changes; shadows move from west (morning) toward east (afternoon).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Globe model
*Student:* Use a flashlight and globe to model day and night and shadows on a toothpick placed on California.

*How to teach:* Model with a flashlight, globe and toothpick on California. Darken the room; shine a flashlight on the globe. Rotate it and watch the toothpick's shadow.

- **Q:** What causes day and night?
  **A:** Earth's rotation.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Build a simple sundial and write an explanation of how Earth's rotation causes shadow patterns.

*How to teach:* Build a sundial; explain how rotation causes shadow patterns. Your child builds a paper sundial and writes an explanation.

- **Q:** How does a sundial tell time?
  **A:** The shadow moves predictably as Earth rotates.

**Assessment:** Shadow graph and written explanation.

**Answer key:** Graph: time vs length, U-shaped. Explanation rubric: 4 = Earth's rotation, sun's apparent motion, length and direction changes; 3 = mostly; 2 = partial; 1 = incorrect.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Crossing the Appalachians and new immigrants

**Prepare:** Map of 1850 U.S.; 'Low Bridge, Everybody Down' lyrics.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking canals were slow.
- Thinking immigrants came only for gold.
- Confusing the Cumberland Gap's location.

### Day 1 (Monday): Daniel Boone and the Cumberland Gap
*Student:* Trace the Wilderness Road through the Cumberland Gap and explain why it mattered.

*How to teach:* Trace the Wilderness Road through the Cumberland Gap. Pause after the reading to ask your child to retell the key facts in their own words before writing anything.

- **Q:** Why important?
  **A:** Opened Kentucky to settlement.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Canals and steamboats
*Student:* Compare travel times by wagon, flatboat, canal and steamboat and sing the Erie Canal song.

*How to teach:* Compare travel times; sing the Erie Canal song. Model the first item yourself, then let your child finish while you check accuracy against the source.

- **Q:** When did the Erie Canal open?
  **A:** 1825.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Immigrants
*Student:* Read about German and Irish immigrants before 1850 and why they came.

*How to teach:* Read about German and Irish immigrants. Ask 'How do you know?' and have your child point to the source or map for each answer.

- **Q:** Why did Irish come?
  **A:** Potato famine (1840s).

### Day 4 (Thursday): 1850 map
*Student:* Label the states and territories of 1850 and major rivers and mountain ranges.

*How to teach:* Label the 1850 map. Before finishing, have your child explain their work aloud in 2–3 sentences; correct any errors then.

- **Q:** Newest state in 1850?
  **A:** California.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on transportation and the 1850 map.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** What did the Erie Canal connect?
  **A:** Hudson River and Lake Erie.

**Assessment:** 1850 map and quiz.

**Answer key:** 1850 map: states, territories, rivers. Quiz key: Cumberland Gap, Erie Canal, steamboats, immigrants.

## Art & Music 5: Revising and presenting the composition

**Prepare:** Have the composition, feedback checklist, recorder or keyboard. Teach expressive marks: tempo words (Largo = slow, Andante = walking, Allegro = fast) and dynamics (p = soft, mf = medium loud, f = loud, crescendo/decrescendo).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking revision means starting over. Revision improves specific parts based on feedback.
- Leaving out tempo and dynamics. They tell performers how the music should feel.

### Day 1 (Monday): Revising
*Student:* Revise the composition based on feedback and document what changed.

*How to teach:* Revise based on feedback; document changes (before/after). Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** Why document changes?
  **A:** To show growth and explain choices.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Expressive marks
*Student:* Decide on tempo and dynamics and add them to the notation.

*How to teach:* Add tempo and dynamics to the notation. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What does 'p' mean? 'f'?
  **A:** p = piano (soft); f = forte (loud).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Practicing
*Student:* Practice performing the melody on recorder or keyboard, or prepare the digital version.

*How to teach:* Practice performing on recorder/keyboard or prepare the digital version. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** What tempo marking means 'fast'?
  **A:** Allegro.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Program note
*Student:* Write a program note explaining the title, mood and choices.

*How to teach:* Write a program note: title, mood, choices. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** What is a program note?
  **A:** A short written explanation for the audience about a piece.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 8 assessment
*Student:* Present the composition with its program note and evaluate it using the class criteria.

*How to teach:* Present with the program note; evaluate with criteria. Set up a simple 'stage' and audience, have your child introduce the work, and hold applause until the end; afterward ask them to name one strength and one thing to improve before you add one specific comment.

- **Q:** How do your dynamics support the mood?
  **A:** Answer varies; should connect markings to mood (e.g., soft for calm).

**Assessment:** Final composition and program note rubric.

**Answer key:** Rubric (1–4 each): revised melody with documented changes; tempo and dynamics marked; performance or polished digital version; program note explains title, mood and choices. Proficient = 3+ each.

## PE 5: FITT principle and planning

**Prepare:** Gather baseline FITNESSGRAM scores, a planning template (calendar), and an activity log. Know the FITT principle. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking more is always better. Increase gradually (overload) and include rest days.
- Confusing type with intensity. Type = what activity; intensity = how hard.

### Day 1 (Monday): FITT
*Student:* Learn the FITT principle and classify 10 activities by type and intensity.

*How to teach:* Explain FITT (Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type). Classify 10 activities (walking, sprinting, jump rope, yoga, push-ups, etc.).

- **Q:** What does each letter of FITT stand for?
  **A:** Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Reviewing baseline
*Student:* Review your baseline FITNESSGRAM scores and choose two goals.

*How to teach:* Review baseline scores and choose two goals. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** Why base goals on your scores?
  **A:** Scores show which fitness areas need improvement.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Drafting a plan
*Student:* Draft a 4-week plan using FITT for each goal.

*How to teach:* Draft a 4-week FITT plan for each goal. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** Write a FITT plan for building aerobic capacity.
  **A:** Example: F—4 days/week, I—moderate to vigorous, T—20–30 minutes, Type—jogging or jump rope.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Activity log
*Student:* Start a daily activity log counting minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity.

*How to teach:* Start a daily activity log of moderate-to-vigorous minutes. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** How many minutes of activity per day are recommended for kids?
  **A:** At least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Do the first workout of your plan and record it.

*How to teach:* Do the first workout and record it. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** What is the overload principle?
  **A:** To improve, gradually increase the demand (more reps, time or intensity) beyond what you normally do.

**Assessment:** Personal fitness plan draft.

**Answer key:** Fitness plan rubric: 4 = two goals tied to baseline scores, each with complete FITT details, progression across 4 weeks and rest; 3 = FITT complete but no progression; 2 = incomplete FITT; 1 = missing.

## Health 5: Decision making in risky situations

**Prepare:** Write a values list (health, family, sports, school, friendship, honesty, faith) and two scenario cards (sleepover with alcohol; friend's prescription pills). Print decision charts (problem, options, consequences, decision, evaluate).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking there are only two options (go along or lose the friend). Brainstorm several options, including leaving, calling a parent or suggesting another activity.
- Ignoring long-term consequences. Consider health, family rules, legal and future goals.

### Day 1 (Monday): Values
*Student:* Rank personal values and explain how they guide decisions about drugs.

*How to teach:* Your child ranks values and explains how they guide decisions. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** How can your values help you decide about drugs?
  **A:** If you value health or sports, using drugs conflicts with those values, so you choose not to.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Decision chart 1
*Student:* Use the decision chart for a sleepover scenario where someone brings alcohol.

*How to teach:* Complete the decision chart for the sleepover scenario. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** What are two good options if someone brings alcohol to a sleepover?
  **A:** Call a parent to pick you up; tell the host's parent; suggest another activity; refuse and stay away.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Decision chart 2
*Student:* Use the decision chart for a scenario involving a friend's prescription pills.

*How to teach:* Complete the chart for the prescription pills scenario. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** A friend offers you a pill from their mom's prescription for a headache. What should you do?
  **A:** Refuse—prescriptions are only for the person they're prescribed to—and tell a trusted adult.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Negotiation
*Student:* Practice negotiating a safe alternative activity with a friend.

*How to teach:* Practice negotiating a safe alternative with a friend. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** What is negotiation?
  **A:** Working out a compromise both people can agree to, such as choosing a safe activity instead.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a decision-making assessment with a new scenario.

*How to teach:* Decision-making assessment with a new scenario. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What is the last step of the decision model?
  **A:** Evaluate the decision—was it healthy and would you do it again?

**Assessment:** Decision-making assessment.

**Answer key:** Decision assessment rubric: 4 = clearly states the problem, lists 3+ options, weighs positive and negative consequences, makes a healthy decision, evaluates; 3 = all steps but limited options; 2 = skips steps; 1 = unhealthy decision or incomplete.
