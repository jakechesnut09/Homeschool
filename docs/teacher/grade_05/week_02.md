# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 2

## ELA 5: 'The Ransom of Red Chief' by O. Henry; character and plot

**Prepare:** Download 'The Ransom of Red Chief' (O. Henry, in Whirligigs on Project Gutenberg) and preview it. Parental note: the story uses dated 1900s vocabulary and includes the boy playing 'Indian' with stereotyped language; plan to discuss why that is hurtful today. Prepare a story map and Venn diagram.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking irony is just coincidence. Situational irony is when the outcome is the opposite of what is expected (kidnappers pay the father).
- Writing a summary that retells every detail. A summary hits beginning, conflict, turning point and ending in about 5 sentences.
- Confusing prepositions with adverbs. A preposition always has an object: 'up the tree' (prepositional phrase) vs. 'climbed up' (adverb).

### Day 1 (Monday): Reading the story
*Student:* Read 'The Ransom of Red Chief' (partner reading with an adult; the story contains dated language, which is discussed) and mark the moments where the plan backfires.

*How to teach:* Partner-read the story, stopping to explain old words (ransom, diatribe, palatable). Your child marks each moment the plan backfires with a sticky note. Discuss the stereotyped 'Indian' play and why it is offensive.

- **Q:** What is Sam and Bill's plan, and how does it backfire?
  **A:** They kidnap Ebenezer Dorset's son to collect a ransom, but the boy ('Red Chief') loves camping out and torments Bill so badly that the kidnappers end up desperate to get rid of him.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Word study: prefixes
*Student:* Study prefixes un-, re-, dis-, mis-, pre- with 20 multisyllabic words, sorting and defining them; read 10 tricky words from the story by syllables.

*How to teach:* Teach the five prefixes (un- = not, re- = again, dis- = not/opposite, mis- = wrongly, pre- = before). Your child sorts and defines 20 words, then reads 10 long story words by syllables.

- **Q:** What does 'misjudge' mean? 'preheat'?
  **A:** To judge wrongly; to heat before.
- **Q:** Divide and read 'philanthropist'.
  **A:** phil-an-thro-pist.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Comparing characters
*Student:* Use a Venn diagram to compare Bill and Sam with Red Chief, citing three details for each, and write a 5-sentence summary of the plot.

*How to teach:* Fill the Venn diagram with three details for Bill and Sam and three for Red Chief (e.g., Red Chief: wild, energetic, enjoys himself). Your child writes a 5-sentence plot summary.

- **Q:** How does the ransom end?
  **A:** Dorset refuses to pay and instead offers to take his son back if the kidnappers pay him $250; Sam and Bill pay and hurry away.
- **Q:** What do Bill and Sam have in common?
  **A:** Both are small-time crooks who want money and both end up exhausted and outsmarted by the boy.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Narrative planning
*Student:* Choose a small moment from the notebook and fill a story arc (beginning, rising action, climax, resolution); mini-lesson on prepositions and prepositional phrases.

*How to teach:* Your child picks a small moment and fills the story arc. Mini-lesson: underline prepositional phrases (in the cave, after dinner); write 5 sentences with them.

- **Q:** Underline the prepositional phrase: Red Chief hid behind the rock.
  **A:** behind the rock (preposition: behind; object: rock).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a reading check on situational irony and plot sequence and a prefix quiz; share the story arc aloud.

*How to teach:* Give a short reading check (situational irony, sequence of 5 events) and a 10-word prefix quiz, then your child tells the story arc aloud. Check that your child explains the irony in their own words (kidnappers end up paying) and gets the 5 events in order.

- **Q:** Why is the ending situational irony?
  **A:** We expect the father to pay the kidnappers, but the kidnappers pay the father.

**Assessment:** Summary and Venn diagram; prefix quiz; narrative plan check-in.

**Answer key:** Summary/Venn: 4 = 5-sentence summary with conflict, turning point and the twist ending (kidnappers pay $250), three accurate details per character; 3 = accurate but missing one element; 2 = retelling with errors; 1 = incomplete. Prefix quiz key (sample): unhappy = not happy; reread = read again; disagree = not agree; misspell = spell wrongly; preview = view before; unlock, redo, dishonest, misplace, prepay similarly. Narrative plan: beginning, rising action, climax, resolution all filled for one small moment.

## Math 5: Powers of 10 and exponents

**Prepare:** Place value chart, metric ruler or tape measure, calculator for checking only. Choose 5 household objects to measure (book, spoon, pencil, remote, cup).

**Watch for:**
- Counting zeros in the power of 10 but 'adding zeros' to decimals (3.42 × 100 = 3.4200). Use the chart: digits shift left two places, giving 342.
- Reading 10^3 as 10 × 3 = 30. Have the child write 10^3 = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000.
- Forgetting placeholder zeros when dividing (45 ÷ 1,000 written as 0.45). Shift digits three places right and fill empty places with zeros: 0.045.

### Day 1 (Monday): Multiply by powers of 10
*Student:* Solve 20 problems like 3.42 x 100 on a place value chart, then describe the pattern of how many places digits shift.

*How to teach:* Write 3.42 on the chart and multiply by 10, 100 and 1,000, moving digits left one, two, three places. The child solves 20 problems and writes the pattern: the number of zeros in the power of 10 tells how many places digits shift left.

- **Q:** 3.42 × 100 = ?
  **A:** 342
- **Q:** 0.075 × 1,000 = ?
  **A:** 75

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Divide by powers of 10
*Student:* Solve 20 problems like 45 / 1,000 = 0.045 and explain why zeros must be added as placeholders.

*How to teach:* Model 45 ÷ 10, ÷ 100, ÷ 1,000 on the chart, shifting digits right and filling empty places with zeros. The child solves 20 problems and explains why placeholder zeros are needed.

- **Q:** 45 ÷ 1,000 = ?
  **A:** 0.045 — digits shift three places right; a zero holds the tenths place.
- **Q:** 6.2 ÷ 100 = ?
  **A:** 0.062

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Exponents
*Student:* Write 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000 as 10^1 through 10^4; expand numbers like 6 x 10^3 and solve 15 mixed exponent problems.

*How to teach:* Show 10^1 = 10, 10^2 = 100, 10^3 = 1,000, 10^4 = 10,000: the exponent tells how many 10s are multiplied (and how many zeros). Do 6 × 10^3 = 6,000 together, then the child solves 15 mixed problems.

- **Q:** Write 10,000 as a power of 10.
  **A:** 10^4
- **Q:** 2.5 × 10^2 = ?
  **A:** 250
- **Q:** 73.2 ÷ 10^2 = ?
  **A:** 0.732

### Day 4 (Thursday): Metric connection
*Student:* Measure 5 household objects in centimeters and convert to meters and millimeters by multiplying or dividing by powers of 10.

*How to teach:* Measure 5 objects in centimeters and record in a table. Convert to millimeters (× 10) and meters (÷ 100). Check each conversion makes sense (meters should be the smallest number).

- **Q:** A pencil is 18 cm long. How long is it in mm and in m?
  **A:** 180 mm and 0.18 m.
- **Q:** Why do you divide by 100 to go from cm to m?
  **A:** There are 100 centimeters in a meter, so a centimeter is 1/100 of a meter.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a 15-problem quiz with powers of 10 and exponents and correct any errors using the place value chart.

*How to teach:* Give the 15-problem quiz; the child then fixes each error on the place value chart and says what went wrong. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** 4 × 10^3 + 5 × 10^1 = ?
  **A:** 4,050

**Assessment:** Quiz on multiplying/dividing by powers of 10 and exponent notation; check of the metric measuring table.

**Answer key:** Suggested 15-item key: 1) 2.7 × 10 = 27 2) 0.48 × 100 = 48 3) 5.6 × 1,000 = 5,600 4) 39 ÷ 10 = 3.9 5) 7 ÷ 100 = 0.07 6) 254 ÷ 1,000 = 0.254 7) 10^3 = 1,000 8) 10^5 = 100,000 9) 8 × 10^2 = 800 10) 3.1 × 10^3 = 3,100 11) 64 ÷ 10^2 = 0.64 12) 0.9 × 10^1 = 9 13) 1,000,000 = 10^6 14) 12.5 ÷ 10 = 1.25 15) 0.006 × 100 = 0.6. Metric table: each cm value × 10 = mm and ÷ 100 = m (e.g., 25 cm = 250 mm = 0.25 m); all 5 rows correct = proficient.

## Science 5: Gases are matter too

**Prepare:** Balloons, a ruler or dowel, string, a needle-free syringe, a plastic bottle, a ball and pump, kitchen scale, a cup, a paper towel and a bowl of water. Bookmark PhET 'States of Matter: Basics' (free online).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking air is 'nothing.' Air is matter: it takes up space and has weight.
- Believing a pumped-up ball weighs the same. Extra air particles add a small amount of weight.
- Thinking particles in a gas get smaller when compressed. The particles stay the same size; the spaces between them shrink.

### Day 1 (Monday): Air takes up space
*Student:* Push an upside-down cup with a paper towel inside into a bowl of water and explain why the towel stays dry.

*How to teach:* Stuff a dry paper towel in the bottom of a cup, flip it upside down and push it straight down into the bowl of water. Lift it out and check the towel. Your child explains why it stayed dry.

- **Q:** Why does the paper towel stay dry?
  **A:** Air trapped in the cup takes up space and keeps the water from coming in.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Air has weight
*Student:* Build a balance from a ruler and two balloons, then pop one and observe the balance tip; record findings.

*How to teach:* Hang two equal inflated balloons from each end of a ruler balanced on a string. Pop one balloon (carefully, safety glasses or at arm's length) and observe the tip. Record findings.

- **Q:** Which way does the balance tip after one balloon is popped, and why?
  **A:** It tips toward the full balloon, because the air inside it has weight (the popped balloon lost its compressed air).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Compressing air
*Student:* Pump air into a deflated ball and weigh it before and after; compress air in a capped syringe and describe the particles.

*How to teach:* Weigh a deflated ball, pump it up and weigh again. Then cap a syringe with your finger and push the plunger. Your child describes what happens to the particles.

- **Q:** Why does the pumped ball weigh more?
  **A:** More air particles were added, and air has weight.
- **Q:** What happens to air particles when you push the syringe?
  **A:** They are pushed closer together (compressed), but they are not destroyed.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Particle simulation
*Student:* Use the PhET 'States of Matter: Basics' simulation to compare particle motion in solids, liquids and gases and sketch each.

*How to teach:* Explore the PhET simulation with your child, switching between solid, liquid and gas. Your child sketches particle arrangements and describes motion for each.

- **Q:** How do particles move in a solid, liquid and gas?
  **A:** Solid: vibrate in place, tightly packed; liquid: close together but slide past each other; gas: far apart and moving fast in all directions.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Revise last week's particle model to include gases and explain how particles too small to see can still have weight.

*How to teach:* Your child revises the week 1 particle model to include gases and writes how particles too small to see can still have weight. Lay the two models side by side and ask what needs to change. Check that gas particles are drawn far apart with motion arrows and that the written explanation cites the balloon or ball evidence.

- **Q:** How can particles too small to see have weight?
  **A:** Each particle has a tiny mass; billions together add up to a weight we can measure, like the air in a ball.

**Assessment:** Revised particle model with written explanation; investigation data tables.

**Answer key:** Revised model: shows gas particles far apart and moving, labels solid/liquid/gas, explains that each particle has mass. Data tables: ball weight before < after; balance tipped toward full balloon; towel dry. Proficient explanation uses evidence from at least two investigations.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Peoples of the desert Southwest and Pacific Northwest

**Prepare:** Bookmark NPS photos of Mesa Verde and Taos Pueblo and images of Tlingit or Kwakwaka'wakw totem poles. Make salt dough (2 cups flour, 1 cup salt, ~3/4 cup water).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking all Native peoples lived in tipis. Housing depended on the environment: adobe, cedar plank houses, longhouses, tipis.
- Speaking of Native peoples only in the past tense. The Pueblo, Tlingit and Kwakwaka'wakw nations exist today.
- Thinking totem poles are worshipped idols. They tell family histories, honor ancestors and record events.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cliff dwellers
*Student:* Explore NPS photos of Mesa Verde cliff dwellings and list how the people used the land, water and stone.

*How to teach:* Look at the Mesa Verde photos together and ask: what materials did they build with? Where did water come from? Your child lists at least 5 ways the Ancestral Puebloans used land, water and stone.

- **Q:** Why build homes in cliff alcoves?
  **A:** For protection from weather and enemies; stone overhangs gave shade in summer and shelter in winter.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Pueblo life
*Student:* Read about Pueblo farming of corn, beans and squash and build a model adobe dwelling from salt dough.

*How to teach:* Read about the 'Three Sisters' (corn, beans, squash) and adobe building. Your child shapes a multi-story adobe dwelling with ladders and explains its features.

- **Q:** Why were adobe homes good for the desert?
  **A:** Thick clay-brick walls keep the inside cool by day and warm at night; materials (clay, straw) were local.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Pacific Northwest
*Student:* Read about the Tlingit and Kwakwaka'wakw use of salmon and cedar and analyze a photo of a totem pole as a primary source.

*How to teach:* Read about salmon and cedar in the Pacific Northwest. Analyze a totem pole photo: what figures do you see, who made it, what might it tell?

- **Q:** Why were salmon and cedar so important?
  **A:** Salmon was the main food, plentiful in rivers; cedar was used for plank houses, canoes, clothing and totem poles.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing regions
*Student:* Complete a comparison chart of shelter, food, economy, government and customs for the two regions.

*How to teach:* Your child completes a chart comparing shelter, food, economy, government and customs for the Southwest and Pacific Northwest. Before finishing, have your child explain their work aloud in 2–3 sentences; correct any errors then.

- **Q:** Give one difference in economy.
  **A:** Pueblo peoples farmed corn in a dry land; Northwest peoples relied on abundant fish and trade and held potlatches to share wealth.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining how environment shaped daily life in one region, using 3 details.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a paragraph explaining how environment shaped daily life in one region, using 3 details from the week. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** What is a potlatch?
  **A:** A ceremonial feast in the Pacific Northwest where hosts gave gifts to show status and mark important events.

**Assessment:** Comparison chart and explanatory paragraph.

**Answer key:** Chart: 5 categories filled accurately for both regions. Paragraph rubric: 4 = clear topic sentence, three or more accurate, specific details or quotations from sources, explanation of how they support the point, conclusion; 3 = accurate with two details; 2 = general or partly inaccurate; 1 = off topic or incomplete.

## Art & Music 5: Value and shading

**Prepare:** Gather HB, 2B and 4B pencils (B pencils are softer and darker), a blending stump or tissue, an egg or ball, and a flashlight. Find a Leonardo da Vinci drawing on Google Arts & Culture (e.g., a drapery study or 'Head of a Woman').

**Watch for:**
- Outlining shaded objects with a dark line. Real objects show edges through changes in value, not outlines.
- Forgetting the cast shadow or attaching it wrongly. The cast shadow falls on the surface on the side opposite the light.

### Day 1 (Monday): Value scale
*Student:* Create a 7-step value scale using pencil pressure and another using hatching and cross-hatching.

*How to teach:* Draw a 7-box strip; shade from white to darkest by pressure. Make a second strip using hatching (parallel lines) and cross-hatching (crossed lines) — closer lines are darker.

- **Q:** How do you make a darker value with hatching?
  **A:** Draw lines closer together, add more layers of crossed lines (cross-hatching), or press harder.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Shading forms
*Student:* Shade a sphere, cube and cylinder showing highlight, core shadow and cast shadow.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate one light source; your child shades a sphere, cube and cylinder with highlight, core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow.

- **Q:** If the light comes from the upper left, where will the cast shadow fall?
  **A:** On the surface to the lower right of the object.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Observing light
*Student:* Light an egg with a flashlight and draw it from observation, matching values.

*How to teach:* Darken the room, shine a flashlight on an egg, and draw it, matching values to the value scale.

- **Q:** What is a highlight?
  **A:** The brightest spot on an object, where light hits it most directly.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Artist study
*Student:* Look at a Leonardo da Vinci drawing on Google Arts & Culture and identify how he used value.

*How to teach:* Look at a Leonardo drawing; your child points to areas of hatching and soft blending. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** How did Leonardo show form in his drawings?
  **A:** With careful value changes — hatching lines (often diagonal, since he was left-handed) and soft gradual shading to make forms look round.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Complete a shaded still life of 2 objects and self-assess using a value checklist.

*How to teach:* Your child draws a 2-object still life and checks: full range of values, highlight, core shadow, cast shadow, consistent light direction.

- **Q:** Why must all shadows go the same direction?
  **A:** There's one light source, so all shadows fall consistently away from it; otherwise the drawing looks unrealistic.

**Assessment:** Value scales and shaded still life.

**Answer key:** Value scales: 7 even steps from white to near black (pressure) and with hatching/cross-hatching. Still life checklist: (1) one consistent light source (2) highlight (3) core shadow (4) cast shadow (5) at least 5 distinct values. 4 = all five; 3 = four; 2 = two or three; 1 = one or none.

## PE 5: Heart rate and aerobic capacity

**Prepare:** Gather a stopwatch, two cones 20 m apart, and a FITNESSGRAM PACER audio track (free versions are online). Measure a quarter mile (e.g., one lap of a school track, or map a route). Prepare a simple graph with walk/jog/sprint on the x-axis. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Using the thumb to take a pulse. The thumb has its own pulse; use the index and middle fingers lightly on the wrist (thumb side) or neck.
- Starting the PACER too fast. The early levels are slow on purpose — jogging at the beep, not sprinting, saves energy for later levels.

### Day 1 (Monday): Finding a pulse
*Student:* Find your pulse at wrist and neck and count resting heart rate for 15 seconds x 4.

*How to teach:* Show your child how to find the radial pulse (thumb side of the wrist) and carotid pulse (side of the neck beside the windpipe). Sitting quietly, count beats for 15 seconds and multiply by 4.

- **Q:** You count 21 beats in 15 seconds. What is your heart rate?
  **A:** 84 beats per minute (21 × 4).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Heart rate experiment
*Student:* Measure heart rate after walking, jogging and sprinting for 1 minute each and graph the results.

*How to teach:* Walk 1 minute and take pulse; rest until close to resting; jog 1 minute and take pulse; rest; sprint 1 minute (or 30 seconds) and take pulse. Your child graphs the three numbers.

- **Q:** Predict which activity gives the highest heart rate and explain why.
  **A:** Sprinting — working muscles need much more oxygen, so the heart beats faster to pump more blood.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): PACER practice
*Student:* Practice the 20-meter PACER shuttle run with pacing strategy for 10 minutes.

*How to teach:* Explain the PACER: run 20 m before each beep, which gets faster each level. Practice for 10 minutes, coaching 'arrive with the beep, turn, go' rather than racing ahead.

- **Q:** What is the best pacing strategy on the PACER?
  **A:** Run just fast enough to reach the line as the beep sounds; don't sprint early levels, so you save energy for the faster ones.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Steady pace jog
*Student:* Jog-walk a measured quarter mile four times, trying to hold an even pace.

*How to teach:* Time each of four quarter-mile jog-walks and write the times down. Coach your child to keep each lap within about 15 seconds of the first.

- **Q:** Your lap times were 2:30, 2:40, 3:20, 3:45. What does that show about your pacing?
  **A:** The pace was not even — you started too fast and slowed a lot. Start slower so later laps stay close to the first.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain to a parent why heart rate increases with exercise and record a heart rate chart.

*How to teach:* Your child explains to you, using their graph, why heart rate rises with exercise, then records today's resting and post-exercise rates on a chart.

- **Q:** Why does heart rate go up when you exercise?
  **A:** Muscles working harder need more oxygen and fuel, so the heart beats faster to pump more oxygen-rich blood to them.

**Assessment:** Heart rate graph and explanation.

**Answer key:** Heart rate graph: correctly labeled axes; three bars or points with walk < jog < sprint; resting rate typically about 60–100 bpm. Explanation (proficient): 'Exercise makes muscles need more oxygen, so the heart pumps faster to deliver it; harder exercise = higher heart rate.' Rubric 4 = accurate graph plus explanation with oxygen/muscle link; 3 = graph plus general explanation; 2 = incomplete graph or vague explanation; 1 = missing.

## Health 5: Body systems working together

**Prepare:** Get butcher paper (or tape printer paper together), markers, a stopwatch and a ruler. Preview the KidsHealth article on the endocrine system ('Your Endocrine System' for kids).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking each body system works alone. Systems constantly work together — e.g., running uses muscles, bones, nerves, heart and lungs at once.
- Thinking blood vessels and the heart are part of the respiratory system. They are the circulatory system; the respiratory system is the airways and lungs.

### Day 1 (Monday): Body map
*Student:* Trace your body on butcher paper and sketch the skeletal, muscular and circulatory systems with labels.

*How to teach:* Trace your child's body. They sketch and label major bones (skull, ribs, spine, femur), a few muscles (biceps, quadriceps, heart muscle) and the heart with main blood vessels.

- **Q:** What is the job of the skeletal system?
  **A:** Support the body, protect organs (skull protects brain, ribs protect heart and lungs), and work with muscles to move; bones also make blood cells.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Heart and lungs
*Student:* Take your resting pulse, do 2 minutes of jumping jacks, retake it and explain how the circulatory and respiratory systems responded.

*How to teach:* Take resting pulse, 2 minutes of jumping jacks, retake. Your child explains what the heart and lungs did.

- **Q:** Why did your breathing and pulse both speed up?
  **A:** Muscles needed more oxygen and had more carbon dioxide to remove, so the lungs breathed faster and the heart pumped faster to carry oxygen in the blood.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Nervous system
*Student:* Do a ruler-drop reaction-time test 5 times and record results; write how brain, nerves and muscles worked together.

*How to teach:* Hold a ruler at the 30 cm mark above your child's open fingers; drop it without warning 5 times and record where they catch it.

- **Q:** Which systems worked together to catch the ruler?
  **A:** Nervous system (eyes send signal to brain, brain sends signal through nerves) and muscular system (hand muscles close).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Endocrine system
*Student:* Read a KidsHealth article on glands and hormones and add the pituitary, thyroid and adrenal glands to your body map.

*How to teach:* Read the endocrine article. Your child adds the pituitary (base of brain), thyroid (front of neck) and adrenal glands (on top of the kidneys) to the body map.

- **Q:** What do glands of the endocrine system make, and how do they travel?
  **A:** Hormones — chemical messengers carried in the bloodstream.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Use the body map to explain to family how three systems work together when you run; complete a labeling quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child explains how three systems work during running, then labels a blank diagram. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** When you run, how do the muscular, circulatory and respiratory systems work together?
  **A:** Muscles move bones; lungs take in oxygen; the heart pumps oxygen-rich blood to muscles and carries away carbon dioxide.

**Assessment:** Labeled body map and quiz.

**Answer key:** Body map: correctly placed and labeled skeletal (skull, ribs, spine, femur), muscular (biceps, quadriceps), circulatory (heart, arteries/veins), endocrine (pituitary, thyroid, adrenals). Labeling quiz: brain, heart, lungs, pituitary (base of brain), thyroid (neck), adrenals (top of kidneys), femur (thigh), ribs (chest). Proficient = 80% correct and an accurate 'running' explanation linking three systems.
