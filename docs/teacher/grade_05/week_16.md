# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 16

## ELA 5: Lyric poetry: Frost and Dickinson

**Prepare:** Print 'The Road Not Taken' and 'Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening' by Robert Frost and 'Hope is the thing with feathers' by Emily Dickinson. Make a list of 12 idioms/proverbs.

**Watch for:**
- Reading 'The Road Not Taken' as simply 'always choose the unusual path.' The speaker admits the roads were 'really about the same'; it is about choices and how we tell our story later.
- Taking idioms literally. Explain figurative meanings.
- Thinking the repeated last line in 'Stopping by Woods' is a mistake. Repetition adds weight.

### Day 1 (Monday): 'The Road Not Taken'
*Student:* Read the poem twice and discuss what the two roads symbolize; write the theme in one sentence.

*How to teach:* Read twice; discuss what the roads symbolize; your child writes the theme in one sentence. Read it twice aloud. Ask: Were the roads really different? (The poem says they were 'really about the same.') What does the speaker say he will tell 'ages and ages hence'?

- **Q:** What do the two roads represent?
  **A:** Choices in life.
- **Q:** Write a theme.
  **A:** The choices we make shape our lives, even when we can't know where they will lead.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): 'Stopping by Woods'
*Student:* Analyze the speaker's point of view and why the last line repeats; mark the rhyme scheme.

*How to teach:* Analyze the speaker's point of view and the repeated last line; mark rhyme scheme. Ask who is speaking, where he is, and what pulls him to stay and what makes him leave. Label end rhymes A, B.

- **Q:** Why does Frost repeat 'And miles to go before I sleep'?
  **A:** It emphasizes the speaker's duties and long journey; the second time it can also suggest life and death.
- **Q:** Rhyme scheme of stanza 1?
  **A:** AABA.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dickinson's metaphor
*Student:* Read 'Hope is the thing with feathers' and chart how hope is compared to a bird in each stanza.

*How to teach:* Chart how hope is compared to a bird in each stanza. Make a three-row chart, one per stanza: what the bird does and what that says about hope.

- **Q:** What does it mean that hope 'never stops - at all'?
  **A:** Hope keeps singing inside us even in hard times.
- **Q:** What does the bird ask in return?
  **A:** Nothing — 'never… asked a crumb' of the speaker.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Idioms and proverbs
*Student:* Learn 12 common idioms, adages and proverbs ('cross that bridge when you come to it') and connect two to the poems.

*How to teach:* Teach 12 idioms/proverbs; your child connects two to the poems. Teach the idioms with literal-vs-real-meaning sketches. Ask which idiom fits the choice in 'The Road Not Taken.'

- **Q:** Meaning of 'cross that bridge when you come to it'?
  **A:** Don't worry about a problem until it happens.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write an extended-metaphor poem of your own (e.g., 'Courage is...') and take an idioms quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child writes an extended-metaphor poem (e.g., 'Courage is…') and takes an idioms quiz. Brainstorm what the abstract idea would look, sound and act like before drafting. Grade the quiz afterward.

- **Q:** What is an extended metaphor?
  **A:** A comparison continued through several lines or the whole poem.

**Assessment:** Original metaphor poem; idioms/proverbs quiz; theme sentences.

**Answer key:** Metaphor poem rubric: 4 = one metaphor sustained across the poem with vivid details; 3 = metaphor sustained most of the poem; 2 = several unrelated comparisons; 1 = no metaphor. Idioms quiz key (sample): 'break the ice' = start a conversation; 'piece of cake' = easy; 'the early bird catches the worm' = acting early brings success; 'don't count your chickens before they hatch' = don't assume success too early. Theme sentences: complete statements about life.

## Math 5: Fraction word problems and estimation

**Prepare:** Number lines labeled 0, 1/2, 1, 1 1/2, 2…; word problem cards. Write out the 8 'reasonable?' solutions for Day 3.

**Watch for:**
- Rounding every fraction to 1. Use the 0, 1/2, 1 benchmarks: is the numerator about half the denominator, near zero, or near the denominator?
- Accepting answers without checking. Always compare the exact answer to the estimate.
- Using the wrong operation for 'how much farther' (adds instead of subtracting). Draw a comparison bar model.

### Day 1 (Monday): Benchmark estimation
*Student:* Estimate 15 sums and differences by rounding fractions to 0, 1/2 or 1 on a number line.

*How to teach:* Model: 7/8 is close to 1, 4/9 is close to 1/2, so 7/8 + 4/9 ≈ 1 1/2. The child estimates 15 sums and differences on number lines.

- **Q:** Estimate 7/8 + 4/9.
  **A:** About 1 + 1/2 = 1 1/2.
- **Q:** Estimate 11/12 − 1/10.
  **A:** About 1 − 0 = 1.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Bar models for word problems
*Student:* Solve 6 word problems with bar models, such as a hiker who walked 2 1/3 miles then 1 3/4 miles.

*How to teach:* Draw a bar for 2 1/3 and another for 1 3/4 joined, then solve with an equation. The child solves 6 problems with bar models and labeled answers.

- **Q:** A hiker walked 2 1/3 miles then 1 3/4 miles. How far in all?
  **A:** 2 4/12 + 1 9/12 = 3 13/12 = 4 1/12 miles.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Spotting unreasonable answers
*Student:* Judge 8 solutions (like 2/5 + 1/2 = 3/7) as reasonable or not using benchmarks and correct the wrong ones.

*How to teach:* Present each worked solution; the child compares it to a benchmark estimate, labels reasonable/not, and fixes wrong ones. Model one: 2/5 + 1/2 must be more than 1/2, so 3/7 can't be right. Your child writes the benchmark reasoning for each of the 8.

- **Q:** Is 2/5 + 1/2 = 3/7 reasonable?
  **A:** No. 3/7 is less than 1/2, but the sum must be more than 1/2. Correct: 4/10 + 5/10 = 9/10.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Write your own
*Student:* Write and solve 4 fraction word problems about a garden or recipe, including one subtraction with regrouping.

*How to teach:* The child writes and solves 4 garden or recipe word problems, including one subtraction requiring regrouping. Check each solution together.

- **Q:** Write and solve a regrouping subtraction: a 4 1/4 ft board, cut off 1 3/4 ft.
  **A:** 4 1/4 − 1 3/4 = 3 5/4 − 1 3/4 = 2 2/4 = 2 1/2 ft.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a word problem quiz requiring a model, equation and estimate check for each problem.

*How to teach:* Give the word-problem quiz; each answer needs a model, equation, answer and estimate check. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** Jo had 3 1/2 cups of flour and used 1 2/3. How much is left?
  **A:** 1 5/6 cups (estimate 3 1/2 − 1 1/2 = 2).

**Assessment:** Word-problem quiz scored on model, equation, answer and estimate.

**Answer key:** Rubric per problem (4 pts): model (bar/number line) 1, equation 1, correct labeled answer 1, estimate check 1. Suggested problems and answers: 1) 1 1/2 + 2 2/3 = 4 1/6 2) 5 − 2 3/8 = 2 5/8 3) 3/4 + 5/6 = 1 7/12 4) 4 1/3 − 2 1/2 = 1 5/6. 14–16 proficient/mastery, 10–13 developing.

## Science 5: Food webs: producers, consumers and decomposers

**Prepare:** Index cards (make 20 kelp forest organism cards: giant kelp, phytoplankton, sea urchin, sea otter, sea star, abalone, rockfish, kelp crab, sea lion, great white shark, bacteria, worms, etc.), yarn, photos of a California kelp forest.

**Watch for:**
- Drawing arrows from eaten to eater as 'eats.' Arrows point toward the eater to show energy flow.
- Forgetting decomposers.
- Thinking removing one species only affects its prey.

### Day 1 (Monday): Roles in ecosystems
*Student:* Sort 20 organism cards from a California kelp forest into producers, consumers and decomposers.

*How to teach:* Your child sorts the cards into producers (kelp, phytoplankton), consumers and decomposers (bacteria). Read each card's diet with your child before sorting. Ask: 'Does it make its own food from sunlight?' to decide producers.

- **Q:** Is giant kelp a producer or consumer?
  **A:** A producer — it makes its own food through photosynthesis.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Food chains
*Student:* Build 4 food chains from the cards with arrows showing the direction of matter and energy.

*How to teach:* Build 4 food chains with arrows showing energy and matter flow. Model one chain on the table with arrows. Remind your child that arrows point from food to eater.

- **Q:** Write a kelp forest food chain.
  **A:** Kelp → sea urchin → sea otter (arrows point to the eater).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Food web
*Student:* Connect the chains with yarn into a food web and identify organisms that connect to many others.

*How to teach:* Connect chains with yarn into a web. Identify organisms with many connections.

- **Q:** What is a food web?
  **A:** Many connected food chains in an ecosystem.

### Day 4 (Thursday): What if?
*Student:* Remove sea otters from the web and predict the effects on urchins and kelp; read about the real kelp forest decline.

*How to teach:* Remove otters and predict. Read about the real kelp forest decline.

- **Q:** What happens without sea otters?
  **A:** Urchins increase and eat kelp, so kelp forests shrink ('urchin barrens').

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draw a labeled food web for a local backyard ecosystem and take a vocabulary quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child draws a backyard food web and takes a vocabulary quiz. Brainstorm backyard organisms together (ants, robins, grass, worms). Check arrow direction and that decomposers are included.

- **Q:** Name a backyard producer, consumer and decomposer.
  **A:** Grass/plants; insects, birds; worms, fungi, bacteria.

**Assessment:** Backyard food web; vocabulary quiz.

**Answer key:** Vocabulary quiz key: producer (makes its own food), consumer (eats others), decomposer (breaks down dead matter), herbivore, carnivore, omnivore, food chain, food web, predator, prey. Backyard web: 8+ organisms, correct arrow direction, includes decomposers.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Seeds of self-government

**Prepare:** Description of the House of Burgesses; family meeting agenda. Write the Unit 4 test.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking everyone could vote.
- Thinking the Magna Carta was American.
- Thinking town meetings were everywhere.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rights of Englishmen
*Student:* Read about the Magna Carta and English Bill of Rights and list rights colonists expected.

*How to teach:* Read about the Magna Carta (1215) and English Bill of Rights (1689). Your child lists 3 rights colonists expected as English subjects.

- **Q:** What did Magna Carta establish?
  **A:** Even the king must obey the law.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): House of Burgesses
*Student:* Learn how the House of Burgesses (1619) worked and who could vote.

*How to teach:* Read about the House of Burgesses (1619). Your child notes who could vote and how it worked.

- **Q:** When did it first meet?
  **A:** 1619.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Town meeting
*Student:* Hold a family town meeting on a real household decision, using majority vote and taking minutes.

*How to teach:* Choose a real household decision. Your child leads a family meeting with an agenda, majority vote and written minutes.

- **Q:** What is majority vote?
  **A:** More than half decides.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Who was left out?
*Student:* Discuss who could and could not vote in colonial assemblies (women, enslaved people, Native Americans, non-landowners).

*How to teach:* Discuss who could not vote in colonial assemblies. Ask why limits existed and how that compares to today.

- **Q:** Who could vote?
  **A:** Mostly white, male landowners.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 assessment
*Student:* Take the Unit 4 test on colonial economy, slavery and self-government.

*How to teach:* Give the Unit 4 test independently. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** What was the first representative assembly in English America?
  **A:** The House of Burgesses.

**Assessment:** Town meeting minutes and Unit 4 test.

**Answer key:** Minutes: date, attendees, motion, vote. Unit 4 test key: mercantilism, slavery laws, Stono, Magna Carta, House of Burgesses, voting limits.

## Art & Music 5: Folk songs of a growing nation

**Prepare:** Print lyrics for 'Low Bridge, Everybody Down (Erie Canal),' 'Shenandoah,' 'Oh! Susanna' and 'Sweet Betsy from Pike'; find recordings. Recorder or virtual keyboard for 'Oh! Susanna.'

**Watch for:**
- Confusing verse and chorus. The chorus (refrain) repeats the same words; verses have new words each time.
- Thinking folk songs have one 'correct' version. Folk songs were passed down orally and changed over time.

### Day 1 (Monday): Erie Canal
*Student:* Learn 'Low Bridge, Everybody Down (Erie Canal)' and identify verse and chorus.

*How to teach:* Learn 'Erie Canal'; your child marks verse and chorus. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What does 'Low bridge, everybody down' warn about?
  **A:** Low bridges over the canal—people on the boat had to duck.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Shenandoah
*Student:* Sing 'Shenandoah' and discuss how the melody's long phrases create a feeling of longing.

*How to teach:* Sing 'Shenandoah'; discuss how long phrases create longing. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** How does the melody of 'Shenandoah' create a feeling of longing?
  **A:** Long, slow, flowing phrases and wide leaps make it sound wistful and yearning.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Oh! Susanna
*Student:* Learn Stephen Foster's 'Oh! Susanna' and play its melody on recorder or virtual keyboard.

*How to teach:* Learn Stephen Foster's 'Oh! Susanna' (1848); play its melody. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** Why did 'Oh! Susanna' become popular with the Forty-Niners?
  **A:** Gold Rush travelers sang it on their way to California.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Accompaniment
*Student:* Add a simple body-percussion or drum accompaniment to one folk song.

*How to teach:* Add a body-percussion or drum accompaniment. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What is accompaniment?
  **A:** Music that supports the melody (like a drum or chords).

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 assessment
*Student:* Lead a family sing-along of 3 American songs and write a reflection on what the songs teach about history.

*How to teach:* Lead a family sing-along of 3 songs; write a reflection. Set up a simple 'stage' and audience, have your child introduce the work, and hold applause until the end; afterward ask them to name one strength and one thing to improve before you add one specific comment.

- **Q:** What can folk songs teach about history?
  **A:** They show daily life, work, travel and feelings of people in the past (e.g., canal workers, pioneers).

**Assessment:** Unit 4 sing-along performance and reflection.

**Answer key:** Sing-along rubric: 4 = leads 3 songs confidently with accurate lyrics and an accompaniment; 3 = minor errors; 2 = needs much support; 1 = incomplete. Reflection proficient if it links at least two songs to historical events or daily life (e.g., Erie Canal transportation, Gold Rush).

## PE 5: Ultimate disc and team cooperation

**Prepare:** Gather a disc and cones for two end zones about 30–40 m apart. Read the basic Ultimate rules: no running with the disc, 10-second stall count, a dropped or intercepted pass changes possession, score by catching in the end zone. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Running with the disc. Once you catch, stop and pivot; you may only move your non-pivot foot.
- Thinking 'spirit of the game' means no rules. Players follow the rules and call their own fouls honestly.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pivoting
*Student:* Practice pivoting and faking before throwing to a partner.

*How to teach:* Teach the pivot: keep one foot planted, step the other to fake and find an open lane. Practice faking then throwing.

- **Q:** What is a pivot foot?
  **A:** The foot that stays planted after you catch the disc while the other foot steps to fake or throw.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ultimate rules
*Student:* Learn modified Ultimate rules and the 'spirit of the game' self-officiating tradition.

*How to teach:* Explain modified rules and 'spirit of the game': players referee themselves, respect opponents and play fair.

- **Q:** What happens if a pass is dropped?
  **A:** The other team gets the disc where it landed (turnover).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Game play
*Student:* Play a small-sided Ultimate game with family or friends.

*How to teach:* Play a small-sided Ultimate game (2-v-2 or 3-v-3). Walk through the rules and boundaries first, then play; pause once to ask a quick strategy question ('Where is the open space?') and praise fair play and effort as much as skill.

- **Q:** How do you get open for a pass?
  **A:** Cut quickly to empty space away from your defender and make eye contact with the thrower.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Including everyone
*Student:* Play a version where every player must touch the disc before scoring.

*How to teach:* Play with the rule that everyone must touch the disc before the team can score. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** How did the 'everyone touches' rule change how your team played?
  **A:** The team had to make more passes and include all players, so communication and teamwork increased.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 4 assessment
*Student:* Complete a throwing/catching skills test and write about how you showed spirit of the game.

*How to teach:* Skills test: 10 backhand throws to a partner at 10 m and 10 catches. Your child writes about spirit of the game.

- **Q:** Give one example of spirit of the game.
  **A:** Calling your own foul, admitting a disc was out, or complimenting an opponent's play.

**Assessment:** Skills test and spirit-of-the-game reflection.

**Answer key:** Skills test: throws caught by partner /10 and catches /10 (proficient ≈ 6+ each with correct backhand form). Reflection rubric: 4 = explains spirit of the game and gives two specific personal examples; 3 = one example; 2 = general; 1 = missing.

## Health 5: Sleep and a daily health routine

**Prepare:** Print a 4-night sleep log (bedtime, screen-off time, wake time, total hours, how I felt). Know the recommendation: children 6–12 need 9–12 hours of sleep per night.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking you can 'catch up' on sleep only on weekends. Regular sleep schedules work best.
- Thinking screens right before bed help you relax. Screen light and stimulating content can delay sleep.

### Day 1 (Monday): Why sleep?
*Student:* Read about sleep and growth hormone, memory and mood, and list four effects of too little sleep.

*How to teach:* Read about sleep; your child lists four effects of too little sleep. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** Name four effects of too little sleep.
  **A:** Any four: trouble concentrating, poor memory, grumpiness/mood swings, slower reaction time, more illness, less energy for activity.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Sleep log
*Student:* Start a 4-night sleep log with bedtime, wake time and screen-off time.

*How to teach:* Start the 4-night sleep log. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** How many hours should a 10-year-old sleep?
  **A:** 9–12 hours per night.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Wind-down plan
*Student:* Design a 30-minute screen-free bedtime routine.

*How to teach:* Design a 30-minute screen-free wind-down routine. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** Give two wind-down activities.
  **A:** Reading, a warm shower, journaling, quiet music, stretching.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Daily routine
*Student:* Combine hygiene, dental care, sleep and activity into a daily health routine chart.

*How to teach:* Combine hygiene, dental care, sleep and activity into a daily routine chart. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** Why does a routine help health?
  **A:** Habits become automatic, so healthy behaviors happen consistently.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Analyze your sleep log, set a sleep goal and take the Personal Health unit quiz.

*How to teach:* Analyze the sleep log, set a goal, and take the unit quiz. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** What hormone is released during deep sleep that helps you grow?
  **A:** Growth hormone.

**Assessment:** Routine chart and unit quiz.

**Answer key:** Unit quiz: 1) sweat/oil glands more active in puberty 2) deodorant vs antiperspirant 3) brush 2×2 min, floss daily 4) plaque + sugar → acid 5) 9–12 hours sleep 6) growth hormone in sleep 7) screens delay sleep. Routine chart rubric: 4 = includes hygiene, dental, sleep schedule and activity; 3 = 3 areas; 2 = 2; 1 = 1.
