# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 10

## ELA 5: Informational text structures: navigating the oceans

**Prepare:** Print ReadWorks articles on exploration and navigation, text-structure graphic organizers and a dictionary. Write 10 short paragraphs (or use the articles) for sorting.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up cause/effect and problem/solution. Problem/solution names a problem and how people fixed it; cause/effect explains why something happened.
- Picking structure by one signal word only. Read the whole paragraph's purpose.
- A summary with opinions or tiny details. Summaries are objective and focus on main ideas.

### Day 1 (Monday): Text structure overview
*Student:* Learn five text structures and their signal words, then sort 10 short paragraphs about exploration by structure.

*How to teach:* Teach five structures and signal words: description (for example), sequence/chronology (first, then, in 1492), compare/contrast (however, both), cause/effect (because, as a result), problem/solution (the problem was, solved). Your child sorts 10 paragraphs.

- **Q:** Signal words 'first, next, in 1519' suggest which structure?
  **A:** Chronological (sequence).
- **Q:** 'Sailors couldn't find longitude, so the chronometer was invented' — structure?
  **A:** Problem/solution.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Comparing structures
*Student:* Read two articles on navigation tools (one chronological, one problem/solution) and compare their structures in a chart.

*How to teach:* Read two navigation articles and chart their structures and what each structure helps the reader understand. Ask: 'Why did each author choose that structure?' The child should connect structure to purpose.

- **Q:** Why might an author use chronology for navigation tools?
  **A:** To show how tools developed over time, from early methods to modern ones.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Domain vocabulary
*Student:* Use context and a dictionary to define 10 navigation terms (compass, sextant, latitude) and draw a labeled picture of two.

*How to teach:* Your child defines 10 navigation terms with context and dictionary and draws two. Your child writes the context clue, the dictionary definition, and a sentence of their own for each word.

- **Q:** What is latitude?
  **A:** Distance north or south of the equator, measured in degrees.
- **Q:** What is a compass used for?
  **A:** Finding direction; its magnetic needle points north.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Main ideas
*Student:* Identify two main ideas in an article about the Age of Exploration and summarize it in 5 sentences.

*How to teach:* Identify two main ideas in an Age of Exploration article; your child writes a 5-sentence summary. Model finding the main idea of one paragraph by asking what most sentences are about. Check that the summary has no opinions.

- **Q:** Name a main reason for the Age of Exploration.
  **A:** Europeans wanted new sea routes to Asia for spices and trade (also wealth, power and spreading religion).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a text structure quiz and a domain vocabulary check.

*How to teach:* Give the text-structure quiz and vocabulary check. Score both, then review any structure your child confused using the signal-word chart.

- **Q:** What does a sextant measure?
  **A:** The angle between the horizon and the sun or a star, used to find latitude.

**Assessment:** Text structure quiz; vocabulary check; written summary.

**Answer key:** Text structure quiz: student correctly names description, sequence, compare/contrast, cause/effect and problem/solution for given paragraphs (8/10 = proficient). Vocabulary check key: compass (direction), sextant (angle of sun/star above horizon), latitude (N–S of equator), longitude (E–W of prime meridian), navigator, chart (sea map). Summary: objective, 5 sentences, two main ideas.

## Math 5: Multiplying decimals

**Prepare:** Hundredths grids and colored pencils; a price per pound for apples (e.g., $1.29/lb) for the word problems.

**Watch for:**
- Lining up decimal points to multiply. In multiplication, multiply as whole numbers, then place the point.
- Believing multiplication always makes bigger. Show 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2 as 'half of 4 tenths.'
- Counting decimal places but dropping a needed zero (0.3 × 0.6 = 1.8). The product has 2 decimal places: 0.18.

### Day 1 (Monday): Decimal x whole number
*Student:* Solve 12 problems like 4 x 0.36 using repeated addition, disks, and then multiplication, comparing the results.

*How to teach:* Solve 4 × 0.36 three ways: 0.36 + 0.36 + 0.36 + 0.36, with disks, and 4 × 36 = 144 → 1.44. The child solves 12 problems and compares methods.

- **Q:** 4 × 0.36 = ?
  **A:** 1.44
- **Q:** 6 × 0.7 = ?
  **A:** 4.2

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Tenths x tenths
*Student:* Shade overlapping areas on a hundredths grid to model 0.3 x 0.6 = 0.18 for 8 problems.

*How to teach:* Shade 3 columns (0.3) in one color and 6 rows (0.6) in another; the overlap is 18 small squares = 0.18. The child models 8 problems.

- **Q:** 0.3 × 0.6 = ?
  **A:** 0.18
- **Q:** 0.5 × 0.5 = ?
  **A:** 0.25

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Placing the decimal point
*Student:* Multiply 12 problems like 2.4 x 1.3 as whole numbers, then place the decimal by estimating and by counting place values.

*How to teach:* Multiply 24 × 13 = 312, then estimate 2.4 × 1.3 ≈ 2 × 1 = 2 or 3 to place the point: 3.12. Confirm by counting two decimal places. The child solves 12 problems both ways.

- **Q:** 2.4 × 1.3 = ?
  **A:** 3.12
- **Q:** 0.25 × 1.6 = ?
  **A:** 0.4 (0.400)

### Day 4 (Thursday): Decimal products in context
*Student:* Solve 6 word problems such as the cost of 3.5 pounds of apples at $1.29 per pound.

*How to teach:* Solve the apple problem together with an estimate (3.5 × 1.3 ≈ 4.55). The child solves 6 context problems, rounding money to the cent.

- **Q:** 3.5 lb of apples at $1.29 per pound costs?
  **A:** 3.5 × 1.29 = 4.515, about $4.52.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on 10 decimal multiplication problems and explain why 0.5 x 0.4 is smaller than both factors.

*How to teach:* Give the 10-problem quiz and have the child explain in writing why 0.5 × 0.4 is smaller than both factors. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** Why is 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2 smaller than both factors?
  **A:** You are taking half of 0.4 (less than 0.4), and 0.4 of 0.5 (less than 0.5). Multiplying by a number less than 1 makes the product smaller.

**Assessment:** Quiz on decimal multiplication with written explanation.

**Answer key:** Suggested key: 1) 3 × 0.8 = 2.4 2) 7 × 0.25 = 1.75 3) 0.4 × 0.7 = 0.28 4) 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81 5) 1.5 × 2.2 = 3.3 6) 3.6 × 0.5 = 1.8 7) 4.25 × 6 = 25.5 8) 0.2 × 0.05 = 0.01 9) 12.3 × 1.1 = 13.53 10) 2.75 × 4 = 11. Explanation (proficient): states that multiplying by a factor less than 1 takes a part of the other number, so the product is smaller than each factor.

## Science 5: Designing a water filter: define and generate

**Prepare:** Plastic bottles (cut top off and invert as a funnel), sand, gravel, cotton balls, coffee filters, aquarium activated charcoal (rinse first), dirty water (soil, leaves, a drop of food coloring), play money for budgets. Safety: filtered water is never for drinking.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking 'clear' means 'safe.' Filters do not remove all germs.
- Changing many things at once. Test materials one at a time.
- Confusing criteria and constraints. Criteria = what success looks like; constraints = limits.

### Day 1 (Monday): The problem
*Student:* Read about communities without clean water and define the design problem with criteria (clearest water) and constraints (materials, budget of play money).

*How to teach:* Read about communities without clean water. Your child writes the design problem, criteria (clearest water, enough volume) and constraints (materials, budget).

- **Q:** Is 'cost under $10 of play money' a criterion or constraint?
  **A:** A constraint (a limit).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Testing materials
*Student:* Test each filter material alone and record how clear the dirty water becomes on a 1–5 turbidity scale.

*How to teach:* Your child tests each material alone with the same amount of dirty water and rates clarity 1–5. Pour the same amount (e.g., 100 mL) through each material in a funnel. Your child compares to a clarity scale you make (1 = muddy, 5 = clear).

- **Q:** Why test materials one at a time?
  **A:** To know which material does what, so the design uses each wisely.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Brainstorming designs
*Student:* Sketch 3 different filter designs with labeled layers and estimated cost.

*How to teach:* Your child sketches 3 filter designs with labeled layers and costs. Remind your child to label each layer and its cost. Ask why they put each layer in that order.

- **Q:** Which layer usually goes at the top and why?
  **A:** Gravel, to catch large particles first so finer layers don't clog.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Choosing a design
*Student:* Compare the 3 designs in a decision matrix against criteria and constraints and choose one to build.

*How to teach:* Your child compares designs in a decision matrix and chooses one. Make a table with designs as rows and criteria (clarity, cost, speed) as columns, scoring 1–3. The highest total wins, unless a constraint rules it out.

- **Q:** What is a decision matrix?
  **A:** A table scoring each design on the criteria and constraints to compare them fairly.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Build the first prototype and write a prediction of how it will perform.

*How to teach:* Build the first prototype and write a prediction of how it will perform. Help cut and invert the bottle top. Your child writes a prediction with a reason before any water is poured.

- **Q:** Predict a weakness.
  **A:** Example: sand may wash through cotton, making water cloudy.

**Assessment:** Design notebook: criteria/constraints, sketches and decision matrix.

**Answer key:** Design notebook rubric: 4 = clear problem, criteria and constraints, 3 labeled sketches with costs, decision matrix with reasoning; 3 = minor gaps; 2 = missing a sketch or matrix; 1 = incomplete.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Plymouth and the Massachusetts Bay Colony

**Prepare:** Text of the Mayflower Compact; excerpt of William Bradford's Of Plymouth Plantation.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the 1621 harvest was the first 'Thanksgiving' as celebrated today.
- Thinking Pilgrims and Puritans were identical.
- Thinking Squanto learned English from the Pilgrims.

### Day 1 (Monday): Pilgrims and Wampanoag
*Student:* Read about the Pilgrims' voyage and the Wampanoag people, including Massasoit and Tisquantum (Squanto).

*How to teach:* Read about the 1620 voyage and the Wampanoag. Your child notes who Massasoit and Tisquantum were and how they shaped the colony's survival.

- **Q:** How did Tisquantum know English?
  **A:** He had been kidnapped and taken to Europe years earlier.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Primary source: Mayflower Compact
*Student:* Read the Mayflower Compact, paraphrase it, and explain why it matters to self-government.

*How to teach:* Read the Compact aloud (it is one sentence). Your child paraphrases it in plain words and explains how it is an early example of self-government.

- **Q:** Why does it matter?
  **A:** Colonists agreed to make and obey fair laws for the common good — an early example of self-government.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Bradford's account
*Student:* Read an excerpt of Bradford's Of Plymouth Plantation on the first winter and the 1621 harvest and compare it to a Wampanoag perspective.

*How to teach:* Read Bradford's excerpt on the first winter and the 1621 harvest. Then read a Wampanoag perspective (e.g., from Plimoth Patuxet's resources) and compare.

- **Q:** What happened the first winter?
  **A:** About half the Pilgrims died.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Puritans
*Student:* Read about John Winthrop's 'city upon a hill' and Puritan life and write what Puritans valued.

*How to teach:* Read about John Winthrop and the 1630 Massachusetts Bay Colony. Your child lists 3–4 Puritan values (hard work, education to read the Bible, strict community rules).

- **Q:** What did 'city upon a hill' mean?
  **A:** Their community should be a model for the world.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on the New England founding and write a two-sentence summary of the Mayflower Compact.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz, then your child writes a 2-sentence summary of the Compact. Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** When was the Mayflower Compact signed?
  **A:** 1620.

**Assessment:** Mayflower Compact paraphrase and quiz.

**Answer key:** Paraphrase: captures agreement to form a government and obey just laws. Quiz key: 1620, Plymouth, Wampanoag, Massasoit, Squanto, Bradford, Winthrop, Massachusetts Bay 1630.

## Art & Music 5: The Hudson River School and American landscapes

**Prepare:** Open Thomas Cole's 'The Oxbow' (1836, Metropolitan Museum) and Albert Bierstadt's 'Looking Down Yosemite Valley' or 'Valley of the Yosemite' on Google Arts & Culture, plus a photo of Yosemite Valley. Find a Native American design or a modern landscape for comparison.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking landscapes are exact copies of nature. Hudson River School painters often exaggerated light, scale and drama to stir emotion and celebrate American wilderness.
- Thinking distant mountains should be painted darkest. Atmospheric perspective makes distant things lighter, bluer and less detailed.

### Day 1 (Monday): Looking closely
*Student:* Study Thomas Cole's 'The Oxbow' for 5 minutes and list what you see, think and wonder.

*How to teach:* Your child studies 'The Oxbow' for 5 minutes and lists see/think/wonder. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What contrast does Cole show between the left and right sides of 'The Oxbow'?
  **A:** The left is wild, stormy wilderness; the right is sunny, calm, cultivated farmland along the river.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Atmospheric perspective
*Student:* Identify how Cole and Bierstadt make distant mountains lighter and bluer, then practice with a 3-layer paint study.

*How to teach:* Point out lighter, bluer distant mountains. Your child paints a 3-layer study: light blue far, medium middle, dark near.

- **Q:** What is atmospheric perspective?
  **A:** Making distant objects lighter, bluer/grayer and less detailed to show depth, as the air does.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): California connection
*Student:* Compare Bierstadt's Yosemite painting with a photograph of Yosemite and discuss how the artist exaggerated for effect.

*How to teach:* Compare Bierstadt's Yosemite with a photo. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** How did Bierstadt exaggerate Yosemite?
  **A:** He made cliffs taller and more dramatic, and the golden glowing light more intense than in reality, to create a sense of awe (the sublime).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing styles
*Student:* Compare a Hudson River School painting to a Native American landscape design or a modern landscape and discuss how each would be judged.

*How to teach:* Compare to a Native American design or modern landscape; discuss different criteria for judging each.

- **Q:** Why might you judge a Navajo weaving differently from a Hudson River School painting?
  **A:** They have different purposes and traditions—one uses symbolic geometric patterns, the other aims at realistic, dramatic scenery.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph interpreting the mood of one landscape using evidence from color, light and composition.

*How to teach:* Write a paragraph interpreting the mood of one landscape with evidence. Talk through ideas aloud first, then have your child write; check that the work uses this week's vocabulary and specific examples rather than general statements.

- **Q:** Which evidence supports a mood interpretation?
  **A:** Specific colors, light, weather, composition and details in the painting.

**Assessment:** Written interpretation paragraph and atmospheric perspective study.

**Answer key:** Paragraph rubric: 4 = states the mood and supports it with at least three specific details (color, light, composition); 3 = two details; 2 = one; 1 = no evidence. Atmospheric perspective study: three layers clearly lighter/bluer with distance.

## PE 5: Passing and receiving

**Prepare:** Gather a ball, a wall or rebounder, and cones for 3–4 gates (two cones about 1 m apart). Review cues: plant foot beside the ball, toe up, ankle locked, strike with inside of foot, follow through to target. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Toe-kicking passes. Use the inside of the foot with the toe up and ankle locked for accuracy.
- Stopping the ball dead and then looking. A good first touch pushes the ball slightly into space ready for the next move.

### Day 1 (Monday): Push pass
*Student:* Practice the inside-of-foot push pass against a wall 50 times, alternating feet.

*How to teach:* Model the push pass: plant foot beside the ball pointing at the target, toe up, ankle locked, strike with the inside of the foot. Your child does 50 passes against a wall alternating feet.

- **Q:** Where should your plant foot point?
  **A:** At the target, beside the ball.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Trapping
*Student:* Trap passes with the sole, inside of foot and thigh, then pass back.

*How to teach:* Toss or roll passes; your child traps with the sole, then the inside of the foot (cushion by pulling back slightly), then the thigh, and passes back.

- **Q:** How do you 'cushion' a trap?
  **A:** Relax and give with the ball, pulling the foot or thigh back slightly as it arrives so it doesn't bounce away.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Passing gates
*Student:* Pass through cone gates to a partner, scoring a point for each gate.

*How to teach:* Set gates; your child passes through gates to you; score one point per successful gate pass in 2 minutes.

- **Q:** Why does following through toward the target matter?
  **A:** It sends the ball in the direction your foot finishes, improving accuracy.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Lead passes
*Student:* Pass ahead of a moving partner so they receive the ball in stride.

*How to teach:* Have your child jog across the yard; pass ahead of them so they receive without stopping. Then switch roles.

- **Q:** Where should you aim a lead pass?
  **A:** In front of the moving teammate, into the space they are running to.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Analyze a partner's pass using cues (plant foot, ankle locked, follow-through) and give feedback.

*How to teach:* Your child watches you pass and fills a feedback form using the cues: plant foot, ankle locked, follow-through.

- **Q:** My pass went to the left of you. What might have caused it?
  **A:** The plant foot or follow-through pointed left, or the ball was struck off-center.

**Assessment:** Passing gates score and peer feedback form.

**Answer key:** Passing gates: record points in 2 minutes (compare with later tries). Peer feedback form proficient if it correctly checks plant foot, locked ankle, inside-foot contact and follow-through, and gives one specific fix.

## Health 5: 60 minutes a day: planning activity

**Prepare:** Gather a calendar and jump rope; plan music for dancing. Learn the guidelines: kids 6–17 need 60+ minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily, including vigorous, muscle-strengthening and bone-strengthening activity at least 3 days a week each.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the 60 minutes must be done all at once. It can be added up across the day.
- Thinking screen time is harmless if you're sitting quietly. Long sedentary time cuts into activity and sleep.

### Day 1 (Monday): Activity benefits
*Student:* Read about how activity helps the heart, bones, sleep and mood, and list five benefits.

*How to teach:* Read about benefits; your child lists five (stronger heart and bones, better sleep, better mood, less stress, better focus, healthy weight).

- **Q:** Name three benefits of regular physical activity.
  **A:** Any three: stronger heart and lungs, stronger bones and muscles, better sleep, improved mood, lower stress, better concentration.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Moderate vs. vigorous
*Student:* Try walking, jumping rope and dancing for 3 minutes each and use the talk test to classify intensity.

*How to teach:* Try walking, jumping rope and dancing for 3 minutes each; use the talk test. Have your child work independently; afterward, go over any errors together and let them explain the correct answer back to you.

- **Q:** How does the talk test tell moderate from vigorous?
  **A:** Moderate: you can talk but not sing. Vigorous: you can only say a few words before needing a breath.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Weekly plan
*Student:* Create a 7-day plan with at least 60 minutes daily including muscle- and bone-strengthening activity 3 days.

*How to teach:* Create a 7-day plan of 60+ minutes daily with muscle- and bone-strengthening 3 days. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** Name a bone-strengthening activity.
  **A:** Jumping rope, running, hopscotch, basketball — activities with impact.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Screen time swap
*Student:* Track screen time for a day and plan two active swaps.

*How to teach:* Track screen time for a day and plan two active swaps. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** Give an active swap for 30 minutes of video games.
  **A:** A bike ride, shooting hoops, or a dance video.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Follow day one of your plan and complete a short quiz on activity guidelines.

*How to teach:* Do day one of the plan; take the guidelines quiz. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** How many minutes of activity do kids need daily?
  **A:** At least 60 minutes.

**Assessment:** Weekly activity plan and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: 1) 60 minutes daily 2) moderate = can talk, not sing 3) vigorous = few words 4) muscle-strengthening 3 days/week (push-ups, climbing) 5) bone-strengthening 3 days/week (jumping, running). Plan rubric: 4 = 60+ minutes every day with intensity labeled and strength/bone activities on 3+ days; 3 = small gaps; 2 = under 60 minutes most days; 1 = incomplete.
