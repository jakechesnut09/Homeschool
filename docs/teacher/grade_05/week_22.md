# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 22

## ELA 5: Comparing stories: Esperanza Rising and Hatchet; compare-contrast essay

**Prepare:** Have both novels and a compare-contrast organizer. Choose two short stories with similar themes for the cold read.

**Watch for:**
- Writing two separate summaries instead of comparing. Each paragraph should mention both books.
- Overusing 'also.' Use contrast transitions: however, on the other hand, while.
- Thinking dialect is incorrect English. It reflects characters and culture.

### Day 1 (Monday): Planning the comparison
*Student:* Use an organizer to compare how Brian and Esperanza face loss and change, with 2 quotations from each book.

*How to teach:* Fill the organizer on how Brian and Esperanza face loss and change, with 2 quotations each. Model one row of the organizer, then your child finds 2 quotations from each book with page numbers.

- **Q:** How are Brian and Esperanza similar?
  **A:** Both lose their old lives suddenly and must learn new skills and attitudes to survive.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Drafting
*Student:* Draft a compare-contrast essay using point-by-point or block structure and contrast transitions.

*How to teach:* Draft the essay with block or point-by-point structure and contrast transitions. Explain both structures with a quick sketch, then let your child choose. Check that each paragraph mentions both books.

- **Q:** How do they differ?
  **A:** Brian faces physical survival alone in nature; Esperanza faces poverty and hard work with her family and community.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Dialect and language
*Student:* Compare the use of Spanish words and dialect in Esperanza Rising with Paulsen's plain style and add a paragraph on language.

*How to teach:* Compare language: Spanish words in Esperanza Rising vs. Paulsen's plain, short sentences; add a paragraph.

- **Q:** Why include Spanish words?
  **A:** They show Esperanza's culture and the setting.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Revising and editing
*Student:* Revise with a peer/parent checklist, edit, and type the final essay.

*How to teach:* Revise with a checklist, edit and type the final essay. Use the rubric as a checklist; your child types the final copy after revisions.

- **Q:** Name a contrast transition.
  **A:** However.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 assessment
*Student:* Discuss the two novels in a book talk and take a cold-read assessment comparing two short stories with similar themes.

*How to teach:* Discuss the novels in a book talk; give the cold-read comparison assessment. In the book talk, ask which character changed more and why. Give the cold read independently.

- **Q:** Shared theme of both novels?
  **A:** Adversity can make a person stronger and wiser.

**Assessment:** Compare-contrast essay rubric; Unit 5 cold-read assessment.

**Answer key:** Essay rubric: 4 = clear thesis, organized comparison with similarities and differences, 2+ quotations per book explained, transitions, language paragraph; 3 = all parts, some thin; 2 = summaries without comparison; 1 = incomplete. Cold-read rubric (choose an unfamiliar grade-5 story from CommonLit): 4 = states a precise theme/central idea, explains character with 2+ exact quotations correctly punctuated and explained; 3 = accurate answer with at least one relevant quotation and explanation; 2 = accurate but general answer, evidence paraphrased or loosely connected; 1 = inaccurate or no evidence.

## Math 5: Dividing unit fractions and whole numbers

**Prepare:** Fraction strips, paper strips, number lines; draw a 'pan of brownies' rectangle.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing 1/2 ÷ 3 with 3 ÷ 1/2. Read it as a story: half a pan shared by 3 vs. how many halves in 3.
- Thinking division always gives a smaller number. 3 ÷ 1/4 = 12 because quarters are small pieces.
- Not connecting to multiplication. Check: quotient × divisor = dividend.

### Day 1 (Monday): Unit fraction / whole
*Student:* Share 1/2 of a pan of brownies among 3 people with a drawing and solve 8 problems like 1/3 / 4.

*How to teach:* Draw a pan, shade half, split the half into 3 equal pieces: each is 1/6 of the pan. The child solves 8 problems with drawings.

- **Q:** 1/2 ÷ 3 = ?
  **A:** 1/6
- **Q:** 1/3 ÷ 4 = ?
  **A:** 1/12

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Whole / unit fraction
*Student:* Use number lines to find how many 1/4s are in 3 and solve 8 problems like 5 / 1/3.

*How to teach:* Draw a number line 0 to 3 in fourths and count jumps of 1/4: 12. The child solves 8 problems.

- **Q:** How many 1/4s are in 3?
  **A:** 12
- **Q:** 5 ÷ 1/3 = ?
  **A:** 15

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Checking with multiplication
*Student:* Check 10 division answers with multiplication (1/12 x 4 = 1/3) and explain the relationship.

*How to teach:* Check each quotient with multiplication: 1/3 ÷ 4 = 1/12 because 1/12 × 4 = 1/3. The child checks 10 answers and explains the inverse relationship.

- **Q:** Check 4 ÷ 1/5 = 20.
  **A:** 20 × 1/5 = 4 ✓

### Day 4 (Thursday): Story contexts
*Student:* Write story contexts for 1/4 / 2 and 6 / 1/2, then solve both with models.

*How to teach:* Write a story for 1/4 ÷ 2 (1/4 of a pizza shared by 2 people) and 6 ÷ 1/2 (how many half-cup servings in 6 cups), then solve with models. Your child draws a picture for each story and writes the equation beneath it. Ask: 'Why is one answer smaller than the starting amount and one larger?'

- **Q:** 1/4 ÷ 2 = ?
  **A:** 1/8
- **Q:** 6 ÷ 1/2 = ?
  **A:** 12

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on dividing unit fractions and whole numbers with models.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz with a model for each problem. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** 1/5 ÷ 2 = ?
  **A:** 1/10

**Assessment:** Quiz on unit fraction division.

**Answer key:** Suggested key: 1) 1/2 ÷ 4 = 1/8 2) 1/6 ÷ 2 = 1/12 3) 1/3 ÷ 3 = 1/9 4) 2 ÷ 1/3 = 6 5) 4 ÷ 1/4 = 16 6) 7 ÷ 1/2 = 14 7) 3 people share 1/4 lb of cheese = 1/12 lb each 8) 5 yd of ribbon cut into 1/3-yd pieces = 15 pieces. Each needs a matching model.

## Science 5: Atmosphere and weather interactions

**Prepare:** Jar, hot water (adult), ice in a plate on top, food coloring, clear container, thermometer, weather log sheet. Look up average temperatures for San Francisco and Sacramento.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking clouds are water vapor. Clouds are tiny liquid droplets.
- Thinking the ocean has no effect on land temperatures.
- Mixing up weather and climate.

### Day 1 (Monday): Cloud in a jar
*Student:* Make a cloud in a jar with hot water and ice and explain the role of water vapor and cooling.

*How to teach:* Pour hot water in a jar, place a plate of ice on top; observe a cloud. Adult pours hot water; place ice on a plate on top. Optional: light a match briefly and drop it in for smoke particles (adult only) to help droplets form.

- **Q:** Why does a cloud form?
  **A:** Warm water vapor rises, cools near the ice and condenses into tiny droplets.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ocean currents
*Student:* Model warm and cold water currents with colored hot and cold water in a clear container and observe.

*How to teach:* Model currents with colored hot and cold water in a clear container. Fill a clear container with room-temperature water. Gently add blue ice water and red hot water at opposite ends and observe.

- **Q:** What does cold water do?
  **A:** Sinks because it is denser; warm water rises.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Weather tracking
*Student:* Record temperature, cloud cover and precipitation daily for the week in a weather log.

*How to teach:* Your child records temperature, clouds and precipitation daily. Record at the same time each day in a log. Discuss changes at the end of the week.

- **Q:** Weather vs. climate?
  **A:** Weather is day-to-day conditions; climate is the long-term average.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Coastal climate
*Student:* Compare average temperatures of San Francisco and Sacramento and explain how the ocean affects climate.

*How to teach:* Compare SF and Sacramento temperatures; explain ocean effects. Look up average monthly highs. Your child graphs both cities and explains the ocean's moderating effect.

- **Q:** Why are SF summers cooler than Sacramento's?
  **A:** The cool Pacific Ocean and fog moderate SF; inland Sacramento heats up (July highs about 90s°F vs. upper 60s°F in SF).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Draw a model showing how the atmosphere, hydrosphere and biosphere interact during a rainstorm.

*How to teach:* Your child draws a model of a rainstorm with spheres interacting. Your child draws the storm with arrows labeled with sphere names and processes.

- **Q:** Describe sphere interactions in a rainstorm.
  **A:** Ocean water evaporates into the atmosphere, falls as rain on land, and plants and animals use it.

**Assessment:** Rainstorm sphere-interaction model; weather log.

**Answer key:** Model rubric: 4 = three spheres, arrows for evaporation, condensation, precipitation, uptake by living things; 3 = two spheres; 2 = one; 1 = incomplete. Weather log: 5 days complete.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: The Continental Congresses and the first shots

**Prepare:** Patrick Henry's speech excerpt (1775); map of Lexington and Concord.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking independence was declared in 1775.
- Thinking Revere rode alone.
- Thinking all colonists were Patriots.

### Day 1 (Monday): First Continental Congress
*Student:* Read about the 1774 Congress and list its decisions.

*How to teach:* Read about the First Continental Congress (1774). Your child lists decisions.

- **Q:** Decisions?
  **A:** Boycott British goods and meet again.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Patrick Henry
*Student:* Read and perform an excerpt of Patrick Henry's 1775 speech and explain its argument.

*How to teach:* Read Henry's speech excerpt; your child performs it with expression and explains its argument. Model the first item yourself, then let your child finish while you check accuracy against the source.

- **Q:** Argument?
  **A:** Fight for freedom.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Lexington and Concord
*Student:* Map Revere's and Dawes's rides and the battles at Lexington and Concord.

*How to teach:* Map Revere's and Dawes's rides and the battles (April 19, 1775). Ask 'How do you know?' and have your child point to the source or map for each answer.

- **Q:** Who rode with Revere?
  **A:** William Dawes (and Samuel Prescott).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Second Continental Congress
*Student:* Explain how the Second Continental Congress created the Continental Army and chose Washington.

*How to teach:* Explain how the Second Continental Congress created the army and chose Washington. Before finishing, have your child explain their work aloud in 2–3 sentences; correct any errors then.

- **Q:** Who led the army?
  **A:** George Washington.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a Patriot vs. Loyalist T-chart of beliefs and take a quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child makes a Patriot vs. Loyalist T-chart, then takes the quiz.

- **Q:** Loyalist belief?
  **A:** Stay loyal to the king.

**Assessment:** Speech performance and T-chart.

**Answer key:** Performance: expressive. T-chart: 3 beliefs each.

## Art & Music 5: Major and minor; scales

**Prepare:** Open Chrome Music Lab (Piano Roll or Chords) or a virtual keyboard. Choose 6 excerpts (3 major, e.g., 'Happy Birthday'; 3 minor, e.g., 'Greensleeves,' Grieg's 'In the Hall of the Mountain King'). Major scale pattern: W W H W W W H.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking minor always means sad. Minor often sounds darker or mysterious but can be energetic.
- Thinking a half step skips a key. A half step is the very next key (black or white); a whole step skips one key.

### Day 1 (Monday): Major vs. minor
*Student:* Listen to 6 excerpts and identify major or minor, describing the mood of each.

*How to teach:* Play 6 excerpts; your child identifies major or minor and describes mood. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What mood do minor keys often create?
  **A:** Darker, sad, mysterious or serious.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Scale patterns
*Student:* Build a C major scale using whole and half steps on a virtual keyboard.

*How to teach:* Build C major using W W H W W W H on a keyboard. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** Where are the half steps in C major?
  **A:** Between E–F and B–C.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Playing scales
*Student:* Play the G major scale on recorder (G-A-B-C-D-E-F#-G) slowly and evenly.

*How to teach:* Play G major on recorder: G A B C D E F# G. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** Which note is sharp in G major?
  **A:** F-sharp.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Minor melody
*Student:* Play 'Hot Cross Buns' in major, then change it to minor on a keyboard and compare.

*How to teach:* Play 'Hot Cross Buns' in major (E D C), then minor (E-flat D C); compare. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** What change turned 'Hot Cross Buns' from major to minor?
  **A:** Lowering the third note of the scale (E to E-flat).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a listening quiz on major and minor and perform the G major scale.

*How to teach:* Listening quiz; perform the G major scale. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** What is the tonic?
  **A:** The first and home note of a scale.

**Assessment:** Listening quiz and scale performance.

**Answer key:** Listening quiz: 6 excerpts correctly labeled major/minor (proficient 5/6). Scale performance: correct notes including F#, even rhythm. Scale pattern: W W H W W W H.

## PE 5: Dances from around the world

**Prepare:** Find music for La Raspa, the Hora (e.g., 'Hava Nagila'), and tinikling. For tinikling use two long poles, broom handles or two ropes on the ground. Read a short background for each dance before teaching. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking folk dances are just steps. Each comes from a culture and often has meaning (celebration, imitating nature).
- In tinikling, standing between the poles when they close. Hop out on the 'together' beat; poles stay low and moved gently.

### Day 1 (Monday): La Raspa
*Student:* Learn the Mexican folk dance 'La Raspa' with its hop-step and elbow swing sections.

*How to teach:* Teach La Raspa: the bleking (hop-step: hop and switch feet, heels forward) for 3 then a pause/clap, repeated; then the elbow-swing section with a partner.

- **Q:** What country does La Raspa come from?
  **A:** Mexico.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): The Hora
*Student:* Learn the Israeli hora circle step and dance to music.

*How to teach:* Teach the hora: circle with hands joined; basic step 'side, behind, side, kick, side, kick' moving around the circle.

- **Q:** Where is the hora traditionally danced?
  **A:** It's a circle dance of Israel (and also danced in Romania and Jewish communities); it's common at celebrations like weddings.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Tinikling
*Student:* Learn the Philippine tinikling steps with ropes or sticks on the ground, then with movement.

*How to teach:* Teach tinikling: poles tapped twice apart, then brought together once (a 3-beat pattern). Practice the hop pattern beside ropes on the ground first, then with gentle moving poles.

- **Q:** What does tinikling imitate?
  **A:** The tikling bird stepping between bamboo traps/grass stalks in the Philippines.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Culture connection
*Student:* Read about the origins of each dance and share one fact before dancing it.

*How to teach:* Your child reads about each dance and shares one fact before dancing it. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** Share one cultural fact about tinikling.
  **A:** Example: It is the national/folk dance of the Philippines and imitates the tikling bird dodging bamboo traps.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Perform two world dances and measure heart rate afterward.

*How to teach:* Perform two dances; take pulse afterward. Set up a simple 'stage' and audience, have your child introduce the work, and hold applause until the end; afterward ask them to name one strength and one thing to improve before you add one specific comment.

- **Q:** Was your heart rate after dancing in an exercise range? What does that show?
  **A:** Usually yes (well above resting) — dancing is aerobic activity.

**Assessment:** World dance performance and cultural fact.

**Answer key:** Performance (4 = correct steps and rhythm for two dances with no prompts; 3 = minor errors; 2 = needs frequent prompts; 1 = cannot perform). Cultural fact must be accurate: La Raspa—Mexico; Hora—Israel/Romania circle dance at celebrations; Tinikling—Philippines, imitates the tikling bird.

## Health 5: Talking with trusted adults

**Prepare:** Prepare to have a 10-minute conversation with your child about puberty — decide what you're comfortable sharing from your own experience. Bookmark KidsHealth.org and AMAZE.org and find one random video comment section about puberty to compare. Review body-safety language: private parts, safe/unsafe touch, 'No, Go, Tell.' PARENT PREVIEW: watch the videos and read materials before your child does; decide how you'll answer personal or values questions, and keep the anonymous question box open.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking parents don't want to talk about puberty. Most do; starting with a simple question helps.
- Thinking secrets about touch should be kept if an adult asks. Unsafe touch or secrets about touch should always be told to a trusted adult; it's never the child's fault.

### Day 1 (Monday): Conversation starters
*Student:* Write three conversation starters for talking with a parent about puberty.

*How to teach:* Your child writes three conversation starters (e.g., 'What was puberty like for you?' 'Can I ask you something private?').

- **Q:** Give a good conversation starter about puberty.
  **A:** Example: 'I learned about growth spurts today—when did yours happen?'

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Practice talk
*Student:* Have a 10-minute conversation with a parent or guardian using a starter and record one thing you learned.

*How to teach:* Have the 10-minute talk using a starter. Your child records one thing they learned. Start with a slow demonstration, then have your child repeat it several times while you give one piece of specific feedback at a time and praise effort.

- **Q:** Why is talking with a parent helpful?
  **A:** Parents can give accurate information, family values and support.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Valid sources
*Student:* Compare KidsHealth, AMAZE and a random video comment on a puberty question and judge reliability.

*How to teach:* Compare KidsHealth, AMAZE and a comment section on the same question; judge reliability. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** Why is a random video comment not a reliable source?
  **A:** Anyone can write it, with no medical expertise or fact-checking.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Body autonomy
*Student:* Learn about personal boundaries, safe and unsafe touch, and how to tell a trusted adult.

*How to teach:* Teach personal boundaries: your body belongs to you; safe vs. unsafe touch; if anyone makes you uncomfortable say No, Go, Tell.

- **Q:** What should you do if someone touches you in an unsafe way?
  **A:** Say no, get away, and tell a trusted adult right away—and keep telling until someone helps.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Make a pocket card listing trusted adults and valid sources and complete a role-play check.

*How to teach:* Make a pocket card of trusted adults and valid sources; role-play asking for help. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** Name two valid sources of puberty information.
  **A:** KidsHealth.org, AMAZE.org, a doctor or school nurse.

**Assessment:** Role-play and pocket card.

**Answer key:** Role-play rubric: 4 = clearly states concern to a trusted adult, uses 'I' statements, asks for help, persists if not heard; 3 = clear but no persistence; 2 = vague; 1 = can't. Pocket card: at least 3 trusted adults and 2 valid sources (e.g., KidsHealth, AMAZE, doctor).
