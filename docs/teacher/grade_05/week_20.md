# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 20

## ELA 5: Esperanza Rising: Las Ciruelas, Las Papas, Los Aguacates

**Prepare:** Preview 'Las Ciruelas,' 'Las Papas' and 'Los Aguacates.' Find a ReadWorks/CommonLit article on 1930s farmworkers and Mexican repatriation and read it first.

**Watch for:**
- Confusing homographs with homophones. Homographs are spelled the same (wind/wind); homophones sound the same (to/too).
- Thinking one side of the strike is simply right. Show both have reasons.
- Ignoring historical context. The Great Depression shaped events.

### Day 1 (Monday): Las Ciruelas
*Student:* Read 'Las Ciruelas' and list the arguments of Marta and Miguel about the strike.

*How to teach:* Read 'Las Ciruelas'; your child lists Marta's and Miguel's arguments about striking. Your child makes a two-column chart and adds a page number for each argument.

- **Q:** What does Marta want?
  **A:** Workers should strike for better pay and living conditions.
- **Q:** What does Miguel think?
  **A:** He wants to keep working and support his family; striking could cost them their jobs.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Las Papas
*Student:* Read 'Las Papas' and explain how Mama's illness changes Esperanza's responsibilities.

*How to teach:* Read 'Las Papas'; explain how Mama's illness changes Esperanza's responsibilities. Ask your child to list three new jobs or worries Esperanza takes on and the evidence for each.

- **Q:** What illness does Mama get?
  **A:** Valley Fever, after a dust storm.
- **Q:** How does Esperanza respond?
  **A:** She takes a job to earn money and care for the family.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Informational connection
*Student:* Read an informational article on 1930s farmworkers and Mexican repatriation and note 3 facts the novel reflects.

*How to teach:* Read the article; note 3 facts the novel reflects. Read the article together first. Your child writes each fact and the matching event from the novel side by side.

- **Q:** What was Mexican repatriation?
  **A:** During the Great Depression, many Mexicans and Mexican Americans (including U.S. citizens) were pressured or forced to leave the U.S.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Los Aguacates
*Student:* Read 'Los Aguacates' and practice synonyms, antonyms and homographs (wind/wind, lead/lead) with 15 words.

*How to teach:* Read 'Los Aguacates'; practice synonyms, antonyms, homographs. Model homographs by saying both pronunciations. Your child sorts 15 words into synonyms, antonyms and homograph pairs.

- **Q:** Two meanings of 'lead'?
  **A:** To guide (rhymes with seed); a metal (rhymes with bed).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a 'Two Sides' paragraph on whether Miguel or Marta has the better plan and take a word relationships quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a 'Two Sides' paragraph and takes a word relationships quiz. The paragraph must explain both sides fairly before giving an opinion; then grade the quiz.

- **Q:** Antonym of 'generous'?
  **A:** Selfish.

**Assessment:** Two-sides paragraph; informational notes; word relationships quiz.

**Answer key:** Two-sides paragraph: 4 = both positions explained with evidence and a reasoned opinion; 3 = both sides, little evidence; 2 = one side; 1 = off topic. Word quiz: synonyms, antonyms and homographs correctly identified (e.g., wind = moving air / to turn; bow = bend / ribbon).

## Math 5: Multiplying fractions by fractions and area

**Prepare:** Paper squares for folding, grid paper, colored pencils, ruler. Optional: cut a 3/4 in by 2/3 in rectangle and squares of 1/4 by 1/3 in.

**Watch for:**
- Finding a common denominator to multiply. That's for adding; to multiply, multiply numerators and denominators.
- Expecting the product to be larger. Fraction × fraction (both < 1) is smaller than both.
- Multiplying mixed numbers by whole parts only (2 1/2 × 1 1/3 = 2 1/6). Convert to improper fractions or use all four partial products.

### Day 1 (Monday): Folding paper
*Student:* Fold paper to show 1/2 of 1/3 and 2/3 of 3/4 and record the product for 6 problems.

*How to teach:* Fold a paper in thirds, shade 1/3, fold in half the other way and double-shade half of the third: 1/6 of the paper. The child folds and records 6 problems.

- **Q:** 1/2 of 1/3 = ?
  **A:** 1/6
- **Q:** 2/3 of 3/4 = ?
  **A:** 6/12 = 1/2

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Area models
*Student:* Shade overlapping regions to solve 10 fraction x fraction problems, then notice the numerator x numerator / denominator x denominator rule.

*How to teach:* Draw a square, shade 2/3 vertically and 4/5 horizontally; count overlap: 8/15. After 10 problems, ask the child to state the rule.

- **Q:** 2/3 × 4/5 = ?
  **A:** 8/15
- **Q:** What rule do you notice?
  **A:** Multiply numerators and multiply denominators: (a × c)/(b × d).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Fractional side lengths
*Student:* Tile a 3/4 inch by 2/3 inch rectangle with unit fraction squares and compute areas of 8 rectangles by multiplying sides.

*How to teach:* Tile a 3/4 in by 2/3 in rectangle with 1/4-by-1/3 squares (each 1/12 sq in): 3 × 2 = 6 tiles = 6/12 = 1/2 sq in. The child finds 8 more areas by multiplying sides.

- **Q:** Area of a 3/4 in × 2/3 in rectangle?
  **A:** 1/2 square inch.
- **Q:** Area of a 1 1/2 ft × 2/3 ft rectangle?
  **A:** 3/2 × 2/3 = 1 square foot.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Mixed numbers
*Student:* Multiply 8 mixed number problems like 2 1/2 x 1 1/3 using area models with partial products.

*How to teach:* Draw a 2 1/2 by 1 1/3 area model split into four parts: 2 × 1, 2 × 1/3, 1/2 × 1, 1/2 × 1/3. The child solves 8 problems and checks with improper fractions.

- **Q:** 2 1/2 × 1 1/3 = ?
  **A:** 2 + 2/3 + 1/2 + 1/6 = 3 1/3 (also 5/2 × 4/3 = 20/6 = 3 1/3).

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a quiz on multiplying fractions and finding areas with fractional sides.

*How to teach:* Give the quiz independently. Score it with the assessment key, then have your child rework each missed problem aloud, naming the error, before you move on.

- **Q:** 3/8 × 2/5 = ?
  **A:** 6/40 = 3/20

**Assessment:** Quiz on fraction multiplication and fractional area.

**Answer key:** Suggested key: 1) 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/8 2) 3/4 × 1/3 = 1/4 3) 2/5 × 5/6 = 1/3 4) 5/8 × 4/5 = 1/2 5) 1 1/2 × 2/3 = 1 6) 3 × 1 1/4 = 3 3/4 7) 2 1/3 × 1 1/2 = 3 1/2 8) area 4/5 m × 1/2 m = 2/5 sq m 9) area 2 1/4 ft × 2 ft = 4 1/2 sq ft.

## Science 5: Introducing Earth's four spheres

**Prepare:** Clipboard, colored pencils, NASA Earth images; NASA Space Place video on Earth's systems.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking people aren't part of the biosphere.
- Mixing up atmosphere and hydrosphere for clouds. Clouds are water (hydrosphere) in the atmosphere.
- Thinking spheres work separately.

### Day 1 (Monday): Four spheres
*Student:* Watch a NASA Space Place video on Earth's systems and create a four-quadrant chart with examples of each sphere.

*How to teach:* Watch the video; your child makes a four-quadrant chart: geosphere (rocks, soil), hydrosphere (water, ice), atmosphere (air), biosphere (living things). Watch together and pause after each sphere. Your child adds two examples to each quadrant.

- **Q:** Name the four spheres with an example each.
  **A:** Geosphere: rocks; hydrosphere: ocean; atmosphere: air; biosphere: trees.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Outdoor survey
*Student:* Survey a yard or park and list 10 things you observe, labeling each with its sphere.

*How to teach:* Survey a yard or park; list 10 things and label each sphere. Walk the yard with a clipboard. Your child writes 10 observations and labels each sphere.

- **Q:** Which sphere is a puddle? A bird?
  **A:** Hydrosphere; biosphere.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Finding interactions
*Student:* Identify 5 interactions from the survey (rain soaking soil, roots breaking rock) and draw arrows between spheres.

*How to teach:* Identify 5 interactions and draw arrows. Model one interaction with an arrow and label. Your child adds 5 interactions from the survey.

- **Q:** Example interaction.
  **A:** Rain (atmosphere/hydrosphere) soaks soil (geosphere).

### Day 4 (Thursday): Satellite views
*Student:* Examine NASA satellite images of a hurricane and a wildfire and identify which spheres are interacting.

*How to teach:* Examine hurricane and wildfire images; identify interacting spheres. View the images together. Ask which spheres are causing changes and which are being changed.

- **Q:** Which spheres interact in a hurricane?
  **A:** Warm ocean water (hydrosphere) evaporates into the atmosphere, fueling storms that affect land and living things.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a spheres quiz and explain one interaction in a paragraph.

*How to teach:* Give the spheres quiz; your child explains one interaction in a paragraph. Give the quiz and have your child write a paragraph explaining one interaction with cause and effect.

- **Q:** How do plant roots affect rocks?
  **A:** Roots (biosphere) grow into cracks and break rocks (geosphere).

**Assessment:** Sphere interaction chart and quiz.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: geosphere = rock/soil; hydrosphere = water; atmosphere = air; biosphere = life. Interaction chart: 5 arrows labeled. Paragraph: names 2 spheres and explains cause and effect.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Taxation without representation

**Prepare:** Image of a 1765 tax stamp; Stamp Act broadside (Library of Congress).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking taxes were very high.
- Thinking all colonists protested.
- Confusing Stamp Act and Townshend Acts.

### Day 1 (Monday): Britain's debt
*Student:* Role-play Parliament and colonists debating who should pay for the French and Indian War.

*How to teach:* Role-play Parliament and colonists. Your child argues both sides.

- **Q:** Britain's argument?
  **A:** The war protected colonists, so they should help pay.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Stamp Act
*Student:* Examine an image of a tax stamp and a protest broadside and list items that were taxed.

*How to teach:* Examine the tax stamp and broadside. Your child lists taxed items.

- **Q:** What was taxed?
  **A:** Newspapers, legal documents, playing cards.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Resistance
*Student:* Read about the Stamp Act Congress, boycotts and the Sons and Daughters of Liberty.

*How to teach:* Read about the Stamp Act Congress, boycotts and the Sons and Daughters of Liberty. Ask 'How do you know?' and have your child point to the source or map for each answer.

- **Q:** What did the Daughters of Liberty do?
  **A:** Made homespun cloth to boycott British goods.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Townshend Acts
*Student:* Explain the Townshend Acts and the boycott of British goods; calculate the cost of taxed items in a simple chart.

*How to teach:* Explain the Townshend Acts. Your child calculates costs of taxed items.

- **Q:** What was taxed?
  **A:** Glass, lead, paint, paper, tea.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a letter as a colonist to Parliament protesting the taxes using 'no taxation without representation.'

*How to teach:* Your child writes a protest letter using 'no taxation without representation.' Use the assessment key to score it, then reteach any missed idea with the week's sources.

- **Q:** What does 'no taxation without representation' mean?
  **A:** Colonists had no elected members in Parliament.

**Assessment:** Colonist letter and broadside analysis.

**Answer key:** Letter rubric: 4 = persuasive, accurate taxes, uses phrase; 3 = minor; 2 = weak; 1 = incomplete.

## Art & Music 5: Quilts and collaborative design

**Prepare:** Find images of Harriet Powers's Bible Quilt (1886, Smithsonian) and a Gee's Bend quilt. Gather colored paper, glue, ruler, graph paper, and invite family members to each make a square.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking quilts are only for warmth. Many quilts tell stories, preserve history and are considered fine art.
- Thinking unity means everything is the same. Unity is a sense of belonging together; variety adds interest.

### Day 1 (Monday): Quilts as stories
*Student:* Study Harriet Powers's Bible Quilt and a Gee's Bend quilt and discuss how quilts tell stories.

*How to teach:* Study Powers's quilt and a Gee's Bend quilt; discuss storytelling. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** How did Harriet Powers tell stories in her quilt?
  **A:** Appliquéd panels showing Bible stories with figures and symbols.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Block patterns
*Student:* Draw 3 traditional quilt block patterns (Log Cabin, Flying Geese, Star) on graph paper.

*How to teach:* Draw Log Cabin, Flying Geese and Star blocks. Show a quick example, watch your child's first few minutes of work, then step back and check in with one specific suggestion.

- **Q:** What shape is common in Flying Geese?
  **A:** Triangles (a large triangle with two smaller ones).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Original square
*Student:* Combine ideas to design an original paper quilt square that tells something about your family.

*How to teach:* Design an original quilt square telling about your family. Demonstrate the first step briefly, then let your child work independently; coach with one specific tip at a time rather than fixing the work for them.

- **Q:** What principle of design is shown by repeating shapes?
  **A:** Repetition (pattern), which creates rhythm and unity.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Family quilt
*Student:* Join squares from family members into a paper 'quilt' showing unity and variety.

*How to teach:* Join family squares into a paper quilt. Pause often to have your child explain what they see, hear or read in their own words, and write down two or three key points together.

- **Q:** How does the quilt show unity and variety?
  **A:** Common colors or borders unify; different designs add variety.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 assessment
*Student:* Present the quilt square and answer questions about how early American art carried messages.

*How to teach:* Present the square and answer questions on early American art and messages. Set up a simple 'stage' and audience, have your child introduce the work, and hold applause until the end; afterward ask them to name one strength and one thing to improve before you add one specific comment.

- **Q:** Name two early American artworks that carried messages.
  **A:** Revere's Boston Massacre engraving, 'Join, or Die,' Harriet Powers's quilt.

**Assessment:** Quilt square and Unit 5 reflection.

**Answer key:** Quilt square rubric: 4 = original design, clear family story, neat craftsmanship, uses repetition/symmetry; 3 = minor issues; 2 = unclear story; 1 = incomplete. Unit reflection: explains how prints, portraits and quilts carried messages with examples.

## PE 5: Basketball games and fair play

**Prepare:** Gather a basketball and hoop. Review the rules: traveling (moving pivot foot or walking without dribbling), double dribble (dribbling with two hands or dribbling again after stopping), fouls (illegal contact like pushing or hitting arms). Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking any contact is a foul. Incidental contact is normal; a foul is illegal contact that gives an advantage (pushing, holding, hitting).
- Arguing calls. Use respectful words and agreed methods to settle disputes.

### Day 1 (Monday): Rules
*Student:* Learn traveling, double dribble and fouls and practice calling them fairly.

*How to teach:* Act out traveling, double dribble and a foul; your child names each, then calls them fairly during play.

- **Q:** You stop dribbling, then start dribbling again. What violation is that?
  **A:** Double dribble.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Skill stations
*Student:* Rotate through dribble, pass and shoot stations as a warm-up, then play 3-on-3.

*How to teach:* Rotate through 3-minute stations (dribble, pass, shoot) then play 3-on-3 or 1-on-1. Set clear boundaries and a safety reminder before starting, keep rounds short so everyone stays active, and finish by asking what strategy worked best.

- **Q:** Name two violations you watched for in the game.
  **A:** Any two: traveling, double dribble, fouls, out of bounds.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Modified game
*Student:* Play a modified game where every player must receive a pass before a shot.

*How to teach:* Play with the rule that every player must receive a pass before a shot. Model it once yourself (or show a short video), let your child try, and give one specific cue-based correction before the next attempt; finish with a full run-through.

- **Q:** How does the 'everyone receives a pass' rule help the team?
  **A:** It involves all players and builds passing and teamwork.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Conflict resolution
*Student:* Role-play resolving three common game disputes respectfully.

*How to teach:* Role-play three disputes (ball out of bounds? foul? who gets the ball?) and practice calm solutions: listen, state your view, agree on a fair fix (redo, rock-paper-scissors).

- **Q:** What's a respectful way to settle a disputed call?
  **A:** Calmly explain what you saw, listen, then agree on a fair solution like a redo or rock-paper-scissors.

### Day 5 (Friday): Unit 5 assessment
*Student:* Complete a basketball skills test (dribble, pass, shoot) and a sportsmanship self-rating.

*How to teach:* Skills test: dribble the cone course, 10 passes to a target, 10 close shots. Your child completes a sportsmanship self-rating.

- **Q:** What does fair play look like?
  **A:** Following rules, calling your own violations honestly, encouraging others, and respecting opponents and calls.

**Assessment:** Basketball skills test and sportsmanship self-rating.

**Answer key:** Skills test: dribble (both hands, control), passes (hits target 7+/10 proficient), shooting (BEEF form; makes 4+/10 close shots proficient). Sportsmanship self-rating 1–4 on: follows rules, honest calls, encourages others, accepts results; child gives one example for each.

## Health 5: Menstruation and other normal changes

**Prepare:** Prepare a simple menstrual-cycle diagram (28-day circle) and optionally samples of pads, tampons, and period underwear. Preview AMAZE videos on periods and on erections/nocturnal emissions. PARENT PREVIEW: watch the videos and read materials before your child does; decide how you'll answer personal or values questions, and keep the anonymous question box open.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking periods are dirty or a sign of illness. Menstruation is a normal, healthy part of the cycle.
- Thinking wet dreams or erections mean something is wrong. They are normal and involuntary during puberty.

### Day 1 (Monday): Menstrual cycle
*Student:* Watch an AMAZE video and draw a simple 28-day cycle diagram with labels.

*How to teach:* Watch the video; draw a 28-day cycle: days ~1–5 menstruation, lining builds, ovulation around day 14, lining sheds if no pregnancy.

- **Q:** What is menstruation?
  **A:** The monthly shedding of the uterine lining through the vagina when no pregnancy occurs.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Period supplies
*Student:* Learn about pads, tampons and period underwear, and pack a period-preparedness kit list.

*How to teach:* Learn about pads, tampons and period underwear; list items for a preparedness kit (pads, spare underwear, wipes, a small bag).

- **Q:** What should go in a period-preparedness kit?
  **A:** Pads (or other products), spare underwear, wipes, and a small bag.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Male changes
*Student:* Read about voice changes, erections and nocturnal emissions as normal parts of puberty.

*How to teach:* Read about voice changes, erections and nocturnal emissions as normal. Go through it once together, then a second time while your child notes what they observe; prompt with 'What do you notice? What makes you say that?'

- **Q:** What is a nocturnal emission?
  **A:** Release of semen during sleep ('wet dream'); a normal, involuntary part of puberty.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Hygiene connection
*Student:* Write a hygiene plan for managing periods or sweat, odor and shaving.

*How to teach:* Write a hygiene plan for periods or for sweat, odor and shaving. Let your child draft independently, then read it together and ask for one more specific detail or example wherever the answer is vague.

- **Q:** How often should pads be changed?
  **A:** Every 4–6 hours or as needed.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Answer a myth-or-fact quiz on puberty changes and discuss with a parent.

*How to teach:* Myth-or-fact quiz; discuss with a parent. Give it without notes, then review each missed item together using the answer key below and have your child correct it in their own words.

- **Q:** Myth or fact: You can't exercise during your period.
  **A:** Myth — exercise is fine and can even ease cramps.

**Assessment:** Myth-or-fact quiz.

**Answer key:** Myth/fact key: Periods are normal — fact; Cycle is usually about 21–35 days (often ~28) — fact; You can't swim during a period — myth; Wet dreams are normal — fact; Voice cracks are temporary — fact; Only girls have hormones — myth. Proficient = 80%.
