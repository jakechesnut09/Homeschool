# Teacher's Guide — 5th Grade, Week 1

## ELA 5: Reading 'Eleven' by Sandra Cisneros; writer's notebook launch

**Prepare:** Find 'Eleven' by Sandra Cisneros (from Woman Hollering Creek, or an anthology) and read it yourself first; it is short (about 3 pages). Gather a composition notebook and highlighters. Have a timer ready for the 1-minute fluency read.

**Watch for:**
- Treating a quotation as evidence without explaining it. Teach 'quote, then explain': follow every quotation with 'This shows…'.
- Copying words without quotation marks or changing the wording. Evidence in quotation marks must match the text exactly.
- Confusing 'because' (conjunction joining a reason) with a sentence fragment ('Because she was sad.'). A 'because' clause needs a main clause attached.

### Day 1 (Monday): Narrator's voice
*Student:* Read 'Eleven' aloud together and highlight every simile Rachel uses for being eleven; discuss what the comparisons reveal about her feelings.

*How to teach:* Read 'Eleven' aloud, then reread the opening while your child highlights each comparison Rachel makes about growing older (an onion, the rings inside a tree trunk, little wooden dolls that fit one inside the other). Ask what the comparisons have in common and what they show about how she feels on her birthday.

- **Q:** What does Rachel compare growing older to, and what does that show?
  **A:** An onion, the rings inside a tree trunk, and nesting wooden dolls — each year sits inside the next. It shows she feels that all her younger ages (like three and ten) are still inside her, which is why she can feel little and cry even though she is eleven.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Quoting evidence
*Student:* Answer 4 inference questions about Rachel and Mrs. Price, quoting exact sentences with quotation marks; mini-lesson on conjunctions (and, but, because).

*How to teach:* Model one inference question with an exact quotation ('Mrs. Price says…') and explain it. Your child answers 4 inference questions, copying sentences word for word inside quotation marks. Mini-lesson: and joins, but contrasts, because gives a reason; have them write one sentence with each.

- **Q:** How does Mrs. Price treat Rachel? Quote the text.
  **A:** She does not believe Rachel: she insists the ugly red sweater is Rachel's because Sylvia Saldívar said so, puts it on Rachel's desk and makes her put it on. Strong answers quote her words that she remembers Rachel wearing it, or her order to put it on.
- **Q:** Join with a conjunction: Rachel cried. The sweater wasn't hers.
  **A:** Rachel cried because the sweater wasn't hers.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Writer's notebook
*Student:* Set up a writer's notebook and create a 'heart map' and 'memory timeline' of 10 small moments that could become narratives.

*How to teach:* Show your own notebook page: a heart drawn and filled with people, places and things you care about. Your child makes a heart map and a timeline of 10 small moments (a few minutes long each, not whole vacations) to use for narratives.

- **Q:** Which is a 'small moment': 'my summer vacation' or 'the second my dog ran into the lake'?
  **A:** The second one — a narrative works best focused on a short, specific event.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Discussion and fluency
*Student:* Hold a family book discussion on 'Is Rachel's reaction fair?' using sentence stems, then do a 1-minute fluency read of a passage and record words per minute.

*How to teach:* Discuss 'Is Rachel's reaction fair?' using stems such as 'I agree/disagree because on page __ it says…'. Then have your child read a passage aloud for exactly 1 minute; count words read correctly and record the WPM as a baseline.

- **Q:** Why doesn't Rachel just tell Mrs. Price clearly that it isn't hers?
  **A:** She is a child and Mrs. Price is the adult in charge; she feels small and her younger ages take over, so she can only get out a few words before she starts to cry.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a paragraph explaining how first-person point of view affects the story, with two quotations, and complete a conjunction identification quiz.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a paragraph explaining how first-person point of view (Rachel telling her own story) affects the story, with two quotations. Then give a 10-sentence conjunction quiz (circle the conjunction, label its job).

- **Q:** How does first person affect the story?
  **A:** We hear Rachel's private thoughts — what she wishes she had said and her wish to be 102 — so we feel her embarrassment directly; we only see Mrs. Price through Rachel's eyes.

**Assessment:** Evidence paragraph scored for accurate quoting; conjunction quiz; baseline fluency WPM.

**Answer key:** Evidence paragraph rubric: 4 = clear claim about point of view, two exact quotations with correct quotation marks, each explained; 3 = claim and two quotations with partial explanation; 2 = one quotation or paraphrase only; 1 = no evidence. Conjunction quiz: student circles and labels and (adds), but (contrasts), because (reason), or (choice), so (result) correctly in 8 of 10 sentences for proficiency. Fluency: record WPM (typical fall grade 5 is roughly 110–125 WPM).

## Math 5: Place value relationships to millions and thousandths

**Prepare:** Print or draw a place value chart from millions to thousandths; gather place value disks (or paper squares labeled 1,000,000 down to 0.001) and a whiteboard. Prepare a list of 10 numbers to dictate, e.g. 4,382,615.207.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking the decimal point moves when multiplying by 10. Show on the chart that the digits shift one place left while the point stays put.
- Reading 0.07 as 'seven tenths.' Have the child say the name of the place of the last digit: 7 hundredths.
- Believing a longer decimal is larger. Compare values with disks: 0.4 (four tenths disks) beats 0.399.

### Day 1 (Monday): Place value chart
*Student:* Build a place value chart from millions to thousandths and write 10 numbers dictated aloud into it, naming the value of each underlined digit.

*How to teach:* Draw the chart together, naming each place aloud from millions to thousandths. Dictate 10 numbers; the child writes each in the chart and names the value of the digit you underline. Check that zeros hold empty places.

- **Q:** In 4,382,615.207, what is the value of the 8? Of the 7?
  **A:** 8 is in the ten-thousands place, so 80,000. 7 is in the thousandths place, so 7 thousandths (0.007).
- **Q:** Write 'two million, fifty thousand, three and six hundredths' in standard form.
  **A:** 2,050,003.06

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Ten times as much
*Student:* Use place value disks to show 0.4 becoming 4 and 40 when multiplied by 10; record 8 'times 10' statements with a diagram showing digits shifting left.

*How to teach:* Lay out 4 tenths disks, then show 10 groups of them: trade every 10 tenths for a one to get 4 ones, then repeat to get 40. The child records 8 'times 10' statements with arrows showing each digit moving one place left.

- **Q:** What is 0.36 × 10? Explain with place value.
  **A:** 3.6 — the 3 tenths become 3 ones and the 6 hundredths become 6 tenths; each digit moves one place left.
- **Q:** 0.4 × 10 × 10 = ?
  **A:** 40

### Day 3 (Wednesday): One tenth of
*Student:* Model dividing 300, 30 and 3 by 10 with disks (trading for smaller units); write 8 '1/10 of' statements such as 0.07 is 1/10 of 0.7.

*How to teach:* Model 300 ÷ 10 by trading each hundred disk for 10 tens and sharing into 10 groups (30 each); repeat for 30 and 3 (3 becomes 30 tenths, so 0.3 each). The child writes 8 '1/10 of' statements and draws digits shifting one place right.

- **Q:** What is 1/10 of 0.7?
  **A:** 0.07 (7 hundredths).
- **Q:** What is 1/10 of 30? Of 0.5?
  **A:** 3; 0.05.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Comparing digit values
*Student:* In numbers like 555.555, compare the value of each 5 to its neighbors and solve 10 'how many times greater' problems.

*How to teach:* Write 555.555 and have the child give the value of each 5 (500, 50, 5, 0.5, 0.05, 0.005). Ask how each compares to its neighbor: 10 times the one on its right, 1/10 the one on its left. Then work the 10 'how many times greater' problems.

- **Q:** In 555.555, how many times greater is the 5 in the tens place than the 5 in the tenths place?
  **A:** 100 times (50 is 100 × 0.5).
- **Q:** In 7,707, how does the value of the first 7 compare to the second 7?
  **A:** The first 7 is 7,000 and the second is 700, so it is 10 times greater.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a 12-question quiz on place value relationships and write one sentence explaining why the decimal point never moves (digits do).

*How to teach:* Give the 12-question quiz below without help, then have the child write one sentence explaining why the decimal point stays fixed. Correct together using disks or the chart.

- **Q:** Why does the decimal point never move when you multiply by 10?
  **A:** The decimal point marks the boundary between ones and tenths; multiplying by 10 makes each digit worth 10 times as much, so the digits shift one place left past the fixed point.

**Assessment:** Friday quiz: 12 items on place value of whole numbers and decimals plus a written explanation of the 10x relationship.

**Answer key:** Suggested quiz and key: 1) Value of 6 in 2,465,301 = 60,000. 2) Value of 3 in 7.034 = 0.03 (3 hundredths). 3) 0.9 × 10 = 9. 4) 0.05 × 10 = 0.5. 5) 1/10 of 400 = 40. 6) 1/10 of 0.2 = 0.02. 7) 6 × 100 = 600. 8) In 33.3, the 3 in the ones place is 10 times the 3 in the tenths place. 9) In 444, the first 4 is 100 times the last 4. 10) 0.008 is 1/10 of 0.08. 11) 70 is 10 times 7. 12) Standard form of 5 million 4 thousand 2 hundredths = 5,004,000.02. Written explanation (proficient): each place is worth 10 times the place to its right, so ×10 shifts digits one place left (÷10 one place right) while the decimal point stays fixed between ones and tenths.

## Science 5: The mystery of disappearing sugar: evidence for particles

**Prepare:** Sugar, clear cups, warm water, kitchen scale (grams), spoons, a dark plate, and a science notebook. Test the scale first; tare it with the empty cup.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking dissolved sugar is gone or 'melted.' Dissolving means sugar particles spread out among water particles; it is still there (you can taste it).
- Thinking dissolved things weigh nothing. The mixture weighs the water plus the sugar.
- Confusing dissolving with melting. Melting needs heat and changes one substance from solid to liquid.

### Day 1 (Monday): Where did it go?
*Student:* Stir a spoonful of sugar into warm water, observe it 'disappear,' then taste a drop and record observations and questions in a science notebook.

*How to teach:* Stir a spoonful of sugar into warm water until you can't see it. Your child tastes a drop and writes observations and at least two questions in the notebook. Ask: 'If you can't see it, how do you know it's there?'

- **Q:** The sugar 'disappeared.' Is it still in the water? How do you know?
  **A:** Yes — the water tastes sweet, so sugar particles are spread throughout the water, too small to see.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Weighing the evidence
*Student:* Weigh water and sugar separately, mix them, and weigh again; record data in a table and discuss what it shows.

*How to teach:* Weigh the water, weigh the sugar, then mix and weigh again. Record in a table and compare the mixture's weight with the sum.

- **Q:** Water 200 g and sugar 20 g are mixed. Predict the weight of the sugar water.
  **A:** 220 g — mass is conserved when sugar dissolves.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Evaporation clue
*Student:* Put a few drops of the sugar water on a dark plate in a sunny window to evaporate and predict what will be left.

*How to teach:* Place a few drops of sugar water on a dark plate in a sunny window. Your child writes a prediction of what will remain after the water evaporates and why.

- **Q:** What do you predict will be left on the plate?
  **A:** Sugar crystals — only the water evaporates; the sugar particles stay behind.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Modeling particles
*Student:* Check the evaporated plate for sugar crystals and draw a particle model of sugar dissolving in water, before and after.

*How to teach:* Check the plate with a magnifier for crystals. Your child draws a before-and-after particle model: sugar particles clumped in a crystal, then spread among water particles.

- **Q:** What does the crystal residue tell us?
  **A:** The sugar was in the water the whole time; when water particles evaporated, sugar particles came back together as crystals.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Write a claim-evidence-reasoning (CER) paragraph answering 'Is the sugar still there?' using weight and evaporation data.

*How to teach:* Your child writes a CER paragraph answering 'Is the sugar still there?' using the weight data and evaporation evidence. Review it with the rubric.

- **Q:** Give a model claim and the two pieces of evidence.
  **A:** Claim: The sugar is still in the water as particles too small to see. Evidence: the mixture weighed the same as the water plus the sugar, and sugar crystals were left after evaporation.

**Assessment:** CER paragraph and particle model drawing.

**Answer key:** CER rubric: 4 = clear, accurate claim; specific evidence with numbers from the data; reasoning that links evidence to the science idea (particles, conservation, etc.); 3 = accurate claim and evidence, reasoning partial; 2 = claim with vague evidence; 1 = claim only or inaccurate. Particle model: before = sugar particles packed together in a crystal, water separate; after = sugar particles evenly spread among water particles; labels and a key.

## United States History and Geography: Making a New Nation: Geography of North America and map skills

**Prepare:** Print blank outline maps of North America and the U.S., gather an atlas (or online map), colored pencils, and make Northeast state/capital flashcards.

**Watch for:**
- Mixing up latitude and longitude. Latitude lines run east–west but measure north–south ('lat is flat'); longitude lines run pole to pole.
- Reading the map scale as exact distance without measuring. Use a ruler or paper strip against the scale bar.
- Thinking New York City is New York's capital. The capital is Albany.

### Day 1 (Monday): Map skills
*Student:* Review map parts (title, compass rose, scale, legend) and find 5 places using latitude and longitude on a world map.

*How to teach:* Point to each map part (title, compass rose, scale, legend) and ask what it tells us. Model finding a place at a latitude/longitude by tracing the two lines with two fingers until they meet, then your child finds 5 places.

- **Q:** What city is near 34°N, 118°W?
  **A:** Los Angeles, California.
- **Q:** What does a map legend do?
  **A:** Explains what the symbols and colors on the map mean.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Landforms
*Student:* Label the Rocky Mountains, Appalachian Mountains, Great Plains, Mississippi River, Great Lakes and coasts on an outline map of North America.

*How to teach:* Name each landform as you trace it on the atlas, then your child labels the outline map. Ask how each landform might help or block travel.

- **Q:** Which mountains are older and lower, the Rockies or the Appalachians?
  **A:** The Appalachians, in the East; the Rockies in the West are higher and more rugged.
- **Q:** Into what body of water does the Mississippi River flow?
  **A:** The Gulf of Mexico.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Climate regions
*Student:* Shade climate and vegetation regions (desert, forest, grassland, tundra) and write how each might affect how people live.

*How to teach:* Your child shades desert, forest, grassland and tundra regions using the atlas and writes one sentence per region about how people might live there (housing, food, clothing). Ask 'How do you know?' and have your child point to the source or map for each answer.

- **Q:** How might living in the desert Southwest differ from living in the Eastern forests?
  **A:** Desert peoples must conserve water and build with clay and stone; forest peoples have wood for homes and abundant game and plants.

### Day 4 (Thursday): States strand: Northeast
*Student:* Begin the 50-states strand by learning the 11 Northeast states and capitals with a blank map and flashcards.

*How to teach:* Introduce the 11 Northeast states on a blank map, saying each state and capital aloud. Play flashcards in short rounds, keeping missed cards in a 'practice' pile.

- **Q:** What is the capital of Pennsylvania? Of Vermont?
  **A:** Harrisburg; Montpelier.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Take a map quiz on North American landforms and Northeast states and capitals.

*How to teach:* Give the map quiz without the atlas. Review any missed items by having your child find them again on the atlas.

- **Q:** Name the large flat region between the Mississippi River and the Rocky Mountains.
  **A:** The Great Plains.

**Assessment:** Map quiz on landforms, latitude/longitude and Northeast states.

**Answer key:** Quiz key: landforms correctly labeled (Rockies, Appalachians, Great Plains, Mississippi River, Great Lakes, Atlantic and Pacific coasts); latitude/longitude items located within a few degrees; Northeast capitals: Maine–Augusta, New Hampshire–Concord, Vermont–Montpelier, Massachusetts–Boston, Rhode Island–Providence, Connecticut–Hartford, New York–Albany, New Jersey–Trenton, Pennsylvania–Harrisburg, Delaware–Dover, Maryland–Annapolis.

## Art & Music 5: Line and contour drawing

**Prepare:** Gather a sketchbook, pencils, a fine-tip marker, and a few objects with interesting edges (a shoe, a houseplant, a kitchen whisk). Set a timer for gesture drawings. Contour = a line drawing of edges; blind contour = drawing while looking only at the object.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking blind contour drawings should look 'right.' They are meant to look wobbly — the goal is training the eye to look closely, not a realistic picture.
- Drawing slowly and stiffly in gesture drawing. Gesture is fast, loose and about the action/movement, not details.

### Day 1 (Monday): Line sampler
*Student:* Fill a sketchbook page with 12 line types (zigzag, hatched, wavy, broken) and label the mood each suggests.

*How to teach:* Show examples of line types and talk about moods (zigzag = energetic or tense, wavy = calm, broken = uncertain). Your child fills a page with 12 labeled line types.

- **Q:** What mood might a thick, jagged zigzag line suggest? A thin, wavy line?
  **A:** Zigzag: excitement, anger, danger or energy. Wavy: calm, gentle, flowing movement.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Blind contour
*Student:* Draw your own hand three times with blind contour, never looking at the paper.

*How to teach:* Your child places their non-drawing hand in a pose and draws it three times without looking at the paper, moving the pen slowly as the eyes trace each edge.

- **Q:** Why draw without looking at the paper?
  **A:** It forces you to look carefully at the object's edges and connects the eye and hand, improving observation.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Modified contour
*Student:* Draw a shoe or houseplant with modified contour, glancing at the paper only occasionally.

*How to teach:* Draw a shoe or plant with modified contour: mostly look at the object, glance at the paper only to place the pen at a new edge.

- **Q:** How is modified contour different from blind contour?
  **A:** In modified contour you can glance at the paper occasionally to check placement; in blind contour you never look.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Gesture drawing
*Student:* Do ten 30-second gesture drawings of a family member in action poses.

*How to teach:* Have a family member strike action poses for 30 seconds each; your child draws the whole body quickly with flowing lines, starting with the line of action (spine).

- **Q:** What is the goal of a gesture drawing?
  **A:** To capture the movement and overall pose quickly, not the details.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Choose your best contour drawing and write two sentences using art vocabulary about its line quality.

*How to teach:* Your child picks the best contour drawing and writes two sentences with art vocabulary (line weight, contour, continuous, varied).

- **Q:** What is line quality?
  **A:** The character of a line — thick/thin, light/dark, smooth/rough — that gives a drawing expression.

**Assessment:** Sketchbook check of contour and gesture drawings with written reflection.

**Answer key:** Sketchbook check: line sampler with 12 labeled lines and moods; 3 blind contours; 1 modified contour; 10 gestures. Reflection rubric: 4 = two sentences using at least two art terms (contour, line weight, gesture) accurately about a specific drawing; 3 = one term; 2 = general description; 1 = missing.

## PE 5: Warm-ups, safety and the five components of fitness

**Prepare:** Gather cones, a timer and a water bottle; mark a play area about 15 × 15 m. Write the five fitness components on index cards with their definitions on separate cards for matching. Before each session: check the space for hazards, have water ready, wear athletic shoes, and stop if your child shows signs of heat illness (dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps).

**Watch for:**
- Thinking stretching means holding a stretch before exercise. Before activity use moving (dynamic) stretches; save held (static) stretches for the cool-down when muscles are warm.
- Confusing muscular strength with muscular endurance. Strength is how much force a muscle can exert once (a heavy lift); endurance is how many times or how long it can keep working (many curl-ups).

### Day 1 (Monday): Dynamic warm-up
*Student:* Learn a 5-minute dynamic warm-up (high knees, butt kicks, arm circles, lunges, skips) and play a tag game.

*How to teach:* Demonstrate each warm-up move for about 30 seconds (high knees, butt kicks, arm circles, walking lunges, skips) with one cue each, e.g. 'knees up to hip height.' Your child copies, then play 5 minutes of tag.

- **Q:** Why do we warm up before exercise?
  **A:** To raise heart rate and muscle temperature and loosen joints so the body is ready to work and less likely to be injured.

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Components of fitness
*Student:* Do one station for each fitness component (jog, push-ups, plank, stretch) and match each to its definition.

*How to teach:* Set up four stations (jog in place, push-ups, plank, sit-and-reach stretch), 1 minute each. Afterwards your child matches each component card to its definition and names the station that trained it.

- **Q:** Which station trained aerobic capacity, and what does that term mean?
  **A:** The jog — aerobic capacity is how well the heart and lungs deliver oxygen to working muscles over a long time.
- **Q:** What is body composition?
  **A:** The proportion of fat to lean tissue (muscle, bone, organs) in the body.

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Safety and space
*Student:* Set up a safe activity area, review safety rules and play 'Sharks and Minnows' using space awareness.

*How to teach:* Walk the space together and point out hazards (sprinklers, curbs, rocks); set boundary cones. Review rules: stay in bounds, look up, call 'stop' if someone gets hurt. Play Sharks and Minnows, coaching your child to move into open space.

- **Q:** Name two things you check before playing in a space.
  **A:** Any two: hazards on the ground (holes, rocks, glass), clear boundaries away from streets or walls, enough room between players, water and shade available.

### Day 4 (Thursday): Cool-down
*Student:* Learn a static stretching cool-down for major muscle groups after a 10-minute jog-walk.

*How to teach:* Jog-walk for 10 minutes, then lead a 5-minute static cool-down: hamstrings, quadriceps, calves, chest/shoulders, hip flexors, holding each 15–30 seconds without bouncing.

- **Q:** How long should you hold a cool-down stretch, and should you bounce?
  **A:** About 15–30 seconds, gently to mild tension, with no bouncing.

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Lead the warm-up for a family member and explain the five components of fitness.

*How to teach:* Your child leads you (or a sibling) through the warm-up, giving each move's cue. Then ask them to name and define all five components.

- **Q:** Name the five components of health-related fitness.
  **A:** Aerobic capacity, muscular strength, muscular endurance, flexibility, and body composition.

**Assessment:** Student-led warm-up and oral check of fitness components.

**Answer key:** Warm-up (4 = leads all five moves in order with clear cues and correct form; 3 = one move or cue missing; 2 = needs several prompts; 1 = cannot lead). Oral check: aerobic capacity = heart/lungs supplying oxygen over time; muscular strength = maximum force at one time; muscular endurance = working many times without tiring; flexibility = range of motion at a joint; body composition = ratio of fat to lean mass. Proficient = all five named and four or more defined correctly.

## Health 5: Health skills and the wellness triangle

**Prepare:** Get a health journal and markers. Print or write the eight California health education standards (essential concepts, analyzing influences, accessing valid information, interpersonal communication, decision making, goal setting, practicing health-enhancing behaviors, health promotion/advocacy). Prepare a bedtime-vs-video-game scenario card.

**Watch for:**
- Thinking health only means not being sick. Health has physical, mental/emotional and social sides, and all three affect each other.
- Writing vague goals ('eat better'). A SMART goal is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.

### Day 1 (Monday): Wellness triangle
*Student:* Draw a wellness triangle and list three current habits on each side; star the strongest side.

*How to teach:* Draw a triangle labeled physical, mental/emotional and social. Your child lists three current habits on each side and stars the strongest side; ask how a weak side affects the others.

- **Q:** Name the three sides of the wellness triangle and give a habit for each.
  **A:** Physical (e.g., sleeping 9–10 hours), mental/emotional (e.g., journaling when upset), social (e.g., playing with friends, getting along with family).

### Day 2 (Tuesday): Eight health skills
*Student:* Read a one-page list of the eight CA health skills (access info, analyze influences, communicate, decide, set goals, practice, advocate) and match each to a real-life example.

*How to teach:* Read the list of health skills together. Your child matches each skill to a real example (e.g., 'reading a cereal label' = accessing valid information).

- **Q:** Which health skill is it when you ask your doctor whether a vitamin is safe?
  **A:** Accessing valid information (also uses communication).

### Day 3 (Wednesday): Decision steps
*Student:* Learn a 5-step decision model (state the problem, list options, weigh consequences, decide, evaluate) and apply it to a bedtime vs. video-game scenario.

*How to teach:* Teach the 5 steps, then walk through the scenario: it's bedtime but you want to finish a level. Your child lists options and consequences aloud before deciding.

- **Q:** List the five decision steps in order.
  **A:** 1) State the problem 2) List options 3) Weigh consequences 4) Decide and act 5) Evaluate the result.

### Day 4 (Thursday): SMART goal
*Student:* Write one SMART health goal for the first month (e.g., drink water instead of soda at lunch 5 days a week) and make a tracking chart.

*How to teach:* Explain SMART. Your child writes one health goal for the month and makes a tracking chart with boxes for each day.

- **Q:** Make 'drink more water' a SMART goal.
  **A:** Example: 'I will drink water instead of soda at lunch 5 days a week for the next 4 weeks and check off each day on my chart.'

### Day 5 (Friday): Review & check
*Student:* Explain the wellness triangle and decision steps to a parent and complete a 6-question quick check.

*How to teach:* Your child teaches you the triangle and decision steps, then answers a 6-question quick check orally or on paper.

- **Q:** Why should you evaluate your decision at the end?
  **A:** To learn whether it worked out and what you'd do differently next time.

**Assessment:** Quick check and SMART goal chart.

**Answer key:** Quick check answers: 1) Physical, mental/emotional, social 2) Example habit for each side 3) Decision steps: problem, options, consequences, decide, evaluate 4) SMART = Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound 5) A health skill example such as reading labels = accessing information 6) Health sides affect each other (e.g., poor sleep → grumpy → arguments). SMART goal chart rubric: 4 = all five SMART parts plus a daily tracking chart; 3 = missing one part; 2 = vague goal; 1 = missing.
