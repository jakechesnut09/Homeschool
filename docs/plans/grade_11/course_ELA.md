# English 11 – American Literature (Grade 11)

A chronological survey of American literature from Puritan writing through contemporary voices, read alongside the founding documents and speeches that shaped the nation. Students write rhetorical analyses, literary arguments, a personal narrative suitable for college applications, and a sustained research paper, with grammar and vocabulary woven into each week. Seminars and recitations build formal speaking skills.

*Framework:* California Common Core State Standards for ELA/Literacy (grades 11–12); 2014 California ELA/ELD Framework  
*Time:* 60 min/day
*UC/CSU a–g:* b · *Credits:* 10.0

## Units

### Unit 1: Puritan and Colonial Voices (weeks 1–4)
Early American writing reveals how faith, fear, and community shaped a new society—and how drama can use that past to critique the present.

- Week 1: Origins: Native American and Puritan narratives
- Week 2: Puritan poetry and sermon
- Week 3: The Crucible, Acts I–II
- Week 4: The Crucible, Acts III–IV and literary argument

### Unit 2: The Rhetoric of Revolution (weeks 5–7)
The founders used persuasion and reasoned argument to justify independence and define American ideals.

- Week 5: Franklin and the Enlightenment self
- Week 6: Paine, Henry, and the Declaration
- Week 7: Rhetorical analysis essay: voices excluded and included

### Unit 3: Romanticism and Transcendentalism (weeks 8–11)
American Romantics celebrated the individual, nature, and intuition while Dark Romantics probed sin and the irrational.

- Week 8: Romantic fiction: Irving and Poe
- Week 9: Transcendentalism: Emerson and Thoreau
- Week 10: Dark Romanticism: Hawthorne and Melville
- Week 11: Poetry of a new nation: Whitman and Dickinson

### Unit 4: Voices of Freedom: Slavery and Civil War (weeks 12–15)
Enslaved and free Americans used narrative and oratory to claim the nation's promise of liberty.

- Week 12: Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, Ch. 1–6
- Week 13: Douglass Ch. 7–11 and Harriet Jacobs
- Week 14: Oratory of the Civil War era
- Week 15: Argument essay: the American promise

### Unit 5: Realism and Regionalism (weeks 16–18)
After the Civil War, writers turned to ordinary lives, local speech, and the gap between ideals and reality.

- Week 16: Mark Twain and regional humor
- Week 17: Realist and Naturalist short fiction
- Week 18: Semester review and exam

### Unit 6: Modernism and the Harlem Renaissance (weeks 19–23)
Modern writers fractured old forms to capture disillusionment, the city, and the search for identity and the American Dream.

- Week 19: Modernism and the poetry of disillusionment
- Week 20: The Great Gatsby, Ch. 1–3
- Week 21: The Great Gatsby, Ch. 4–9
- Week 22: The Harlem Renaissance
- Week 23: Hemingway, Faulkner, and the Modern short story

### Unit 7: The Research Paper (weeks 24–26)
Sustained research means asking a real question, weighing sources, and building an original argument with honest citation.

- Week 24: Research: question, sources, and evaluation
- Week 25: Research: notes, outline, and drafting
- Week 26: Research: revision and presentation

### Unit 8: Mid-Century America: Drama and Civil Rights (weeks 27–30)
Postwar drama and civil rights rhetoric tested whether the American Dream was open to all.

- Week 27: A Raisin in the Sun, Acts I–II
- Week 28: A Raisin in the Sun, Act III and performance
- Week 29: Civil rights rhetoric
- Week 30: Personal narrative and the college essay

### Unit 9: Contemporary and Diverse American Voices (weeks 31–34)
Contemporary writers expand who tells the American story, exploring language, memory, war, and belonging.

- Week 31: War and memory: The Things They Carried
- Week 32: Language and identity
- Week 33: Contemporary poetry and Native American voices
- Week 34: Argument and media: the American Dream today

### Unit 10: Synthesis and Final Portfolio (weeks 35–36)
Revisiting the year's texts shows how American literature argues with itself across centuries.

- Week 35: Year-end synthesis and portfolio
- Week 36: Final exam
