# Modern World History (Grade 10)

Grade 10 World History, Culture, and Geography: The Modern World, following CA HSS standards 10.1–10.11. Students trace the ideas of democracy from Athens and the Enlightenment through the Atlantic revolutions, industrialization, imperialism, two world wars, totalitarian regimes, the Cold War, decolonization, and globalization. Each week centers on primary sources (documents, speeches, photographs, maps) analyzed with sourcing and corroboration, building toward document-based essays and a research project.

*Framework:* California History–Social Science Content Standards (Grade 10); 2016 CA History–Social Science Framework  
*Time:* 55 min/day
*UC/CSU a–g:* a · *Credits:* 10.0

## Units

### Unit 1: Foundations of Democratic Thought (weeks 1–2)
Modern democracy draws on ancient philosophy, religious ethics, and law.

- Week 1: Greek and Roman roots of democracy
- Week 2: Judeo-Christian ethics and the spread of constitutional ideas

### Unit 2: The Age of Democratic Revolutions (weeks 3–5)
Enlightenment ideas sparked revolutions that redefined political legitimacy.

- Week 3: The Enlightenment and the English Revolution
- Week 4: The American Revolution and Latin American independence
- Week 5: The French Revolution and Napoleon

### Unit 3: The Industrial Revolution (weeks 6–8)
New energy and machines transformed work, cities, and economic ideas.

- Week 6: Origins of the Industrial Revolution
- Week 7: Industrial society: cities, labor and reform
- Week 8: Capitalism and its critics; global industrialization

### Unit 4: The Age of Imperialism (weeks 9–12)
Industrial powers sought resources and markets, reshaping colonized societies who resisted.

- Week 9: Motives and methods of New Imperialism
- Week 10: The Scramble for Africa
- Week 11: Imperialism in India and Southeast Asia
- Week 12: China, Japan and Latin America; independence movements

### Unit 5: World War I and Its Aftermath (weeks 13–16)
Total war destroyed empires, killed millions, and produced an unstable peace.

- Week 13: Causes of World War I
- Week 14: Fighting the Great War
- Week 15: The Armenian Genocide and human costs
- Week 16: Treaty of Versailles and the postwar world

### Unit 6: The Rise of Totalitarianism (weeks 17–19)
Economic crisis and disillusionment opened the door to dictators.

- Week 17: The Russian Revolution and Stalin
- Week 18: Semester 1 review and exam
- Week 19: Fascism in Italy and Nazi Germany

### Unit 7: World War II and the Holocaust (weeks 20–24)
Aggression, appeasement, and genocide led to history's deadliest war.

- Week 20: Aggression and appeasement
- Week 21: The course of World War II
- Week 22: The Holocaust
- Week 23: Home fronts, the atomic bomb and human costs
- Week 24: WWII assessment

### Unit 8: The Cold War and Decolonization (weeks 25–29)
Two superpowers competed globally as colonies won independence.

- Week 25: Origins of the Cold War
- Week 26: China's revolution and the Cold War in Asia
- Week 27: Decolonization in South Asia, Africa and the Middle East
- Week 28: Cold War crises and Eastern Europe
- Week 29: The end of the Cold War

### Unit 9: Nation-Building in the Contemporary World (weeks 30–32)
New and transformed nations face challenges of freedom, development, and identity.

- Week 30: Nation-building in the Middle East
- Week 31: Nation-building in Africa and Latin America
- Week 32: Nation-building research project

### Unit 10: Globalization and Review (weeks 33–36)
Technology and trade connect the world, raising new opportunities and challenges.

- Week 33: Globalization and the information revolution
- Week 34: Global challenges today
- Week 35: Cumulative review
- Week 36: Final exam

## Standards

- **10.1** Relate the moral and ethical principles in ancient Greek and Roman philosophy, Judaism, and Christianity to the development of Western political thought.
- **10.1.1** Analyze similarities and differences in Judeo-Christian and Greco-Roman views of law, reason and faith, and duties of the individual.
- **10.1.2** Trace the development of the Western political ideas of the rule of law and illegitimacy of tyranny, using Plato's Republic and Aristotle's Politics.
- **10.1.3** Consider the influence of the U.S. Constitution on political systems in the contemporary world.
- **10.10** Analyze instances of nation-building in the contemporary world in at least two of the following regions: Middle East, Africa, Mexico and other parts of Latin America, and China.
- **10.10.1** Understand the challenges in the regions, including their geopolitical, cultural, military, and economic significance and the international relationships in which they are involved.
- **10.10.2** Describe the recent history of the regions, including political divisions and systems, key leaders, religious issues, natural features, resources, and population patterns.
- **10.10.3** Discuss the important trends in the regions today and whether they appear to serve the cause of individual freedom and democracy.
- **10.11** Analyze the integration of countries into the world economy and the information, technological, and communications revolutions (television, satellites, computers).
- **10.2** Compare and contrast the Glorious Revolution of England, the American Revolution, and the French Revolution and their enduring effects worldwide.
- **10.2.1** Compare the major ideas of philosophers (Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Jefferson, Madison) and their effects on democratic revolutions.
- **10.2.2** List the principles of the Magna Carta, English Bill of Rights, Declaration of Independence, and Declaration of the Rights of Man.
- **10.2.3** Understand the unique character of the American Revolution, its spread to Latin America, and its effect on the French Revolution.
- **10.2.4** Explain how the ideology of the French Revolution led France to develop from constitutional monarchy to democratic despotism to the Napoleonic empire.
- **10.2.5** Discuss how nationalism spread across Europe with Napoleon but was repressed for a generation under the Congress of Vienna.
- **10.3** Analyze the effects of the Industrial Revolution in England, France, Germany, Japan, and the United States.
- **10.3.1** Analyze why England was the first country to industrialize.
- **10.3.2** Examine how scientific and technological changes and new forms of energy brought about economic and social change.
- **10.3.3** Describe the growth of population, rural to urban migration, and growth of cities.
- **10.3.4** Trace the evolution of work and labor, including slavery's demise, child labor, unions, and the role of women.
- **10.3.5** Understand the connections among natural resources, entrepreneurship, labor, and capital.
- **10.3.6** Analyze the emergence of capitalism and responses to it, including utopianism, socialism, and communism.
- **10.3.7** Describe the emergence of Romanticism in art and literature and social criticism by writers such as Dickens.
- **10.4** Analyze patterns of global change in the era of New Imperialism in at least two regions (Africa, Southeast Asia, China, India, Latin America, Philippines).
- **10.4.1** Describe the rise of industrial economies and their link to imperialism and colonialism.
- **10.4.2** Discuss the locations of the colonial rule of such nations as England, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, Russia, Spain, Portugal, and the United States.
- **10.4.3** Explain imperialism from the perspective of the colonizers and the colonized and the varied immediate and long-term responses.
- **10.4.4** Describe the independence struggles of the colonized regions, including Gandhi, Sun Yat-sen, and others.
- **10.5** Analyze the causes and course of the First World War.
- **10.5.1** Analyze the arguments for entering into war presented by leaders from all sides of the Great War.
- **10.5.2** Examine the principal theaters of battle and the role of geography, including the Western, Eastern and Ottoman fronts.
- **10.5.3** Explain how the Russian Revolution and the entry of the United States affected the course and outcome of the war.
- **10.5.4** Understand the nature of the war and its human costs on all sides, including the effects of total war on civilians.
- **10.5.5** Discuss human rights violations and genocide, including the Ottoman government's actions against Armenian citizens.
- **10.6** Analyze the effects of the First World War.
- **10.6.1** Analyze the aims and negotiating roles of world leaders, the terms and influence of the Treaty of Versailles, and Wilson's Fourteen Points.
- **10.6.2** Describe the effects of the war and resulting peace treaties on population movement, the international economy, and shifts in the geographic and political borders of Europe and the Middle East.
- **10.6.3** Understand the widespread disillusionment with prewar institutions, authorities, and values that resulted in a void later filled by totalitarians.
- **10.6.4** Discuss the influence of World War I on literature, art, and intellectual life in the West.
- **10.7** Analyze the rise of totalitarian governments after World War I.
- **10.7.1** Understand the causes and consequences of the Russian Revolution, including Lenin's use of totalitarian means.
- **10.7.2** Trace Stalin's rise to power, the collectivization of agriculture, the Ukrainian famine, and the Great Purge.
- **10.7.3** Analyze the rise, aggression, and human costs of totalitarian regimes (Fascist and Communist) in Germany, Italy, and the Soviet Union.
- **10.8** Analyze the causes and consequences of World War II.
- **10.8.1** Compare the German, Italian, and Japanese drives for empire in the 1930s.
- **10.8.2** Understand the role of appeasement, nonintervention, and the domestic distractions in Europe and the U.S. prior to the outbreak of World War II.
- **10.8.3** Identify and locate the Allied and Axis powers on a map and discuss the major turning points of the war.
- **10.8.4** Describe the political, diplomatic, and military leaders during the war.
- **10.8.5** Analyze the Nazi policy of pursuing racial purity, especially against European Jews; its transformation into the Final Solution; and the Holocaust.
- **10.8.6** Discuss the human costs of the war, with particular attention to civilian and military losses in Russia, Germany, Britain, the United States, China, and Japan.
- **10.9** Analyze the international developments in the post–World War II world.
- **10.9.1** Compare the economic and military power shifts caused by the war, including the Yalta Pact, the development of nuclear weapons, Soviet control over Eastern Europe, and the economic recoveries of Germany and Japan.
- **10.9.2** Analyze the causes of the Cold War, with the free world on one side and Soviet client states on the other.
- **10.9.3** Understand the importance of the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan, NATO, the Warsaw Pact, SEATO, and their roles in the Cold War.
- **10.9.4** Analyze the Chinese Civil War, the rise of Mao Tse-tung, and the subsequent political and economic upheavals in China.
- **10.9.5** Describe the uprisings in Poland (1952), Hungary (1956), and Czechoslovakia (1968) and those countries' resurgence in the 1970s and 1980s.
- **10.9.6** Understand how the forces of nationalism developed in the Middle East and how the Holocaust affected world opinion regarding the need for a Jewish state.
- **10.9.7** Analyze the reasons for the collapse of the Soviet Union, including the weakness of the command economy, burdens of military commitments, and growing resistance to Soviet rule.
- **10.9.8** Discuss the establishment and work of the United Nations and the purposes and functions of the Warsaw Pact, SEATO, NATO, and the Organization of American States.
- **HSS-AC** Historical and Social Sciences Analysis Skills (9–12): chronological and spatial thinking; historical research, evidence, and point of view; historical interpretation.
